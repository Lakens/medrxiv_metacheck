Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). Statcheck might still miss one-sided tests that are described in another sentence, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

If you want to extend the package to be able to download files from additional data repositories reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. Abstract

Cognitive decline begins in early stages of Parkinson's disease (PD) for many individuals and remains largely untreated. The nucleus basalis of Meynert (NBM), the brain's cortical cholinergic hub, and its lateral efferent fiber bundle degenerate early in PD and are linked to cognitive and cognitive-motor impairment. Pre-clinical evidence indicates intermittent stimulation of the NBM improves cognition. The objective of this study is to establish the feasibility of combined continuous subthalamic nucleus (STN) and intermittent NBM DBS for cognitive impairment in people with PD. Introduction

Cognitive decline begins in early stages of Parkinson's disease (PD) and progresses to dementia in approximately 50% of people with PD after ten years and over 80% after 20 years [1] [2] [3] [4] . Dopaminergic medication and deep brain stimulation (DBS) provide long-term improvement of cardinal motor symptoms in PD, but cognition remains largely unaddressed despite a long window for potential intervention before dementia occurs 5 . The only currently approved treatment for worsening cognition in PD are cholinesterase inhibitors, which are of limited efficacy and can cause troublesome side effects 6 . Treatment for cognitive impairment typically only starts when it reaches later stages of mild cognitive impairment or dementia, by which time significant cortical cholinergic denervation results in marginal presence of acetylcholine remaining in cortical synaptic clefts, thus limiting the effect of any acetylcholine-enhancing therapy 1 . There is currently no approved treatment that reverses or even stabilizes cognitive decline in PD.

The cortical cholinergic network plays a crucial role in cognitive domains such as attention, visuospatial ability, and executive function 7 . The nucleus basalis of Meynert (NBM) is considered the cortical cholinergic hub as it is the primary source of cholinergic innervation to the cortex 8 . The NBM is comprised of approximately 90% cholinergic neurons and has two major outflow fiber tracts, the medial and lateral efferent fiber bundles, which provide cholinergic innervation to vast expanses of the cortical mantle, including the parolfactory, cingulate, pericingulate, and retrosplenial cortices, as well as the frontoparietal operculum, insula, superior temporal gyrus, and other portions of the frontal, parietal, and temporal cortex 9, 10 . Post-mortem studies in PD show significant loss of cholinergic neurons in the NBM and degeneration of its cholinergic fiber projections 11, 12 . The NBM is known to degenerate early in PD, with 32% cell loss in non-demented patients, rising to 54-70% in PD dementia [12] [13] [14] [15] [16] [17] [18] . Invivo MRI and PET imaging have found similar results and shown that degeneration of the NBM and its fiber projections is predictive of cognitive impairment [19] [20] [21] [22] [23] [24] . This degeneration appears to precede development of substantial cognitive symptoms 20 . Similarly, cognitive-motor impairments such as non-dopaminergic aspects of gait are also related to NBM degeneration 25, 26.

The NBM has been identified as a potential therapeutic neurostimulation target for cognitiverelated symptoms 27, 28 . A substantial body of pre-clinical literature over the last thirty-five plus years has demonstrated that neurostimulation of the NBM increases cortical acetylcholine and neurotrophic growth factor release and increases cerebral blood flow in rodents [29] [30] [31] [32] [33] . NBM stimulation in non-human primates has demonstrated improvements in working and spatial memory and sustained attention on the order of 30-45% improvement, which has been replicated in rodent models of cognitive impairment [34] [35] [36] [37] [38] [39] [40] [41] . Crucially, these beneficial effects were only seen with intermittent stimulation (60 Hz pulse train for 10-20 seconds followed by 40-50 seconds of no stimulation for only 1 hour/day) and performance was impaired during continuous stimulation 34 . Intermittent stimulation is hypothesized to efficiently maximize cortical acetylcholine release and promote longer neuroplastic effects, whereas continuous stimulation has been shown to deplete neurotransmitter reserves 40, 42 . This highlights a very different approach to neuromodulation of the cortical cholinergic network to treat the cognitive-motor syndrome compared to traditional DBS approaches to treat motor symptoms in PD.

Despite the positive therapeutic potential of NBM DBS for cognition in pre-clinical studies, the clinical studies in PD have had mixed efficacy [43] [44] [45] [46] [47] [48] , which may be due to three major limitations: 1) A potentially "too little too late" timing of NBM DBS, when people with PD already had dementia and thus likely substantial cholinergic cell loss; 2) A single vertical trajectory to target both motor globus pallidus interna (GPi) and a portion of the NBM that may have resulted a suboptimal volume of tissue activated (VTA) 49 ; and 3) The use of continuous neurostimulation, which impaired cognition in preclinical studies, instead of intermittent neurostimulation (60 Hz, cycling 10 sec on, 40 sec off for only 1 hour/day) that in preclinical studies has demonstrated increased cortical blood flow, cortical cholinergic tone, release of growth factors, and best performance. We sought to apply the three predictors of a successful outcome for motor (dopaminergic) DBS to cognitive (cholinergic) DBS: 1) select well-characterized candidates before the stage of dementia; 2) optimize target selection, lead location, and the generated VTA; and 3) use intermittent neurostimulation patterns which are optimized for the cognitive symptoms.

Here we present the initial proof of concept for the feasibility of optimizing surgical trajectories and stimulation parameters for DBS of the NBM for cognitive impairment in PD and the resulting preliminary efficacy. We designed and implemented two separate trajectories for the NBM, one based on the pre-clinical non-human primate evidence and one optimized for the unique geometry of the NBM and its lateral efferent bundle in the human brain 50 . An imagingbased pipeline and automated optimization algorithm was then utilized to determine optimal DBS settings for overlapping with the target NBM structure and minimizing overlap with nearby regions that could elicit side effects based on subject-specific lead location and anatomy. Neuropsychological outcomes were evaluated after six months of combined STN and intermittent NBM DBS.

Study Design

The current study is part of an ongoing single-site open label pilot clinical trial testing the safety and tolerability of the combination of continuous STN DBS and intermittent NBM DBS for cognitive and cognitive-motor deficits in people with PD (NCT05968703). Patients were randomized to either receive a Vertical Trajectory targeting the anterior portion of the NBM or a Lateral Trajectory targeting the NBM lateral efferent fiber bundle. All participants were implanted with multinodal DBS in the STN and NBM using four leads in the same procedure (Figure 1) 51 . Baseline assessments occurred approximately 1 month after surgery, prior to Initial Programming, with a follow-up assessment after 6 months of combined STN and NBM DBS.

Designing Surgical Trajectories

A vertical trajectory was designed to target the anterior portion of the NBM. The placement of the vertical NBM leads used the same burr hole as was used to place the STN leads. This trajectory was modeled after previous literature in the non-human primate that demonstrated consistent cognitive improvement when targeting 2 mm posterior to the crossing of the globus pallidus by the anterior commissure, and in the floor of the Globus Pallidus at the intersection of the Globus Pallidus Internus (GPi) and Externus (GPe) 34, 35, 37, 52 . The current target followed suit, while considering human histological evidence for the highest density of cholinergic neurons approximately 4 mm posterior to the anterior commissure 9 . This target is approximately a 5 mm distance from the standard clinical target for GPi.

Vertical NBM trajectories have been used in previous approaches but may not be optimal for targeting the NBM. Considering the NBM is a horizontal, open, nearly flat area of the basal forebrain adjacent to and below the floor of the globus pallidus, the vertical approach places only one level of electrodes on a DBS directional lead within the target and limits the VTA to a small volume of the NBM. However, the diffuse NBM channels much of its outflow into a tight lateral efferent fiber bundle, which projects to the dorsal frontoparietal neocortex, middle and inferior gyri, inferotemporal cortex, parahippocampal gyrus, frontoparietal operculum, insula, and superior temporal gyrus via the external capsule, claustrum, and uncinate fasciculus 10 . Moreover, this bundle is located on the lateral side of the NBM and is thus a surgically approachable target. Therefore, a lateral trajectory that aligns the axis of the DBS lead with the NBM outflow to the lateral efferent bundle may allow the VTA to encompass most of the efferent fiber bundle, thus maximizing widespread cortical acetylcholine release.

Successful DBS Lead Placement Across Both Trajectories

Patients underwent a modified two stage DBS implantation procedure with all 4 leads placed in stage one, followed by a 32-channel rechargeable implantable pulse generator (IPG) in stage two. Three patients received the Vertical Trajectory and two received the Lateral Trajectory. For the Lateral trajectory, the NBM lateral efferent fiber bundle was reconstructed from a diffusion weighted imaging scan using a previously validated individualized tractography pipeline 50 . The lateral trajectory was approached via a small twist-drill craniostomy immediately behind the ear, with an anteromedial trajectory to the lateral efferent bundle target. Intraoperative imaging (Loop-X®, Brainlab) was performed on a 15mm above cannula to confirm that the planned trajectory resulted in sufficient distance from the middle cerebral artery before the final placement of the lead at target depth. Importantly, a 0mm above or "to target" cannula was not used, to prevent a rigid object traversing past the deep Sylvian cistern.

All five patients successfully underwent the DBS procedure with lead location confirmed both immediately post-op and on a follow-up CT one month after surgery. Image-Guided Stimulation Parameters

An automated modeling-and imaging-based approach was used to determine the NBM stimulation configuration since, unlike STN DBS for motor symptoms, acute stimulation-related behavior changes cannot be used to guide setting up NBM DBS. Therefore, the DBS Illumina 3D™ algorithm (Boston Scientific) was used 53 . Illumina 3D™ is an optimization algorithm that uses patient-specific imaging to find the stimulation settings that maximizes overlap with a target region of interest (ROI) while minimizing stimulation of regions of avoidance (ROAs) that could result in unknown or undesired side effects, all within stimulation charge density, total charge, and amplitude safety limits. The algorithm allows user-selected weightings that set the priority of not stimulating the avoidance regions (controlled by a slider to set 'avoidance ratio'), as well as the priority of reduced Stimulation Field Model (SFM) volume (controlled by a slider to set the 'background ratio').

The Optic Tract, Amygdala, and Globus Pallidus Externus (GPe) were set as ROAs for both the Vertical and Lateral Trajectory, whereas the NBM was set as the ROI for the Vertical Trajectory and one combined object consisting of the NBM and the reconstructed lateral NBM tract was set as the ROI for the Lateral Trajectory. Discussion

In this first in human study, we present the early results demonstrating clinically meaningful cognitive improvement after six months of intermittent patterned neurostimulation of the NBM in 5/5 participants with PD, PD-MCI and PD-D. The three participants with PD-MCI improved into the PD-normal range of the PD-CRS and the one PD-D participant moved into the PD-MCI range. All received bilateral STN and bilateral NBM DBS lead placement in a single procedure, with standard micro-electrode recording (MER) for the STN lead placement and image guided NBM target lead placement. A pre-clinical, evidence-based vertical trajectory for the NBM was used in three patients and a novel lateral trajectory targeting the immediate fiber outflow of the NBM lateral tract was used in two other patients. Intra-and post-operative lead reconstructions confirmed accurate lead placements relative to the STN and NBM targets. Initial programming was performed following standard of care for STN DBS, which was on continuously at 130 Hz. Given the lack of behavioral symptom feedback to guide manual programming, an image-guided automated algorithm (Illumina 3D™) was used to determine the current and anodal or cathodal polarity on each NBM DBS electrode so as to maximize target overlap while minimizing overlap of nearby regions that may have elicited side effects. NBM DBS was on intermittently for one hour every morning. During that hour NBM DBS was ON (60Hz) for 10 seconds and OFF for 40 seconds. The stimulation programming was well tolerated by all patients.

Assessments following six months of combined continuous STN and intermittent NBM DBS showed substantial clinical improvements in all five participants, that exceeded the established Minimally Clinically Important Difference, Fig. 6B. The addition of the two leads in the NBM did not negatively affect the beneficial motoric effects of STN DBS as all five participants showed expected improvements with STN DBS in motor symptoms at 6-months compared to baseline (52.5±13.9% improvement in MDS-UPDRS III).

There are currently no FDA-approved treatments for PD-MCI. The only notable positive RCT for PD-D was for the cholinesterase inhibitor rivastigmine, which is currently only approved for mild-to-moderate PD-D and only demonstrated minor benefits (2.1±8.2 point or 8.8% improvement on the ADAS-cog), with only 19.6% of patients showing clinically meaningful improvements 6 . Furthermore, it was associated with bothersome side effects such as nausea, vomiting, and increased tremor. Rivastigmine failed to show efficacy in a single randomized placebo-controlled trial for PD-MCI 58 , as did the PD MAO-B inhibitor rasagiline 59 . Meanwhile, here we observed a mean improvement of 12.6±4.0 points on the global PD-CRS (17.5±5.7% improvement) with all 5 patients exceeding the established MCID after 6 months of combined STN and intermittent NBM DBS. This replicates previous findings in the non-human primate where intermittent NBM DBS significantly outperformed administration of the cholinesterase inhibitor donepezil 37.

Previous attempts of NBM DBS for cognition have had variable results. The initial case study reported by Freund and colleagues in 2009 showed cognitive improvements in a PD-D patient in the Rey Auditory Verbal Learning Test and Trails A following a 4 lead STN and NBM DBS approach 43 . However, the patient remained in the severely cognitively impaired range. Meanwhile, two different randomized, double-blind crossover trials in 6 patients each with PD-D or PD-MCI showed no consistent improvements in cognitive outcomes using a combined 2-lead GPi and NBM DBS approach 44, 47 . The major critical difference between these studies and the current study is the type of stimulation used for the NBM. The previous studies all used continuous low-frequency 20 Hz stimulation 24 hours per day. This was chosen based on the rationale that NBM neurons' firing rate is typically around 20 Hz. However, evidence from the non-human primate and rodent both show that intermittent stimulation (60 Hz cycling ON for 10-20 second and OFF for ~40 seconds) for only 1 hour a day improves cognitive performance, whereas continuous low frequency stimulation does not and can even impair performance 34, 35, 37, 39 . The importance of this intermittent pattern of stimulation was recently demonstrated by Sasikumar et al., who found that 3 patients who previously received no benefit on continuous 20 Hz NBM DBS, showed improvements in attention on a computerized task after 4 weeks of intermittent NBM DBS 48.

The temporal dynamics of cortical acetylcholine release suggests why intermittent cycling (60 Hz ON for 10-20 second and OFF for ~40 seconds) for only 1 hour a day may be optimal for improving cognition. Continuous NBM stimulation leads to an initial peak of cortical acetylcholine within the first 20 seconds, but from there it begins to decline back to baseline or even lower within 3 minutes, likely due to depletion of acetylcholine reserves 38, 52 . Meanwhile, an intermittent pattern such as 10-20 seconds ON followed by 40 seconds OFF enables the system to recapture the peak cortical acetylcholine response during each stimulation cycle 38 . Shanazz and colleagues investigated the dynamics of cortical acetylcholine release under different NBM stimulation settings in mice and found that 60 Hz and higher frequency stimulation led to significantly higher peak cortical acetylcholine release compared to 20 Hz; furthermore, 10 seconds of 60 Hz stimulation was sufficient to achieve peak acetylcholine release in the NBM, and that recovery occurred within approximately 15-20 seconds, which is likely governed by acetylcholine hydrolysis, diffusion, and choline transport 40 . Therefore, the intermittent stimulation pattern repeatedly perfuses the cortex with acetylcholine during each bout of stimulation followed by a recovery period.

Only one hour of intermittent NBM DBS, carried out automatically by the Chronos software, was used in the current protocol, as opposed to the continuous 20 Hz stimulation 24 hours/day used previously in the NBM DBS pilot studies. One hour has been sufficient to induce cognitive improvements in both the non-human primate and rodent models of dementia, whereas there was no further improvement seen with 24 hours of intermittent stimulation 34, 35, 37, 39 . Given the key role the cholinergic system plays on sleep and arousal, constant stimulation may actually impair sleep, as it has been shown that NBM stimulation applied during sleep disrupts the normal sleepwake cycle 60 . Additionally, periods of no stimulation may be necessary for the beneficial effect of increased nerve growth factor (NGF) and other neurotrophic factor release as increases in NGF release is seen 5 to 10 hours after NBM stimulation and is actually reduced during active stimulation periods, possibly due to increased NGF clearing from extracellular fluid due to the acute increase in cortical blood flow during stimulation 32 . It is also possible that the vasodilative effect is unable to be maintained for prolonged periods of time.

Several potential mechanisms may underlie the cognitive improvement observed following 6 months of intermittent NBM stimulation. NBM stimulation is expected to enhance activity and acetylcholine release in multiple cortical areas, such as the prefrontal, parietal, and temporal association cortices due to the diffuse cholinergic projections of the NBM. Animal studies have shown that the positive cognitive effects are cholinergic-dependent 35 . It also has a wellestablished vasodilative effect due to cholinergic receptors on blood vessels at NBM projection sites, which is important since increased cerebral blood flow is thought to be neuroprotective as it can prevent cell death and also supports clearance of potentially toxic metabolites 30, 31, 61 . Stimulation of the NBM may act not only on cortical blood vessels to increase cortical blood flow, but also on cortical neurons to enhance the release of neurotrophic factors, such as NGF, which acts as an endogenous neuroprotective factor in the cerebral cortex 32 . NGF prevents glucose deprivation-induced or glutamate-induced neuronal damage in cortical cell cultures 62, 63 . Cholinergic neurons in the NBM itself are also dependent on extracellular NGF for maintaining their properties, and thus stimulation of the NBM may be beneficial both at the cortical and subcortical target regions of NBM projection axons, but also the NBM itself 64, 65 . NBM DBS in the rodent has even been shown to increase cholinergic fiber length in the cingulate cortex and neurogenesis in the granule layer of the dentate gyrus 36.

A major novelty of the current approach was the creation of an optimal trajectory for targeting the NBM that considered its unique geometry and anatomy. All studies to date in both humans and animals have utilized some variation of a vertical trajectory such that the axis of the DBS lead is almost perpendicular to the NBM. From the perspective of maximizing the VTA with the NBM, this trajectory is not ideal, as the NBM is inherently thin in the dorsal/ventral plane (only 1-3 mm) and spread approximately 13-14 mm antero-posteriorly and 16-mm medio-laterally 9 . Even with directional electrodes and steered current, there is no way to overlap a significant portion of the NBM with a vertical approach without significant overlap of neighboring regions such as the globus pallidus, putamen, amygdala, and/or optic tract. Therefore, we set out to design a trajectory that was optimal for the NBM anatomy given current DBS hardware constraints. Since the NBM funnels its projection fibers via 2 major fiber bundles: the medial and lateral tracts, this provided a mechanism to have widespread impact on the cortical cholinergic system using a directional electric field or VTA that encompassed most of the lateral efferent bundle as it exited the NBM 10 . We specifically targeted the lateral tract rather than the medial tract as there is greater evidence for its degeneration in PD, it projects to more cognitively relevant cortical areas and is easier to access surgically given its lateral location 20, 50 . Tractography-based DBS approaches have grown increasingly popular as it is a means to have a large network effect with a small electric field due to the tight bundling of axons prior to their radiation to cortical areas [66] [67] [68] [69] . Given the thin nature of the NBM, rather than approach the lateral tract vertically, we opted to approach from a lateral angle to align the axis of the DBS lead with the outflow tract and maximize overlap between the VTA and the immediate outflow of the lateral NBM tract in a subset of participants. This approach was then modified for practical surgical considerations. Specifically, the entry point was shifted to just posterior to the ear, creating a slight posterior-anterior angle. This served three purposes: first, a straight on trajectory would have necessitated an entry point from in front of the ear near the temple which was not practical; second, it allowed for coverage across the maximal dimension of the NBM lateral tract, thus theoretically maximizing the number of fibers modulated; and third, this avoided a trajectory through the insula and inferior limiting sulcus. Additionally, the dorsal-ventral trajectory was optimized to ensure the middle cerebral artery was avoided. The directional electrodes allowed us to prioritize steering current dorsal towards the NBM and its lateral tract and away from the amygdala, which is placed just ventral to the NBM.

Limitations

The current study used an open label design in a small sample as it is a pilot safety and tolerability study which may lead to a placebo effect. A six-month interval and alternate forms were used between neuropsychological assessments to minimize practice effects and the primary neuropsychological assessment (PD-CRS) was not done as part of the evaluation neuropsychological battery. An alternative possibility is that the improvement seen at 6-months is a return to baseline after an initial deterioration from the surgery. Neuropsychological testing was done approximately one month after surgery to try and minimize this possibility and no clinical worsening in cognition was noted. Any image-guided approach is susceptible to potential registration errors or misidentified electrode orientations. We minimized potential errors due to brain shift by collecting a secondary CT scan approximately one month after surgery. However, detecting lead orientation for the lateral trajectory had greater uncertainty since the trajectory falls closer in plane to the CT gantry. The Loop-X® scanner (Brainlab) allowed us to minimize this issue as it could be rotated to a more orthogonal position to the trajectory, but this scan was done intra-op and immediately following the surgery. Additionally, the Illumina 3D algorithm does not incorporate fiber activation as part of its stimulation field modeling, nor does it consider frequency.

Conclusion

This first-in-human study of intermittent neurostimulation of the NBM and its outflow resulted in improvement in cognition in five people with Parkinson's disease. All participants showed clinically meaningful improvements in cognition after six months of combined STN and NBM DBS such that they moved from the PD-MCI to PD-normal range of the PD-CRS, or from the PD-D to PD-MCI range. Both a pre-clinical based vertical trajectory targeting the anterior portion of the NBM and a novel lateral trajectory targeting the immediate outflow of the lateral NBM tract were successfully implemented. Stimulation parameters from an automated imagingbased pipeline based on lead position and patient-specific anatomy was well tolerated in all participants. These results are preliminary but impactful in that they present the first evidence of reversal of cognitive decline in people with Parkinson's disease and suggest that this procedure can be a safe adjunct to STN DBS for motor impairment in PD and should be performed prior to the development of PD-D.

Target Identification

Surgical Procedure

All four leads for the bilateral STNs and NBMs were placed in one procedure for both the vertical and lateral trajectory. The 8-contact Boston Scientific Vercise™ Cartesia™ DBS leads (DB-2202) were used, as well as the Vercise Genus™ R32 IPG. Below are the specific details for each trajectory.

Stage 1: NBM Vertical Trajectory

An initial intraoperative CT scan was obtained after placement of 5 bone fiducials, which was then loaded into the StealthStation™ (Medtronic Inc.) intraoperative workstation and image fusion was performed between this, the preoperative CT scan, and the multiple MRI scans. A target was chosen in the STN bilaterally at approximately 11 mm lateral to the ACPC line, 5 mm inferior to the ACPC plane, and 3 to 4 mm posterior to the midpoint of ACPC. Targets were also chosen in the NBM bilaterally at approximately 18 mm lateral to the ACPC line, 6-7 mm inferior to the ACPC plane, and 10 mm anterior to the midpoint of ACPC. A reference arc was then attached to the FESS frame, and the entry points were located. A 1 mm twist drill was used to make small divots in the skull and then 14 mm burr holes were drilled bilaterally, and 2 SureTek bases were screwed into place. The bases of both Nexframe towers were screwed into the skull, and a reference arc was attached to the left and right Nexframe tower. The trajectory was aligned to each STN target and the guide cannula was lowered. MER was conducted according to standard practices, and an O-arm scan was done to confirm placement. The DBS electrodes were placed and the outer cannula withdrawn. Following securing of the STN leads, the tops of both Nexframes were reattached and the trajectory was aligned to the left and right NBM target. The guide cannula was lowered, and an O-arm scan was done to confirm placement, followed by introducing the two DBS electrodes. The DBS electrodes were placed and the outer cannula withdrawn. The leads were placed into their slots in each SureTek base and caps applied.

Stage 1: NBM Lateral Trajectory

An initial intraoperative CT scan was obtained after placement of 5 bone fiducials, which was then loaded into the ROSA ONE® robot (Zimmer Biomet) and image fusion was performed between this, the preoperative CT scan, and the multiple MRI scans. Image-guided computerassisted targeting and trajectory were confirmed. The patient was placed supine in the Leksell head holder and registered the robot using the bone fiducials and confirmed sub millimetric registration accuracy. Subthalamic nucleus leads were planned and executed according to standard of care. For the NBM trajectories, two additional small linear incisions were planned and twist craniostomy holes were made using Rosa guidance. The left NBM was the first target. The guide cannula was lowered to 15 mm above the final target, and an initial Loop-X spin was taken to confirm accuracy. The 8-contact lead was measured to the appropriate length and placed to target, and the outer cannula was withdrawn. The lead was fastened with a silastic sleeve and dog bone anchor. This process was repeated for the right NBM trajectory, and another Loop-X spin was taken to confirm accuracy of both leads. The STN trajectories were carried out after the NBM and included MER and standard processes using the Rosa robot. A final Loop-X spin was conducted to confirm accuracy of the four leads.

Stage 2 Procedure

For stage 2, the patients were brought to the operating room where general anesthesia was induced. They were then placed into the supine position with a roll under the left shoulder. The head was turned toward the right. The chest and left side of the neck and scalp were shaved, prepped and draped in the usual sterile fashion. After infiltration of a half-and-half mixture of 0.5% Marcaine and 1% lidocaine with 1:100,000 epinephrine, a linear incision was made over the connectors. The DBS electrodes were located and the connectors were externalized, the set screws were loosened, and the connectors were removed from the leads and discarded. At this point, an incision was planned approximately 2-1/2 fingerbreadths below the left clavicle, and this area was infiltrated with local anesthetic solution. Following skin incision, a pocket was made in the plane above the pectoralis fascia for the pulse generator. The pulse generator was tested and was found to fit well within the pocket. Meticulous hemostasis was achieved with bipolar cautery. A subcutaneous pass was made from the scalp incision down to the chest incision, and 2 dual 60 cm extensions were brought through. The leads were attached to the proximal ends of the extensions, and the set screws tightened with a torque wrench. The distal ends of the extensions were inserted into the pulse generator, and the set screws tightened. Channels L1 and R1 were the left and right NBM leads, and channels L2 and R² were the STN leads. The pulse generator was then placed into a TYRX antibacterial envelope. The excess extension was coiled beneath the pulse generator which was placed into its pocket. Impedances were tested and were found to be in an acceptable range. Both incisions were then irrigated copiously with bacitracin solution and closed in multiple layers using inverted interrupted sutures of 3-0 Vicryl for the subcutaneous tissue, inverted interrupted sutures of 2-0 Vicryl for the galea, staples for the scalp incisions and 4-0 nylon for the chest wound. It should be noted that prior to closure, vancomycin powder was sprinkled into the scalp incision. The wounds were dressed in a sterile fashion. The patient was awakened from general anesthesia and brought to the post anesthesia recovery room awake, alert, and unchanged from their preoperative neurological condition.

Postoperative CT Acquisition

Following completion of the surgery, an immediate post-operative head CT scan was performed following the standard clinical protocol at Stanford. Scans were conducted on a Revolution CT scanner (GE Healthcare) in axial mode with a slice thickness of 0.625 mm, spacing between slices of 0.625 mm, FOV of 248 mm, matrix size of 512x512 mm and Tube voltage of 120 kVp. A secondary post-operative CT scan was performed approximately one-month post-surgery with the same settings to account for potential lead movement due to brain shift.

Lead Localization

Leads were localized using Brainlab Elements Image fusion module (Brainlab, Munich, Germany) lead localization software, combining both the pre-operative MRI and post-operative CT scans. The ideal CT scan for performing lead localization should use slices as orthogonal to the leads as possible. The orientation of the lateral trajectory means the leads are closer to parallel with the axial slices on a typical head CT scan, leading to suboptimal artifact generation for determining lead position and orientation. To remedy this issue, CT scans completed during surgery were used to aid determination of lead position and orientation. The Brainlab Loop-X allowed for rotation of the gantry to the desired orientation, improving resolution of the lead position. Lead position and orientation was confirmed by an expert rater (VC).

DBS Illumina 3D Algorithm

The DBS Illumina 3D™ algorithm aims to optimize the stimulation parameters by considering user-set regions of interest (ROI) and regions of avoidance (ROA), maximizing ROI stimulation and minimizing ROA stimulation. Choice of ROA/s can be advised by characteristics unique to the ROI and the surrounding areas where stimulation could cause adverse side effects. The Illumina 3D™ algorithm incorporates the Boston Scientific directional DB-2202 Cartesia leads. For each DBS lead, the optimal amplitude and fractionalization (i.e., the unique arrangement of current driven to each electrode, expressed as a percentage of the total current) are determined from the user-set weighted importance of the fill of the ROI stimulation relative to stimulation spill, as well as the penalty for overlap with ROAs. Additional details regarding the Illumina 3D™ algorithm can be found in Malekmohammadi et al., 2022 53 . For the vertical trajectory, the NBM was set as the ROI and for the lateral trajectory, a combined single object of the NBM and the lateral tract was set as the ROI. The ROAs were set to Optic Tract, Amygdala, and Globus Pallidus Externus due to their proximity to the NBM and potential for eliciting side effects.

Selection of Stimulation Parameters

The Illumina 3D™ algorithm was run across 11 different weightings for both fill/spill and ROI/ROA ratios, resulting in a total of 121 different potential stimulation configurations. For each configuration, the percent overlap with the ROI and each ROA was determined, as well as the overall spill (i.e., portion of the stimulation field that does not overlap with the ROI). Stimulation configurations with excessive overlap of ROAs were not considered. Visualizations were created of the remaining potential configurations comparing the target overlap compared to overall spill volume to identify the best candidates that efficiently overlapped with the target. Visualizations of the resulting Stimulation Field Model (SFMs) for the candidate configurations alongside the nearby ROAs were then produced. The clinician (HBS) determined the final optimum stimulation configuration considering maximization of overlap with the target, minimum overlap with nearby anatomy/ROAs, and overall efficiency of the SFM.

Chronos

The Chronos Research Engine is an investigational software from Boston Scientific that allows complex control of patterned DBS in human subjects to a much finer level than permitted by commercial DBS systems. It allows multiple stimulation programs to be scheduled to turn on and off at pre-specified times. Chronos works with existing Boston Scientific commercial Vercise™ Genus™ rechargeable pulse generators and connected DBS leads, and patients can switch from investigational to commercial DBS programs with their regular patient programmer. No change (no new firmware) is required for the stimulator: Chronos simply permits access to stimulation capability that already exists in the Vercise Genus stimulators. All stimulation delivered with Chronos obeys commercial safety limits on charge density, total charge, and amplitude. Chronos was used to deliver the scheduled intermittent stimulation necessary for the current paradigm. The use of Chronos was FDA IDE approved.

Initial Programming

Initial Programming occurred approximately one month after the DBS surgery. It was split across two days. The first day followed standard clinical practice for programming the STN off medication. The NBM programming was performed on the second day with STN DBS turned ON and on medication. Stimulation of the NBM was not expected to produce immediate symptoms to determine the effectiveness of the stimulation. Therefore, during the programming only possible side effects were tested such as the stimulation of the optic tract, nausea, mood changes, body temperature regulation issues, tingling, pulling, dizziness, or tightness of the muscle of the face or arms. Programming was performed with Boston Scientific's investigational Chronos software. This software allows for different programs to be scheduled for a certain amount of time. For programming, Program 2 contained only the STN leads and Program 3 contained both the STN and NBM leads. Program 2 was set to run for 23 hours and program 3 was set to run for 1 hour, allowing for an automatic switch on and off for the NBM leads for 1 hour per day. Chronos also allows cycling to be used which is required for the NBM stimulation. Cycling was set to 10 seconds ON and 40 seconds OFF with a 100-microsecond pulse width. A symmetric biphasic pulse (i.e., active recharge) was used. Left and right sides were turned on separately during programming with 0.5 mA increments for each side until the targeted amplitude was reached. After each increase, the participants were asked if they experienced any of the abovementioned symptoms. If they did not, then a test was performed to check eyesight and motor symptoms. Stimulation was subsequently increased and tested again until reaching the final selected stimulation amplitude. Once both hemispheres were set, the participant sat resting for fifteen minutes to allow for stimulation wash-in and to see if any adverse events occurred from the stimulation of the NBM.

Study Design

The current study is part of a single-site open label pilot clinical trial testing the safety and tolerability of the combination of continuous STN DBS and intermittent NBM DBS for cognitive and cognitive-motor deficits in people with Parkinson's disease. The study will involve 10 participants in total. Half the participants will receive a 'Vertical' trajectory targeting the anterior portion of the NBM using the same burr hole as the STN, and the other half will receive a 'Lateral' trajectory targeting the lateral efferent NBM fiber bundle from an entry point behind the ear. The current study reports on the feasibility of each of these surgical approaches in the first five participants. Interim analysis of neuropsychological outcomes was required at 6 months as part of the study protocol to ensure no significant cognitive deterioration occurred on NBM DBS that would require turning off the NBM leads.

Study Pipeline

All potential participants are first approved for STN DBS through the standard multidisciplinary Stanford DBS review board. Participants undergo standard clinical preoperative imaging for target identification and surgical planning. The participants receiving the Lateral trajectory also underwent a research MRI at the Stanford Lucas Center for Imaging. The anterior NBM target was identified on the structural imaging, and the lateral efferent fiber bundle was reconstructed using probabilistic tractography from the diffusion imaging. Participants underwent a modified two stage DBS implantation procedure with all 4 leads placed in stage one, followed by the implantable pulse generator in stage two. Lead localization was confirmed via in-surgery imaging, immediate post-op CT scans, and a follow-up 1 month CT scan. The lead location and patient-specific imaging was incorporated into Illumina 3D to determine the optimal stimulation parameters for the NBM. These stimulation parameters were tested at the Initial Programming approximately one month after the DBS procedure.

Vertical Trajectory

A vertical trajectory was utilized to target the anterior portion of the NBM. These leads use the same burr hole as the STN leads. This trajectory was modified from previous literature in the non-human primate that demonstrated consistent cognitive improvement when targeting 2 mm posterior to the crossing of the globus pallidus by the anterior commissure, and in the floor of the globus pallidus at the intersection of the GPi and GPe. The current target followed suit, while considering human histological evidence for the highest density of cholinergic neurons approximately 4-5 mm posterior to the anterior commissure.
