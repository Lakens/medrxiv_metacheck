Reporting p values imprecisely (e.g., p < .05) reduces transparency, reproducibility, and re-use (e.g., in p value meta-analyses). Best practice is to report exact p-values with three decimal places (e.g., p = .032) unless p values are smaller than 0.001, in which case you can use p < .001.

To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 6 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Background: Psychological care is an important component of cancer care, addressing psychological distress and supporting patients as they cope with illness. Meditation-based approaches, including mindfulness-based interventions (MBIs), have been incorporated into supportive cancer care internationally. Clinical Meditation (Rinsho Meiso-ho), a meditation method developed in Japan, integrates traditional Buddhist meditation with a contemporary medical care perspective. However, its feasibility and psychological outcomes in patients with cancer have not been sufficiently studied.

Objective: This study aimed to evaluate the feasibility of a Clinical Meditation intervention and to explore psychological outcomes and participants' experiences among patients with cancer who were receiving or scheduled to receive pharmacotherapy at the time of recruitment.

Methods: This single-arm pilot study used a convergent mixed-methods design to evaluate an 8week program consisting of a 4-week structured phase (1 face-to-face session and 3 brief online group sessions, with prescribed home practice) and a 4-week self-directed phase (1 individual online session, with voluntary home practice). Feasibility was assessed descriptively using retention, attendance, practice frequency, and poststudy feedback. Anxiety, depression, insomnia, fatigue, spiritual well-being (SWB), and posttraumatic growth (PTG) were assessed at baseline, 4 weeks, and 8 weeks. Changes were examined using Friedman tests and exact Wilcoxon signedrank tests, with adjustment for multiple comparisons. Free-text responses were analyzed thematically and integrated with questionnaire findings at the participant level.

Results: Eleven patients were enrolled; 9 (81.8%) completed the 4-week assessment and 8 (72.7%) completed the 8-week assessment. Median practice frequency declined from 5-6 days per week during the structured first month to 3-4 days per week during the self-directed second month (P=.031). Quantitative analyses included 9 participants, of whom 8 completed all 3 assessments; qualitative analyses included 8 participants. SWB and PTG showed significant overall changes (adjusted P=.02 and P=.04, respectively). SWB was higher than baseline at 4 weeks (adjusted P=.012), but the difference was not significant at 8 weeks. PTG was higher than baseline at both 4 and 8 weeks (both adjusted P=.047). No significant changes were observed in anxiety, depression, insomnia, or fatigue. Five themes were identified, encompassing experiences such as relaxation, greater distance from rumination, and meditation as a source of support. Participants' accounts generally aligned with increases in SWB and PTG scores, although perceived benefits did not always correspond to changes in all questionnaire measures.

Conclusions: This mixed-methods pilot study suggests that Clinical Meditation may be feasible and acceptable for patients with cancer, although sustaining meditation practice remains challenging. Exploratory increases in SWB and PTG warrant further investigation. Larger controlled studies, comparisons with established MBIs, and deeper qualitative research are needed to evaluate outcomes and clarify the distinctive features of Clinical Meditation.

Introduction

During diagnosis and treatment, patients with cancer frequently experience psychological distress, including anxiety and depression, as well as related symptoms such as insomnia and fatigue, which can adversely affect quality of life (QOL). Psycho-oncologic interventions have been associated with small-to-moderate improvements in emotional distress and QOL [1, 2]. Among these, meditation-based approaches, particularly mindfulness-based interventions (MBIs) such as mindfulness-based stress reduction (MBSR) and mindfulness-based cognitive therapy (MBCT), have been among the most extensively studied complementary approaches to symptom management in patients with cancer. Meta-analyses of randomized controlled trials have shown that MBIs reduce anxiety and depression [3, 4] and alleviate cancer-related fatigue [5]. For sleep, MBIs have been associated with improved sleep quality, although evidence for reducing insomnia severity remains limited [4, 6]. On the basis of this evidence, the joint guideline from the Society for Integrative Oncology and the American Society of Clinical Oncology strongly recommends MBIs for reducing anxiety and depressive symptoms in patients with cancer [1].

Beyond symptom relief, meditation may also support positive dimensions of psychological adjustment. A cancer diagnosis can challenge patients' sense of meaning and raise existential questions about life and death. Spiritual well-being (SWB) encompasses a sense of meaning, peace, and, for some patients, faith, and has been associated with better QOL in patients with cancer [7]. Posttraumatic growth (PTG) refers to positive psychological changes that may emerge through the struggle with a major life crisis, including a greater appreciation of life, closer relationships, new possibilities, personal strength, and spiritual change [8]. Such growth can coexist with psychological distress and is not simply the absence of symptoms. A meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials of MBIs in patients with cancer reported improvements in spiritual outcomes and personal growth [9]. A more recent meta-analysis focusing on perceived PTG also found a moderate pooled effect of mindfulness-and acceptance-based interventions in cancer populations [10]. However, the limited number of trials, their heterogeneity, and risk of bias warrant cautious interpretation. These findings support examining SWB and PTG as exploratory outcomes of meditation interventions that address existential concerns and the meaning of illness.

In Japan, an 8-week randomized controlled trial of MBCT in patients with breast cancer reported improvements in psychological distress, SWB, and QOL compared with a wait-list control group [11]. A small uncontrolled study reported reduced psychological distress following a brief mindfulness-based meditation program in Japanese patients receiving cancer treatment [12]. Nevertheless, evidence on mindfulness and meditation in Japanese cancer populations remains limited. A 2020 review also noted that MBIs had not been widely integrated into oncology practice in Japan [13]. Moreover, the time demands of participation may hinder clinical implementation. Standard MBSR programs typically involve weekly sessions over approximately 8 weeks and regular home practice [14, 15]. In a study of MBSR for women with metastatic breast cancer, the 8-week time commitment was cited as a reason for nonparticipation and was challenging for those who participated [15]. These considerations highlight the need to evaluate shorter, less demanding meditation interventions that are feasible, acceptable, and manageable alongside cancer treatment in clinical settings.

Clinical Meditation (Rinsho Meiso-ho), a meditation method developed in Japan by Daien Ohshita, integrates traditional Buddhist meditation with a contemporary medical care perspective [16] (see Multimedia Appendix 1 for a detailed description of the method and its background). It can be practiced regardless of religious affiliation or belief and incorporates elements that engage directly with spiritual questions concerning life, death, and the meaning of life. By offering opportunities to explore these questions and reflect on the experience of illness, Clinical Meditation may support SWB and aspects of PTG, such as appreciation of life and spiritual change, among patients with cancer. A simplified meditation guidance program based on this method was previously developed, and its preliminary feasibility was reported in a small pilot study of patients with mild cognitive impairment [17]. However, evidence in patients with cancer remains limited, and its psychological effects, including those on SWB and PTG, warrant further investigation.

Mixed-methods designs, which combine questionnaire outcomes with participants' accounts, allow both psychological changes and patients' experiences of an intervention to be examined. Previous mixed-methods studies of MBIs in patients with cancer have shown that qualitative interviews can reveal perceived benefits, such as increased awareness, acceptance, and personal growth, that may not be fully captured by questionnaire outcomes [18, 19]. A convergent mixedmethods design extends this approach by analyzing quantitative and qualitative data separately and then integrating the findings to examine whether they confirm, expand, or are discordant with each other [20, 21]. Using this design, Eyles et al examined the feasibility and acceptability of an 8-week MBSR program for women with metastatic breast cancer by combining questionnaire assessments with qualitative accounts from patients and health care professionals [15].

Against this background, we conducted a single-arm pilot study using a convergent mixedmethods design among patients with cancer who were receiving or scheduled to receive pharmacotherapy. To reduce the burden of participation, the intervention concentrated structured guidance into the first 4 weeks-an initial face-to-face session followed by 3 brief weekly online group sessions-and was followed by a self-directed month that included 1 individual online session. The primary objective was to evaluate the feasibility of a Clinical Meditation guidance intervention; the secondary, exploratory objectives were to examine psychological outcomes and participants' experiences.

Methods

Participants and Recruitment

Eligible participants were patients with cancer aged 20 years or older who were receiving outpatient care at IMSUT Hospital, The Institute of Medical Science, The University of Tokyo, and who were receiving or scheduled to receive pharmacotherapy. Patients with severe psychiatric illness were excluded. Participants were recruited on a rolling basis through flyers distributed by attending physicians and posted within the hospital, each containing a QR code linking to the study explanation document. In addition, the principal investigator provided direct explanations of the study as needed. The planned recruitment target was 10 participants; as this was a pilot study, the sample size was not based on a formal power calculation.

Ethical Considerations

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Institute of Medical Science, The University of Tokyo (approval number: 2025-3-0501). Online informed consent was obtained after participants had reviewed and understood the study explanation document. Participants were free to withdraw at any time. Participants received no financial or other compensation for their participation. Participant names were replaced with coded identifiers (eg, Patient_A) before analysis, and the key linking these identifiers to participants' identities was stored separately from the study data.

Intervention Program

The intervention comprised a 4-week structured guidance phase followed by a 4-week selfdirected phase (Figure 1) and included 3 types of sessions: (1) an initial face-to-face group session (approximately 1 hour) in a seminar room at the Institute of Medical Science, The University of Tokyo, including a lecture on Clinical Meditation and practical instruction centered on "Loosening Meditation" (Yurumeru Meiso); (2) 3 weekly online group sessions (approximately 30 minutes each) involving meditation practice and group sharing; and (3) an individual online session (approximately 30 minutes) conducted 5-7 weeks after the initial session, providing individualized guidance and an opportunity to discuss personal concerns.

The program was designed by Tomoko Koshiyama (TK) and Daien Ohshita (DO). Meditation guidance was delivered by TK, an experienced Clinical Meditation instructor, under the supervision of DO. As participants enrolled, small groups of 1-3 were formed for the initial faceto-face session and subsequent online group sessions. The principal investigator, Atsushi Niida (AN), attended the initial and online group sessions, but not the individual sessions.

During the first month, participants were asked to practice meditation for 15 minutes, using a YouTube audio guide, on days without scheduled sessions. During the second month, continued practice was encouraged but left to participants' discretion. Further details of the Clinical Meditation method, theoretical background, and session protocols are provided in Multimedia Appendix 1.

Questionnaire Data Collection

Participants completed a web-based questionnaire at 3 time points (Figure 1): 1-3 days before the initial session (Pre), 4 weeks after the initial session (28-31 days; Mid), and 8 weeks after the initial session (54-57 days; Post). Six psychological outcomes-anxiety, depression, insomnia, fatigue, spiritual well-being (SWB), and posttraumatic growth (PTG)-were assessed using the Japanese versions of the following 5 questionnaires.



Anxiety and depression: the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS), comprising a 7-item anxiety subscale (HADS-A) and a 7-item depression subscale (HADS-D), each scored from 0 to 21. Conventional cutoffs classify subscale scores of 0-7 as noncases, 8-10 as borderline cases, and 11-21 as probable cases [22]. The Japanese version has been evaluated in patients with cancer [23].  Insomnia: the Insomnia Severity Index (ISI), a 7-item questionnaire assessing insomnia severity (0-28 points). Scores of 0-7 indicate no clinically significant insomnia, 8-14 subthreshold insomnia, 15-21 moderate insomnia, and 22-28 severe insomnia [24, 25].  Fatigue: the Brief Fatigue Inventory (BFI), comprising 9 items assessing fatigue severity and its interference with daily functioning. Each item is scored from 0 to 10, and the global score is calculated as the mean of the completed items, provided that at least 5 of the 9 items are answered, per the original scoring rule [26, 27].  SWB: the 12-item Functional Assessment of Chronic Illness Therapy-Spiritual (FACIT-Sp-12), with scores ranging from 0 to 48 and higher scores indicating better SWB [7, 28].  PTG: the Japanese version of the Short Form of the Expanded Version of the Posttraumatic Growth Inventory (PTGI-X-SF-J), comprising 10 items scored from 0 to 5 [29]. In this study, the mean item score (0-5) was used.

In addition, at the Mid and Post assessments, participants rated their frequency of meditation practice during the preceding month using a 5-point categorical scale (0, 1-2, 3-4, 5-6, or 7 days per week), with attendance at the meditation sessions counted as meditation practice.

After completion of the Post assessment, participants were invited to complete an additional, anonymous online questionnaire assessing their experience of study participation, administered separately from the Pre/Mid/Post outcome measures described above and not linked to individual participant identifiers. This questionnaire asked participants to rate, on a 0-10 scale, their satisfaction with the face-to-face session, the online group sessions, and the individual online session; the burden associated with completing the questionnaires and with daily home meditation practice (higher scores indicating less burden); their satisfaction with the administrative support provided during the study; their willingness to continue meditation practice; and their overall satisfaction with study participation. Because responses were collected anonymously, they could not be linked to individual participants or to their Pre/Mid/Post questionnaire data.

Feasibility Evaluation

Feasibility was evaluated descriptively using retention at the Mid and Post assessments, attendance at each scheduled session, and self-reported meditation practice frequency. Retention and attendance were summarized as counts and percentages using all enrolled participants as the denominator, with additional summaries restricted to those who continued beyond the initial session. Acceptability was assessed using the anonymous poststudy questionnaire described above. No criteria for determining feasibility were prespecified.

Statistical Analysis of Questionnaire Data

For each of the 6 outcome scores derived from the 5 questionnaires, overall change across the 3 time points (Pre, Mid, and Post) was assessed using the Friedman test, including only participants with complete data at all 3 time points. No single primary outcome was prespecified; all 6 measures were assessed on an equal, exploratory basis. To account for testing 6 outcome measures, Friedman P values were adjusted for multiplicity using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure [30] (adjusted P<.05). For outcome measures with a significant Friedman result, post hoc pairwise comparisons between time points were conducted using the 2-tailed Wilcoxon signed-rank test using the exact conditional sign-flip distribution of the observed absolute paired differences; because these 3 comparisons (Pre-Mid, Mid-Post, Pre-Post) constitute all pairwise comparisons among the 3 time points, the resulting P values were adjusted for multiplicity within each outcome using Shaffer's modified sequentially rejective procedure [31]. Each pairwise comparison included participants with data at both corresponding time points. Zero paired differences were excluded from the signed-rank statistic, and tied absolute differences were assigned average ranks. Paired differences were rounded to 10 decimal places before ranking to avoid numerical artifacts in the handling of ties. As the effect-size measure for the pairwise comparisons, we calculated the matched-pairs rank-biserial correlation (r), which is derived directly from the Wilcoxon signed-rank statistic [32]. The Hodges-Lehmann estimate was calculated as the median of the Walsh averages of all paired differences, including zero differences. Confidence intervals were obtained by inverting the same 2-tailed exact signed-rank test across hypothesized location shifts; differences and ranks were recalculated at each shift, and the closure of the nonrejected set was reported. A location-shift interpretation assumes symmetry of the paired-difference distribution. Full Friedman statistics for all 6 outcome measures, together with medians, interquartile ranges, Hodges-Lehmann estimates of the paired location shift (the pseudomedian of paired differences) with unadjusted 95% CIs, and post hoc exact Wilcoxon results, are provided in Tables S1 and S2 in Multimedia Appendix 2.

To explore how the outcome measures co-varied, we estimated correlations among the 6 measures at 2 levels. Within-person associations were estimated using repeated measures correlation (rmcorr) [33], which removes between-participant differences in overall score levels; this analysis used all available observations. Between-person associations were estimated using Pearson correlation of each participant's mean score across time points. Correlation matrices ordered by hierarchical clustering, correlation coefficients with 95% CIs obtained using the Fisher z transformation, and scatter plots are provided in Figure S4 in Multimedia Appendix 2. Statistical analyses were performed in R (version 4.5). All analysis code was generated with the assistance of large language models (LLMs; Claude Opus 4.8, 5.0, and 5.5; Anthropic) and was executed, reviewed, and verified by AN.

Qualitative Analysis and Mixed-Methods Integration

In addition to the questionnaire items, participants provided free-text responses to 2 open-ended questions regarding their psychological state and any insights gained through meditation practice. Q1 ("In addition to the above questions, please freely describe anything you have noticed recently about your own state of mind.") was administered at all 3 time points (Pre, Mid, and Post), whereas Q2 ("Please freely describe any insights you have gained, or changes you have felt, through your meditation practice.") was administered only at the latter 2 time points (Mid and Post).

Thematic analysis followed Braun and Clarke's 6-phase framework [34] and was conducted with assistance from an LLM. Before analysis, personally identifying information was manually removed from the Japanese-language free-text responses. The LLM (Claude Opus 4.8, Anthropic), configured to prevent the submitted data from being used for model training, was instructed to identify meaningful text units; generate candidate codes; compile a codebook containing code definitions, inclusion and exclusion criteria, and all text passages assigned to each code; and organize the codes into candidate themes based on their semantic relationships without weighting by code frequency.

AN then reviewed the LLM-generated codes, candidate themes, and supporting quotations against the original free-text responses, revised and consolidated codes, reconsidered themes as needed, and made the final decisions regarding the codebook and thematic structure. The finalized codebook and themes were reviewed by Naomi Iwane (NI), a qualitative research methodologist who was not involved in the initial coding. Her feedback informed further refinements but did not constitute independent recoding or a comprehensive audit.

Regarding researcher reflexivity, both AN and NI had completed the Clinical Meditation instructor training program, providing them with theoretical and practical familiarity with Clinical Meditation that informed their interpretation but may also have predisposed them toward interpretations favorable to the method. In addition, AN was involved in participant recruitment and attended the group sessions (see Intervention Program). All stages of thematic analysis were conducted in Japanese. The resulting themes and coded passages were subsequently translated into English with LLM assistance, and all translations were checked by AN. Reporting was guided by the Standards for Reporting Qualitative Research (SRQR) [35] (Multimedia Appendix 3). The final codebook, including the thematic structure and English translations of all coded passages, is provided in Multimedia Appendix 4.

Consistent with the convergent mixed-methods design, questionnaire data and qualitative freetext responses were collected at the same time points and analyzed separately [20]. The findings were then integrated at the individual participant level in a joint display to explore correspondence and divergence between participants' reported experiences and changes in questionnaire scores [21]. Contextual information that participants shared during the group sessions, observed by AN, was used only to aid the interpretation of individual cases.

Results

Participant Characteristics and Feasibility

We enrolled 11 participants, whose cancer types included colorectal cancer (n=7; colon, n=5; rectum, n=2), gastric cancer (n=2), endometrial cancer (n=1), and duodenal cancer (n=1); disease stage ranged from III to IV (Table 1). All participants except 1 (Patient_D), who had completed a course of adjuvant chemotherapy shortly before the intervention began, were undergoing pharmacotherapy at the start of the intervention. Four participants (Patient_D, Patient_H, Patient_I, and Patient_K) reported limited prior experience with meditation, such as meditation during yoga sessions or school classes, use of meditation apps, or brief participation in another meditation program; none reported an established, ongoing meditation practice (Table 1).

All 11 participants completed the baseline (Pre) questionnaire and the initial face-to-face group session. Two participants discontinued after the initial session. One of them had an unstable clinical condition; however, the reasons for discontinuation were not confirmed for either participant. No additional data were collected from these 2 participants (Figure 2a). One additional participant discontinued after the Mid assessment for an unconfirmed reason. Among the 11 enrolled participants, 81.8% completed the Mid assessment and 72.7% completed the Post assessment; among the 9 participants who continued beyond the initial session, this corresponds to 100% completion at Mid and 88.9% completion at Post. Attendance among all 11 enrolled participants was 45% (5/11) for the first online group session, 64% (7/11) for the second, 73% (8/11) for the third, and 64% (7/11) for the individual online session; among the 9 continuing participants, these correspond to 55.6% (5/9), 77.8% (7/9), 88.9% (8/9), and 77.8% (7/9), respectively (Figure S1 in Multimedia Appendix 2).

The self-reported frequency of meditation practice, including both session attendance and home practice, was a median of 5-6 days per week during the first month of the intervention but significantly decreased to 3-4 days per week during the second month (Figure 2b; P=.031). This decline coincided with the shift from the first month, which included regular online sessions and prescribed audio-guided practice, to the second month, during which continuation of practice was left to participants' own initiative.

Participants' satisfaction with the intervention was assessed via an anonymous poststudy questionnaire completed by 6 of the 8 participants who completed the Post assessment (summarized in Figure S2 in Multimedia Appendix 2). On a 0-10 scale, the median score was 8 or higher for all assessed items, suggesting generally favorable participant experiences. Because responses were both anonymous and voluntary, response bias cannot be excluded, and these results should therefore be interpreted with caution.

Collectively, these findings provide preliminary information on participation and acceptability among patients who continued beyond the initial face-to-face session. Questionnaire Results

Statistical analyses of questionnaire data were conducted among the 9 participants who continued beyond the initial face-to-face session, excluding the 2 who discontinued at that point (Figure 3 and Figure S3 in Multimedia Appendix 2). Overall changes across the 3 time points were assessed for each outcome using the Friedman test in the 8 participants with complete data (Methods). Significant overall changes were observed for SWB (χ²=11.81, adjusted P=.02) and PTG (χ²=8.86, adjusted P=.04), whereas anxiety (HADS-A; χ²=5.43, adjusted P=.13), depression (HADS-D; χ²=0.48, adjusted P=.91), insomnia (ISI; χ²=3.27, adjusted P=.29), and fatigue (BFI; χ²=0.19, adjusted P=.91) did not show significant changes.

For SWB and PTG, post hoc pairwise comparisons were performed using the exact Wilcoxon signed-rank test (Methods). SWB increased significantly from Pre to Mid (adjusted P=.012, r=1.00) and subsequently decreased from Mid to Post (adjusted P=.047, r=-0.89); the overall Preto-Post change was not significant (adjusted P=.125, r=0.61). PTG also increased significantly from Pre to Mid (adjusted P=.047, r=1.00) and remained significantly higher at Post than at Pre (adjusted P=.047, r=1.00), whereas the change from Mid to Post was not significant (adjusted P=.398, r=0.36).

Anxiety and insomnia scores showed decreasing trends from Pre to Post, whereas depression and fatigue showed no consistent direction of change, with similar numbers of participants showing decreases and increases; none of these 4 measures showed a significant overall change in the Friedman tests (full statistics are provided in Tables S1 and S2 in Multimedia Appendix 2). In a separate exploratory analysis, correlations among the 6 outcome measures (Figure S4 in Multimedia Appendix 2) showed that SWB and PTG were positively correlated at both the within-person (r=0.62, 95% CI 0.21-0.84) and between-person (r=0.77, 95% CI 0.22-0.95) levels, the only pair of the 15 examined whose 95% CIs excluded zero at both levels. In hierarchical clustering, SWB and PTG formed a cluster separate from the 4 symptom measures at both levels, and correlations between the 2 clusters were predominantly negative. Given the small sample and the number of pairs examined, these associations should be regarded as exploratory.

Overall, in this small sample, SWB and PTG were the only outcomes that changed significantly over time, and they were also positively correlated with each other, whereas the 4 symptom measures showed no significant change. Descriptively, however, their trajectories differed: the gain in SWB at Mid was not maintained at Post, whereas PTG remained above baseline.

Qualitative Findings

Of the 9 participants who provided questionnaire data, 8 provided free-text responses at 1 or more time points. The participant who did not provide free-text responses (Patient_H) was not a native speaker of Japanese; although the reason for non-response was not confirmed, writing free-text responses in Japanese may have been difficult for this participant. In total, these 8 participants contributed 34 free-text responses across the Pre, Mid, and Post time points combined. Response length varied considerably across participants, ranging from 16 to 322 characters per response (mean 83 characters; Figure S5 in Multimedia Appendix 2). From these responses, 72 coded passages were extracted (Figure S6 in Multimedia Appendix 2). Qualitative analysis organized these participants' narratives into 5 themes, described below; a subtheme was also identified within each of 2 themes (Theme 1 and Theme 3) and is described alongside its parent theme. The distribution of these themes across participants and time points is summarized visually in Figure 4a.

Theme 1: Uncontrollable Anxiety and Rumination

At Pre, comments related to anxiety, fear, and rumination were observed in 4 of the 8 participants. Patient_G stated, "I feel anxious about the worsening side effects of chemotherapy" (Pre, Q1). Patient_A described being "tired of confronting the sudden fear and memories of unpleasant past events that come to mind" (Pre, Q1), and, regarding the hospital's handling of care, remarked, "I sometimes feel as though I am being carried on a conveyor belt toward the end" (Pre, Q1), adding self-critically, "I feel ashamed that I imagine such terrible things" (Pre, Q1). Patient_D noted that, compared with the time of diagnosis and before surgery for colorectal cancer, "I now think about my illness less, and my mind has become calmer" (Pre, Q1), while also stating, "I sometimes feel a sudden anxiety about the future" (Pre, Q1).

In addition, Patient_C stated at Pre that "anxiety about side effects was substantial at the start of treatment" (Pre, Q1), indicating the presence of anxiety prior to program participation. In a retrospective account at Post, the same participant stated, "After being diagnosed with cancer, for a while I felt anxiety, fear, and a sense of loss, wondering whether my life was coming to an end" (Post, Q1), indicating that this participant had experienced profound existential fear in the early period of diagnosis and treatment.

A related but more specific concern, identified in 1 of the 8 participants and labeled Theme 1sub (concern about the practice of meditation itself), was raised by Patient_I at Pre: "Since I sometimes wake up in the middle of the night when I meditate, I feel anxious about whether I can continue" (Pre, Q1). Unlike the broader anxiety and rumination captured in Theme 1, this narrative reflected concern about the practice of meditation itself rather than about the illness or its treatment. The background to this concern and this participant's subsequent trajectory are discussed under Integrating Quantitative and Qualitative Findings at the Individual Level.

Theme 2: Relaxation Through Attention to Breath and Body

Narratives related to breath and physical relaxation were observed in 4 of the 8 participants. At Mid, Patient_C described having learned the importance of focusing attention on the inflow and outflow of breath (Mid, Q2), and at Post reported specific physical changes: "After meditating, my mind and body feel more relaxed, and I feel I fall asleep more easily," and "I have become able to sleep 7-8 hours" (Post, Q2). Patient_G stated, "Meditating seems to ease my tension" (Mid, Q1). Patient_F similarly stated, "I felt that I was getting a kind of rest different from sleep" (Mid, Q2). Patient_D reported that "bringing attention to my breath and taking deep breaths has become more common in daily life" (Mid, Q2), indicating that awareness of breathing had become embedded in daily life. . Patient_G stated, "There is no point in feeling impatient or worrying about things that cannot be helped" (Mid, Q2), and Patient_B remarked, "I have come to hear an inner voice telling me there is no need to be troubled" (Mid, Q2). Patient_E reported, "I have become able to spend my days more calmly than before" (Mid, Q1), and Patient_F reported, "The unexplained sense of anxiety has felt somewhat eased" (Mid, Q1).

Within this theme, a more specific subtheme (Theme 3sub: the observing self and acceptance) was identified in 6 of the 8 participants (Patient_A, Patient_C, Patient_D, Patient_E, Patient_F, and Patient_G). It comprised narratives in which participants described learning to observe their own minds nonjudgmentally and to accept them as they are. These accounts overlap with aspects of metacognitive awareness and decentering [36, 37], as well as acceptance and attentional reorientation, although these processes were not directly measured. For example, Patient_D stated, "I feel I have become able to pause my thinking and bring my thoughts back to the present moment" (Mid, Q2); Patient_A described having come to know "another version of myself within, one calm enough to accept and embrace everything" (Post, Q2); and Patient_C stated, "I have become able to accept myself as I am" (Mid, Q1). These accounts suggest changes in participants' relationship with their own thoughts and emotions, without establishing a specific underlying process. Theme 4: Existential and Transcendent Awareness Beyond Life and Death In contrast, this theme appeared in the narratives of only 1 of the 8 participants (Patient_B). At Mid, Patient_B described having come to hear an inner voice conveying that "I am being sustained by a great life force that runs from the center of the earth to the edge of the universe" (Mid, Q2), and stated, "I feel I have arrived at my own answer to why people are born and die" (Mid, Q1). At Post, the participant stated, "I sometimes feel a sense of reassurance, as though I have become one with the mysterious power of the great universe" (Post, Q2). Because this theme was identified in only a single participant, it should not be interpreted as a generalizable change; nevertheless, it is valuable as an individual case illustrating an existential experience reported during participation in Clinical Meditation. Theme 5: Internalization as a Source of Psychological Support, Extending Into Daily Life Descriptions in which meditation had become embedded in daily life and served as a source of psychological support, with effects extending to other areas of life such as concentration, relationships, and gratitude, were observed in all 7 participants who provided Post responses (Patient_G had discontinued after the Mid assessment). Patient_D described meditation as "something like a protective charm for the mind" (Post, Q2), Patient_C stated, "Meditation seems to have become my tranquilizer" (Post, Q2), and Patient_F remarked, "Knowing that I can always find calm through meditation has become a source of psychological support for me" (Post, Q2). These accounts suggest that meditation had come to function as an inner resource rather than merely a learned technique. Others described effects extending beyond the practice itself: Patient_E reported, "I have become able to keep my mind calm, and my concentration has increased" (Post, Q2), and Patient_I stated, "I am truly grateful to the research team for creating this opportunity; I will do my best to keep it up on my own" (Post, Q2), expressing an intention to continue practicing independently. In addition, Patient_A stated, "I have become better than before at offering my own solutions to conflicts involving people close to me, in ways that do not burden them" (Post, Q1), suggesting a perceived change in interpersonal coping during the intervention period. Even while hospitalized because of worsening illness, Patient_B stated, "I was grateful that I could meditate even while in bed" (Post, Q1), illustrating that meditation could serve as a psychological resource beyond the context of daily routine.

Taken together, the 5 themes summarize participants' reported experiences during the Clinical Meditation intervention. Comments related to uncontrollable anxiety and rumination (Theme 1) were most common at Pre, and relaxation via attention to breath and body (Theme 2) was described by 4 participants, while disengagement from rumination and the attainment of calmness (Theme 3) were described by all 8 participants over the course of the intervention. Existential and transcendent awareness beyond life and death (Theme 4), by contrast, was identified in only a single participant and should be regarded as an individual, deviant-case observation rather than a shared feature of the sample. Integrating Quantitative and Qualitative Findings at the Individual Level

Among the 8 participants who provided both quantitative and qualitative data, most showed engagement with a broad range of qualitative themes-particularly Theme 3 (disengagement and calmness) and Theme 5 (internalization as psychological support)-alongside increases in SWB and/or PTG scores (Figure 4). The following cases illustrate this correspondence and its limits.

Patient_C showed the broadest concordance between the two data sources: the largest increase in SWB (28 to 38) and the largest decreases in anxiety (-6) and fatigue (-7.8) in the sample were accompanied by narratives spanning nearly every qualitative theme, including an explicit account of improved sleep (Theme 2; "I have become able to sleep 7-8 hours") that corresponded to this participant's improvement in insomnia (-5). Patient_E, Patient_F, and Patient_G showed a similar, if more modest, concordant pattern, with increases in both SWB and PTG accompanying narratives centered on Theme 3 and Theme 3sub (for Patient_G, who discontinued after the Mid assessment, Pre-to-Mid changes).

Another example was Patient_I, whose concern at Pre that meditation itself might disrupt sleep (Theme 1sub) was, according to his remarks during the face-to-face group session, rooted in a previous unsuccessful experience with a different meditation program. Contrary to his initial concern, this participant showed a Pre-to-Post decrease in insomnia (-2), together with a marked decrease in anxiety (-5, from the highest Pre score in the sample) and narratives of disengagement from rumination (Theme 3) and internalization of meditation as an ongoing resource (Theme 5). He also remarked in an online group session that he had been breathing incorrectly in that earlier practice and had learned the correct way to breathe through the present program. Together, these observations suggest that the guidance provided in this program may have helped him acquire an appropriate meditation technique, including breathing, although this was not directly assessed.

Correspondence was not, however, uniform across measures within individual participants. Patient_A showed the largest increase in PTG in the sample (0.7 to 2.7) together with an increase in SWB (9 to 14), both from the lowest Pre scores in the sample, alongside narratives moving from uncontrollable anxiety and rumination at Pre (Theme 1) to self-acceptance by Post (Theme 3 and Theme 3sub; "another version of myself within, one calm enough to accept and embrace everything"); yet depression worsened (+4), and insomnia and fatigue worsened slightly (+1 and +1.4, respectively). Conversely, Patient_D showed favorable changes on every measure except PTG, which was unchanged (3.6 to 3.6), including the largest decrease in insomnia in the sample (-6), alongside narratives of disengagement from illness-related rumination (Theme 3; "I no longer think much about my illness").

Patient_B represented a more pronounced divergence: despite the richest qualitative narrative in the study, including the only substantial existential and transcendent content (Theme 4), PTG did not increase from Pre to Post (2.8 to 2.8, after peaking at 4.4 at Mid) and SWB declined markedly (37 to 28), coinciding with an unstable clinical status, including hospitalization for multiple complications, around the Post assessment (see Theme 5).

In aggregate, these participant-level comparisons suggest that quantitative and qualitative changes generally moved in the same direction for most participants, particularly with respect to Theme 3, Theme 5, and increases in PTG or SWB, but that the correspondence was neither uniform across outcome measures within a given participant (Patient_A, Patient_D) nor guaranteed even when qualitative content was rich (Patient_B).

Discussion

Principal Findings

In this mixed-methods pilot study, a Clinical Meditation guidance intervention was delivered to 11 patients with cancer who were receiving or scheduled to receive pharmacotherapy at recruitment. Of the 11 enrolled participants, 9 (81.8%) completed the Mid assessment and 8 (72.7%) completed the Post assessment, and attendance at the online group sessions ranged from 45% to 73%. Self-reported meditation practice frequency, including guided sessions and home practice, decreased from a median of 5-6 days per week in the structured first month to 3-4 days per week in the self-directed second month. SWB and PTG were the only outcomes showing significant overall change: SWB increased by 4 weeks but declined by 8 weeks, whereas PTG increased and remained above baseline at 8 weeks. Anxiety, depression, insomnia, and fatigue showed no significant change. In the qualitative analysis, comments related to uncontrollable anxiety and rumination were most common before the intervention, whereas 4 participants later described relaxation through attention to breath and body and all 8 described disengagement from rumination. At Post, 7 participants described meditation as an ongoing source of psychological support. At the individual level, integration of questionnaire findings and participants' accounts revealed both correspondence and divergence.

Comparison With Prior Work

With respect to feasibility, the 8-week attrition rate in our study (27.3%) was numerically similar to the average of 25.3% reported in a systematic review of digitally delivered mindfulness-based programs for patients with cancer, although differences in populations and attrition definitions limit direct comparison [38]. All 11 participants attended the initial face-to-face session, and 8 of the 9 participants (88.9%) who continued beyond it completed the 8-week assessment. In a qualitative study of a mindfulness-based group program in primary care, participants reported that having first met in person helped them engage in subsequent videoconference sessions [39]. Initial in-person contact followed by ongoing support may therefore have helped sustain participation in our study. However, 2 participants discontinued after the initial session, and the transition from face-to-face to online delivery may have been a barrier to participation. One of these participants had an unstable clinical condition, and the reasons for discontinuation were not confirmed for either. Future studies should identify barriers during this transition and compare hybrid and fully online formats to clarify the advantages and challenges of hybrid delivery.

Among the 9 participants who continued beyond the initial session, mean attendance at the 3 online group sessions was 74.1% (20/27 session attendances; 55.6%, 77.8%, and 88.9% for each session), lower than the mean attendance of 90% reported in a 4-week online Mindfulness-Based Cancer Recovery (MBCR) trial [40]. In addition to live group instruction, that trial provided video recordings of sessions to absent participants, an online messaging group for sharing materials and communication, daily practice logging via a smartphone app, and training in the use of the videoconferencing system. Although the populations, programs, and calculation methods differed, and the effect of each strategy was not examined individually, these strategies may be useful for supporting participation. Why participants missed sessions, and whether such support improves session attendance and continued practice, remain to be examined.

In the self-directed second month, median practice frequency declined from 5-6 days per week to 3-4 days per week, even though 1 individual online session was provided. Because practice frequency included session attendance and home practice changed from prescribed to voluntary, this decline partly reflects changes in the program structure. The role of individual support in sustaining practice also remains unclear. In a retrospective study of online MBSR, adding brief individual interviews to weekly group classes was associated with greater improvements in psychological well-being, but home practice time was shorter than without interviews [41]. Individual sessions may therefore contribute to outcomes through mechanisms other than increasing practice. Masheder et al identified factors relevant to sustaining practice, including self-efficacy, experiencing the rewards of practice, social support, and the relationship with the teacher [42]. On this basis, future studies should evaluate several strategies for sustaining practice, along with the time and staff they require, while considering the burden on both participants and instructors. These include adding brief online group sessions in the second month, adjusting the timing and frequency of individual support, providing regular feedback, and developing practice plans tailored to participants' daily lives.

Regarding psychological outcomes, the transient increase in SWB in our study contrasts with the sustained increase in PTG. Previous trials suggest that SWB benefits may vary over time. Park et al reported improvements that were maintained 4 weeks after completion of MBCT [11], whereas the improvement in meaning and peace reported by Bower et al was no longer significantly greater than that in controls at 3-month follow-up [43]. Similarly, Würtzen et al found a betweengroup difference in SWB at 6 months but not at 12 months, with no significant effect in the longitudinal mixed-effects analysis [44]. In our study, the decline in SWB coincided with reduced practice during the less structured second month. Nevertheless, 7 of the 8 participants remained above baseline at 8 weeks; the only participant below baseline (Patient_B) was hospitalized for complications around the Post assessment, which likely contributed to the nonsignificant Pre-to-Post difference in this small sample. Whether continued guidance could help sustain practice and SWB warrants further study.

The PTG findings can also be considered alongside trials of mindfulness-based interventions. Carlson et al found greater increases in FACIT-Sp and Posttraumatic Growth Inventory (PTGI) total scores with MBCR than with supportive expressive group therapy, with PTGI total scores continuing to increase relative to the comparison group during follow-up [45]. Zhu et al also reported higher postintervention PTGI scores following an 8-week MBSR program among women receiving chemotherapy for breast cancer [46]. These findings provide context for the PTG changes observed in our study, although differences in intervention content, populations, and study design limit direct comparison. Importantly, PTG can also develop without any psychological intervention: in observational studies of patients with breast cancer, PTG scores increased over the months following diagnosis [47, 48], with most of the increase occurring within the first few months [47]. Unlike the trials above, our pilot study had no control group, so part of the observed increase in PTG may reflect this natural course, and we cannot determine whether Clinical Meditation promoted PTG beyond it.

The qualitative findings offer a complementary perspective on these outcomes. Against the background of anxiety and rumination described in Theme 1, attention to breathing and bodily relaxation in Theme 2 may have provided an initial respite, allowing participants to gain distance from rumination, as described in Theme 3, and, as captured in Theme 3sub, to observe and accept their thoughts and feelings as they are. This shift may have opened opportunities for existential reflection, reported by one participant in Theme 4, and for meditation to become an ongoing source of support, as reflected in Theme 5. This pattern of themes is consistent with PTG theory and mindfulness-to-meaning theory. PTG theory proposes that growth may emerge as individuals reconsider beliefs disrupted by a major life crisis and reconstruct meaning through deliberate reflection [8]. Similarly, mindfulness-to-meaning theory describes how decentering-viewing thoughts and feelings as passing experiences-may broaden awareness and support more flexible interpretations of adversity [49]. These themes suggest a possible pathway through which relief from distress and greater distance from rumination could support meaning-making and changes in SWB and PTG [50].

Related experiences have also been reported in qualitative studies of mindfulness in cancer care. Ando et al [51] identified adaptive coping, positive meaning, personal growth, and spirituality in Japanese patients with cancer following mindfulness-based meditation. Similarly, Mackenzie et al [52] identified personal growth and spirituality among the major themes in interviews and a focus group with cancer patients who had completed MBSR and continued attending meditation sessions. These findings provide context for interpreting our participants' accounts of existential reflection and meditation as a source of psychological support. In a recent mixed-methods study of online MBCR, Gaignard et al [19] assessed SWB and PTG alongside participants' experiences. Qualitative interviews revealed enhanced well-being, skills acquisition, and engagement with cancer-related memories, which some participants found challenging. Such accounts help illuminate how participants understand changes in well-being, coping, and their relationship with illness.

In our study, integrating participants' own accounts with their questionnaire scores illustrated both correspondence and divergence between the two data sources. For Patient_C, accounts of relaxation and improved sleep accompanied an increase in SWB and decreases in several symptom scores. Patient_A described greater self-acceptance alongside increased PTG and SWB, although depression, insomnia, and fatigue scores increased. Conversely, Patient_B's existential reflections accompanied unchanged PTG and declining SWB during a period of clinical instability. These patterns show that perceived benefits may coexist with difficulties in other domains and that existential reflection does not necessarily coincide with measured growth or improved well-being. Qualitative accounts therefore added context to the questionnaire findings by describing what participants experienced and how meditation featured in their daily lives.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. Regarding feasibility, no criteria for recruitment, retention, or adherence were prespecified, so the observed engagement provides descriptive information rather than a definitive assessment of feasibility. Participants who consented may have had a preexisting interest in meditation. Because the reasons for the 2 discontinuations after the initial session were not confirmed, the remaining sample may represent a subgroup more willing or able to continue. Future studies should define feasibility criteria in advance, drawing on established frameworks [53].

For the questionnaire outcomes, the quantitative sample was small (n=9), and the single-arm design precludes attributing the observed changes to the intervention. Natural course, regression to the mean, and repeated-testing effects cannot be ruled out. Six outcome measures were assessed at 3 time points without a prespecified primary outcome; despite adjustment for multiplicity, the significant findings require confirmation. Differences in cancer type, stage, and treatment could not be accounted for, and the 8-week follow-up was too short to assess longerterm trajectories, particularly the persistence of changes in SWB and PTG.

Regarding the qualitative findings, the data were limited to free-text responses rather than semistructured interviews, and some responses were brief, so experiences not spontaneously described may have been missed. With 8 participants, theme saturation was not confirmed, and Theme 4 was based on a single participant. Social desirability bias may have influenced positive narratives such as those in Theme 5, while AN's involvement in recruitment and group sessions may also have influenced participants' responses and the interpretation of the qualitative data. The integration of questionnaire scores and participants' accounts was exploratory and intended to aid interpretation of individual experiences. Although the themes offer possible interpretations of participants' experiences in relation to PTG theory and mindfulness-to-meaning theory, the present data do not establish a temporal sequence among the themes or demonstrate that these experiences accounted for changes in SWB or PTG. Accordingly, the potential mechanism suggested by the qualitative findings warrants further investigation using measures of rumination, such as the Event-Related Rumination Inventory [54], and decentering, such as the Experiences Questionnaire [37].

Conclusions

This mixed-methods pilot study suggests that Clinical Meditation may be feasible and acceptable for patients with cancer. Combining an initial face-to-face session with brief online group and individual sessions may offer a practical approach to providing support during cancer treatment, although the decline in meditation practice frequency highlights challenges in sustaining engagement. Exploratory increases in SWB and PTG, together with participants' accounts of gaining distance from ruminative thoughts and finding support through meditation, warrant further investigation. Larger controlled studies with longer follow-up are needed to further assess feasibility and evaluate psychological outcomes. Comparisons with established MBIs may help clarify the distinctive features of Clinical Meditation, while more in-depth qualitative research could clarify how patients experience and respond to the intervention.
