How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

At a virtual crossing without tactile and auditory cues, adults with severe-to-profound visual impairment took longer than sighted adults to step off the curb. Time spent at the curb may inform orientation and mobility assessment and accessible crossing design. INTRODUCTION

Pedestrians account for an increasing proportion of traffic fatalities in the United States 1.

Pedestrians struck at non-signalized crossings sustain more severe injuries and have higher mortality than those struck at signalized crossings 2 . At an uncontrolled crossing, no signal is available, and the decision to initiate crossing depends entirely on the pedestrian's own assessment of the traffic.

Severe vision loss is associated with a greater risk of death from unintentional injury 3.

For example, pedestrians with severe peripheral field loss identify fewer crossable gaps and more often accept gaps that are too short 4 . However, pedestrians with low vision who travel independently judge crossing gaps as accurately as normally sighted pedestrians; without visual cues, both groups judge them less accurately 5 . These findings can be reconciled if vision loss limits the acquisition of gap information rather than its evaluation. This question remains unresolved in people with severe-to-profound visual impairment (SPVI), whose residual vision is limited and, in ultra-low vision, rudimentary at best 6 . Yet in this population, mobility is among the activities for which people report benefiting from their residual vision 7 . The open question is therefore not whether crossing outcomes differ with vision loss, but how the information required for crossing is acquired.

Orientation and mobility training teaches pedestrians with low vision to look in both directions before entering the roadway. If degraded vision provides less information per look, visually impaired pedestrians would be expected to scan more. The clearest evidence of compensatory scanning comes from homonymous hemianopia, in which pedestrians scan farther and more often toward the blind than toward the seeing hemifield 8 ; in a driving simulator, early large head scans toward the blind hemifield were associated with earlier fixation of, and faster responses to, hazards on that side 9 . Hemianopia, however, is a special case, because visual acuity is typically good and there is a defined direction toward which to compensate. Without such a direction, the prediction is not supported: pedestrians with age-related macular degeneration, for instance, turn their heads less often than normally sighted pedestrians at a real intersection 10 . In laboratory gaze tasks, people with early to moderate retinitis pigmentosa increased head movement at the expense of eye movement, without increasing total gaze movement 11 . Even in hemianopia, the evidence is inconsistent: a virtual street-crossing study comparing clinical, simulated and absent hemianopia found no group differences in scanning behavior 12 . Importantly, none of these studies examined street crossing at the severe end of visual impairment; in the one that included late-stage retinitis pigmentosa, these participants made minimal gaze movements 11 . Head and eye movements have been recorded in a headmounted display while patients with central, peripheral or combined impairment detected approaching vehicles and identified safe crossing opportunities in a virtual street 13 . In the combined-loss subgroup of that study, the group closest to SPVI, head turns were less frequent than in controls. Because those participants judged a recorded scene while stationary, continuous head-yaw kinematics have not been measured while people with clinical SPVI physically cross a street.

Crossing is a decision made under uncertainty: the pedestrian accumulates information about approaching vehicles and initiates crossing when it is judged safe. Models of decision making predict that the duration of this decision depends both on the rate at which visual information about approaching vehicles is acquired and on the amount of that information required before committing [14] [15] [16] . The time spent at the curb, and its distribution across crossings, may therefore indicate the consequences of degraded vision.

To test this, we compared normally sighted adults, the same adults under simulated SPVI, and adults with clinical SPVI as they crossed a virtual street at graded levels of environmental complexity. The simulation separated the immediate effect of acuity loss from adaptation, orientation and mobility training, and the habits that accompany long-standing vision loss.

Because participants completed every crossing, measures of head scanning and of the time spent at the curb characterize how a crossing was approached, not whether the decision was safe. We asked whether people with severe vision loss compensate by increasing head scanning or by spending more time at the curb.

MATERIALS & METHODS

Participants

Twenty-seven adults participated: 10 normally sighted controls and 17 participants with SPVI (Table 1), all recruited from the Johns Hopkins Department of Ophthalmology. SPVI was defined as visual acuity of 20/200 (1.0 logMAR) or worse, spanning the severe (20/200-20/400) and profound (worse than 20/400, including near-total) categories of the International Classification of Diseases, Ninth Revision, Clinical Modification; the profound category corresponds broadly to ultra-low vision 7 , for which orientation and mobility assessments have been developed for vision-restoration trials 17 . Acuity was estimated with the Berkeley Rudimentary Vision Test 18 . Controls had no ocular disease or surgery and an acuity of 20/25 (0.1 logMAR) or better in each eye. We excluded participants with self-reported cognitive, hearing, or mobility impairment, and participants wore their habitual correction. Controls were tested under normal vision (NV) and under simulated SPVI (sSPVI), produced with stacked Bangerter filters that reduced acuity to approximately 20/2000 (2.0 logMAR), as in previous work from our laboratory 19 . The two conditions were run as separate sessions in counterbalanced order (all controls completed both; one session in the NV condition did not have head movement data). Mean age was 47.5 years (SD = 18.8; age bands from 21-25 to 86-90 years; 2 not recorded) for SPVI participants, and 8 of the 16 with recorded gender were women. Controls had a mean age of 35.1 years (SD = 10.5), and 5 of the 10 were women 20.

Participants with SPVI completed the protocol with and without a long cane; only trials without a mobility aid (cane or guide dog) were analyzed.

Apparatus

The virtual environment was developed in Unity and presented on an HTC VIVE Pro Eye head-mounted display (HTC Corporation, New Taipei City, Taiwan; 1440 × 1600 pixels per eye, 110° diagonal field of view, 90 Hz), with head pose tracked in six degrees of freedom by external base stations. Participants physically walked each crossing in the tracked laboratory space (Figure 1). An experimenter walked beside the participant in every trial. No auditory or tactile traffic information was provided; the only non-visual cue was a pair of wooden platforms on the floor at the positions of the two virtual curbs.

Virtual environment and task

The environment consisted of a straight roadway with a curb on each side and a marked crosswalk (Figure 2). Each trial began with the participant standing on the near curb, facing the crosswalk. Participants could view the scene for as long as they wished before crossing to a target on the opposite curb, approximately 4.6 m away. At levels A and B, the street was oneway without a center line, and at levels C and D, it was two-way with a double yellow center line. Level A was empty; level B contained parked cars and moving pedestrian avatars; level C contained moving vehicles in both directions that yielded at the crosswalk; and level D was level C with moving pedestrian avatars added. For analyses of pairs of levels, levels A and B, which contained no moving vehicles, were grouped as low demand, and levels C and D, which contained moving vehicles, as high demand.

Procedure

Street crossing was the first of three virtual environments in each session. Levels were presented in a fixed order from A to D, so that every participant encountered the least demanding environment first. Each level was preceded by an unrecorded practice trial. Participants were instructed to cross at a comfortable speed and to avoid cars and pedestrians. Each session contributed one crossing per level, for a total of 144 trials (NV 36, sSPVI 40, SPVI 68).

Head-movement recording and preprocessing

Head position (X along the crossing, Y vertical, Z lateral) and rotation were logged at approximately 9.1 Hz. Of the three rotation angles, only yaw, the rotation about the vertical axis, was analyzed. Yaw was referenced to 0° across the crosswalk, with positive values leftward, within -180° to 180°, and was not filtered; sessions that initialized facing the opposite direction (circular mean heading over the first 30 samples outside ±90°) were rotated by 180°. Walking speed was computed as the horizontal (X-Z) displacement of the head between samples divided by the sampling interval, then smoothed with a 10-sample (approximately 1.1 s) moving average.

Data cleaning.

A guide-dog session for one participant with SPVI was excluded. Where a level was recorded twice, the first recording that reached the far curb, at least 4.3 m from the start, was analyzed; the earlier recordings were a false start and an abandoned attempt. Trials in which the participant reached the far curb and then moved back toward the start by more than 1 m were ended at the farthest point (three trials for one control participant).

Study Variables

Step-off detection and epoch boundaries.

Step-off was the first sample at which smoothed walking speed reached 0.3 m/s. The pre-initiation epoch extended from trial onset to the preceding sample, and its duration was the latency to step-off. Latency to step-off agreed with the latency detected by the curb step-down criterion of our recent work 20 (a head drop of at least 0.1 m within 0.5 m of the start) in the 136 trials in which a step-down was detected, with high correlation (Spearman r(134) = 0.87, Pearson r(134) = 0.98, both p < 0.001). The two latency distributions did not differ (Figure S1). The speed criterion was retained because it marks the onset of sustained walking rather than the step-down. The walking bout was the first run of at least 25 consecutive samples (approximately 2.7 s) above 0.3 m/s. We identified it in 139 of 144 trials (NV 36, sSPVI 40, SPVI 63), and reported its duration as walking-bout duration.

Cumulative absolute yaw and scanning rate. The primary measure, cumulative absolute yaw (Ψ scan = Σ|Δψ|), was the sum of absolute sample-to-sample yaw changes, each wrapped to ±180°, computed over the whole trial, the pre-initiation epoch and the walking bout (Figure 3). Cumulative absolute yaw quantifies the total angular excursion of the head irrespective of direction; a 40° turn to the left and then back, for example, contributes 80°.

Scanning rate (°/s) was Ψ scan divided by the duration of the corresponding epoch (latency to stepoff or walking-bout duration).

Pre-post scanning index. The division of head scanning between the pre-initiation epoch and the walking bout was quantified for each trial as (Ψ post -Ψ pre )/(Ψ post + Ψ pre ), which ranges from -1 (head scanning only in the pre-initiation epoch) to +1 (head scanning only in the walking bout). Because both terms depend on the durations of the pre-initiation epoch and the walking bout, we computed a rate-normalized pre-post scanning index in the same way from the two scanning rates as a sensitivity analysis.

Statistical analysis

Analyses were performed in MATLAB R2025b (MathWorks, Natick, MA, USA). Each log-transformed outcome was analyzed with a linear mixed-effects model 21 2). The random intercept was grouped by participant, so each participant entered the model once.

Pairwise comparisons between viewing conditions were estimated by refitting the model to each pair of conditions with additive fixed effects. Results are reported as ratios of geometric means, and a Bonferroni correction was applied across the three comparisons per outcome (Table 3).

Pearson correlations across trials related cumulative absolute head yaw to the duration of the corresponding epoch. Both pre-post scanning indices were averaged within participants at low and at high demand, and the two averages were compared within each viewing condition with paired t-tests, Bonferroni-corrected across the three viewing conditions, using Cohen's d (mean difference divided by the standard deviation of the differences) as the effect size. Other tests used α = 0.05. Descriptive statistics are medians with median absolute deviations (MAD), except for the pre-post scanning index (mean, SD).

Ex-Gaussian model of latency to step-off.

Because a mean response time does not distinguish a shift of the distribution from a longer slow tail 14 , latencies to step-off were fitted by maximum likelihood (fminsearch, best of seven starting values) using an ex-Gaussian distribution, the sum of a Gaussian and an exponential component. The Gaussian mean μ and standard deviation σ describe the position and spread of the peak of the latency distribution, and the exponential mean τ describes its slow tail: a larger μ means that every crossing is delayed by a similar amount, whereas a larger τ means that some crossings are delayed much more than others; the mean of the distribution is μ + τ. In attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder, an elevated τ has been attributed to problems in arousal and state regulation and to difficulty detecting signal from noise 15 , and in healthy adults, τ is inversely associated with resting heart rate variability 22 , although no physiological measures were recorded here. Because latencies to step-off were approximately twice as long at high as at low demand (Figure S2), pooling the four levels would combine two distributions and increase the estimate of τ; the model was therefore fitted separately at low and at high demand in each viewing condition. Confidence intervals and two-sided p-values were obtained from 10,000 refits that resampled participants rather than trials, so that trials of the same participant were not treated as independent 23 , with the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of the resampled distribution as bounds 24, 25 . Comparisons between low and high demand were paired within participants, and comparisons between viewing conditions were unpaired, with Bonferroni correction within each family of three comparisons. σ reached its lower bound of one sampling interval (0.11 s) in four of the six fits and was not compared. Goodness of fit was assessed with Kolmogorov-Smirnov tests.

DISCUSSION

Time rather than scanning

Of the two forms of compensation considered in the Introduction, only additional time at the curb was observed: participants with SPVI took longer to step off than normally sighted participants, whereas cumulative absolute head yaw did not differ significantly between SPVI and normal or simulated vision, either in the pre-initiation epoch or in the walking bout. Because cumulative absolute head yaw accumulates with time, the additional time at the curb would by itself be expected to increase it; its smaller, non-significant increase in SPVI reflects a scanning rate that was, if anything, lower than under normal vision (Table 3, rows 5 and 11). These null results for cumulative absolute head yaw have wide confidence intervals and do not demonstrate equivalent head scanning; moreover, they were not consistent across complexity levels: in the walking bout, cumulative absolute head yaw increased steeply with complexity under normal and simulated vision but only slightly in SPVI (Figure 6A). Across viewing conditions, head scanning was influenced more by environmental complexity than by vision.

Compensatory scanning at the severe end of vision loss

The prediction that degraded vision requires more extensive head scanning was not supported at the severe end of vision loss. Compensatory head and eye scanning presupposes sufficient residual form vision for directed head movements to be informative: it has been documented where a defined blind field provides a direction for head and eye scanning, as in hemianopia 8, 9 , and where a useful visual field remains, as in early to moderate retinitis pigmentosa, in which head movement increases at the expense of eye movement, whereas participants with late-stage disease made minimal gaze movements 11 . With severely degraded vision, the available compensation may instead be additional time. The absence of increased head scanning in SPVI is consistent with reduced or unchanged head turning in macular degeneration 10 , reduced eye scanning in retinitis pigmentosa with tunnel vision 26 , and fewer head turns in the combined-loss group closest to SPVI in a study in which patients, judging a recorded scene while stationary, also identified safe gaps later than controls 13 . Earlier studies measured the accuracy of gap judgments in pedestrians with less severe vision loss or, with hearing alone, in blind pedestrians 4, 5 ; we measured latency to step-off on a task that every participant completed. Together, these findings are consistent with the view that vision loss limits the acquisition of information rather than its evaluation, so that degraded input increases the time required; whether accuracy declines when that time is not available was not tested here.

The present analysis quantified the extent of head yaw, not its direction.

Locus of the additional time

In clinical SPVI, the slow tail of the latency distribution was longer than under normal vision at both low and high demand, without a detectable shift of its peak, a dissociation that distributional analyses of response times are designed to detect 14 . The fastest crossings in SPVI were therefore initiated almost as promptly as under normal vision, whereas most were delayed by a variable rather than a fixed amount. In diffusion-model simulations of speeded two-choice decisions, poorer-quality evidence, expressed as a lower drift rate, results mainly in a larger τ; a longer non-decision time (sensory encoding and motor execution) results only in a larger μ; and greater caution, expressed as a wider decision boundary, results in a larger τ but also in a substantially larger μ 16,27 . Of the two determinants of the time spent at the curb outlined in the Introduction, a longer slow tail of the latency distribution without a significant shift of its peak is therefore more consistent with slower acquisition of visual information about approaching vehicles than with a larger amount of information required before committing, although μ was imprecisely estimated under the participant-level bootstrap, ex-Gaussian parameters do not map uniquely onto diffusion-model parameters, and the two determinants cannot be fully separated without a measure of accuracy 16 . If the longer slow tail of the latency distribution reflected attentional lapses, to which an elevated τ has been attributed in attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder and in healthy adults 15, 22 , only occasional crossings, rather than most, would be expected to be prolonged. The slow tail of the latency distribution was already longer at levels A and B, which contained no moving vehicles, so the effect of vision loss did not depend on traffic; at levels C and D, which contained moving vehicles, the slow tail of the latency distribution was significantly longer than at levels A and B only in SPVI, but this increase did not differ significantly between viewing conditions, so whether traffic adds to the effect of vision loss remains unresolved.

Two alternative explanations are also consistent with the data on latency to step-off.

Because the task provided no auditory or tactile traffic information and cane trials were excluded, prolonged latencies to step-off may reflect the absence of visual evidence about approaching vehicles rather than its slow accumulation. In addition, the vehicles at levels C and D yielded, which makes these latencies to step-off incomparable with the gap-acceptance literature; because yielding is likely to be difficult to detect at this level of vision, part of the slow tail of the latency distribution may reflect the time taken to abandon the judgment of whether vehicles had yielded rather than to complete it.

Acute simulation and long-standing vision loss

Comparing simulated with clinical SPVI separates the immediate effect of acuity loss from adaptation to long-standing vision loss. On mean latency to step-off, the sighted controls with the Bangerter filters showed most of the additional latency to step-off of clinical SPVI, and simulated and clinical SPVI did not differ significantly on any measure (Table 3, rows 3, 6, 9, 12, 15, 18 and 21); the additional latency to step-off therefore does not require long-standing vision loss. Cumulative absolute head yaw in the pre-initiation epoch was also higher with the Bangerter filters but not in clinical SPVI; the increase with the filters may reflect a more conservative decision criterion under unfamiliar blur. The smaller increase in cumulative absolute head yaw with complexity in the walking bout in SPVI, which was not observed under simulated SPVI, together with walking bouts that were no shorter than under normal vision, suggests that participants with long-standing vision loss made the decision to step off before initiating crossing and then crossed with little further visual updating of the traffic scene, whereas participants under the acute blur produced by the Bangerter filters continued to scan as sighted pedestrians do; neither difference was tested directly. The slow tail of the latency distribution was longest in SPVI, although acuity with the Bangerter filters was poorer than that of 11 of the 14 participants with SPVI whose acuity was recorded (Table 1); any excess over simulation, if confirmed, would therefore reflect aspects of clinical SPVI that the Bangerter filters do not simulate, such as field loss, disease-specific visual characteristics and age, or strategies adapted to non-visual cues. After vision loss, some auditory abilities have been proposed to improve and others to decline 28 , and pedestrians who are blind rely on hearing to detect approaching vehicles where there is no traffic control 29 ; because no auditory traffic information was presented in the virtual task, participants with long-standing vision loss were tested without the cues on which their adapted strategies depend. The outcome under simulation can therefore match that of clinical vision loss even when the underlying process differs, a caveat for the routine use of acuity simulation, including in our previous work 19.

Distribution of head scanning before and during the crossing

At high complexity, participants with clinical SPVI allocated a larger proportion of their cumulative absolute head yaw to the pre-initiation epoch (Figure 8); under simulated SPVI, the shift was of similar size but not significant after correction. Computed from scanning rate, the pre-post scanning index did not change significantly, so the larger proportion reflects a longer pre-initiation epoch rather than a higher scanning rate at the curb relative to that during the walking bout: with moving vehicles, participants with SPVI spent longer at the curb, whereas walking bouts were not longer at higher complexity and their cumulative absolute head yaw in the walking bout increased only slightly. Consistent with this, cumulative absolute head yaw was more strongly correlated with the duration of the pre-initiation epoch than with that of the walking bout (Figure 7), although the narrower range of walking-bout durations limits this comparison. The redistribution of head scanning toward the curb is therefore a consequence of the additional time at the curb rather than a separate form of compensation, consistent with a decision to step off that was completed before crossing was initiated.

Implications for orientation and mobility

The additional time at the curb could reflect slower acquisition of visual information about approaching vehicles or a larger amount of information required before committing, and the two have different implications. If vision loss slows acquisition, as the present results suggest, instructing a patient or client to scan more would not be expected to help, whereas preserving time at the curb would; if it increases the amount of information required, the time spent at the curb reflects a strategy that may be amenable to training. In either case, limited head movement at a curb does not by itself indicate that a patient or client is not surveying the street, and training environments could be graded by environmental complexity rather than by acuity.

Both remain hypotheses. To our knowledge, no study has shown that increased head or eye scanning improves crossing safety in low vision; in drivers with hemianopia, auditory reminders increased early head scanning, and early scans were associated with faster responses to hazards in a simulator, but whether this improves safety has not been tested prospectively 9 . Controlled evidence for orientation and mobility training in low vision is limited 30, 31 , and we are not aware of any trial of street-crossing training in adults with SPVI. Because the additional time at the curb was variable and often long rather than fixed, crossing aids timed to average pedestrian performance could be insufficient for many crossings; whether a vehicle has yielded could instead be conveyed non-visually, for example by a wearable vibrotactile display calibrated to the wearer's vibrotactile sensitivity 32.

Limitations

Crossing initiation is delayed in virtual environments relative to a real test track, and effects observed in simulators can be absent on the track 33 , so neither the absolute latencies to step-off nor necessarily their pattern may generalize directly to real streets; differences between age groups, however, were reproduced in both a CAVE simulator and a head-mounted display for most crossing measures 34 , and conventional orientation and mobility assessment is itself imperfectly standardized 35 . The ex-Gaussian fits pooled two trials per participant at each pair of levels, far fewer than are typically used for such fits; τ therefore describes the group distribution and cannot distinguish long slow tails of the latency distribution within individuals from consistently slower participants, and the goodness-of-fit test, computed on the same trials, is anticonservative. With 9, 10 and 17 sessions per condition, the between-condition comparisons had limited statistical power. Among the 14 participants with SPVI whose acuity was recorded, acuity was concentrated between 1.0 and 1.6 logMAR (10 of 14; Table 1), too narrow a range to test whether the effects varied with acuity; in an exploratory analysis, acuity was not significantly correlated with latency to step-off (geometric mean per participant), Spearman r(12) = 0.27, p = 0.347. Head yaw is not equivalent to gaze direction. The virtual environment posed no physical risk; and the complexity levels were presented in a fixed order, so their effects are confounded with practice. Whether head-yaw kinematics predict travel incidents outside the laboratory remains to be determined in prospective studies.

Conclusions

Pedestrians with SPVI took longer to step off than normally sighted pedestrians, and the additional time was variable and often long rather than fixed; they did not scan at a higher rate, so any increase in their cumulative head scanning resulted from the additional time. When vehicles were moving, a larger share of their head scanning occurred before step-off, because they spent longer at the curb rather than because their scanning rate at the curb rose relative to that during walking. For pedestrians with SPVI, the time spent at the curb, and its variable, rightskewed lengthening, may therefore be a more informative target than the extent of head scanning, both for orientation and mobility assessment and for the design of accessible crossings, particularly uncontrolled crossings at which no non-visual information about yielding vehicles is available.
