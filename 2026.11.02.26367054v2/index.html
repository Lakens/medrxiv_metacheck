Power Analysis Check

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). Abstract

Observational studies using electronic health records are being increasingly used to answer research questions of interest in deprescribing, where a medication is intentionally discontinued or the dose reduced.

Target trial emulation (TTE) is particularly attractive as it is guided by sound theoretical principles based on causal inference. In the field of deprescribing, the evaluation of TTE within a simulation setting can provide evidence on optimal approaches for implementation and compare different estimation approaches which theory cannot easily inform. Deprescribing studies are susceptible to time-dependent confounding, competing risks due to early adverse events, time-dependent effects and other data complexities which may render estimates biased if such aspects are not properly addressed. In this work, a Monte-Carlo simulation study was conducted to estimate the performance (bias and coverage) of seven methods within several synthetic data generating mechanisms involving the above characteristics. The methods implemented were Cox regression, inverse probability of treatment weighted (IPTW) Cox regression, naive sequential Cox regression, IPTW weighted sequential Cox regression, extended Cox regression and the parametric g-formula. The IPTW Cox regression and IPTW sequential Cox regression were the only methods achieving nominal coverage and unbiasedness under the scenarios evaluated.

INTRODUCTION

Deprescribing is defined as stopping or tapering a medication that is considered by a medical practitioner to be of no clinical benefit. 1 As it is the removal of a therapy, there are no regulatory requirements for demonstrating the efficacy or safety of deprescribing using, for example, conclusive randomised controlled trials (RCTs). Several RCTs have been conducted in order to determine the risks and benefits of deprescribing certain drug classes compared to continuation. While RCTs are the gold standard for assessing the causal effect of an intervention, they are particularly difficult to conduct when examining the deprescribing of medicines, since the eligible population of interest are typically older with significant multi-morbidity; hence are inherently more vulnerable than younger patients.

To address these challenges, observational data such as electronic health records (EHR) may be analysed in an attempt to estimate causal effects. 2 In non-randomised studies, the risk of bias is inherently greater than in a randomised controlled trial. Residual confounding between experimental and comparison groups, as well as heterogeneous treatment effects may lead to incorrect conclusions depending on modelling choices. In deprescribing interventions specifically, there is often a highly unpredictable treatment course, due to the lack of clinical guidance, and intercurrent events may occur such as restarting drug treatment, that may modify the probability and/or interpretation of the outcomes of interest. Such events must be accounted for in the causal question to prevent biased inference.

When studying the effect of drug discontinuation a new-user design may be used, where the control group is those continuing on the medication of interest and the exposed group are those for whom deprescribing of the medication is initiated. However, deprescribing may not be initiated for certain people such as those who do not experience major adverse events, and so determining the effects in these patients may be difficult without interventional data. Within deprescribing studies, the outcomes of interest may range from the occurrence of major adverse events (e.g., acute myocardial infarction, stroke, cardiovascular death) to indicators of medication burden (e.g., drugs with significant anticholinergic or sedative effects). The cause-effect relationship between interventions and clinical outcomes is typically summarised using time-to-event methods, incorporating not only whether the event occurs but also when it occurs.

From a time-to-event analysis perspective, there are several quantitative approaches for estimation of causal effects in nonrandomised settings, and the G-methods are necessary for valid inference when treatment affects the failure time of interest. 3 The application of G-methods in observational studies on deprescribing is natural due to the time-dependent nature of treatment, where time-dependent confounding may be present. However, to date, the performance of the various methods under realistic data scenarios has not been tested.

Target trial emulation (TTE) is being increasingly recognised as a framework which may enable the estimation of causal effects, given certain assumptions, similar to those that may be obtained from an RCT. 4 In TTE, a question-first approach is taken by specifying the causal question, defining inclusion criteria and treatment strategies for a hypothetical RCT which would successfully generate the data required to answer the question, and then determining the modelling/data requirements. While TTE is not a quantitative method of estimation, it can be considered a study design or framework which formalizes the notion of the causal estimand and should impact the definition of risk sets as well as estimation in the analysis stage.

The aim of this study is to evaluate the performance of several estimation methods within the TTE framework for causal inference using semi-synthetic data. The simulated data represents longitudinal event histories which exhibit time-dependent confounding, stochastic treatment switching and terminal competing risks. We define the data generating mechanism in section 5 using a conceptual model of how event histories may be generated in routine care. We then describe the details of a target trial based on a clinical research question that may be emulated using the observed data presented in section 6. The research question focusses on the effect of deprescribing versus continuing a medication, on the occurrence of a major adverse event

3 CAUSAL INFERENCE IN NON-MARKOV MULTI-STATE MODELS

Multi-state modelling is a useful starting point for analysing and generating complex event histories such as those that may be found in observational deprescribing data. In this work, the data generating mechanism (DGM) is based on a multi-state model and we also incorporate such models in one of the estimation methods. Multi-state models are highly flexible models capable of describing processes that may change over time, such as an individual's health status. A multi-state model is a generalization of the competing risks model, which generalizes the basic survival model. 10 . Thus, multi-state modelling is a useful starting point for analysing and generating complex event histories such as those that may be found in observational deprescribing data. While popular for prediction, these models are not typically used for causal inference. 11 However, certain parameters of multi-state models can be given a causal interpretation when the assumptions of causal inference hold. Additionally, central to the utility of such models is a property known as modularity or local independence, which means that interventions on a causal node (e.g., modification of a transition intensity) does not directly affect other transition intensities. 12, 13 This assumption is required to hold in order for the effects of interventions on transition intensities to be identifiable. Multi-state modelling approaches have been used to describe randomised controlled trials, 14, 15, 16 however, to our knowledge, they have not been used within the target trial emulation framework for conducting observational analyses.

A multi-state regression model can incorporate an arbitrary number of states, but as the number of states becomes larger it may become computationally infeasible to use them. The number of states is usually kept as small as possible, with the potential for extension including covariates that may modify the behaviour of transitions. Irreversible transitions, where a state can only be entered once are common (e.g., the three-state healthy-diseased-dead model), while reversible models, which are suited to e.g., relapsing-remitting conditions are less popular. A notable characteristic of reversible models is that the maximum number of state transitions is theoretically unbounded, which requires consideration for inference. 17

The aim of the simulation study was to evaluate the performance of different estimation approaches under various datageneration scenarios relevant to deprescribing where a drug is abruptly stopped (i.e. not tapered). 5.2 Observable data from the DGM

Our data available for inference was intended to represent routinely collected healthcare data, since this study focusses on using the TTE framework to infer causal effects from observational data. We distinguish between two distinct types of observation that captures the underlying state of individuals in an observational setting; event-or symptom-driven visits and scheduled visits. 29 Event-driven visits occur due to some change in health state (e.g. worsening symptoms) and is influenced by the patients' desire or need to see their provider. Scheduled visits are specified by the healthcare provider at a prior visit, usually for monitoring the starting/stopping of a treatment. Scheduled visits may thus be uneventful and not record any change in health state unless there was an incidental finding, whereas event-driven visits always record a change in health state, and the patient's covariates may be measured. 6.3 Defining the true estimand

The true estimand was defined as the causal hazard ratio of deprescribing versus continuing to be prescribed with the failure time being the time to the first major adverse event (V = 3). We both ignored and accounted for treatment deviation by defining an intention-to-treat (ITT) effect and per-protocol (PP) effect respectively. The true ITT effect was estimated from an oracle . dataset which was generated using a hypothetical intervention characterised by setting the initial state occupied by the whole population at simulation time zero. This intervention relates to immediately deprescribing the whole population at time zero. Thus, two counterfactual datasets were generated, each having identical baseline covariates, however the exposed group had A = 1 and the unexposed group had A = 0. We termed the latter dataset the "natural course", since it represents the natural sequence of events for our population, i.e. the population at t 0 was defined to be those prescribed and without any episodes of prior deprescribing, whom may stochastically enter the deprescribed state over time. The dataset with A = 1 was termed the intervened dataset. All inference was performed on the natural course dataset only. The estimand was computed using Monte-Carlo integration. 34 We note that in order to ensure positivity for the treatment strategies, the population at time zero of analysis should consist of people with both types of exposure status. Thus, all of the analyses begin at time t start > t 0 . The choice of t start affects the characteristics of the simulated population, for example by reducing the proportion of those patients alive and event free over time or by changing the proportion of exposed patients. To align the analysis and oracle populations in this study, the oracle dataset was modified to consist only of those patients who were alive and event-free within the natural course at t start , and their covariates were updated to those that would be measured at t start . This was possible since all covariates were adapted processes based on known deterministic functions.

The ITT estimand was determined using the natural course and intervened datasets. The PP estimand was obtained using two additional datasets with initial state intervention and modified transition intensities. A Cox proportional hazard model was fitted with the only covariate being the dataset indicator (exposed/unexposed) and the hazard ratio was obtained. We note that under a general non-proportional hazards DGM, the estimate obtained from the Cox partial likelihood estimator is not a timeaveraged hazard ratio over the follow up period, the so-called average hazard ratio (aHR) is a separate quantity 35 . We note that the parameter obtained from the Cox partial likelihood estimate depends on the censoring distribution. 36, 31 Thus, the estimand in this simulation study can be viewed as a projection of the true (time-varying) HR onto a PH working model. A natural solution is to include more general models, however as the PH assumption is used in both oracle and inference estimation, it will not affect the performance measures. We thus assume that the true estimand is the value obtained from the partial likelihood estimator.

Furthermore, in this study, we assumed prescribing data and relevant outcome data (e.g., when someone is prescribed a medicine and the diagnosis of an adverse event) is all that is available for inference. Dispensing data may also be available but we assume this is not the case in this study. In this case, an intention-to-treat effect can be defined based on prescribing status at a single point in time. Estimation of the ITT effect in the oracle dataset used the initial state occupation as the sole exposure status, regardless of the post-baseline status. Estimation of the PP effect used intervention on cause-specific hazards to set the hazard of treatment switching to zero. This consisted of setting all exposure specific transition hazards to zero, keeping all other hazards at the value defined by the natural course. This hypothetical intervention assumes that prevention of treatment switching is independent of potential outcomes (modularity assumption).

In summary the ITT estimand focused on a point-in-time exposure Ā = A(0) = a and the PP estimand focused on a sustained exposure over time Ā = A(t) = a. These strategies are deterministic static treatment strategies. For identifying the PP estimand, we make the assumption that treatment switching is independent of the hazard and artificially censor when this occurs. Thus, conditional on the relevant covariates, the counterfactual hazard for those who change exposure status is assumed equal to that of those who remain on their assigned treatment up to time t.

7 SIMULATION METHODS

We focused on the most common continuous-time parametric and semi-parametric likelihood-based methods. Non-parametric methods based on hazard estimation require some transformation (e.g., smoothing) to summarise the risk measure 21 and so these methods were not assessed. We used two common approaches to correct for the lack of exchangeability, inverse probability of treatment weighting and standardisation using parametric outcome regression. Matching was not considered as it may exclude some eligible individuals from the analysis, depending on the matching approach used. The methods implemented were naive Cox regression, inverse probability of treatment weighted (IPTW) Cox regression, the naive sequential Cox method, IPTW weighted sequential Cox regression, extended Cox regression and a parametric multi-state model based on the g-formula.

7.1 Crude Cox proportional hazards model

The Cox model is the most ubiquitous modelling method used for failure times in epidemiology. The semi-parametric form of the Cox model provides useful flexibility in terms of the absolute risk of events, while often making the assumption of proportional hazards for modelling simplicity. It has been stated that the Cox model (and other models providing contrasts of counterfactual hazards) do not generally allow causal interpretation of the contrasts. 37 . This is because the hazard function conditions on survival to time t, but the population at t may be affected by prior effects of treatment, or non-treatment effects (selection effects), and therefore is not identical to the randomised population. This is a feature of period-specific contrasts, which can be ignored when taking a functional view of hazards over time. 18 The functional view of hazards discourages point-in-time interpretations due to the above selection effects, but also prevents any interpretation of hazards as being direct effects.

7.3 Crude sequential Cox model

The analysis of times-to-events may be applied in a sequential trial approach, whereby a number of cohort entry times are chosen from which to measure follow-up. 39 Exposure is assessed prior to this time such that each time zero mimics the randomisation time of a hypothetical trial. This approach will generally lead to greater data utilisation compared to using a single calendar-based cohort entry time. since a patient can contribute to more than one trial if they meet the inclusion criteria more than once. This approach was implemented for both estimands using an approach similar to the method in section 7.1. The major difference is that in this method, several different baseline times were used to assess inclusion and treatment status at pre-specified intervals. There are two time-related quantities affecting the interpretation of this method; the interval between entry times and the follow-up time of each trial. The trial interval ∆ trial is the difference in calendar time between time zero of one trial and the next. In real world settings, the interval should be chosen based on the frequency of the events of interest and exposure changes and other information, such as the treatment dynamics. After defining the sequence of trial start times, the analysis proceeds identically to section 7.1 by constructing multiple risk sets and pooling all trial data to obtain the marginal hazard ratio.

One consideration in a closed-cohort setting such as that in this work is the effect of covariate drift on the construction of the risk sets. Necessarily some time-dependent covariates (e.g., age) will vary systematically, and if these have a non-zero effect on the outcome, then it may be preferable to stratify analysis by this variable or use trial-specific random effects. In the simulation, we expect covariate drift to have a negligible effect on the pooled HR estimate due to the magnitude of time-dependent effect modification.

7.7 Clone-censor weighting

The final method implemented was the increasingly popular clone-censor weight (CCW) method. 45, 4 CCW was originally introduced in order to estimate the parameters of a marginal structural model of which the target was a dynamic treatment regime. 46 This was motivated by the fact that a person's treatment status may temporarily be consistent with multiple treatment strategies. Thus, replication of a sample (cloning) ensures that the modelling accounts for the multiple types of exposure received. Another application of CCW is when the treatment is a random static regime, such as "treat within 10 days of baseline". In the context of failure-time outcomes, artificial censoring may be used to handle intercurrent events. CCW consists of the application of cloning individuals at baseline, artificially censoring when the treatment status is no longer consistent with that of the clone and weighting the sample by the inverse probability of being censored to ensure that artificial censoring is conditionally independent of the outcome. In this work, CCW was implemented using data augmentation and standard Cox regression.

The data augmentation consisted of choosing a time window for assessing the start of treatment status. In this study we chose 90 months. Each patient was then cloned at baseline in to a group representing each treatment status (prescribed/deprescribed). If, by the end of the window, a person had entered in to the deprescribed state, the patient in the prescribed group would have their follow-up artificially censored at that time. If a patient did not enter the deprescribed state by the end of the interval, those in that group would have their follow-up censored at the end of the interval.

10 DISCUSSION

This simulation study has found meaningful differences in bias and coverage across different methods to estimate the causal effect of medication deprescribing using the target trial emulation framework. Each of the performance measures will be discussed in detail in separate sections below.

10.1 Bias

While bias is inherently quantitative, it is useful to categorise bias according to either the known or likely source. In simulation studies, the source of bias can be differentiated based on knowledge of the DGM and estimators used. An analogue of bias is the concept of statistical error in single samples. Kawahara et al. 48 distinguished between three sources of statistical error (the difference between a sample estimate and its true value); (1) estimand error, (2) structural error and (3) error due to random noise. This categorisation can be used to similarly describe bias sources. However, since bias is the long-run difference between the estimate and its true value, random noise by definition should not contribute to bias. In a simulation study with a finite number of simulation repetitions, however, the performance measures are subject to Monte Carlo error, making it difficult to distinguish residual random variation from systematic bias. We assume that the number of simulation repetitions is sufficiently large that the Monte Carlo standard errors of the performance measures are negligible relative to the differences in bias being investigated. For all DGM factors, estimands and methods, there were no outliers that had a disproportionate effect on the bias. We first focus on estimand error, also known as estimand misalignment. This source of bias is likely to occur when the research question is either ill-defined and/or the translation into a statistical estimand is not correct. In target trial emulation, clearly specifying the question and the estimand is paramount to the methodology and heavily encouraged. 49, 50 In our example target trial, the question concerns the effect of stopping a drug immediately versus continuing, which describes a single-point exposure. The other method which suffers from estimand error is the time-dependent Cox (TD-Cox) model. As mentioned in section 7.5, this model targets the partial transition rate. This estimator estimates a parameter from a common multi-state model, the three-state reversible model with 1 absorbing state, also known as the healthy-illness-death model with recovery. The estimand obtained in the TD-Cox model corresponds to the ratio of the partial transition rates of illness to death and healthy to death. The standard Cox model incorporating the proportional hazards assumption either implies a constant hazard ratio over time or a projection of the true hazard ratio on to a constant in a Cox partial likelihood sense, which is dependent on the censoring mechanism and length of follow-up. When using a TD-Cox model where the hazard may depend on the current value of exposure, then the estimate obtained further depends on the exposure process, which may change at different times for different people. When the underlying multi-state process is Markov, the ratio of partial transition rates equals the ratio of transition intensities. In this simulation, since the hazards were non-Markov, the estimator resulting from the TD-Cox model does not target the correct estimand. This is expected because the ratio of partial transition rates closely resembles the true hazard ratio, for this estimand. For per-protocol estimands, only information from exposure consistent with that at baseline contributes to the estimate. The small amount of bias is due to time-dependent effect modification that is present.

We now discuss the presence of structural bias, due to the failure of identifying conditions required for inference. The identifying conditions always required are the three basic causal inference assumptions of positivity, exchangeability and consistency. When estimands include time-to-event outcomes, independent censoring is also required. Since administrative censoring in this study was generated independently for all patients, this assumption is fulfilled. As mentioned in section 3.1, consistency is fulfilled in this study by design. Modularity is only required for models employing outcome regression, such as the g-formula. Positivity and exchangeability are thus the two potential causes of structural bias for the methods. It should be noted that the clone-censor-weight method is particularly invulnerable to structural bias, since exchangeability at the index time is guaranteed and the random nature of the treatment strategy provides variation in the allowable initiation time. However, as already mentioned, the random nature of the exposure is also potentially undesirable when answering certain research questions that have a deterministic exposure, such as "begin treatment at time zero".

For both estimands, the two methods which do not account for confounding, the crude Cox and crude sequential trials methods are severely biased. Confounding was due to both baseline values of covariates and the time-updated values of covariates affecting the risk of the outcome and exposure. For the ITT estimand, the bias in the crude Cox model is entirely due to nonexchangeability, since positivity was ensured by the estimand definition. The crude sequential trials method enjoys greater precision due to the use of a larger number of samples, however since it does not account for confounding it was biased as a result.

In the base case DGM, the parametric g-formula had a bias of 0.232 (0.213, 0.250) for the ITT estimand and 0.248 (0.215, 0.280) for the PP estimand. This was expected due to the parametric form of the outcome model used. Since the deprescribed to AE-major event hazard was generated from a Weibull distribution, this resulted in violation of the Markov assumption. Thus, the cause-specific regression model was misspecified for this transition. The result was severely biased away from the null for all other DGM factors except factors 8 and 4. In deprescribing, time-varying hazards of the outcome of interest are expected. Reasons for this are that the physiological effects of stopping a medication may not be immediate and there may be short-term withdrawal effects soon after deprescribing that cease after some time. Certain benefits of deprescribing, such as increased focus and sleep quality 51 may also take some time to occur.

The interval estimate of bias for the marginal structural model and IPTW sequential trials methods included zero for four DGM factors and for both estimands. However the point estimate was close to zero for all factors. There was no apparent trend in the bias with respect to the number of observations or the true effect size. For the large value of n obs , the IPTW sequential trials method had a bias that included zero, while the marginal structural model had a nonzero bias for both estimands respectively. However, the upper estimate for the ITT estimand was -0.003 (less than 1 % relative bias), and we expect the estimate would tend to zero as n sim increases.

Interestingly, the PP estimate for the methods that accounted for non-exchangeability was also slightly biased due to practical violations of positivity, as seen in Figure 3. The IPTW sequential trials method had a bias of -0.028 (-0.062, 0.005) for the base case. After inspecting individual replicate cumulative incidence curves, the cause of this bias was attributed as follows.

In order to estimate the causal effect in the hypothetical scenario where subjects do not switch treatment status, each method artificially censors subjects at the point of a treatment deviation intercurrent event. Consequently, within some simulation . replicates, few or no individuals remained at risk of the outcome at longer follow-up times. When no individuals remain at risk, the outcome process cannot be estimated beyond that time point. When only a small number of patients remained at risk, estimation also became unstable. This can be particularly problematic for estimands based on the hazard ratio, which depends on the composition of the risk sets over time. When the true hazard ratio varies over time, this truncated follow-up will induce a different estimate to the one where no censoring occurs due to the aforementioned dependence on the follow-up time. Marginal risk estimands such as the risk difference are also affected by insufficient information at later follow-up times, but do not depend on the evolving risk set composition as does a hazard ratio. When using sequential trials approaches, there is thus a need to ensure positivity in all trials, to prevent estimation failure and ensure correct inference. Within empirical studies, this may be achieved by using information on propensity scores to alter the dataset using truncation. However, modifying the data in this way has the potential unwanted effect of changing the population of interest.

10.2 Coverage

The coverage of each method reflects the joint performance of a method's point estimation and variance estimation procedures. Confidence interval coverage is expected to be close to the nominal level when the estimator is approximately unbiased and the estimated standard errors accurately reflect the true sampling variability. The methods with large bias all had anti-conservative coverage, as expected. Even though all methods, where possible, used robust standard errors, the bias due to estimand and structural sources led to nominal coverage not being obtained. The CCW method had zero coverage for the ITT estimand for all DGM factors since the standard errors were zero for all replicates. This was due to the estimand definition as explained in section 10.1. For the PP estimand, censoring at treatment deviation from baseline led to a coverage probability of 0.888 (0.857, 0.918). The TD-Cox method had extremely low coverage of 0.010 (0.000, 0.020) for the base case and ITT estimand. The coverage for the PP estimand was 0.930 (0.905, 0.955). While this is unexpected due to the estimand misalignment, the coverage decreased accordingly with the increase in bias, as γ decreased. As such, the nominal coverage for this particular factor was coincidental. This highlights the importance of examining several performance measures in a simulation study, as well as several factors in the DGM. A time-dependent Cox model is particularly tempting for scenarios where patients who experience improvement in symptoms are more likely to discontinue treatment, because treatment status changes frequently. This method may utilise more information compared to a Cox model using baseline exposure status only, and thus precision in the estimate may be higher. However, these results show that such a model may target a fundamentally different estimand from the effect of a well-specified deprescribing strategy. Consequently, a TTE using TD-Cox model could answer a different question from the one clinicians are interested in, while still producing apparently precise estimates due to the small standard errors it provides.

The coverage for the crude Cox model for the ITT estimand was 0.488 (0.439, 0.536) and for the PP estimand was 0.785 (0.745, 0.825). Coverage for the both estimands had a trend that was positively correlated with the γ parameter, and negatively correlated with n obs . The crude sequential trials method had a coverage of 0.552 (0.504, 0.601) for the ITT estimand and 0.868 (0.834, 0.901) for the PP estimand. The bias present in both of these methods was the source of low coverage, since the relative error in the model standard error was negligible.

The parametric g-formula had coverage of 0.610 (0.562, 0.658) for the ITT estimand and 0.760 (0.718, 0.802) for the PP estimand. Both bias and inaccuracy in the standard errors contributed to the observed anti-conservative coverage. The model-based standard errors differed from the empirical standard errors by approximately 30%, likely reflecting the relatively small number of bootstrap samples used to estimate uncertainty. We acknowledge this as a limitation of the study, as inaccurate standard error estimates may have affected confidence interval coverage. However, substantial bias was also present, as shown in Figure 3, indicating that the poor coverage was not solely attributable to variance estimation. Consequently, our conclusion that the implementation of a multi-state Markov model for time-varying hazards produced substantial bias remains unchanged. The poor performance observed for the parametric g-formula should not be interpreted as a limitation of the g-formula itself, but rather of the outcome model used to implement it. In this study, transition probabilities were estimated using a multi-state Markov model, which assumes that future transitions depend only on the current state and covariate history through the specified hazard model. When the underlying data-generating mechanism exhibits complex time-varying hazards, misspecification of the transition process may lead to biased estimates of intervention effects. Future research could investigate more flexible approaches for modelling transition hazards within the g-formula framework, including semi-Markov models, flexible parametric survival models, spline-based hazard models, or machine learning approaches. Such methods may better accommodate non-Markovian . behaviour and complex hazard trajectories while retaining the ability of the g-formula to estimate causal effects under dynamic treatment strategies.

The marginal structural model had a coverage of 0.927 (0.902 ,0.953) for the ITT estimand and 0.943 (0.920, 0.965) for the PP estimand. The nominal coverage was achieved as expected due to all identifying assumptions being met. The IPTW sequential trials method had a coverage of 0.940 (0.917, 0.963) for the ITT estimand and 0.943 (0.920, 0.965) for the PP estimand. Although practical positivity violations introduced a small amount of bias for the PP estimand, this bias was insufficient to meaningfully affect confidence interval coverage, which remained close to the nominal level. There was no observed trend in the point estimate of coverage with respect to any DGM factors. This is expected for the type of factors varied in the study, since they had little impact on structural bias, and given that the methods were correctly specified and their identifying assumptions satisfied.

The results demonstrate that the methods which appropriately accounted for the confounding structure and targeted the estimand of interest provided approximately nominal confidence interval coverage for the effects of deprescribing interventions in the presence of time-varying confounding. In contrast, methods affected by structural biases, estimand misalignment, or outcome-model misspecification in the case of the parametric g-formula produced confidence intervals that failed to contain the true effect of the intervention. These findings suggest that when analysing observational deprescribing data, obtaining valid confidence intervals depend as much on selecting an appropriate causal framework and estimand as on accurate estimation of standard errors and correct modelling choices.

11 CONCLUSIONS

This study evaluated the performance of several methods commonly used in target trial emulation for estimating the effects of deprescribing interventions from observational data, in the context of deprescribing a medication as the intervention of interest. By generating data from a multi-state model with time-varying confounding, recurrent events, treatment switching, and non-Markov outcome processes, the study provided a realistic setting in which both methodological and practical challenges encountered in deprescribing research could be assessed. The definition of a primary estimand is crucial for correct interpretation of clinical trials and observational studies emulating clinical trials. In this study, it was shown that within the target trial emulation framework, estimator choice can have a drastic impact on the performance of methods in terms of the ability to estimate the true value of an estimand as well as quantify the uncertainty in the estimate. While explicit specification of estimators is difficult without some knowledge of the data structure, it can be a vital tool in preventing failed estimation (through positivity violations) or misleading inference (through bias). Among the methods evaluated, the marginal structural model and inverse-probability-weighted sequential trials approach consistently demonstrated the best overall performance. Both methods produced negligible bias and achieved close-to-nominal confidence interval coverage across the range of data-generating mechanism factors investigated. Small departures from unbiasedness for the per-protocol estimand were observed due to practical positivity violations arising from artificial censoring at treatment deviations, but these effects were modest and had little impact on statistical inference. The use of multiple index times within the sequential trials method can improve precision since a larger sample size is obtained compared to using a single index time. The choice of IPTW ST versus an MSM should be based on the potential for covariate drift. This occurs when time-dependent effect modifiers systematically vary with respect to the time scale on which the trials are defined. For example, within closed cohort settings, the distribution of age and disease severity may be systematically different between early and later trials when using calendar time as the time scale. Alternative designs defining sequential trials based on age bands rather than calendar time may mitigate this. Such covariate drift shifts the population in which the causal effect is defined. If this is not consistent with the research question, then using a single index date (e.g., by employing an MSM) or estimating causal effects stratified by the index time may be required. These findings suggest that methods based on inverse-probability weighting provide a robust framework for estimating both intention-to-treat and per-protocol effects of deprescribing interventions when the required identifying assumptions are plausible.

The study also highlighted the importance of outcome-model specification when using outcome-regression approaches. Although the g-formula is theoretically capable of estimating the target estimand under the same identifying assumptions (since it is algebraically equivalent to the inverse weighting method), the implementation based on a multi-state Markov model performed poorly when the underlying hazards were non-Markov and varied over time. This suggests that future applications of the g-formula to deprescribing research may benefit from more flexible approaches for modelling transition hazards and event histories, when such behaviour is expected. The primary advantage of the parametric g-formula compared to weighting-based . estimators is the ability to extrapolate over sparse regions of the covariate space, which can solve issues relating to positivity violations. This is however only possible when the conditional regressions are correctly specified.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. The simulation scenarios considered a specific multi-state structure and a limited set of confounding mechanisms, treatment processes, and outcome dynamics. Performance may differ under alternative data-generating mechanisms, particularly in settings with stronger positivity violations, measurement error, unmeasured confounding, or more complex treatment strategies involving longitudinal and/or dynamic regimens. Future work may examine the performance of the methods tested herein under these scenarios. We also did not systematically evaluate computational complexity of the methods, which is an important part of empirical studies. In addition, uncertainty for the g-formula was estimated using a limited number of bootstrap samples, which may have contributed to inaccuracies in variance estimation. Consequently, the findings should be interpreted as evidence for the scenarios studied rather than as absolute rankings of performance.

The pre-specification of several different estimands using various strategies to account for intercurrent events can provide clinicians with a hierarchy of evidence on the effects of varying levels of interventions. For example, the effect of initiating deprescribing using a treatment policy estimand may be useful for indicating whether or not it is effective to initiate deprescribing for a certain population. Other estimands based on hypothetical or while-on-treatment intercurrent event strategies may be used to infer the average treatment effect of deprescribing in a population over the whole course of the treatment (e.g., without restarting the medicine or using other rescue therapies), which may be more relevant for patients.

Overall, this work demonstrates that target trial emulation can be a valuable framework for estimating the effects of deprescribing interventions using observational data, but only when careful attention is paid to estimand specification, confounding adjustment, and model selection. In particular, methods that target the correct causal effect of interest and appropriately account for time-varying confounding were able to recover the true effects with minimal bias and approximately nominal confidence interval coverage. These findings provide practical guidance for researchers designing observational studies of deprescribing and highlight the importance of considering estimand alignment alongside traditional concerns regarding confounding and model misspecification. Currently, there are limited recommendations relating to deprescribing interventions 52 partly owing to limited high-certainty evidence on the benefits and harms of deprescribing. 53 The application of methods that account for the above complexities within observational research studies on the effects of deprescribing will support generation of less biased and more robust evidence on benefits and harms which may support future trial conduct and inform policy and practice.
