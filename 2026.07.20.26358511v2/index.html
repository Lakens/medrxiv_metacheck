How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Biochemical predictors of survival are essential to control heterogeneity and identify associations of disease progression in amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS). We performed high-depth Proximity Extension Assay proteomics using longitudinal serum (n=851) and CSF (n=244) samples from 426 people with ALS, replicated in a further 460 patients. Cox regression identified five CSF proteins, including tropomyosin 3 (TPM3), and 57 serum proteins, including neurofilament light chain (NEFL) and peripherin, associated with survival. Lasso regression identified an eightprotein serum panel, including NEFL, peripherin, EDA2R and CALCA -reflecting both disease extent and progression rate -that improved survival prediction beyond clinical parameters and NEFL: external validation c-statistic 0.78 versus 0.74 (Δ 95% CI 0.00-0.08), p=0.066; 1-year AUC 0.89 versus 0.84 (0.00-0.11), p=0.047. Joint modelling identified survival-associated trajectories of serum EDA2R and CALCA, highlighting their potential to measure disease progression. These findings identify diverse serum proteins that enhance survival stratification and are associated with disease progression in ALS.

INTRODUCTION

Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) is a degenerative disorder of the corticomotoneuronal network, characterised by progressive motor neuron loss leading to muscle weakness and respiratory failure (Brown et al., 2017). ALS is typically life-shortening, with a median survival of 30 months from symptom onset (Brown et al., 2017). However, disease progression is highly variable, with up to 10% of people with ALS surviving beyond 10 years (Chiò et al., 2011). This heterogeneity reflects a complex and poorly understood pathobiology of disease progression, involving molecular, systemic and neural connectome factors (Brown et al., 2017).

Riluzole, the only widely approved treatment for sporadic ALS, extends life expectancy by an average of only two to three months, highlighting the need to better understand the factors determining disease progression (Miller et al., 2012).

Variation in ALS prognosis presents a major challenge for the development of effective disease-modifying therapies (Mitsumoto et al., 2014). Prognostic models grounded in the biology of disease progression are therefore urgently needed (Van Den Berg et al., 2019). However, limited understanding of the mechanisms underlying survival heterogeneity means that established prognostic models largely rely on clinical observations remote from disease biology, and which are susceptible to non-linearity, ceiling and floor effects, and inter-rater variability (Westeneng et al., 2018; Van Eijk et al., 2021).

Incorporating biochemical data into prognostic models may offer a route towards precision medicine in ALS. To date, this approach has focused predominantly on neurofilament light chain (NEFL) protein levels measured in cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) and blood, which reflect the rate of axonal degeneration (Gaiani et al., 2017; Benatar et al., 2020; Thompson et al., 2022; Benatar et al., 2024). Deeper integration of more complex biochemistry may capture additional biological processes associated with disease progression, providing objective biomarkers for prognostic modelling and therapeutic trials.

We combined high-depth biofluid proteomic and clinical data from multiple large, well-characterised longitudinal cohorts to identify prognostic biomarkers in ALS and evaluate their performance relative to clinical predictors and NEFL levels. We further applied functional enrichment and genetic causal analyses to investigate the identified prognostic candidates with disease progression.

METHODS

Participants and sampling

People with ALS were diagnosed by neurologists specialised in motor disorders, based on upper and lower motor neuron (UMN; LMN) dysfunction in at least one region or LMN dysfunction in at least two regions, with investigations excluding other causes. For the discovery cohort, participants attending the Oxford MND Care Centre, John Radcliffe Hospital, Oxford, were recruited into six research ethics committee (REC)-approved sample collection cohorts between 2009 and 2023 (Supp. Table 1). All participants provided informed consent. There were no additional exclusion criteria, although lumbar puncture was not offered to individuals taking clopidogrel or anticoagulants.

Peripheral blood and CSF were collected in serum separator and polypropylene tubes, respectively, and processed according to consensus biomarker guidelines (Teunissen et al., 2009). Samples were centrifuged at 2300 x g for 10 minutes at 4°C, aliquoted into polypropylene tubes and frozen within 2 hours of collection.

AMBRoSIA cohort samples were stored in vapour phase nitrogen (VPN; -180°C), whereas samples from the remaining cohorts were stored at -80°C. AMBRoSIA and PRoSNeB samples had not previously been thawed, whereas samples from the remaining cohorts had been thawed once before (Supp. Tables 2 3).

Clinical measurements

Clinical measurements at sampling included symptom onset date and site (bulbar or non-bulbar), ALS Functional Rating Scale-Revised (ALSFRS-R), Edinburgh Cognitive and Behavioural ALS Screen (ECAS), neurologic examination, body mass index (BMI) and forced vital capacity (FVC, % predicted). Genetic testing was performed through standard clinical pathways. Progression rate was calculated as (48-ALSFRS-R)/ symptom duration (months). Clinical UMN and LMN burden was assessed across five body regions using hyperreflexia, wasting and fasciculation (score range -10 to +10, representing most flaccid to most spastic; Gao et al., 2022).

Date of death or censoring was ascertained from electronic health records in 2024.

Oxford discovery Proximity Extension Assay

Samples were thawed on ice and pipetted onto 96-well plates using layouts designed to distribute biological and technical factors within and across plates (Taylor et al., 2025). CSF and serum proteomic profiling were performed using Olink Explore 3072 (v3.8.1; Thermo Fisher Scientific) and Explore HT (v6.7.2), respectively, at the Centre for Human Genetics (CHG), University of Oxford.

Proximity Extension Assay (PEA) uses oligonucleotide-tagged antibody pairs that, upon binding their target protein in solution, bring the oligonucleotides into proximity for enzymatic extension, generating DNA barcodes. These are amplified and quantified using next-generation sequencing (Illumina NovaSeq) to generate relative, log 2 -transformed Normalised Protein eXpression (NPX) values (Wik et al., 2021).

Three internal controls were spiked into each sample to monitor background signal, DNA polymerase activity and sequencing performance. External sample and negative controls were used to assess intra-and inter-plate assay coefficient of variation (CV), and limit of detection (LOD), respectively. LOD was defined as 3 SD above the median negative control value for each plate. Dilution series were used mitigate the hook effect and extend the reliable detection range. Followiing plate layout optimisation, intensity normalisation was applied to equalise median assay values across plates.

Quality control

Median (Q1-Q3) intra-and inter-plate CVs were 7.1% (3.6%-15.5%) and 11.5% (7.5%-19.8%) for CSF, and 23.7% (12.5%-41.4%) and 21.2% (12.4%-34.7%) for serum, respectively. The median proportion of samples below the assay-specific LOD per protein was 16.9% (0%-71.6%) for CSF and 87.8% (4.9%-100%) for serum (Supp. Fig. 1). Samples were excluded according to predefined criteria (>5 SD from the mean for PC1, PC2, mean sample NPX or NPX IQR), resulting in the removal of no CSF samples and six serum samples before further analysis (Supp. Fig. 2; Sun et al., 2023). Six CSF and eight serum assays had quality control (QC) warnings but were retained following inspection of raw count histograms (Supp. Fig. 3). In accordance with standard PEA analysis pipelines, values below LOD were retained for analysis. Associations between biological and technical factors and PC1-10 were assessed using linear models and R² . PEA measurements were compared to existing Meso Scale Discovery (MSD) electrochemiluminescence and ELISA measurements from the same samples, as previously described (Thompson et al., 2022).

Tel Aviv mass spectrometry replication

Patients with ALS were assessed at Tel Aviv Sourasky Medical Center, Israel, between 2006 and 2019 (Klimovski et al., 2026). First-visit serum samples (n=349) were analysed using data-independent acquisition mass spectrometry (MS).

Samples were collected within an IRB-approved protocol (0245-10-TLV) by informed consent and stored at -80°C. Protein abundances underwent QC filtering, log 2 transformation, normalisation and batch correction using linear modelling. These data were used for significant serum protein Cox replication, with survival defined by time to death or tracheostomy.

Sheffield PEA replication

External serum PEA replication samples were obtained from patients with ALS attending the Sheffield MND Care Centre between 2017 and 2023 (n=111; REC London -South East 16/LO/2136; Strange et al., 2026). Peripheral blood was collected in serum separator tubes, allowed to clot for 30 minutes, centrifuged at 2350 x g for 10 minutes at 4°C, aliquoted, frozen overnight at -80°C and subsequently stored in VPN. Samples had not previously undergone freeze-thaw cycles and were analysed using Olink Explore HT at CHG in 2026. Samples from patients with ALS, individuals with other neurologic conditions and healthy volunteers were balanced over two plates, and NPX were intensity normalised. The primary survival endpoint was death. These data were used for both significant serum protein Cox replication and external validation of multivariable Cox models.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed in R (v4.4.0). No a priori power calculations were performed. Using the first (baseline) sample from each patient, individual CSF and serum proteins were analysed using Cox proportional hazards models, with time from sampling to death or censoring as the outcome. Models were adjusted for age at sampling and sex, and stratified by sample collection cohort using package 'survival'. P-values were corrected for multiple testing using the Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) procedure, with FDR-adjusted p-values<0.05 considered statistically significant (Benjamini et al., 1995). Wald statistics were extracted to compare effect estimates for proteins measured in both CSF and serum.

Functional enrichment analysis

Gene set variation analysis (GSVA) was performed in the discovery Oxford PEA data for CSF and serum separately, with Gene Ontology biological process, cellular component and molecular function, Kyoto Encyclopaedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) and Reactome pathways. Analyses used packages 'GSVA', 'clusterProfiler' and 'msigdbr', with gene sets restricted to 15-500 genes. Associations between pathway scores and survival were assessed using Cox models adjusted for age at sampling and sex, stratified by sample collection cohort, and with p-values adjusted by FDR procedure.

Lasso Cox regression

To identify PEA proteins that improved prognostic prediction beyond established clinical predictors, repeated nested cross-validation with least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (lasso) Cox regression was performed separately for CSF and serum using packages 'glmnet', 'nestedcv' and 'missForest'. Lasso applies an L1 penalty, proportional to the sum of the absolute regression coefficients, to the model loss function, shrinking some coefficients to zero and thereby excluding predictors (Tibshirani, 1996). Nested cross-validation enables hyperparameter tuning, feature selection and out-of-sample performance estimation within a dataset by optimising the regularisation parameter using inner loops, and evaluating prediction performance using outer loops (Iizuka et al., 2003; Varma et al., 2006).

Repeating this process enables assessment of feature selection and prediction performance stability.

Potential ENCALS clinical predictors (age at symptom onset, bulbar onset, C9orf72 hexanucleotide repeat expansion (HRE), definite El Escorial status, diagnostic delay, frontotemporal dementia, FVC and progression rate) were included if missingness was <50%, alongside sex and stratification by sample collection cohort (Westeneng et al., 2018). Continuous clinical predictors were log 2 -transformed and mediancentred. Within each cross-validation loop, missing data for included predictors were imputed from the other included predictors using a 50-tree random forest, and proteins in the lowest 20% of variance were excluded (Bommert et al., 2021). Ten repeats of nested cross-validation were performed, using three inner and outer folds for CSF and 10 inner and outer folds for serum. Within each inner loop, the most regularised model within one standard error of the minimum partial likelihood was selected. Proteins selected in at least seven of 10 repeats were considered selected.

Internal model performance was assessed using mean out-of-sample predictions across the repeated nested cross-validation. Final multivariable coefficients were estimated using post-lasso unpenalised Cox regression, with 50 imputations by chained equations (predictive mean matching) using package 'mice' (Rubin, 1987).

For external validation, final Oxford model coefficients were frozen and applied to the Sheffield serum PEA validation cohort to generate linear predictors. Clinical variables in the Sheffield cohort were centred and scaled to make estimates comparable with the discovery dataset. Oxford and Sheffield PEA data were generated by independent experiments and so while similarly log 2 -transformed were not explicitly centre-matched.

Discrimination was assessed using Harrell's concordance (c)-statistic and inverse probability of censoring weighting time-dependent area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) at 1-5 years post-sampling using packages 'survival', 'riskRegression' and 'timeROC'. Calibration was assessed using the calibration slope, estimated by fitting a Cox model with linear predictor as the sole covariate, and by comparing predicted mean survival with Kaplan-Meier survival curves across four risk groups defined by the 16 th , 50 th and 84 th centiles in the linear predictors (corresponding approximately to the mean + SD of a normal distribution; Royston et al., 2013). Absolute survival predictions in the external validation data were calculated using the Breslow-estimated baseline hazard from the AMBRoSIA development cohort, reflecting shared pre-analytic procedures. Finally, selected proteins were assessed for correlations with clinical variables and with each other in the Oxford data using pairwise-complete Spearman rank correlations adjusted for sample collection cohort.

Longitudinal analysis

Linear mixed-effects models (LMMs) were fitted separately for each CSF and serum protein using package 'nlme' in the Oxford PEA data. Models included patientspecific random intercepts and slopes, with time from first sampling, age, sex and sample collection cohort as fixed-effects. P-values for time-associated effects were adjusted using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure, and Wald statistics were extracted to compare proteins measured in both CSF and serum.

Bayesian joint modelling

Proteins selected by lasso Cox regression and significantly associated with time in the LMM analyses were evaluated using event-time plots to identify candidates for exploratory joint modelling. Bayesian joint models were fitted using package 'JMbayes2', integrating LMM and Cox submodels. The LMM submodel included protein abundance as the outcome, linear time, patient-specific random intercepts and slopes, and age, sex and sample collection cohort as covariates. The Cox submodel included age, sex and samples collection cohort as covariates. Functional forms for both current protein value and its rate of change (slope) were specified.

Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) settings were progressively increased until convergence was achieved, defined by Gelman-Rubin Rhat<1.10 and satisfactory diagnostic plots.

Mendelian randomisation

Two-sample Mendelian randomisation (MR) was used to to evaluate genetic associations between plasma protein abundance and ALS survival. Protein GWAS data were obtained from the UK Biobank Pharma Proteomics Project (UKB-PPP), comprising 2,940 targets (2,923 unique proteins) measured using Olink Explore 3072 in 54,219 European participants (Sun et al., 2023). ALS survival summary statistics were obtained from a GWAS of 4,256 patients with ALS, of whom 3,125 (73.4%) had died (median survival 32.8 months) (Fogh et al., 2016). ALS risk summary statistics were obtained from Project MinE (27,205 cases and 110,881 European-ancestry controls) (van Rheenen et al., 2021).

Genetic instruments were cis-acting biallelic single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) within 1 Mb of the encoding gene (cis-pQTLs), with minor allele frequency (MAF)>0.01, genome-wide significance (p-value<5E-08), linkage disequilibrium (LD) independence (R²<0.001, based on 10,000 unrelated European UK Biobank participants), and F-statistic≥10 (Lawlor et al., 2008). MR estimates were obtained using random-effects inverse-variance weighted (IVW) or Wald ratio methods, as appropriate, using package 'TwoSampleMR' (Bowden et al., 2019). P-values were adjusted using the FDR procedure.

For nominally associated proteins with multiple instruments, weighted median estimates and MR-Egger intercepts were used as sensitivity analyses to assess robustness to invalid instruments and directional pleiotropy, respectively (Bowden et al., 2016; Bowden et al., 2015). Colocalisation of protein and survival association signals across the cis region was assessed using package 'coloc' with default priors; PP.H4>0.8 was considered evidence of colocalisation (Zuber et al., 2022; Giambartolomei et al., 2014).

Rare variant analysis

Rare variant burden testing was performed using Project MinE data freeze 2 wholegenome sequencing (WGS) data: 5,954 ALS cases and 2,238 controls; 5,247 had survival data, including 4,345 deaths (Project MinE ALS Sequencing Consortium, 2018). Variants were annotated using the Ensembl Variant Effect Predictor and analysed using package 'RVAT' (McLaren et al., 2010). Missense variants were defined as those annotated as missense, splice region, or stop variants, with a Combined Annotation-Dependent Depletion (CADD) score≥15 and MAF<0.001 in the Genome Aggregation Database (gnomAD) (Kircher et al., 2014). Loss-of-function (LOF) variants were defined as splice, stop-gained or frameshift variants with SpliceAI scores>0.8 and MAF<0.001 in gnomAD.

Case-control associations were tested using SKAT-O and Firth logistic regression, adjusted for sex, sequencing platform, and the first 10 PCs (Jaganathan et al., 2019; Lee et al., 2012; Firth, 1993). Survival associated were evaluated using Cox regression of missense and LOF burden scores, adjusted for age at symptom onset, sex, sequencing platform, and the first 20 PCs.

RESULTS

Following QC, proteomic data comprised 1,095 samples from 426 people with ALS: 244 CSF samples from 139 participants, and 851 serum samples from 392 participants. Paired first-visit CSF and serum samples were available for 104 participants. A total of 2,884 proteins were quantified in CSF using the Olink Explore 3072 platform, and 5,416 proteins in serum using Olink Explore HT. Baseline characteristics are summarised in Table 1, with cohort-specific stratifications provided in Supp. Tables 2 and 3.

Research cohorts differed in pre-analytic variables, including storage duration, temperature and freeze-thaw history. These differences were also reflected in CSF and serum PCA profiles (Supp. Table 2-3, Supp. Fig. 4 5 6). As these factors were entwined with sample collection cohort and potentially influenced proteomic profiling, collection cohort was included as a covariate in subsequent analyses. Samples from the six collection cohorts were evenly distributed across plates, and there were no significant differences in survival between plates (Supp. Fig. 7 8 9). Kaplan-Meier analyses showed no overall differences in survival between research cohorts in either CSF or serum (Supp. Fig. 10 11). Concordance between PEA and commercial immunoassays for NEFL measured in matched samples was high in both CSF and serum (R² 0.53-0.94; all p-values<0.001) (Supp. Fig. 12).

Cox regression identifies prognostic proteins and survival-associated biological pathways in CSF and serum

Cross-sectional univariable Cox models identified five CSF and 57 serum proteins associated with survival after FDR correction (Fig. 1A-B, Supp. Table 4 5 Seventeen of the 57 candidate survival-associated serum proteins were also quantified by MS in the independent Tel Aviv cohort of 349 people with ALS (Supp.

Tables 5-6, Supp. Fig. 13). Sixteen (94.1%) showed concordant directions of association with the discovery analysis (one-tailed binomial p-value<0.001) and sixe (35.3%) replicated after FDR correction . Effect estimates were also strongly correlated between PEA and MS (R² =0.56 [95% CI 0.28-0.85], p-value<0.001; Fig. 1D).

In the independent Sheffield serum PEA replication of 111 people with ALS, 52 of 57 proteins (91.2%) showed concordant directions of association (p-value=3.20E-11), with 24 (42.1%) remaining significant after FDR correction (Supp. Tables 5 6).

Effect estimates were again strongly correlated (R² =0.44 [95% CI 0.19-0.68], p-value=1.82E-08). Three serum proteins were significantly associated with survival across all three Cox analyses: gelsolin (GSN), inter-alpha-trypsin inhibitor heavy chain H3 (ITIH3) and tumor necrosis factor receptor superfamily member 12A (TNFRSF12A).

Functional enrichment analysis using first-visit Oxford CSF and serum PEA data identified 159 pathways associated with survival, including terms relating to interleukin signalling, extracellular matrix organisation, nervous system and muscle development (Supp. Tables 7 8 9 10 11). No pathways were significantly associated with survival in both compartments.

Lasso Cox identifies an eight-serum protein panel that enhances clinical predictors

Five established clinical predictors were available for more than 50% of first-visit CSF and serum PEA samples and were therefore included in models along with sex and stratification by sample collection cohort: age at symptom onset (no missing data), bulbar vs non-bulbar onset (no missing data), diagnostic delay (29.5% missing in CSF; 24.5% missing in serum), progression rate (7.9% missing in CSF; 37.2% missing in serum) and C9orf72 HRE (12.2% and 8.4% HRE-positive in CSF and serum, respectively). In the repeated nested cross-validation lasso Cox analysis, no CSF PEA proteins were selected in any of the 10 repeats to augment these clinical predictors. The clinical predictor-only model in the CSF cohort achieved a mean (SD) out-of-sample c-statistic of 0.70 (0.02).

In the serum lasso analysis, eight proteins were selected in at least seven of the 10 repeats: a disintegrin-like and metalloprotease with thrombospondin motifs 8 (ADAMTS8, n=10), ADP-ribosyltransferase 3 (ART3, n=10), CALCA (annotated to calcitonin, n=10), tumor necrosis factor receptor superfamily member 27 (EDA2R, n=10), heparan sulfate 6-O-sulfotransferase 2 (HS6ST2, n=9), myosin light chain 11 (MYL11, n=7), NEFL (n=10) and peripherin (PRPH, n=9; Table 2, Fig. 2A-C). Raw count density plots for these proteins are shown in Supp. Fig. 15. Four additional proteins were selected in fewer than seven repeats and therefore not retained: dapper homolog 1 (DACT1, n=1), growth/differentiation factor 8 (MSTN, n=4), thialysine N-epsilon-acetyltransferase (SAT2, n=6) and histone-lysine Nmethyltransferase SETD5 (SETD5, n=6). Of the eight selected proteins, only ADAMTS8 and ART3 were positively associated with survival.

Internal evaluation using out-of-sample predictions from the repeated nested crossvalidation showed that the clinical predictors and serum lasso model achieved a mean (SD) out-of-sample c-statistic of 0.74 (0.01), compared with 0.66 (0.01) for clinical predictors alone and 0.74 (0.01) for clinical predictors plus serum NEFL (Table 2). Internal calibration slopes were closest to 1 in the lasso model, with mean (SD) values of 1.06 (0.05) for the clinical predictors and lasso-selected proteins, compared with 0.85 (0.05) for the clinical predictors alone and 0.85 (0.02) for clinical predictors plus serum NEFL (Table 2).

External validation of the Cox models developed in the Oxford serum cohort was performed in the independent Sheffield serum cohort (Supp. Table 6; Supp. Fig. 14; Fig. 2D-G). Discrimination progressively improved across the three models: the cstatistic increased from 0.69 (95% CI 0.62-0.75) for clinical predictors alone to 0.74 (0.69-0.79) with the addition of NEFL, and further to 0.78 (0.73-0.82) with the addition of lasso-selected proteins (Table 2; Fig. 2D-F). Both pairwise increases were borderline significant: addition of NEFL, Δ c-statistic 95% CI 0.00-0.11, p-value=0.051; and addition of lasso-selected proteins beyond NEFL, Δ 0.00-0.08, p-value=0.066. Similarly, time-dependent AUCs from 1 to 5 years were highest for the model incorporating clinical variables and lasso-selected proteins. The improvement over the NEFL model was statistically significant only at 1 year (AUC 0.89 versus 0.84, Δ 95% CI 0.00-0.11, p-value=0.047; Table 2; Fig. 2G).

Calibration plots comparing predicted and observed survival in the external data indicated increasing underestimation of risk as additional protein predictors were incorporated (Fig. 2D-F, Table 2). This was reflected in the calibration slopes, which progressively decreased from 0.99 (0.71-1.27) for clinical predictors alone to 0.92 (0.69-1.16) with the addition of NEFL, and 0.75 (0.58-0.93) with lasso-selected proteins (Table 2).

Clinical and biochemical correlations of lasso-selected serum proteins reveal groupings and peripheral compartment specificity

Correlation analysis of the eight lasso-selected serum proteins and clinical variables revealed distinct patterns of association (Fig. 2H-I). NEFL and peripherin were strongly positively correlated with each other (ρ=0.56, p-value=8.15E-33), and both were positively associated with progression rate (NEFL ρ=0.51, p-value=2.03E-17; peripherin ρ=0.34, p-value=6.06E-08) and age at symptom onset (NEFL ρ=0.25, p-value=5.27E-07; peripherin ρ=0.39, p-value=2.65E-15). Similarly, ADAMTS8 and ART3 were positively correlated with each other (ρ=0.43, p-value=4.32E-19), FVC (ADAMTS8 ρ=0.47, p-value=2.12E-03; ART3 ρ=0.33, p-value=3.66E-02) and ALSFRS-R score (ADAMTS8 ρ=0.32, p-value=5.29E-07; ART3 ρ=0.39, p-value=2.11E-10).

CALCA, EDA2R, HS6ST2 and MYL11 were strongly positively correlated with one another across all pairware comparisons (ρ=0.46-0.63, all p<8.00E-22). All four proteins were positively associated with LMN-UMN burden score (ρ=0.24-0.38) and male sex (ρ=0.07-0.41), and negatively with bulbar onset (ρ=-0.08 to -0.37).

However, associations between CALCA and LMN-UMN burden score or bulbar onset, and between EDA2R and sex, were not statistically significant. EDA2R was also negatively associated with ALSFRS-R score (ρ=-0.36, p-value=5.24E-09) and positively associated with age (ρ=0.39, p-value=1.93E-15). Six of the eight serum proteins (ADAMTS8, ART3, CALCA, EDA2R, HS6ST2 and NEFL) were also measured in CSF. In 104 paired CSF and serum samples, NEFL was the only protein that showed a significant correlation between compartments (Supp. Fig. 16).

Longitudinal analysis reveals dynamic protein alterations in CSF and serum

Longitudinal analyis identified significant changes in three CSF proteins, all of which increased over time, and 324 serum proteins, of which 273 increased and 51 decreased (Fig. 3A-B, Supp. Table 4 5 Six of the eight lasso-selected serum proteins also showed significant longitudinal changes. ADAMTS8 and ART3 decreased over time, with estimated changes of -0.013 NPX/month (-0.019 to -0.007, p adjust =7.13E-04) and -0.016 NPX/month (-0.020 to -0.012, p adjust =2.74E-12), respectively. Conversely, CALCA, EDA2R, HS6ST2 and MYL11 increased over time, with estimated changes of 0.028 (0.021 to 0.035, p adjust =3.70E-11), 0.035 (0.030 to 0.040, p adjust =2.54E-29), 0.016 (0.011 to 0.021, p adjust =1.57E-07) and 0.029 NPX/month (0.020 to 0.038, p adjust =1.93E-07), respectively. NEFL and peripherin showed nominal increases over time, but these did not remain significant after FDR correction (Supp. Table 5).

Bayesian joint modelling identifies survival-associated trajectories of serum CALCA, EDA2R and NEFL and potentially enables dynamic risk prediction

Six serum proteins (ADAMTS8, ART3, CALCA, EDA2R, HS6ST2 and MYL11) were selected by the lasso Cox analysis and also showed significant longitudinal associations in the LMM analysis. Their trajectories were therefore examined using event-time plots, alongside serum NEFL and ALSFRS-R as reference measures (Fig. 4A-H). CALCA and EDA2R showed the clearest visual associations between longitudinal trajectories and time to death or censoring and were therefore selected for joint modelling alongside NEFL. CSF proteins were not included in joint modelling due to limited longitudinal data.

Joint models showed that serum CALCA, EDA2R and NEFL all increased over time (Supp. Tables 12 13 14). Estimated mean monthly increases were 0.038 NPX for CALCA (95% credible interval [CrI] 0.030-0.046), 0.044 NPX for EDA2R (0.038-0.049), and 0.013 NPX for NEFL (0.006-0.020). Faster increases in all three proteins were associated with shorter survival. For each additional 0.01 NPX/month in the rate of increase, the hazard of death increased by 33% for CALCA (95% CrI 12-72%), 67% for EDA2R (31-144%), and 42% for NEFL (0-201%). Corresponding Rhat values ranged from 1.00 to 1.05, and model diagnostic plots indicated satisfactory convergence and sampling efficiency in all three joint models (Supp.

Fig. 17 18 19). These findings identify these serum proteins as candidate dynamic biomarkers, with serial measurements potentially enabling quantification of changes in individual patients' risk over time.

Genetic analysis of survival-associated proteins

Exploratory genetic analyses, incorporating two-sample MR, missense and LOF rare variant analyses, were integrated to investigate potential causal associations between ALS survival or risk for four of five CSF proteins, and 50 of 57 serum proteins identified as survival-associated in the adjusted univariable Cox analyses.

Of the eight serum lasso proteins, genetic analyses were not possible for ART3, EDA2R, HS6ST2 or NEFL. Among proteins with available genetic data, only MMP13 showed evidence of association with survival in rare variant analysis after correction for multiple testing (β LOF =2.04, p adjust =0.009; Supp. Table 15). The direction of effect was concordant with the observational association but not with the MR estimate.

Four additional proteins showed nominally significant associations in at least one genetic analysis, with effect directions concordant with their observational findings. IFT43 (β LOF+miss =0.390, p-value=0.013) and ACP5 (β LOF+miss =0.380, p-value=0.043) were associated with survival in the combined LOF and missense rare variant analysis, while KLK4 (β MR =0.31, p-value=0.003) and SERPINA3 (β MR =0.15, p-value=0.048) showed nominal associations in the MR analysis. MR sensitivity analyses were possible only for KLK4; the weighted median estimate was consistent with the IVW estimate, with no evidence of directional pleiotropy (Supp. Table 15).

Colocalisation analysis provided additional evidence of shared genetic signals between ALS survival and both KLK4 and SERPINA3.

DISCUSSION

High-depth CSF and serum PEA in a large longitudinal ALS cohort identified five CSF and 57 serum proteins associated with survival, and an eight-protein serum panel that complements clinical parameters and NEFL in prognostic stratification. This panel improved discrimination, particularly over shorter time horizons, and captured a broader range of biological signals than NEFL alone, although its suboptimal calibration highlights the need for absolutely quantitative assay development prior to clinical translation. Longitudinal analyses identified assay trajectories associated with survival, including dynamic changes in serum CALCA and EDA2R that were associated with subsequent mortality in joint models.

Exploratory genetic epidemiological analysis provided little evidence for a causal role of these protein alterations in determining survival, suggesting that many of the observed changes may be downstream consequences rather than drivers of disease progression.

The serum and CSF proteomes provided distinct information. A broader range of longitudinal and survival-associated alterations was detected in serum than in CSF, and only serum proteomic data improved prognostic models beyond established predictors. While these differences may reflect the smaller CSF samples size and reduced statistical power, it is consistent with previous findings that plasma, but not CSF, NEFL levels improve established prognostic models (Thompson et al., 2022).

More broadly, the limited overlap between compartments suggest that CSF and serum capture different aspects of ALS biology. NEFL was the only protein associated with survival in both biofluids after correction for multiple comparisons, while functional enrichment analyses also showed little overlap.

The added prognostic value of the serum proteome may seem counterintuitive given the predominantly central nervous system pathology of ALS. However, survival also reflects the systemic consequences of motor system degeneration, including respiratory dysfunction, nutritional impairment, muscle atrophy and inflammation, alongside biological factors such as ageing, and these processes are reflected in the circulating proteome. Consistent with this, the clinical correlates of the optimised prognostic panel extended beyond measures of disease aggressiveness to include proteins associated with overall disability, respiratory impairment and age. The proteins also mapped to biological pathways involving inflammatory signalling, extracellular matrix and muscle contractile machinery.

Several proteins showed particularly strong associations with survival. In univariable CSF analysis, the post-synaptic actin-binding protein TPM3 showed a stronger association with survival than NEFL. CSF TPM3 has previously been reported as elevated in ALS compared with controls using both PEA and aptamer-based proteomics, in people with FTD and pathogenic TPM3 variants have been linked with congenital myopathies (Chia et al., 2025; Martilla et al., 2014; De Houwer et al., 2026). The absence of a comparable serum association may indicate a central source of this signal, implicating neuronal rather than striated muscle function in its association with survival. Serum EDA2R, a member of the tumor necrosis factor (TNF) receptor superfamily, was associated with survival in both univariable and multivariable feature-selected analyses. Further, longitudinal increases in EDA2R were associated with shorter survival. EDA2R has previously been reported as elevated in ALS and to increase during the years preceding symptomatic onset in ALS gene carriers (Chia et al., 2025; Ran et al., 2025). These findings suggest that EDA2R may reflect biological processes that precede symptomatic disease and continue during clinical progression. EDA2R has been proposed as a tissue-independent ubiquitous marker of ageing, with activation linked to NF-kappa B and JNK signalling and to parainflammatory responses, including sarcopaenia and insulin resistance (Barbera et al., 2025; Sinha et al., 2002; Bilgic et al., 2023; Awazawa et al., 2017).

Serum CALCA and CALCB were also strongly associated with survival in univariable analysis. CALCA was one of eight proteins selected in the lasso Cox regression, and steeper longitudinal increases in CALCA were associated with shorter survival, supporting a role in measuring disease progression as well as cross-sectional risk.

Plasma CALCB has previously been reported as elevated in people with ALS compared with controls, while serum CALCA increases have been observed before the symptomatic onset in individuals at genetic risk (Imam et al., 2025; Ran et al., 2025). CALCA and CALCB are annotated to the related proteins calcitonin and calcitonin gene-related peptide 2 (β-CGRP), respectively, although precise specificity of the assays is not known and may not distinguish mature from precursor or related proteoforms. Calcitonin and calcitonin gene-related peptide 1 (α-CGRP) are produced through alternative splicing of the CALCA gene but have different functions, with calcitonin's primary role in calcium homeostasis and α-CGRP having roles in vasomotor function and neuromodulation. β-CGRP, encoded by CALCB, is proposed to have vasoactive and anti-inflammatory roles, and both peptides can readily form fibrillar aggregates (Russo et al., 2023; Reches et al., 2002).

Serum peripherin, a peripheral nerve intermediate filament protein, was also strongly negatively associated with survival in univariable analysis and was selected by lasso Cox regression. Notably, although peripherin levels were all below the reported limit of detection of the assay, the finding was reproduced with comparable magnitude in the external PEA dataset. This result contrasts with two smaller ELISA studies of CSF, serum and plasma peripherin, which reported either non-significant or positive associations with survival (Sabbatini et al., 2021; Bombaci et al., 2025). This discrepancy may relate to cohort differences, matrix effects or, given that PEA relies on the proximity of antibody probes binding to a target in solution, could therefore reflect protein fragments, complexes or isoforms not measured by sandwich ELISA.

Further work is required to determine whether these discrepant findings reflect differences in platform, matrix, cohort characteristics or sample size.

Several limitations are noted. First, there is selection bias among patients with ALS motivated and able to attend a regional referral centre and donate biofluids. Although our data capture a broad range of phenotypes, it is weighted towards individuals with more slowly progressive disease and is less diverse than the global ALS population.

Samples were also typically collected later than diagnosis, limiting the extrapolation of our findings to initial clinical presentation, and the collection period spanned an evolution in diagnostic criteria without explicit harmonisation.

Missing data for some established clinical predictors, including genotype, FVC and some cognitive measures, led to the exclusion of potentially relevant factors from some analyses. This may have influenced lasso Cox regression protein selection and limits the interpretation of associations between proteins and clinical features.

As genotype has been associated with CSF and circulating proteomic differences in ALS, the limited genotyping in this cohort means that confounding by genetic variation cannot be excluded for some identified proteins (Chia et al., 2025; Trautwig et al., 2025). Other potentially important prognostic factors, including riluzole use and nutritional and respiratory support, were also not systematically captured. While the discrepancy in outcome definitions between the Oxford and Sheffield cohorts (death), and Tel Aviv cohort (death or tracheostomy) may influence effect estimates, the consistency of findings supports the robustness of the identified associations.

The CSF and serum cohorts differed in some clinical characteristics, including the prevalence of ALS-causing genetic variants, potentially confounding comparisons across compartments. The relatively smaller CSF sample size with consequently reduced statistical power means that some negative findings could reflect an inability to detect significance. In addition, some promising candidate ALS survival biomarkers such as ubiquitin carboxyl hydrolase L1 (UCHL1) are not included in Olink PEA and could not be assessed (Dellar et al., 2024). A number of additional protein candidates, including post-translationally modified forms and those not analysed in this work, have shown promise in discriminating ALS cases from healthy controls with high accuracy (Kim et al, 2026); our panel cannot therefore be considered definitive. The absence of exclusion criteria for recent infection, inflammatory conditions or renal dysfunction may have introduced biological confounding of circulating protein levels. Lasso Cox regression was chosen for its embedded feature selection and relative interpretability, whereas more complex modelling approaches such as random forests or neural networks might have identified different protein subsets with different validation performance.

Although significant proteins prioritised using PEA showed high concordance with MS for proteins measured on both platforms, orthogonal validation is required to confirm the identity and prognostic associations of these candidate proteins, particularly those with high CVs or uncertain LOD profiles, noting that a high proportion of samples were below LOD in a substantial proportion of samples and that intra-and inter-plate CVs were above those accepted in clinical assays. External validation of the prognostic models was limited by the relatively small validation cohort and comparisons between PEA datasets with different median-centring.

For the genetic causal analyses, instruments derived from affinity-based proteomic platforms may reflect epitope-altering variants rather than true differences in protein abundance. Furthermore, the limited number and strength of some instruments reduced power to detect causal effects of plausible magnitude. Null findings should therefore not be interpreted as excluding causality. Although deriving exposure and outcome data from populations of European genetic ancestry reduces potential bias from mismatched LD structures, it limits generalisability to other ancestral groups.

Finally, case-only survival analyses are susceptible to index-event bias, whereby conditioning on disease occurrence can induce spurious associations between genetic determinants of disease susceptibility and subsequent survival, potentially biasing estimates in either direction.

In conclusion, this study identified a serum protein signature that improves prognostic stratification when combined with clinical parameters, and captures biological processes associated with ALS progression and survival.
