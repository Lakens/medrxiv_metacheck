Reporting p values imprecisely (e.g., p < .05) reduces transparency, reproducibility, and re-use (e.g., in p value meta-analyses). Best practice is to report exact p-values with three decimal places (e.g., p = .032) unless p values are smaller than 0.001, in which case you can use p < .001.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (e.g., “decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 29 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Marginal Significance works

List all sentences that describe an effect as ‘marginally significant’.

The marginal module searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The list of terms is a subset of those listed in a blog post by Matthew Hankins. The module returns all sentences that match terms describing ‘marginally significant’ results.

Some of the terms identified might not be problematic in some contexts, and there are ways to describe ‘marginal significance’ that are not detected by the module.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs) are first-line treatments for depression, but response is highly variable, and no robust genetic predictors have been identified. We conducted a genome-wide association meta-analysis of self-reported SSRI response in 62,879 individuals of European ancestry with depression (72.6% responders) across six cohorts (seven datasets), followed by gene-set, transcriptomic, proteomic, drug-target and polygenic score (PGS) analyses. SNP-based heritability was modest but significant (h² = 3.1%, SE = 1.4%) with no genome-wide significant variants detected. Gene-set analyses implicated gammaaminobutyric acid (GABA)ergic and glutamatergic neurotransmission, and a gene involved in glutamate transport (SLC17A6) showed consistent sub-threshold evidence across pathway, transcriptomic and proteomic analyses. Drug-target enrichment for hypnotics, anxiolytics and general anesthetics further supported the GABAergic signal. A leave-one-out PGS from the discovery meta-analysis was associated with self-reported SSRI response in independent cohorts (meta-analysis OR=1.05, 95% CI 1.02-1.08, p=0.002). Higher PGS for depression, attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), anxiety, post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), autism, addiction and schizophrenia were associated with lower odds of reported SSRI response, with ADHD, PTSD, autism and addiction remaining after adjustment for depression PGS. In contrast, educational attainment PGS was associated with better SSRI response. Selfreported SSRI response captures genetic insights relevant to antidepressant response, implicating GABAergic and glutamatergic neurotransmission. Individuals with higher genetic loading for psychiatric conditions report poorer response, and higher genetic loading for educational attainment PGS a better reported response. Although larger studies are needed to identify specific variants, self-report offers a practical, patient-centered and scalable route to collect the larger samples required.

1. Introduction

Major depressive disorder (MDD) is among the most prevalent and disabling conditions globally (1). Selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs) are a first-line pharmacological treatment (2) and the most widely prescribed antidepressant class (3), yet response is highly variable. Clinician-rated remission over 6-12-week trials occurs in around one-third of individuals (4), whereas up to 75% self-report benefit (5). This gap may reflect differences in outcome definition (remission versus perceived benefit), trial duration, recall bias, or how clinicians and patients judge meaningful improvement, and understanding it may provide insight into the biological mechanisms underlying SSRI response.

Genetic variation contributes to differences in antidepressant treatment outcomes (6) (7) (8) (9), with single nucleotide polymorphism-based heritability estimated at 13% (7). As with many traits in psychiatry, antidepressant treatment response is highly polygenic (6) (7) (8) 10), and genetic liability to other psychiatric disorders has been associated with poorer outcomes (11). To date, genome-wide association studies (GWAS) have found no robustly replicated loci. A recent fivecohort meta-analysis of SSRI response identified a single novel locus by combining electronic health record-based, self-reported and clinical trial response phenotypes, but combining phenotypes trades larger samples against greater heterogeneity (8).

Self-reported response mitigates this trade-off. A comparable self-report question across studies provides consistent ascertainment at larger sample sizes than clinical phenotypes allow. Collected via large-scale questionnaires, self-report is patient-centered and scalable, and may capture aspects of treatment outcome not routinely recorded in clinical settings (5).

Despite recall bias, the resulting sample size gains may increase power for locus discovery and improve genetic architecture estimates. . One explanation for the disparity between clinically assessed and self-reported response is that self-report captures improvement over longer periods than trials, as real-world antidepressant use often extends over several years (12). If so, genetic and transcriptomic signals for self-reported response should be enriched in pathways implicated in antidepressant action and depression biology.

Here, we present the largest GWAS meta-analysis of self-reported SSRI response. We characterize its genetic architecture, test this hypothesis through pathway and drug-class enrichment analyses, and assess whether genetic liability to other psychiatric conditions is associated with reported outcome.

2. Methods

>Methods

2.1 Primary GWAS

Primary GWAS of self-reported SSRI response were conducted in three cohorts: All of Us Research Program (AoU; (13)), Genetic Links to Anxiety and Depression (GLAD; (14)), and UK Biobank (UKB; (15)) (Supplementary Note1). Binary SSRI response was derived from a retrospective, self-reported effectiveness question answered for each SSRI taken. SSRIs included citalopram, fluoxetine, paroxetine and sertraline in all cohorts, plus escitalopram and fluvoxamine in GLAD. For participants rating multiple SSRIs, majority positive ratings were classified as response and majority negative ratings as non-response. Conflicting ratings (e.g., one positive and one negative) were excluded. Analyses were limited to participants with lifetime MDD, defined by self-reported diagnoses (GLAD) or DSM-5 criteria applied to Composite International Diagnostic Interview -Short Form (CIDI-SF; AoU and UKB) (16).

Participants reporting bipolar disorder, schizophrenia, schizoaffective disorder or psychosis were excluded. Analyses were restricted to participants genetically similar to European-. ancestry reference individuals (EUR-like) due to small sample sizes in other ancestry groups (Supplementary TableS2).

In each cohort, GWAS was conducted using REGENIE (17), following sample-and variant-level quality control (Supplementary Note1). Logistic regression tested associations between SSRI response and sequenced genotypes (AoU, UKB) or imputed dosages (GLAD) of common variants (MAF >0.01, INFO >0.7 in GLAD), adjusting for age at recruitment, genetic sex, the first six genetic principal components (PCs), and cohort-specific technical factors. REGENIE's approximate Firth correction was applied at p-values <0.01.

2.2 Genome-wide meta-analyses

In addition to the primary GWAS (AoU, GLAD and UKB), summary statistics of self-reported SSRI response were obtained from published GWAS in the Australian Genetics of Depression Study [AGDS; (18)], Estonian Biobank [EstBB; (19)], and 23andMe Research Institute [23andMe; (20, 21)], as previously described (8) (Supplementary Note2).

Quality-controlled summary statistics were harmonized and meta-analyzed using an inversevariance-weighted fixed-effect (FE) model in bcftools v1.22 (22) with score plugins +munge, +liftover and +metal ((23); https://github.com/freeseek/score#run-meta-analysis), using the --overlap option to correct for sample overlap. As a sensitivity analysis, Han and Eskin's modified random-effects (RE2) meta-analysis was implemented using METASOFT ((24); https://hanlab-snu.github.io/METASOFT/). SNP-level heterogeneity between cohorts was assessed using I² and Cochran's Q statistics for SNPs in ≥3 cohorts (Supplementary Note3, Note4). Genome-wide and suggestive significance were defined as p <5×10⁻⁸ and p <5×10⁻⁶, respectively.

2.3 Functional annotation and gene-based analyses

Independent genomic loci were defined in FUMA SNP2GENE (v1.8.2) (25) at the suggestive threshold (p <5×10⁻⁶), using the 1000 Genomes Phase 3 European reference panel (1KGP3 EUR), and SNPs within these loci were positionally mapped to protein-coding genes (Ensembl v102). MAGMA (v1.08) (26) was used for gene-based association analyses (20,068 proteincoding genes), competitive gene-set analysis (17,012 MSigDB gene sets and GO terms) and tissue-expression analysis (54 specific and 30 general GTEx v8 tissues), with Bonferroni correction within each analysis. Positionally mapped genes were characterized using GENE2FUNC. Analyses were repeated on RE2 summary statistics (Supplementary Note5).

2.4 SNP-based heritability and genetic correlations

Meta-analytic SNP-based heritability (SNP-h²) was estimated using LD score regression (LDSC; (27)) and converted to the liability scale (28), assuming population prevalence equals sample prevalence (0.726). Genetic correlations (rg) with 17 traits, spanning psychiatric conditions, personality, antidepressant response, BMI and educational attainment, were estimated using bivariate LDSC (Supplementary Note5, Supplementary TableS3). Benjamini-Hochberg FDR correction (q < 0.05) was applied across with significant SNP-h² and a successfully estimated rg (Supplementary Note5 and Table S3).

2.5 Polygenic scoring analyses

Polygenic scores (PGS) were calculated with SBayesRC in GenoPred (v2.3.3) (29), using GCTB LD matrices from EUR-like UKB participants and restricted to HapMap3 SNPs (30) (31) (32); Supplementary Note5). To evaluate the predictive performance of a self-reported SSRIresponse PGS, we constructed leave-one-out (LOO) PGS, excluding each target cohort (AGDS, AoU, GLAD) from the meta-analysis in turn. Associations were tested using logistic regression . in unrelated participants, adjusting for age, sex and the first six PCs (AoU, GLAD) or sex and the first ten PCs (AGDS). Variance explained was calculated as the incremental Nagelkerke's R² over a covariates-only model and converted to the liability scale (33), assuming a population prevalence of 0.726. Cohort-specific odds ratios (per SD of the PGS) were combined using fixed-effect inverse-variance meta-analysis (metafor in R; (34)), with heterogeneity assessed using Cochran's Q and I².

To examine shared genetic architecture, PGS for the traits in the genetic correlation analysis (Supplementary TableS3) were tested in AoU and GLAD (individual-level data access), using the same models, and meta-analyzed as above. Each of the 17 PGS other than depression was tested unadjusted and adjusted for the depression PGS (35 tests), with FDR correction applied jointly given the correlation among PGS and models. To identify which components of educational attainment (EA) drive its association, its cognitive (Cog) and non-cognitive (NonCog) components (35) were also tested.

2.6 Pathway, transcriptomic and drug-target analyses Serotonin-Related and all KEGG Pathway Analyses

To extend the FUMA gene-set analysis to all current KEGG pathways, we ran gene-set analyses in MAGMA (v1.10) (26) using the current KEGG release. We tested gene-set enrichment in four pre-specified serotonin-related KEGG pathways (serotonergic synapse, neuroactive ligand signaling, tryptophan metabolism, and folate biosynthesis) (36, 37), and all 369 KEGG pathways. FDR correction was applied separately to each set, and gene-level p-values were examined for enriched pathways to identify signal drivers.

Transcriptome-and proteome-wide association analyses (TWAS and PWAS).

. To test whether self-reported SSRI response is associated with genetically predicted gene expression or protein abundance, we performed transcriptome-and proteome-wide association analyses (TWAS and PWAS) using FUSION (38) and summary-based Mendelian randomization (SMR) (39), with brain and blood expression and protein quantitative trait locus (eQTL, pQTL) panels. FDR correction was applied across panels within each method, separately for TWAS and PWAS.

Prioritizing convergent candidate genes

Genes were prioritized as convergent candidates if they reached p <0.01 in the MAGMA genebased analysis, and in at least one brain panel for both TWAS and PWAS (Supplementary Note5). These thresholds were used for prioritization, not as tests of significance.

Drug-target enrichment

To aid clinical interpretation, we tested whether gene-level associations were enriched for known drug targets using MAGMA (26) and TWAS-based Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (TWAS-GSEA; (40)), with drug-gene interactions from DrugTargetor (41). TWAS-GSEA is directional, testing whether a drug's predicted effect on expression aligns with or opposes the expression profile associated with response. Drug-level results were FDR-corrected. At the Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical (ATC) class level, for classes with at least five drugs, signed TWAS-GSEA statistics within each class were compared with those of all other drugs (twosided Wilcoxon rank-sum test; FDR-corrected across classes). As drugs within a class often share targets, class-level results should be interpreted cautiously.

4. Discussion

In the largest GWAS meta-analysis restricted to self-reported SSRI response, we found modest but significant SNP-based heritability (3.1%), with diagnostics indicating a polygenic signal rather than artefact. However, no locus reached genome-wide significance in the primary fixed-effect analysis, and gene-based analysis was similarly null, with TIAM1 the strongest subthreshold signal. At the current sample size and heritability, this GWAS appears underpowered for common-variant discovery; larger samples or more precise phenotyping will be needed.

Our work differs from a recent GWAS by Koch et al. (8) in that we used a single, consistently ascertained phenotype (self-reported response), restricted analyses to SSRIs and, where possible, to individuals with lifetime MDD, and added a new cohort, All of Us. Koch et al.

combined self-reported, clinician-rated and registry-based outcomes (N ≈135,000), identifying two novel loci for non-response (one SSRI-specific); their self-reported subset (N ≈60,000) overlaps our cohorts, precluding its inclusion here.

In our Han-Eskin random-effects sensitivity analysis, one locus (rs4884091) reached genomewide significance, driven by 23andMe2020. This locus did not overlap Koch et al.'s genomewide significant locus but corresponded to their strongest self-report-restricted signal, which fell just below significance in their full analysis. This likely reflects the same 23andMe-derived association, reported by Li et al. (20), resurfacing in each analysis rather than three independent findings. The lead SNP at our TIAM1 locus, rs6517046, also reached suggestive significance in their full and self-report-restricted analyses, with a concordant direction of effect (8).

TIAM1 encodes a Rac1 guanine nucleotide exchange factor that acts at excitatory synapses and is implicated in neuronal development (43). In mouse models of chronic pain, Tiam1 . stabilizes synaptic N-methyl-D-aspartate (NMDA) receptors in the anterior cingulate cortex (ACC), driving structural plasticity associated with ACC hyperactivity and depressive-like behavior, which low-dose ketamine reverses by blocking this pathway (44).

This places TIAM1 in a plausibly relevant glutamatergic pathway, though the evidence concerns ketamine, not SSRIs, and the finding warrants replication.

SSRIs block serotonin reuptake within hours, yet clinical improvement typically takes weeks, suggesting additional mechanisms, such as neuroplastic adaptation, are required for response (45). We found no enrichment for serotonergic pathway genes, although this null may reflect limited power in small gene sets. Instead, the FDR-significant KEGG nicotine addiction pathway was driven by GABA-A receptor and transporter genes, the glutamate transporter gene SLC17A6, and an AMPA receptor gene (GRIA4), with no contribution from nicotinic receptor genes, suggesting GABAergic and glutamatergic rather than nicotine-specific biology.

Drug-target enrichment for hypnotics, anxiolytics and general anesthetics, driven largely by anesthetics rather than ketamine, likewise points to GABA-A modulation. GABAergic deficits are well documented in MDD (46, 47), glutamatergic and NMDA receptor dysfunction is implicated in MDD neuroimaging (48), and SSRIs also influence GABAergic neurotransmission (49). SLC17A6 showed sub-threshold evidence across gene-level, pathway, transcriptome and proteome analyses, and TIAM1's role in synaptic plasticity offers a further route to downstream circuit change. As these pathways are not SSRI-specific and power may differ across gene sets, we interpret them cautiously. Self-reported response, unconstrained by a fixed assessment window, may capture delayed adaptation, plausibly contributing to the higher rates of self-reported benefit and the non-serotonergic signals observed here.

. In TWAS-GSEA, where direction was interpretable, citalopram, fluoxetine and sertraline showed nominal expression effects opposing the responder-associated profile, as did antipsychotics and corticosteroids (FDR-significant), whereas dopamine-enhancing agents aligned with it. Two non-exclusive interpretations are possible. Opposition suggests a drug counteracting an expression state that predisposes to poorer outcomes, so responders are those whose baseline profile is most amenable to reversal along that drug's axis of action.

Alignment suggests a drug shifting expression towards a profile responders already carry, so reproducing rather than reversing it. Both could operate for different drug classes, and the present data cannot distinguish them; as the responder profile reflects genetically predicted expression rather than measured pre-and post-treatment expression, these results are hypothesis-generating.

The leave-one-out self-reported SSRI-response PGS was significantly associated with selfreported response, indicating the GWAS captures genuine common-variant signal despite insufficient power for locus discovery, as in other psychiatric traits where SNP-based heritability is detectable well before genome-wide significant loci emerge (50, 51). Genetic liability to most psychiatric disorders was associated with poorer self-reported response, extending beyond shared liability to depression for ADHD, PTSD, autism and addiction, but not for anxiety and schizophrenia. The persistent ADHD association is consistent with prior work linking ADHD PGS to treatment-resistant depression (52). Most associations were supported by genetic correlations; those significant only in PGS analyses (autism, addiction and schizophrenia) likely reflect the lower power of rg estimation. These associations may reflect biological non-response in individuals with greater comorbid psychiatric burden, behavioral factors linked to these traits, or misdiagnosis, where depression secondary to an unrecognized condition such as ADHD is treated as MDD, so that apparent non-response.

reflects an unaddressed diagnosis rather than pharmacological failure (53). Although these PGS, like the SSRI-response PGS, explain too little variance to guide individual treatment, if replicated, polygenic loading for comorbid traits could help identify patients who warrant closer monitoring or diagnostic reassessment.

Among personality traits, only agreeableness remained associated with better response after adjustment for depression PGS, whereas the neuroticism association largely reflected shared liability to depression. Agreeableness has been linked to stronger therapeutic alliance and modestly better outcomes in psychotherapy and pharmacotherapy (54), but also to an approval-oriented, socially desirable self-presentation (55), which may inflate self-reported treatment benefit.

EA PGS was among the strongest and most robust predictors of better self-reported response.

It captures genetic influences on cognitive ability, socioeconomic status and health-related behaviors (56,57), and can be decomposed into a cognitive (Cog) component, shared with cognitive performance, and a non-cognitive (NonCog) component, linked to personality, decision-making and lower health-risk behavior (35). Both components were associated with better response, with similar effect sizes, independent of depression PGS. As both are similarly genetically correlated with income (35), socioeconomic factors are a plausible shared contributor. This is consistent with evidence that EA and cognitive performance PGS distinguish treatment-resistant from treatment-responsive depression in electronic health records (58). In conclusion, self-reported SSRI response captures genuine genetic signal relevant to antidepressant response, although current samples remain underpowered for commonvariant discovery. Our findings implicate GABAergic and glutamatergic mechanisms alongside broader psychiatric burden: higher genetic liability for ADHD, PTSD, autism and addiction was associated with poorer self-reported response independent of depression liability, and genetic . propensity for higher educational attainment with better response. As a patient-centered and scalable phenotype, self-report offers a practical route to the larger samples this trait requires.

Several pathways could link these components to response. Antidepressant treatment requires patients to persist through early side effects and delayed benefit, and to recognize when to seek review. Cognitive ability may support such persistence, consistent with links . between health literacy and adherence(59). Non-cognitive traits such as persistence, future orientation and engagement with healthcare may similarly support adherence and follow-up, while socioeconomic advantage, captured by both components, may improve access to and continuity of care. These pathways were not testable here, and the associations may also reflect how response is reported.This study has several limitations. Our phenotype relies on cross-sectional, retrospective selfreport, which is subject to recall and reporting bias and may capture improvements in wellbeing not specific to antidepressant pharmacology, such as changing life circumstances, placebo response or non-specific therapeutic benefit. Question wording and response options differed across cohorts, which may have reduced power for an already low-heritability trait. Genetic contributions to response may also change over the lifespan, warranting longitudinal data. Available data remains overwhelmingly of European ancestry, reflecting the underrepresentation of non-European populations in genetic and mental health research, compounded by underrepresentation in follow-up questionnaires from which response phenotypes were derived. Addressing these gaps will require larger, multi-ancestry samples; functional follow-up of the GABAergic and glutamatergic signals, particularly SLC17A6; and work disentangling general psychiatric liability from treatment-response-specific genetics.
