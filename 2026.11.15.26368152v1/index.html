Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Metacheck does not yet analyze automatically whether sentences which include non-significant p-values are correct, but we recommend manually checking the sentences below for possible misinterpreted non-significant p values.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists (MRAs) are standard therapy for primary aldosteronism (PA). Serum potassium is measured in nearly every treated patient, but the long-term prognostic value of potassium attained early after initiation is undefined.

In the TriNetX Research Network, we identified adults with PA newly initiating spironolactone or eplerenone in 2000-2025, without prior adrenalectomy (n=4,169; 180-day landmark design). Achieved potassium (last value on days 14-180) was categorised as <4.0, 4.0-<4.5, 4.5-<5.0 and ≥5.0 mmol/L, and its change from baseline in tertiles, using cause-specific Cox and Fine-Gray models. The primary outcome was a composite of myocardial infarction, stroke or death; incident heart failure was co-primary.

Over a median 3.0 years, 623 composite events occurred. Estimated risk was lowest at 4.5-<5.0 mmol/L (adjusted hazard ratio 0.65, 95% CI 0.52-0.83, versus <4.0 mmol/L), lower than the 4.0-<4.5 mmol/L band, and not different from ≥5.0 mmol/L.

A larger rise in potassium was associated with lower risk. A similar pattern was seen for incident heart failure (0.54, 0.36-0.80; 248 events). Potassium below the 3.6 mmol/L consensus threshold for hypokalaemia carried no significantly higher risk (1.22, 0.96-1.56). The change in potassium correlated weakly with the change in renin (ρ=0.15).

In MRA-treated PA, cardiovascular and heart-failure risk was lowest at an early achieved potassium of 4.5-<5.0 mmol/L, well above the 3.6 mmol/L accepted by consensus criteria as correction of hypokalaemia; correction alone may therefore be an insufficient treatment target. Whether titrating therapy towards this band improves outcomes requires prospective evaluation.

Introduction

Primary aldosteronism (PA) is the most common endocrine cause of hypertension, and the cardiovascular risk it carries is not explained by blood pressure (BP) alone. Compared with essential hypertension at similar BP, it is associated with higher risks of stroke (odds ratio 2.58, 95% CI 1.93-3.45), coronary artery disease, atrial fibrillation, and heart failure (HF; odds ratio 2.05, 95% CI 1.11-3.78), 1 and that excess is not abolished by treatment: among patients treated with mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists (MRAs), the adjusted hazard of incident cardiovascular events remains almost twice that of matched patients with essential hypertension. 2 Hypokalaemia, the feature that most often prompts testing, is present in only a minority of cases, 3 so potassium serves less as a diagnostic marker than as the parameter monitored in nearly every treated patient.

What constitutes an adequate response to medical treatment therefore has direct prognostic consequences, and it remains unsettled. The Primary Aldosteronism Medical Treatment Outcome (PAMO) consensus defines a complete biochemical response as correction of hypokalaemia without potassium supplementation together with normalisation of renin, and found that only about half of medically treated patients achieve it. 4 Its potassium criterion, like the rest of the definition, was set by Delphi consensus: correction is defined as a serum potassium of at least 3.6 mmol/L, the same threshold used for biochemical success after adrenalectomy in the Primary Aldosteronism Surgical Outcome (PASO) consensus, 5 and one that has not, to our knowledge, been validated against cardiovascular outcomes. Renin is the better-evidenced component: the excess risk is concentrated in patients whose renin remains suppressed, 2 unsuppressed post-treatment renin was associated with fewer cardiovascular events (pooled hazard ratio [HR] 0.43, 95% confidence interval [CI] 0.23-0.80), 6 and the 2025 Endocrine Society guideline recommends monitoring renin and titrating the MRA to raise it when suppressed. 7 Renin, however, is measured in a minority of patients in routine care. Mineralocorticoid receptor activation drives myocardial fibrosis and adverse remodelling, 8 and blockade reduces HF in trials outside PA, 9, 10 so HF is the outcome in which an adequate blockade signal might be most visible.

Potassium occupies an unusual position in this framework. A 2026 review ranks its normalisation second among the three objectives of medical therapy, while stating that no specific target level exists for PA. 11 The target levels that exist come from other populations, where the potassium-mortality relationship is U-shaped and lowest at about 4.0-4.5 mmol/L [11] [12] [13] [14] [15] -so a higher achieved concentration cannot be assumed to be better. We therefore used a landmark design to examine whether the early potassium response to MRA therapy, expressed both as the achieved concentration and as the change from baseline, is associated with subsequent cardiovascular events and incident HF in adults with PA, whether such association is confined to the correction of frank hypokalaemia, and how it relates to the change in renin.

Methods

Data Source

Data were obtained from the TriNetX Research Network, a federated electronic health record network providing de-identified clinical data from 91 contributing healthcare organisations, yielding 10,661 unique patients (10,077 after the diagnosis-code exclusions below). Individual informed consent was waived under Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA). The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Taichung Veterans General Hospital (CE251118C) and follows the RECORD extension of STROBE (Supplementary Table S1).

Study Population

We identified adults with PA newly initiating spironolactone or eplerenone, without a prior MRA prescription or adrenalectomy. PA was ascertained by ICD-10-CM codes beginning E26 (hyperaldosteronism), excluding E26.1 (secondary hyperaldosteronism) and E26.81 (Bartter syndrome), neither of which is PA (code list in Supplementary Table S2); the unspecified codes E26 and E26.9 were retained. The index date was the first prescription of either agent. The case definition remains administrative, with no requirement for confirmatory testing: about half the analytic cohort carried a code specific to PA and most of the remainder only unspecified hyperaldosteronism.

Design and Exposure

We used a landmark design to separate measurement of the early biochemical response from the accrual of outcomes. Baseline potassium was the last value in the 365 days before the index date; achieved potassium was the last value on days 14 to 180, and the landmark was day 180. Follow-up was measured from the landmark, and events before it were exclusions rather than outcomes. Achieved potassium was categorised as <4.0, 4.0 to <4.5, 4.5 to <5.0 and ≥5.0 mmol/L, with <4.0 as the reference; the change from baseline (ΔK) was analysed in tertiles. Patients were required to have paired potassium values, to be free of end-stage renal disease and of a primary-outcome event at the landmark, to remain under observation, and to have complete covariate data (Figure 1; Supplementary Table S3). Details of measurement timing, specimen types and selection weighting are in the Supplementary Methods.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was a composite of myocardial infarction, stroke and all-cause death. Incident HF was a co-primary outcome, analysed in the subset free of HF both at baseline and through the landmark. Non-fatal outcomes were required to be coded at an inpatient, acute-care or emergency encounter, and follow-up continued until the outcome, death, or the last recorded encounter. Because all-cause death contributes a substantial share of composite events and its ascertainment varies by calendar era in this network, we additionally report the composite restricted to myocardial infarction and stroke, and examine the association by calendar period of MRA initiation.

Covariates

Models were adjusted for age, sex, race, MRA agent, baseline potassium, baseline estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), hypertension, diabetes, chronic kidney disease (CKD), atrial fibrillation and ischemic heart disease, and for concomitant medications recorded from 180 days before the index date through the landmark: angiotensin-converting-enzyme inhibitors, angiotensin receptor blockers, thiazides, loop diuretics and potassium supplements. Models were stratified on prevalent cerebrovascular disease (ICD-10 I60-I69) and, for the composite outcome, on prevalent HF. eGFR was estimated with the 2021 CKD-EPI creatinine equation. Race was categorised as recorded in the electronic health record (White, Black or African American, Asian, other or unknown) and was retained as a covariate because it was associated with both achieved potassium and outcome; models with and without race are compared in Supplementary Table S31.

ΔeGFR and achieved BP are measured in the exposure window and were therefore excluded from the primary models and examined in sensitivity analyses (S11, S34); all estimates are multivariable-adjusted associations rather than causal effects.

Statistical Analysis

The proportional hazards assumption was assessed via scaled Schoenfeld residuals for every model term and an explicit interaction between exposure and a split of follow-up at three years. Fine-Gray subdistribution models, treating death as a competing event, were fitted as a sensitivity analysis for incident HF. Three-year cumulative incidence from the landmark was estimated unadjusted by the Aalen-Johansen estimator and, adjusted, by g-formula standardisation over the fitted model, with percentile confidence intervals from 500 nonparametric bootstrap replicates.

Departure from a linear dose-response was examined by nested likelihood-ratio tests comparing a linear term against fractional polynomials and restricted cubic splines, with and without a knot at 5.0 mmol/L; these are global tests of fit, not tests of where a departure lies. Because the reference category pools frank hypokalaemia with low-normal potassium, we also split it at 3.5 mmol/L, repeated the analysis restricted to the reference range (3.5 to <5.0 mmol/L), and estimated the contrast between adjacent categories. Because the PAMO consensus defines correction of hypokalaemia as a serum potassium of at least 3.6 mmol/L, achieved potassium was also dichotomised at 3.6 mmol/L and categorised with 3.6 to <4.0 mmol/L as the reference; among patients with a baseline potassium below 3.6 mmol/L, the three PAMO potassium responses (corrected without supplementation, corrected with supplementation, not corrected) were compared, with supplementation defined as a potassium prescription between the index date and the landmark.

Because renin is the established marker of response to mineralocorticoid blockade, we assembled a bridging sample in which plasma renin activity was available both before MRA initiation and in the days 14-180 window, in the same assay units and paired with potassium from the same windows. Within it we estimated the Spearman correlation between the change in potassium and the change in renin, and the discrimination of an unsuppressed renin at follow-up by the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve. Renin was classified as unsuppressed at a plasma renin activity of 1.0 ng/mL/h or greater; 6 because a suppressed pre-treatment value was not required for entry, this describes renin status at follow-up rather than a transition from suppressed to unsuppressed.

Robustness was examined in pre-specified subgroups and 12 analytic variations listed in the Supplementary Methods, including a stricter two-diagnosis-code requirement (evaluated because two separately dated codes raise reproducibility without necessarily raising specificity). Cataract, herpes zoster and inguinal hernia served as negative control outcomes; achieved systolic BP served as a positive control. E-values were calculated with the common-outcome correction. Two-sided P values are reported; because many secondary and sensitivity analyses were performed, these are interpreted as supportive rather than confirmatory, and no adjustment for multiplicity was made except where stated. Analyses were performed in R version 4.6.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) using the survival (version 3.8.6) package. The fractional-polynomial search and E-values used study-specific code rather than a dedicated package; bootstrap standardisation used the boot package. Analysis code is available from the corresponding author on reasonable request.

Results

Cohort and Early Potassium Response

Of 10,077 adults with PA newly initiating spironolactone or eplerenone, 4,169 had paired potassium measurements, were free of end-stage renal disease, remained event-free and under observation at the day-180 landmark, and had complete covariate data (Figure 1). Over a median follow-up of 3.0 years from the landmark, 623 composite events of myocardial infarction, stroke, or death occurred; 183 patients had a myocardial infarction, 183 a stroke and 379 died, counts that overlap because only the first event contributes.

Baseline and early post-initiation characteristics are shown in Table 1. Median baseline potassium was 3.8 mmol/L (IQR, 3.4-4.1) and median achieved potassium 4.2 mmol/L (IQR, 3.8-4.5), a median rise of 0.3 mmol/L (IQR, 0.0-0.8). Patients reaching higher achieved potassium were progressively older, had lower baseline eGFR, more often had CKD, were less likely to be Black, and were less often taking a potassium supplement.

Achieved Potassium and the Composite Outcome

Estimated risk was lowest at an achieved potassium of 4.5-<5.0 mmol/L, with higher point estimates on either side of it (Table 2). Relative to <4.0 mmol/L, the adjusted HR was 0.65 (95% CI, 0.52-0.83; P < 0.001) for 4.5-<5.0 mmol/L. The two remaining categories did not differ significantly from the reference: 0.88 (95% CI, 0.73-1.07; P = 0.191) for 4.0-<4.5 mmol/L and 0.80 (95% CI, 0.59-1.09; P = 0.159) for ≥5.0 mmol/L, the latter resting on 272 patients with 58 events. With 4.0-<4.5 mmol/L as the reference, 4.5-<5.0 mmol/L gave 0.74 (95% CI, 0.59-0.93; P = 0.010), so it was lower than both categories below it; with 4.5-<5.0 mmol/L as the reference the ≥5.0 mmol/L contrast was 1.23 (0.90-1.69; P = 0.200), so it is not separated from the category above it (Supplementary Table S29).

The change from baseline, which does not depend on these category boundaries, was associated with the composite outcome on a monotone gradient: 0.80 (95% CI, 0.66-0.98; P = 0.032) for the middle and 0.75 (0.60-0.94; P = 0.012) for the highest ΔK tertile, relative to the lowest (Table 2), reproduced with ΔK modelled continuously (Supplementary Figure S1). The two exposures derive from the same measurements and are reported together as complementary expressions of the early biochemical response rather than as independent analyses.

Crude estimates were closer to the null; the ≥5.0 mmol/L estimate moved from 1.21 to 0.80 after adjustment for age, race, eGFR and CKD (Supplementary Table S30).

Time-Varying Association

The association was stronger in the first three years than thereafter (0.48; 95% CI, 0.34-0.69 versus 0.90; 0.64-1.26; P = 0.023 for the interaction) and also varied by calendar period of MRA initiation, which cannot be separated from follow-up duration in these data; the diagnostics are given in Supplementary Tables S4, S12-S14 and S25. The primary HRs are therefore reported as a summary association over follow-up rather than as an effect constant at every time point.

Incident Heart Failure

Among the 3,632 patients free of HF at baseline and through the landmark, 248 incident HF and 227 competing deaths occurred. The same pattern appeared, more sharply. Relative to <4.0 mmol/L the adjusted HR was 0.54 (95% CI, 0.36-0.80; P = 0.002) for 4.5-<5.0 mmol/L, while neither 4.0-<4.5 mmol/L (0.83) nor ≥5.0 mmol/L (0.78) differed significantly from the reference (Table 2). As for the composite outcome, 4.5-<5.0 mmol/L was also lower than 4.0-<4.5 mmol/L (0.65; 0.44-0.95; P = 0.025) but not separated from ≥5.0 mmol/L (Supplementary Table S29). Fine-Gray subdistribution models treating death as a competing event gave the same shape (0.86, 0.58 and 0.89). For the change from baseline the gradient was monotone, with 0.63 (95% CI, 0.44-0.89; P = 0.009) for the highest ΔK tertile.

For this outcome the shape tests were mixed: of five comparisons only the spline given an explicit interior knot at 5.0 mmol/L improved on a linear term (P = 0.011), while the spline without it, and both fractional-polynomial comparisons, did not (Supplementary Table S26). None of these global tests locates a minimum. A departure from linearity near 5.0 mmol/L is therefore suggested for this outcome but not consistently supported.

Absolute Risk

Covariate-standardised three-year cumulative incidence traced the same curve. For the composite outcome it was 11.31% (95% CI, 9.92-13.15), 10.13% (8.84-11.48), 7.78% (6.43-9.25) and 9.37% (7.51-11.79) across ascending categories; only 4.5-<5.0 mmol/L differed significantly from the reference, by 3.53 percentage points (1.71-5.77; Supplementary Table S19). For incident HF, risk fell from 4.44% to 2.61% at 4.5-<5.0 mmol/L, a difference of 1.84 percentage points (95% CI, 0.54-3.23).

The Reference Category and Adjacent Contrasts

Because the reference category pools frank hypokalaemia with low-normal potassium, it was split at 3.5 mmol/L (Supplementary Table S29). Achieved potassium below 3.5 mmol/L did not differ significantly from 3.5-<4.0 mmol/L for either outcome. Restricted to 3.5 to <5.0 mmol/L (3,570 patients with 500 composite events; 3,126 with 197 heart-failure events), 4.5-<5.0 mmol/L gave 0.68 (0.53-0.88) and 0.54 (0.36-0.83) against 3.5-<4.0 mmol/L while 4.0-<4.5 mmol/L did not differ (0.91 and 0.86): the signal sits in the 4.5-<5.0 mmol/L band rather than in normalisation alone.

At the consensus threshold of 3.6 mmol/L, achieved potassium below the threshold was not associated with a significantly higher risk than potassium at or above it (composite outcome HR 1.22, 95% CI 0.96-1.56; incident HF 1.34, 0.91-1.96). With 3.6-<4.0 mmol/L as the reference, risk was again lowest at 4.5-<5.0 mmol/L (0.67, 0.52-0.86; HF 0.56, 0.36-0.85), whereas <3.6 mmol/L (1.06 and 1.11), 4.0-<4.5 mmol/L (0.90 and 0.86) and ≥5.0 mmol/L (0.82 and 0.81) did not differ significantly. Among 1,386 patients with a baseline potassium below 3.6 mmol/L, neither correction with supplementation (1.13, 0.80-1.60) nor failure to correct (1.03, 0.68-1.56) differed from correction without supplementation for the composite outcome; the heart-failure estimates were imprecise, with the highest point estimate for failure to correct (1.54, 0.84-2.84; Supplementary Table S35).

Change in Potassium and Change in Renin

Plasma renin activity was paired to potassium in both protocol windows for 424 patients, of whom 346 also met the criteria for the landmark modelling cohort. The change in potassium correlated weakly with the change in renin and discriminated an unsuppressed renin at follow-up no better than chance.

Subgroup and Sensitivity Analyses

Estimates for 4.5-<5.0 versus <4.0 mmol/L were directionally consistent across prespecified strata and analytic variations, ranging from 0.55 to 0.91, and no interaction test across patient subgroups reached significance (Figure 3; Supplementary Tables S16-S18). Among the 1,917 patients taking no potassium supplement, the group in which a potassium within the reference range corresponds to the PAMO definition of biochemical correction, the estimate was of similar magnitude to the overall estimate (0.68; 95% CI, 0.45-1.04; P for interaction with supplement use = 0.19), as it was among patients with no recorded work-up (0.73; 0.51-1.06); it was closest to the null among patients hypokalaemic at baseline (0.91; 0.55-1.50; P for interaction = 0.14). Estimates were also consistent across strata defined by the number of potassium measurements in the exposure window (Supplementary Table S20 and Supplementary Figure S2), and adjusting for treatment continuity left the ΔK estimates materially unchanged; because continuity is measured in the same window as the exposure, those analyses are reported in full in Supplementary Tables S6 and S24 rather than treated as confounding control.

Patients excluded for missing paired potassium were healthier than those retained, but the 1,294 excluded after that step were substantially sicker (Supplementary Table S3). Inverse-probability-of-selection weighting did not materially change the estimate (Supplementary Table S22). Requiring two separately recorded PA diagnoses, or restricting to codes specific to PA, gave concordant composite estimates; the corresponding heart-failure estimates were attenuated and imprecise, reflecting fewer events in these subsets (Supplementary Tables S27, S28 and S33).

Control Outcomes and Sensitivity to Unmeasured Confounding

The six negative control outcomes were treated as one family, and none was associated with the exposure after correction for multiple comparisons (Supplementary Tables S5 and S23). The positive control, achieved systolic BP, behaved as expected: higher achieved BP was associated with more composite events, and additional adjustment for it left the estimates essentially unchanged (Supplementary Table S11). E-values for the 4.5-<5.0 mmol/L contrast were 2.02 for the composite outcome and 2.44 for incident HF (Supplementary Table S15). Ascertaining outcomes at any encounter type rather than acute-care only left the composite estimates similar but attenuated the heart-failure estimates to include the null (Supplementary Table S32).

Discussion

In 4,169 adults with PA starting an MRA, the potassium concentration attained in the first six months was associated with subsequent cardiovascular risk, and the association was not a simple gradient. Estimated risk was lowest among patients reaching 4.5-<5.0 mmol/L, significantly lower than in either band below it, while the estimate above 5.0 mmol/L was higher than at the minimum but not significantly so. A larger rise from baseline was associated with lower risk on a monotone gradient. The band associated with the lowest risk lies inside the reference range, and the association persisted when frankly hypokalaemic patients were removed.

These findings bear directly on how medical treatment of PA is monitored. Potassium is measured in nearly every treated patient, yet consensus criteria count correction of hypokalaemia, a potassium of at least 3.6 mmol/L, as an adequate response, 4 and guidance sets no potassium target beyond normalisation. 7, 11 In this cohort that threshold did not separate patients by subsequent risk, whereas risk was lowest in a band almost 1 mmol/L higher. If confirmed, a potassium below 4.5 mmol/L under treatment would identify patients whose MRA dose, adherence or supplementation merits review, using a test that is already part of routine monitoring, and the potassium criterion in consensus definitions of treatment response would merit reconsideration. These data do not show that raising potassium lowers risk; they define the target that a titration trial should test.

Potential Mechanisms

Several mechanisms, not mutually exclusive, could link the potassium attained under MRA therapy to subsequent risk. The first treats potassium as a read-out of receptor blockade in the kidney. Aldosterone acting through the mineralocorticoid receptor in the aldosterone-sensitive distal nephron increases sodium reabsorption through the epithelial sodium channel and, through the lumen-negative potential this generates, potassium secretion through ROMK and BK channels; 16 an MRA reverses both, so the rise in potassium after initiation indexes the extent to which renal receptor activity has been blocked. The same receptor mediates the pro-fibrotic and pro-inflammatory actions of aldosterone in the heart and vasculature, 7 and persistent renin suppression under treatment, which marks incomplete blockade, carries the excess cardiovascular risk of medically treated PA. 2, 6 The weak correlation between the change in potassium and the change in renin argues against treating the two as interchangeable, however: normokalaemia is reached at doses that leave renin suppressed in a substantial proportion of patients, 17 plausibly because the tubular response to receptor blockade is also shaped by potassium supplementation, dietary intake, renal function and concomitant renin-angiotensin blockade, whereas renin reflects the restoration of sodium and volume balance.

The second is a direct effect of the potassium concentration itself. Low and low-normal potassium is arrhythmogenic, 18 and in hypertensive, heart-failure and general populations the association between serum potassium and mortality is U-shaped, with its nadir between about 4.0 and 4.5 mmol/L. [12] [13] [14] [15] In experimental hypermineralocorticoid states cardiac fibrosis develops independently of potassium depletion, 19 so a potassium-mediated pathway is more plausibly electrophysiological than structural; it would operate whether or not the concentration was reached with supplementation.

The third is that achieved potassium marks disease severity rather than mediating risk. Hypokalaemic PA is characterised by higher aldosterone, more suppressed renin and more target-organ damage than normokalaemic PA, 3 so patients whose potassium remains below 4.5 mmol/L under treatment may have more severe or less responsive disease. Two observations bear on this. The association was, if anything, stronger among patients normokalaemic at baseline (HR 0.57) than among those hypokalaemic at baseline (0.91), and it persisted among patients taking no potassium supplement, the group in which correction of hypokalaemia corresponds to the PAMO definition of biochemical response, 4 with no evidence of interaction by supplement use (Figure 3). Both suggest that the prognostic information is carried by the concentration attained rather than only by the severity of the pre-treatment deficit or by how the concentration was reached-relevant to routine care, where about half of treated patients receive supplements-but these data cannot separate marker from mediator. Finally, values of 5.0 mmol/L or more accompanied lower eGFR and more CKD and diabetes, and the crude estimate for that category moved from 1.21 to 0.80 with adjustment for renal function, so any upward limb is likely to reflect in part the renal impairment that both raises potassium and constrains MRA dosing, 20 rather than an effect of potassium itself.

A Minimum Inside the Reference Range

Serum potassium in PA is treated as a safety parameter: it confirms that hypokalaemia has been corrected and that hyperkalaemia has not supervened. If its prognostic information were exhausted by that role, the association would have disappeared once frankly hypokalaemic patients were excluded. It did not: within 3.5 to <5.0 mmol/L, the 4.5-<5.0 mmol/L band carried a HR of 0.68 (95% CI, 0.53-0.88) for the composite outcome and 0.54 (0.36-0.83) for incident HF, and it was also lower than the adjacent 4.0-<4.5 mmol/L band (0.74 and 0.65). What the data locate is a band inside the reference range, not a gradient, and the upper end is where the evidence stops. This bears on how an adequate potassium response to medical treatment is defined. The threshold of 3.6 mmol/L shared by the PAMO and PASO definitions was agreed by Delphi panels to classify whether hypokalaemia had been corrected, 4, 5 rather than derived from outcome data; the PASO investigators noted that their criteria could not be validated against hard endpoints. 5 In these data the threshold did not clearly separate patients by subsequent risk, and two features may explain this. First, the group above it is heterogeneous: risk at 4.0-<4.5 mmol/L did not differ significantly from that at 3.6-<4.0 mmol/L and was lowest only at 4.5-<5.0 mmol/L, so a single cut at 3.6 mmol/L averages bands with different risk. Second, the threshold is readily reached: potassium usually normalises within days of starting an MRA, 7 and in one long-term cohort 90% of patients became normokalaemic without supplementation, whereas 48% reached a high-normal potassium of at least 4.3 mmol/L, a level some clinicians already target. 17 Outside PA, the lowest mortality lies near 4.2 mmol/L. 11 Reaching 3.6 mmol/L therefore marks the resolution of hypokalaemia rather than attainment of the band associated with the lowest risk. Whether the potassium criterion for an adequate response should be set higher cannot be established from an observational analysis, and the subgroup hypokalaemic at baseline was too small to exclude a difference, particularly for heart failure.

Comparison With Potassium Ranges Derived From Other Populations

Outside PA the relationship between serum potassium and mortality is U-shaped, with both hypokalaemia and hyperkalaemia associated with excess mortality. The category with lowest risk here matches that reported in a cohort of 21,334 patients with incident HF, where 4.5-<5.0 mmol/L was likewise the reference band. 13 The agreement is in which band carries the lowest risk, not in the shape of a curve, which these data do not resolve; the categories are wide and the formal shape tests were weak, so the correspondence should not be over-read.

Change in Potassium Does Not Substitute for Renin

Renin, followed in only a minority of patients here as elsewhere, is examined next. Among 424 patients with plasma renin activity paired to potassium in the protocol windows, of whom 346 also met the criteria for the landmark modelling cohort, the change in potassium correlated weakly with the change in renin (Spearman rho, 0.15) and discriminated an unsuppressed renin at follow-up no better than chance (area under the curve, 0.523). In an independent cohort of 155 patients on long-term medical therapy, 90% became normokalaemic, yet among the 57 with renin measured, classification of biochemical control by potassium and by renin agreed in only 49.1%. 17 A change in potassium therefore cannot be used as a proxy for renin status. Whether the two reflect different biological aspects of mineralocorticoid blockade is a further claim these data do not establish; weak correlation is equally consistent with measurement error. Nor can we claim that potassium carries information incremental to renin, since the two were never entered into a common model and the paired sample is too small to support that comparison.

Incident Heart Failure

HF was examined as a co-primary outcome; the contrast at 4.5-<5.0 mmol/L was largest for this outcome, and Fine-Gray models allowing for competing death reproduced it. Receptor activation and the adequacy of blockade were not measured, so a lower heart-failure risk in one potassium band does not demonstrate that blockade was more complete in it.

Strengths and Limitations

A landmark design measured the exposure before follow-up began, 21 avoiding outcomes that preceded the exposure. Potassium was identified by LOINC code, not description matching. Death, competing with every non-fatal outcome, was handled by Fine-Gray models and standardisation of the subdistribution. A positive control performed as expected, and none of six negative controls was associated with the exposure after correction for multiple comparisons. E-values of 2.02 and 2.44 indicate the confounding needed to explain the primary associations; 22 this does not establish that such confounding exists. Some limitations qualify these findings. The case definition is administrative; the composite estimate was unchanged when restricted to codes specific to PA, but the heart-failure subsets were then too small to evaluate. MRA dose, titration, adherence, supplement dose and dietary intake were unknown, so the achieved potassium may reflect treatment exposure as well as response to it, and interaction tests by supplement use had limited power. The exposure is a single routine-care value; plasma and serum potassium differ systematically, which would preferentially affect the highest category. The categories are 0.5 mmol/L wide, so these data identify the category with the lowest estimated risk, not the concentration at which risk is lowest, and the upward limb above 5.0 mmol/L is suggested rather than established. The association varied by calendar period, which cannot be separated from follow-up duration; selection occurred in two steps and the weighted analysis is exploratory; the network is predominantly US-based. Finally, the exposure is attained rather than assigned, so these estimates do not establish that altering potassium alters risk.

Conclusions

In adults with PA treated with an MRA, the potassium attained in the first six months was associated with subsequent cardiovascular risk and with incident HF with the lowest estimated risk in the 4.5-<5.0 mmol/L band, significantly lower than in either band below it. That band lies inside the reference range, and the association was not attributable to the correction of frank hypokalaemia alone. The band also lies well above the 3.6 mmol/L that consensus criteria accept as correction of hypokalaemia, so correction alone may be an insufficient treatment target. Whether the early potassium response can serve as a treatment target, rather than a marker of risk, requires prospective evaluation. Any such evaluation would need to compare titration strategies rather than attained concentrations, within the constraints that hyperkalaemia and renal function place on MRA dosing.

Research in context Evidence before this study

We searched PubMed from database inception to July 2026, without language restriction, for studies using the terms ("primary aldosteronism" OR hyperaldosteronism) AND ("mineralocorticoid receptor antagonist" OR spironolactone OR eplerenone) AND potassium AND (cardiovascular OR "heart failure" OR mortality). Medically treated primary aldosteronism carries an excess of cardiovascular events over essential hypertension that is concentrated in patients whose renin remains suppressed, and a 2025 meta-analysis found unsuppressed post-treatment renin associated with fewer cardiovascular events. The PAMO consensus defines a complete biochemical response as correction of hypokalaemia (a potassium of at least 3.6 mmol/L, set by expert agreement without outcome data) without supplementation together with normalisation of renin, and the 2025 Endocrine Society guideline recommends monitoring potassium and renin to guide titration, but neither specifies a potassium target. Target ranges for serum potassium derive from hypertensive and heart-failure populations, in which the potassium-mortality association is U-shaped with a nadir near 4.0-4.5 mmol/L. We found no study relating the potassium concentration attained after MRA initiation to long-term cardiovascular outcomes in primary aldosteronism.

Added value of this study

In 4,169 adults with primary aldosteronism in a multicentre electronic-health-record network, the potassium attained 14-180 days after MRA initiation was associated with myocardial infarction, stroke or death and with incident heart failure over a median of 3 years. Estimated risk was lowest at 4.5-<5.0 mmol/L-about a third lower than at <4.0 mmol/L and a quarter lower than at 4.0-<4.5 mmol/L-a band inside the reference range; the association persisted after excluding frankly hypokalaemic values and among patients taking no potassium supplement, and a larger rise from baseline was associated with lower risk. The change in potassium correlated only weakly with the change in renin, so potassium does not substitute for renin as a marker of treatment response. The consensus threshold for correction of hypokalaemia, 3.6 mmol/L, did not separate patients by subsequent risk.

Implications of all the available evidence

In MRA-treated primary aldosteronism, an achieved potassium below 4.5 mmol/L, including low-normal values, identifies patients at higher subsequent cardiovascular and heart-failure risk from a test already performed in nearly every treated patient.

Correction of hypokalaemia, the potassium criterion in current consensus definitions of treatment response, may therefore be an insufficient target, and that criterion merits reconsideration when those definitions are revised. Because potassium and renin dissociate, both should be monitored, as current guidance recommends. Whether titrating MRA therapy to reach 4.5-<5.0 mmol/L reduces risk, within the constraints that hyperkalaemia and renal function place on dosing, requires a prospective trial of a defined titration strategy.
