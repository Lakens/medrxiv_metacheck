Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Marginal Significance works

List all sentences that describe an effect as ‘marginally significant’.

The marginal module searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The list of terms is a subset of those listed in a blog post by Matthew Hankins. The module returns all sentences that match terms describing ‘marginally significant’ results.

Some of the terms identified might not be problematic in some contexts, and there are ways to describe ‘marginal significance’ that are not detected by the module.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. Some differences come from the ground truth: it lacks p-values with a correction name (“p Bonf = .061”), and counts partial eta squared written with its subscript after it (“η 2 p = .05”), “p = .05”, and some table notes and figure legends (“† p < .10, *p < .05”), which this module leaves out, as non-significant p-values. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

The Effect Size module checks if effect sizes are correctly reported in t-tests and F-tests.

The Effect Size check searches for regular expressions that match typical ways in which effect sizes are reported. It subsequently checks different ways in which Cohen’s d, g, ηp2, and ωp2 can be computed against the reported value. If effects are missing, or might be incorrect, you the module provides a warning. The module was validated on APA reported statistical tests, and might miss effect sizes that were reported in other reporting styles. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Abstract

Background: The CMS GLP-1 Bridge demonstration (July 2026-December 2027) expands access to GLP-1s for additional eligible Medicare Part D beneficiaries for a $50 monthly copay. Its long-run value depends on uptake, discontinuation, and which beneficiaries initiate therapy, in addition to policy decisions.

We used the Future Elderly Model, a microsimulation of Americans aged 55 and older, to model the costs and benefits of expanded GLP-1 access to the newly Bridge-eligible population, assuming that it continues over a 10-year horizon and is accompanied by reduced GLP-1 pricing taking effect for already covered indications in Part D beginning in 2028. We compared a status quo with two expanded-access scenarios: high discontinuation paired with high reinitiation (Rodriguez et al.), and low discontinuation paired with low reinitiation (Understanding America Study). Each scenario was run for two different definitions of the Bridge-eligible population -a broad definition that includes beneficiaries with prior myocardial infarction or stroke and a narrow definition that excludes them.

Results: Expanded access generated positive net social value in every scenario, from $44.3 billion to $58.5 billion over 10 years. Medicare net spending fell in every scenario, with cumulative savings of $21.2-27.1 billion, including $31.9 billion in diabetes drug price savings. Return on investment was 117-216% for Medicare and 198-346% for society. The eligible population mattered more than persistence. Net social value was 23-29% higher in the narrow population, because its initiators are healthier and have more to gain form access to GLP-1 treatment. Low discontinuation raised net social value by only 2-7% but raised Medicare drug spending, so ROI was higher under high discontinuation.

Conclusions: Expanded Medicare access to GLP-1s is projected to produce substantial social value across a range of persistence and eligibility assumptions. Extending the lower demonstration price to previously covered indications would allow the program to pay for itself from Medicare's perspective. Because health gains accrue over many years, evaluating the demonstration only over its 18-month window would understate its benefits.

Introduction

Glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists have transformed the treatment of obesity. In clinical trials, these therapies have produced sustained weight loss of 15-20% or more, along with improvements in cardiovascular risk factors, glycemic control, and other obesity-related conditions. [1] [2] [3] [4] [5] [6] [7] Access, however, has not kept pace with the accumulating evidence on impact. Coverage of GLP-1s prescribed for weight loss remains limited in both public and private insurance. 8 Many payers have been reluctant to expand coverage because of cost and uncertainty about whether patients will stay on treatment (or with the insurer) long enough for the benefits to offset it. 9 Fewer than one in five employers cover GLP-1s for obesity. 8 Medicare has historically been prohibited by statute from covering medications used for weight loss, despite the substantial health and economic benefits that could come from doing so. 4 In December 2025, the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services (CMS) announced the Better Approaches to Lifestyle and Nutrition for Comprehensive hEalth (BALANCE) Model, a voluntary Medicare and Medicaid model to test whether expanding access to GLP-1s and evidence-based lifestyle supports for weight loss could improve health outcomes without generating unsustainable costs. 10 In Medicare, CMS initially proposed a two-part approach to expanded GLP-1 access, including a six-month "Bridge" beginning in July 2026 that would operate outside the Medicare Part D program, followed by a model within the Part D program that would run from January 2027 through December 2031 conditional on participation from manufacturers and Part D plans. Manufacturers agreed to provide GLP-1s at a monthly net price of $245 (including beneficiary cost-sharing) for both the expansion population newly eligible for GLP-1 coverage and for beneficiaries being treated by indications already covered under Part D (e.g., diabetes). However, plan participation did not meet the minimum required threshold of 80% of total national Part D enrollment, due to uncertainty around utilization, selection, and risk protection. 11 In April 2026, CMS announced the indefinite delay of the Medicare BALANCE model and extended Bridge through the end of 2027. The Bridge demonstration operates outside the standard Part D benefit, instead using a central processor to manage prior authorization, claims, and pharmacy payment. Bridge extends GLP-1 coverage beyond Medicare beneficiaries with diabetes and other previously covered conditions, opening eligibility to three additional groups: (1) beneficiaries with a body mass index (BMI) of at least 35; (2) beneficiaries with a BMI of at least 30 and a diagnosis of heart failure, uncontrolled hypertension, or chronic kidney disease stage 3a or above; and (3) beneficiaries with a BMI of at least 27 and a diagnosis of prediabetes, previous myocardial infarction, previous stroke, or symptomatic peripheral artery disease. 12 According to KFF estimates, this expands coverage to roughly 3.8 million newly-eligible Medicare beneficiaries, with anticipated benefits including improved health outcomes, reduced incidence of chronic disease, and lower downstream health care utilization. 13, 14 Beneficiaries pay a $50 monthly copayment. 15 CMS noted that the extended Bridge timeline "would offer a valuable opportunity to provide plans with additional data and facilitate a smoother transition to the potential implementation of BALANCE in Part D." 16 In this paper, we model the costs and benefits of expanded GLP-1 access to the three newly eligible groups describe above, assuming that it continues over a 10-year horizon and is accompanied by reduced GLP-1 pricing for already covered indications beginning in 2028.

We build on prior work by modeling a range of discontinuation and reinitiation scenarios. Claims-based studies report wide variation in first-year discontinuation, ranging from 24% to 65% depending on the population, therapy, and definition used. 17, 18 However, these estimates are drawn from claims-based studies of insured populations and do not capture cash-pay or out-ofnetwork use, leaving open the question of how representative they are of real-world persistence, particularly among the uninsured and underinsured patients who stand to be most affected by coverage expansions.

We use new survey evidence on discontinuation from the nationally representative Understanding America Study (UAS) that addresses some of the limitations in prior claimsbased studies. 19 Using data that capture both insured and cash-pay GLP-1 use, the survey finds lower discontinuation rates than most claims-based estimates-12.8% by 6 months, 22.1% by 12 months, and 33.4% by 24 months-and identifies out-of-pocket cost, rather than side effects, as one of the strongest predictors of discontinuation, on par with vomiting and larger than other commonly cited tolerability concerns. Notably, patients using GLP-1s primarily for weight loss were more likely to discontinue than those using them to treat diabetes, consistent with the former group facing less consistent coverage. Taken together, this evidence suggests that observed adherence patterns may reflect financial access as much as-or more than-clinical tolerability, and that expanding coverage would likely improve persistence.

However, the magnitude and timing of these benefits-and how they weigh against program costs-depend critically on assumptions about uptake and adherence that remain uncertain. We use the Future Elderly Model (FEM), a microsimulation of the health and health care spending of older Americans, to project outcomes for Medicare beneficiaries under a status quo scenario and two "Bridge to BALANCE" demonstration treatment scenarios that differ in GLP-1 discontinuation. For each scenario, the model tracks GLP-1 initiation, discontinuation, reinitiation, and weight regain, and translates changes in BMI into changes in chronic disease, quality of life, and health care spending. By varying adherence assumptions systematically, we characterize how sensitive the program's projected value is to the adherence concerns that have historically limited GLP-1 coverage. Our analysis provides a framework that payers and policymakers can use to evaluate similar coverage expansions going forward.

Methods

The FEM is an economic-demographic microsimulation of the health and economic outcomes of older Americans, developed with funding from the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services, the National Institute on Aging, the Department of Labor, and the MacArthur Foundation. FEM draws on three nationally representative surveys: the Health and Retirement Study (HRS), the Medical Expenditure Panel Survey (MEPS), and the Medicare Current Beneficiary Survey (MCBS). Combining these sources provides more comprehensive information for the model's development than any single survey can provide. FEM advances in two-year cycles that mirror the frequency of the HRS. In each cycle, it simulates individuals' health outcomes, total health care spending, and GLP-1 treatment costs (Figure 1). A full technical description is available online. Health transitions are estimated as first-order Markov processes that depend on age, sex, education, race/ethnicity, existing health conditions, BMI, and functional status. Chronic conditions-heart disease, stroke, diabetes, cancer, and lung disease-are self-reported in the HRS and treated as absorbing states: once a simulated individual develops a condition, they retain it until death. BMI is modeled as a function of age, sex, education, race/ethnicity, marital status, health conditions, functional limitations, smoking status, and prior BMI. Predicted health status feeds into estimates of health care spending, which is modeled as a function of age, sex, race/ethnicity, education, and chronic conditions. For Medicare beneficiaries age 65 and older, estimates are based on the MCBS and additionally include functional limitations, nursing home residency, and mortality. These estimates capture all health care expenditures except GLP-1 treatment costs, which we model separately.

Study population and "Bridge to BALANCE" Demonstration eligibility

We simulated a nationally representative cohort of Americans aged 65 and older. Using FDAlabeled indications, we first identified all individuals clinically eligible for GLP-1 treatment for weight management. We then separated them into beneficiaries who already have GLP-1 coverage through Medicare Part D, because of type 2 diabetes or moderate-to-severe obstructive sleep apnea a , and beneficiaries without such coverage. Among the latter, we identified those who gain access through the Medicare GLP-1 Bridge demonstration (hereafter, Bridge) by applying CMS's clinical criteria. 12,b Established cardiovascular disease creates an overlap between Part D and Bridge eligibility. Wegovy is covered under standard Part D to reduce major adverse cardiovascular events in patients with established cardiovascular disease and overweight or obesity, while the Bridge criteria list previous myocardial infarction, previous stroke, and symptomatic peripheral artery disease as qualifying diagnoses for beneficiaries with a BMI of at least 27. 12 CMS acknowledges this overlap. Because the pathway a patient uses depends on how the prescription is documented, we include these beneficiaries as Bridge-eligible in our main analysis labeled "broad" to reflect the added beneficiaries, and test their exclusion in a sensitivity analysis labeled "narrow." Specifically, in the narrow scenario, beneficiaries with prior myocardial infarction or stroke are excluded from the eligible Bridge population, but initiation estimates are unchanged, so the number of eligible beneficiaries starting GLP-1 is the same and only their health profile differs.

Claims-based studies miss medications purchased out of pocket or obtained outside traditional insurance channels. This is an important limitation because coverage for obesity remains highly variable and fewer than one in five employers cover GLP-1s prescribed primarily for obesity. 8 Our low discontinuation scenario therefore uses data from the Understanding America Study (UAS), a nationally representative internet panel of U.S. adults, in which 33% of respondents using GLP-1 medications discontinued within two years and 30% of users reported treatment gaps longer than 3 months which we associate with reinitiation. 19 The data are self-reported rates of discontinuation and therefore they reflect a broader definition of persistence which may include beneficiaries who take short gaps in treatment.

The two sources differ in data type and in how discontinuation is defined, claims-based versus self-reported, so together they bracket a reasonable range of real-world GLP-1 use. Because stopping and restarting treatment are linked behaviors, each scenario pairs a discontinuation rate with the reinitiation rate observed in the same source. Differences between the scenarios therefore reflect the combined effect of persistence and cycling on and off treatment, not discontinuation alone. BMI trajectories. GLP-1 therapy results in weight loss following an exponential decay trajectory, with weight loss plateauing at approximately 20% from baseline by two years after treatment initiation, approximating recent clinical trials. 6, 22 After GLP-1 discontinuation, simulated individuals' weight returns toward its pre-treatment level along a trajectory estimated in a 2026 systematic review and nonlinear meta-regression of published trial data. 24 The fitted exponential recovery curve shows that weight consistently climbs back after treatment cessation, with 60% of the weight lost during treatment recovered after one year and a plateau at 75.3% of the loss by the second year. We applied these curves to calculate quarterly weight changes.

GLP-1 uptake rate

We modeled GLP-1 initiation among the newly eligible Medicare population, allowing rates to vary by 5-year age group (65-69, 70-74, 75-79, and 80-84) and by simulation year. Our mainanalysis rates draw on early reports of Bridge enrollment 25 and on treatment interest expressed in the UAS. The simulation begins with no GLP-1 users, so first-year initiation must build the initial stock of users. We set 2027 prevalence of GLP-1 use to match UAS rates of current and former use among similarly aged respondents without diabetes: 8% for ages 65-69, 7% for ages 70-74, 5% for ages 75-79, and 4% for ages 80-84. Reaching these levels requires first-time initiation among 9.0%, 7.8%, 5.6%, and 4.5% of each age group, respectively to account for discontinuation. Prevalence then rises over the next three years to a ceiling equal to current and former use plus the share of non-users who report interest in GLP-1 therapy: 16%, 13%, 9%, and 7%, respectively. We hold prevalence at this ceiling through 2036. Initiation rates are calibrated to reach and then sustain these prevalence targets given the persistence and reinitiation assumptions described below. We do not model treatment initiation at age 85 and older, consistent with the low use rates reported in the UAS for this age group.

GLP-1 treatment costs and price extension to previously covered indications

Participating manufacturers agreed to provide GLP-1 therapies through Bridge at a net price of $245 per monthly supply. Medicare pays $195 per month, and beneficiaries pay a $50 monthly copay. 15 We assume these prices and copays would continue for the full 10 years. The current 18-month demonstration applies this price only to prescriptions for weight management and does not extend it to Part D-covered indications. However, BALANCE would extend this pricing to GLP-1s prescribed for those indications (type 2 diabetes, MASH, and obstructive sleep apnea) covered under Part D. 26 In our model, we also extend these pricing reductions to previously covered indications beginning in 2028 (model year 2), which generates Medicare savings on existing prescriptions. We estimated these savings by multiplying the number of beneficiaries who access GLP-1s through existing Part D coverage by the difference between current net prices and the Bridge price. Current net prices are the CMS-negotiated maximum fair price (MFP) for Ozempic (slated to take effect in 2027) and the current net price for Mounjaro. 27 We used Medicare population estimates from the Trustees' Report and assumed constant diabetes prevalence over the 10-year horizon. 28 Based on ASPE estimates of GLP-1 use among beneficiaries with diabetes 15 , we projected that use grows from 15% to 25% over the simulation horizon.

Simulation Outcomes

We simulate each 5-year age cohort under all three scenarios and report cumulative differences between each Bridge scenario (high and low discontinuation) and the status quo for the Medicare population. Outcomes include Medicare spending, Medicare GLP-1 costs, beneficiary out-ofpocket GLP-1 costs, life years lived with diabetes, and net social value. To capture quality-of-life gains from expanded access to GLP-1s, we use Generalized-Risk-Adjusted Cost-Effectiveness (GRACE), which, unlike traditional cost-effectiveness analysis, complies with the Inflation Reduction Act's prohibition on discriminatory value assessment methods. 29, 30 Net social value equals (1) the value of gains in longevity and quality of life, plus (2) lifetime disability expenditure savings, minus (3) incremental lifetime net health care spending, defined as treatment costs minus health care cost offsets. We value each year in perfect health at $150,000, a conventional assumption, 31 and discount all costs and benefits at 3% annually, except in internal rate of return (IRR) calculations, which require no discount rate. 32 We do not model Part D plan behavior.

Net Social Value

Across all discontinuation/reinitiation scenarios (high discontinuation paired with high reinitiation and low discontinuation paired with low reinitiation) and eligible populations (the broad population includes beneficiaries with prior myocardial infarction or stroke; the narrow population excludes them), the Bridge demonstration generated positive cumulative net social value over the 10-year horizon (Figure 2). Net social value was positive in every year of every scenario except 2027 in the high discontinuation scenario with the broad population, where it was slightly negative (-$42 million) before turning positive in 2028.

Cumulative net social value by 2036 ranged from $44.3 billion (high discontinuation, broad) to $58.5 billion (low discontinuation, narrow) (Table 1; Figure 2). The population definition mattered more than treatment persistence. Net social value under the narrow definition was 23-29% higher than that under the broad definition ($54.6-58.5 billion versus $44.3-45.2 billion). Because the number of initiators is the same in both populations, this difference reflects who initiates. The broad population includes beneficiaries with prior myocardial infarction or stroke, who have already developed conditions that GLP-1s might otherwise prevent and who face higher mortality, so each initiator has less to gain. In the narrow population, initiators are healthier on average, and GLP-1s have more potential to prevent chronic conditions and other obesity-related comorbidities.

In contrast, low discontinuation raised net social value by only 2% in the broad population ($45.2 billion versus $44.3 billion) and 7% in the narrow population ($58.5 billion versus $54.6 billion). Diabetes drug price savings, which total $31.9 billion by 2036 and are identical across scenarios, do not contribute to the differences between scenarios. Because each discontinuation scenario is paired with the reinitiation rate observed in the same source, high reinitiation in the high discontinuation scenario partly offsets the treatment lost to discontinuation, and low reinitiation in the low discontinuation scenario partly offsets the benefit of staying on treatment. This pairing may help explain why persistence had a modest effect on net social value, and these differences reflect the combined effect of persistence and cycling on and off treatment rather than discontinuation alone. Return on investment

We calculated 10-year cumulative return on investment (ROI) from two perspectives (Figure 4). The CMS ROI measures Medicare's return on its Bridge GLP-1 spending. It is the reduction in other Medicare spending (medical cost offsets) plus diabetes drug price savings, net of Bridge drug spending, relative to Bridge drug spending. The social ROI measures the return to society on GLP-1 treatment spending: net social value per dollar of total spending on GLP-1 treatment by Medicare and beneficiaries.

The 10-year CMS ROI was positive in every scenario, ranging from 117% (low discontinuation/low reinitiation, broad) to 216% (high discontinuation/high reinitiation, narrow), and the social ROI ranged from 198% to 346%. Diabetes drug price savings are identical across scenarios and add to both returns without changing Bridge spending. ROI was greater under high discontinuation/high reinitiation than under low discontinuation/low reinitiation, and greater under the narrow population definition than the broad definition. The first pattern reflects that when patients stay on treatment less, Bridge drug spending falls proportionally more than net social value, while the diabetes drug price savings are unaffected. However, the net social value remains highest under the low discontinuation and low reinitiation scenario. The second pattern reflects the healthier mix of initiators in the narrow population, who generate larger benefits at similar drug cost. Discussion

In our simulation of a Medicare GLP-1 Demonstration, expanded access generated positive cumulative net social value over 10 years under every combination of discontinuation, reinitiation, and eligibility assumptions we studied. Cumulative net social value by 2036 ranged from $44.3 billion to $58.5 billion, and Medicare's net spending decreased in every scenario, with cumulative net savings of $21.2-27.1 billion by 2036. Whether the expanded coverage for GLP-1s pays for itself from Medicare's perspective depends on the Bridge price extending to GLP-1s prescribed for diabetes and other conditions already covered in Part D. In our simulation, extending the lower price to these indications generated $31.9 billion in savings, producing a positive return for Medicare in every scenario (117-216%).

Our modeling and assumptions imply that persistence has less of an effect on value than cost. Under low discontinuation and low reinitiation, net social value was 2% higher in the broad population and 7% higher in the narrow population, while Medicare drug spending was roughly 40-50% higher. As a result, the ROIs were higher under high discontinuation and high reinitiation. This does not mean that discontinuation is beneficial; it means that, in our model, patients who stop treatment also stop generating spending, and the value forgone is smaller than the spending avoided. Because we paired each discontinuation scenario with the reinitiation rate observed in the corresponding real-world population, our results reflect the combined effect of stopping and restarting treatment, and we cannot separate the two. In real world use, treatment cycling or yoyoing may have detrimental health impacts that our model is not accounting for. 33, 34 Our results also suggest that the beneficiaries who initiate treatment mattered more than our assumptions around adherence. Net social value was 23-29% higher under the narrow definition, even though the number of initiators was identical. The broad population includes beneficiaries with prior myocardial infarction or stroke, who have already developed conditions that GLP-1s might prevent and face higher mortality, so each initiator gains less. This suggests that coverage criteria, in addition to price and persistence, can impact how much value a program delivers per dollar spent. This is consistent with our prior work showing that GLP-1s deliver the most lifetime value when started early, before chronic conditions develop 35 , so limiting access to sicker patients does not raise the program's return on investment.

To put these results in context, CMS Administrator Mehmet Oz reported that approximately 600,000 beneficiaries had enrolled in Bridge by August 31, 2026. 25 The Bridge Demonstration is scheduled to run for 1.5 years, from July 2026 through December 2027. 15 Applying our simulated per-person results at 1.5 years to these early enrollees implies a net social value of $304-5165 per person, or $182-3,099 million in total depending on the scenario, and 21-30 thousand fewer years lived with diabetes. However, these results only capture a small share of the long-term value. Net social value was close to zero in the first year of our simulation and grew in every year thereafter (Figure 2), so an evaluation at 18 months versus 10 years of the demonstration would see mostly costs and little of the benefit that accrues afterward. CMS has said it will review program data before deciding whether to continue the expanded GLP-1 access offered through the Bridge demonstration 36 , but our results highlight the fact that data collected over an 18-month window will understate the benefits of GLP-1 treatment, because the health gains that drive value, including fewer years with diabetes, accumulate over time. Evaluations of the demonstration should therefore combine early utilization and persistence data with models that project longer-term outcomes.

Our study has several important limitations. First, simulated results depend on assumptions about uptake, persistence, and weight loss that remain uncertain. We use discontinuation rates from the published literature at the high and low ends of reported estimates, so that our scenarios bracket the range of plausible real-world persistence. Neither source was designed to describe Medicare beneficiaries with consistent low-cost access to GLP-1s. Second, the diabetes price savings assume that GLP-1 use among beneficiaries with diabetes grows only from 15% to 25% over the horizon, and that the price reductions extend to existing prescriptions starting in 2028. If use grows faster in this population or among other covered indications, savings from the lower price could be substantially larger.

Our model treats discontinuation as random with respect to treatment response, which likely understates the program's value. In practice, the effect of GLP-1s on weight and related outcomes varies across patients, and those who discontinue are probably disproportionately those who benefit least. When these patients stop treatment, the program avoids paying for a drug that is doing little for them, and the average benefit among those who stay on treatment rises. Our discontinuation assumptions are therefore likely conservative, particularly in the high discontinuation scenarios, and the finding that persistence matters less for net social value than for cost would likely be stronger if discontinuation were modeled as selective. Future work should model heterogeneity in treatment response and the relationship between response and persistence.

In summary, expanded Medicare access to GLP-1s is projected to generate substantial social value across the range of discontinuation, reinitiation, and eligibility scenarios we modeled. Moreover, extending the lower demonstration pricing to GLP-1s prescribed for indications already covered in Part D would allow the demonstration to pay for itself from Medicare's perspective.
