How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Improving the quality of antenatal care is essential for reducing persistently high maternal and neonatal mortality in Malawi. Group antenatal care has demonstrated improvements in quality, satisfaction, health behaviors, and outcomes. However, the impact of community building, one of the model's core components, has not previously been quantitatively assessed. This study examined the measurement properties of two community building measures, assessed the impact of group antenatal care on these measures, and explored their potential role as mediators of health outcomes.

This secondary analysis used quantitative data from the parent study testing the effectiveness of group antenatal care at seven clinics in Blantyre District, Malawi. The Perinatal Peer Support Scale and the Provider Connectedness subscale of the Pregnancy Related Empowerment Scale were used to measure community building and were validated using factor analyses. Results supported a two-factor structure for perinatal peer support (relational support and peer network) and a unidimensional structure for provider connectedness. Using generalized linear modeling, we found that participation in group antenatal care was associated with significantly stronger community building across all measures when compared to usual individual antenatal care. Women in group antenatal care demonstrated higher relational support score, peer network, and provider connectedness scores compared with women in individual antenatal care (p < 0.001). However, we found no evidence that community building had direct or indirect (mediating) effects on maternal psychological distress or infant birthweight.

This study strengthens the evidence base for group antenatal care by validating measures of community building and showing that relational support, peer networks, 49 and provider connectedness are important benefits of participation. Although community 50 building did not mediate the outcomes examined here, the findings highlight the need to 51 further test of theoretical pathways linking group antenatal care to maternal and child 52 health outcomes.

Introduction

Malawi experiences some of the poorest maternal and infant outcomes in the world, with a maternal mortality rate of 439 women per 100,000 live births and an infant mortality rate of 46 infants per 1000 live births each year [1]. Improvements in the quality of antenatal care are needed, as low-quality antenatal care can contribute to higher rates of low-birth-weight infants, maternal and neonatal mortality, and poor mental health outcomes [2] [3] [4] [5] [6] [7].

In Malawi, about 95% of pregnant women access antenatal care, which provides a point of contact to the health system; but only half of women attend all recommended visits [1, 8]. Furthermore, women seeking antenatal care reported low-quality interactions with healthcare workers and high costs [9] [10] [11]. Additionally, antenatal care often requires long waits and short visits with the midwife, and women do not always experience respectful maternity care [2, 3, [12] [13] [14]. The Government of Malawi identified challenges to reducing maternal and infant mortality, including shortages in health funding, trained healthcare workers, and medical supplies [15].

CenteringPregnancy is an evidence-based model of group antenatal care that has the potential to reduce some of the challenges in antenatal care that contribute to maternal and infant mortality and morbidity in resource-limited settings. In 2016, Group antenatal care was recommended for research by the World Health Organization, and evidence from recent clinical trials in multiple African countries has found that group care models improve key outcomes, including facility birth and birth planning, antenatal care attendance, postpartum care engagement, quality of care, preparations for birth and awareness of potential complications, family planning knowledge, breastfeeding, health literacy, and mental health [16] [17] [18] [19] [20] [21] [22].

In Malawi, a CenteringPregnancy-based group antenatal care was adapted and implemented at seven clinics in Blantyre District. Pregnant women randomized to the group antenatal care intervention received antenatal care in a group setting facilitated by a trained Malawian midwife and community facilitators. Women randomized to individual antenatal care (usual care) were offered the eight antenatal care contacts recommended by the WHO on a first-come, first-served basis. While waiting together in the waiting room, they received a health lecture from a midwife. Then, they met individually with a midwife for a physical assessment. The full protocol and results from the effectiveness trial are available elsewhere [23, 24].

CenteringPregnancy has three core components: healthcare in a group space, interactive learning, and community building, which are embedded in each of the sessions [25, 26]. Assessments include individual health checks, where women measure and record their own weight and blood pressure and then meet with the midwife to review results (healthcare in group space), facilitated, educational conversations on topics including warning signs, birth preparation, partner communication, and nutrition (interactive learning), and socializing, interaction, and shared experiences with other pregnant women and providers (community building).

Bringing people together in group antenatal care and building peer and provider connections, could transform healthcare and outcomes for women, supported by evidence that social relationships affect health outcomes [27] [28] [29] [30] [31]. It is achieved through a stable group and time to socialize, which create trust, respect, safety, and belonging. These, in turn, allow women to be open and share information and experiences, and they grow into peer support and connection with providers [25]. Relationship-centered care is an important part of group antenatal care, and community building is how the relationships among the women and the women and the providers flourish, becoming a source of peer support and provider connectedness [25]. Grenier and colleagues' theory of change frames group antenatal care as a transformed model of care that fosters a self-reinforcing cycle of increased, higher-quality care, improving health behaviors and maternal and newborn outcomes [32].

Group antenatal care has been shown to improve satisfaction and key outcomes, while also fostering trust and building relationships and community among participants.

However, few studies have directly measured community building within group to determine the effectiveness of a group antenatal care intervention on mothers and infants' outcomes of birth weight and maternal mental health, compared to individual prenatal visits with midwife in Malawi. The protocol and results of the parent study are published elsewhere [23, 24]. The data were first accessed on 06/19/2024.

Setting:

The parent study was implemented at seven antenatal care clinics in Blantyre District in Malawi, which is in the southern region of Malawi. Blantyre District is the home of the second largest city in Malawi and includes peri-urban and rural areas surrounding the city. The seven government-funded health centers provide health care to socioeconomically diverse populations from rural, peri-urban, and urban communities. The clinics also differed in the annual numbers of prenatal patients and midwives. Government funded clinics provide free health care to the populations they serve but also face substantial health workforce shortages, where only 40% of the national midwife positions are filled and there are only 1.48 health workers per 1,000 people, compared to the WHO's minimum recommendation of 4.45 health workers per 1,000 people [33, 34].

Recruitment, Enrollment, and Consent: After a woman's initial prenatal appointment, the midwives referred her to the parent study's research assistants to learn about the study. If the women expressed interest, she was screened for eligibility.

Pregnant women who were less than 24 weeks gestation, 15 years of age or older, and able to consent were eligible for the study. Women older than 17 provided written informed consent and women between the ages of 15-17 assented with written consent from a legal guardian as appropriate. Participants were consented to the study between July 5, 2019, and August 10, 2023. The secondary analysis was approved by Johns Hopkins Medical Institutions (IRB00355902). The parent study was approved by both the Kamuzu University of Health Sciences (COMREC: P.10/18/2498) and the University of Illinois Chicago (IRB: 2018-0845).

Data Collection:

Parent study data was collected longitudinally, at baseline during enrollment (T1) and at three follow up periods: at the end of the intervention (T2), two months postpartum (T3), and six months postpartum (T4). This secondary analysis included the entire sample (both intervention and control) and used data from T1 (covariates, intervention or control assignment), T2 (community building measures), and T3 (maternal and infant health outcomes).

Consistent with this definition, community building was assessed with two measures reflecting its peer and provider dimensions: 1) perinatal peer support scale and 2) provider connectedness, a subscale of the Pregnancy Related Empowerment Scale (PRES). Perinatal Peer Support: During the pilot study predating the parent study, the group based antenatal care intervention was adapted to the Malawian context, and the perinatal peer support scale with 17 items was developed based on qualitative findings related to women's experiences with peers in group care in order to measure how women in both individual and group antenatal care develop relationships with peers, experience emotional support, relate to each other, and exchange information [22]. Balance of Randomization:

All baseline measures were tested using chi-square tests to see if the randomization led to equivalency between the individual and group care groups. Any variables to which balance was not achieved during randomization were included as a covariate in the main analyses.

Differences in Missingness & Loss to follow up: Differences between those with and without missing data were tested with chi-square tests. Variables on which they differ were included in the analyses to reduce bias related to missingness. Similarly, to identify differences in those lost to follow up, baseline demographic variables were tested using t-tests and chi-squared tests among those lost between the start of the intervention (T1), the end of intervention (T2) and 2 months postpartum (T3) compared to those who remained in the study. The approach to handling missing data was determined after reviewing the distribution of missing data. Given that 20% of data were missing for key variables, multiple imputation by chained equations was used after testing that the data satisfied the missing at random assumption, which is appropriate for longitudinal data with both continuous and binary variables [40]. Sensitivity analyses were completed to ensure imputed variables were plausible. Variables that were different due to missingness or loss to follow up were included in analysis as covariates.

Factor analysis (Aim 1a).

Exploratory factor analysis with tetrachoric correlations and principal component analysis was conducted to determine the construct underlying factor structure for the perinatal peer support scale [41]. The tetrachoric correlation matrix was computed to estimate the underlying continuous correlation between binary items, which is necessary because using Pearson correlations with binary data is inappropriate. The resulting matrix is then used to perform exploratory factor analysis to identify latent factors, similar to how continuous data is analyzed [42] [43] [44]. To determine the number of factors, we examined the number of Eigenvalues greater than 1 and a scree plot compared to randomized values from a parallel analysis, as well as the cumulative percentage of variability explained [45].

The provider connectedness subscale of the PRES was previously validated and utilized in Malawi, so confirmatory factor analysis was conducted with a one factor structure [35, 36, 46]. The threshold for factor loadings are above 0.4 indicating acceptable item performance. Model fit was assessed using multiple indices, including the comparative fit index (CFI) and Tucker-Lewis index (TLI), with values ≥0.90 indicating acceptable fit, and the root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA) and standardized root mean square residual (SRMR), with values ≤0.08 indicating acceptable fit [47, 48].

To develop a comprehensive community building measure, higher order factor analysis was attempted to test the validity of a higher-order multidimensional community building construct comprised of the two scales (perinatal peer support and provider connectedness) [49]. The outcomes of this analysis determined the final factor structure of community building used to answer both research aims comparing the community building scores of the group and individual antenatal care groups and examining the effect of community building on maternal and infant outcomes through mediation analyses.

Main Regression Analysis (Aim 1b):

Before conducting the main analyses, the distribution of the variables was examined for outliers and to determine whether they meet the assumptions of planned analyses. If they did not meet the assumptions, generalized linear models allowing for alternative distributions (Poisson, Gamma, Negative Binomial) were considered for the main analyses. Analyses were conducted in Stata (version 18) [39].

Regression Analysis:

One subscale of the perinatal peer support scale (relational support) and the provider connectedness measure were left-skewed, violating the distribution assumptions for regression models. Therefore, the outcomes were reverse transformed (𝑇𝑟𝑎𝑛𝑠𝑓𝑜𝑟𝑚𝑒𝑑 𝑌 = (max(𝑌) +1) -(𝑌)). This positive, right-skewed data was appropriate with a gamma distribution with a log link. The other subscale of the perinatal peer support scale (network) was evenly distributed. To maintain consistency for the interpretation, this was reversed transformed (𝑇𝑟𝑎𝑛𝑠𝑓𝑜𝑟𝑚𝑒𝑑 𝑌 = (max(𝑌) +1) -(𝑌)), similar to the other perinatal peer support subscale. Because of this normal distribution, a Gaussian GLM was used with a log link. Covariates were included where significant and based on the results of missingness and loss to follow up analysis.

Mediation Analysis (Aim 2):

The primary outcomes of this analysis were psychological distress (SRQ-20) and birth weight, collected at two months postpartum (T3), mediated by community building measures of both perinatal peer support subscales (relational support and peer network) and the provider connectedness subscale. The independent variable was intervention status. Generalized structural equation modeling was used to test the model with all the mediators and then separately including covariates [50].

Nonlinear combination of parameters was used as a post-estimation command to test 295 the direct and indirect effects of the mediation model. Covariates included variables 296 shown in previous research to be related to psychological distress or pre-term birth 297 (age, marital status, education, parity, socioeconomic status) as well as other baseline 298 variables related to imbalance in randomization, loss to follow up, or differences in 299 missingness.

Results for Aim 1b: Regression

Participants in group antenatal care had better (lower scores, given the transformation of the scales) scores for the two perinatal peer support subscales: In this study, community building was measured with perinatal peer support, comprising two domains of relational support and peer network, as well as provider connectedness. Conceptually, these measures capture the peer and provider dimensions of community building. Both measures were left-skewed with limited variance, suggesting a ceiling effect in community building. The attempt to combine the measures into one overall community-building score through higher order factor analysis was not successful as they did not converge. This suggests the two measures capture different, though related, parts of community building. While this study examined the construct validity using exploratory factor analysis, it did not address other aspects of validity. In particular, future studies could address criterion validity using social support or other proximal health outcomes [51].

The perinatal peer support scale was developed by a research team as part of the preceding R21 pilot study; therefore, evidence regarding its use and psychosmetric performance remains limited to this program of research [22, 52]. In contrast, the PRES, including the provider connectedness subscale, has been adapted and evaluated in studies in Turkey and Indonesia [53, 54]. Both studies demonstrated acceptable construct validity, with the Turkish adaptation reporting higher factor loadings and fit statistics [53, 54]. These findings support the continued use of the instrument to measure women's perceptions of provider connectedness during pregnancy. This study focuses on community building within group antenatal care, using measures that assess the experiences of relational connections and support within group antenatal care.

Social support has also been used in the group antenatal care literature, with mixed findings: with some evidence of increased reliance on peer networks and improved psychological quality of life, but limited evidence that general social support mediates health outcomes [21, 55, 56]. There is an important difference between what these instruments are measuring, as these community building measure focus specifically on the experience of connecting to peers and providers during antenatal care, whereas social support is a perceived measure of support generally.

This study found a significant relationship between participation in group antenatal care and perinatal peer support (relational support and peer network), as well as provider connectedness. These findings align with the theoretical premise that group antenatal care fosters social connections and trust among participants as well as between women and providers. Grenier and colleagues' theory of change identifies community building as one component of a broader transformation in providing group antenatal care. Together with increased time, decreased hierarchy, interactive learning, self-assessment, and continuity of care, community building is expected to lead to supportive relationships, better communication, empowered partnerships, and more meaningful care. These transformed experiences may contribute to a self-reinforcing cycle of more and better care, and through improved health behaviours, communication, and service use, create opportunities for improved maternal and newborn outcomes [32]. The peer support and provider connectedness measures examined here capture dimensions of this theory of change, but not the full mechanisms outlined. Accordingly, the mediation analysis tests one specific pathway, not the full theory of change.

However, the findings on community building remain mixed. In pilot RCTs in Malawi and Tanzania, significantly higher community building was found using measures of provider connectedness and peer connectedness, while a quasiexperimental study in Tanzania did not find significant differences between those in group and individual antenatal care on community building [36, 57]. The literature in group antenatal care has mixed findings on social support outcomes, with one study in the US finding that group antenatal care participants were significantly more likely to depend on friendship or peer networks for social support (OR 2.81), while two studies did not find significant differences in social support between those in group and individual care [21, 55, 56].

In the present analyses, these specific measures of community building did not mediate the relationship between group antenatal care participation and maternal psychological distress or infant birthweight. The study demonstrating higher social support among those receiving group care also found that the social support mediated the relationship between group antenatal care and psychological quality of life, while another study found that social support did not mediate the relationship between care and postpartum depression by intervention group [55, 58]. A future area of research could explore the pathway from community building to social support to health outcomes or through other more intermediate ways like knowledge change, engagement in care, or health behavior not included in the analyses. This study adds to the growing literature on group antenatal care by establishing the validity of the Perinatal Peer Support Scale, with two distinct domains of relational support and peer network, and the Provider Connectedness scale within the Malawian context. The findings demonstrate significant improvements in these community building measures among women who participated in group antenatal care compared with those in individual care. Although the mediation analysis did not yield significant indirect effects on maternal psychological distress or infant birthweight, these results underscore the importance of continuing to develop and test theoretical models that link the core components of community building, such as trust, engagement, and social connectedness, to maternal and infant health outcomes as well as more proximal outcomes. Building and refining such models will be essential for understanding how community building in group antenatal care leads to changes in outcomes, through the strengthening of social relationships and collective efficacy among women, providers, and communities.

Implications

Research Implications

This study contributes to the growing evidence that community building in group antenatal care can be measured and is strengthened through participation in group antenatal care. Validating the perinatal peer support and provider connectedness scales in the Malawian context provides tools to begin to quantify constructs central to community building. Further work is needed to refine mediation models and develop a pathway linking community building to health outcomes through direct and indirect pathways. Future research should test longitudinal models that explore community building's relationship to proximal and distal health outcomes.

Practice Implications

For practitioners of group antenatal care, the results highlight the value of intentionally designing group antenatal care sessions for community building, fostering bonding among women and linking relationships with providers. Training for midwives and co-facilitators should emphasize facilitation skills that strengthen community building, and the use of relational care may be an approach that would further advance group antenatal care. Relational care is an approach to care that prioritizes the building of meaningful relationships based on respect, trust, and inclusivity, recognizing that human connection is essential for wellbeing. Unlike traditional person-centered care, which can be seen as a collection of needs, relational care focuses on the quality of interactions between the person receiving care, the caregivers, their families, and the community. The goal of relational care is to foster a sense of autonomy, security, and self-worth for everyone involved in the care process.

Policy Implications

The findings suggest that the consequences stemming from community building in group antenatal care can meaningfully enhance women's sense of support, network, and connectedness with peers and providers. As group antenatal care expands, policylevel efforts are needed to preserve community building as a core component to ensure high-quality implementation. Furthermore, investing in the expansion of group antenatal care through insurance coverage and implementation support globally, while maintaining the core components of group antenatal care, may expand access to highquality antenatal care and could improve mental and physical health during the perinatal period.

Limitations

This analysis was based on secondary data collected as part of a randomized controlled trial, which created limitations as the measures were constrained by the parent study design, available variables and measures, and timing of data collection.

Some constructs relevant to community building, such as the quality of group facilitation, the depth of peer interaction, or longitudinal changes in peer or provider relationships, were not captured.

Additionally, the provider connectedness subscale was not originally developed for the Malawian context, which may have influenced item interpretation despite demonstrated internal consistency and factor validity. Both community building scales were significantly left-skewed. Although the generalized linear models accounted for these skewed distributions, there may have been ceiling effects, a measurement limitation where many participants clustered at the highest possible score on community building making it difficult to measure true differences. In addition, the RMSEA for the provider connectedness model exceeded the conventional cutoff, although the other fit indices were acceptable. Finally, causality cannot be inferred beyond the scope of the original trial design, and residual confounding may remain despite statistical adjustment.

DiscussionGroup antenatal care is an evidence-based model that has shown positive effects during the perinatal period, yet one of its core components, community building, is underexplored. This study begins to fill this gap by validating measures of perinatal peer support and provider connectedness and using them to assess community building within a randomized controlled trial of group antenatal care in Malawi. The CenteringPregnancy-based model was adapted and implemented across seven clinics in Blantyre District, where 1,887 participants were randomized to either group or individual antenatal care. After validating each measure, we found that women who participated in group antenatal care reported significantly stronger community building across all indicators. However, in the generalized structural equation model examining these outcomes, community building did not mediate the effect of group antenatal care on maternal psychological distress or infant birthweight. Together, these findings strengthen our ability to begin to measure aspects of community building and show that group antenatal care may meaningfully enhance community building among participants.
