What we found

We found 4 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Marginal Significance works

List all sentences that describe an effect as ‘marginally significant’.

The marginal module searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The list of terms is a subset of those listed in a blog post by Matthew Hankins. The module returns all sentences that match terms describing ‘marginally significant’ results.

Some of the terms identified might not be problematic in some contexts, and there are ways to describe ‘marginal significance’ that are not detected by the module.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). Statcheck might still miss one-sided tests that are described in another sentence, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Cardiac remodeling underlies cardiomyopathy and heart failure, yet how common genetic effects are organized across the four chambers, and whether they influence phenotypic variability beyond additive genetic effects, remains unclear. We analyzed 22 cardiac magnetic resonance phenotypes in up to 77,271 UK Biobank participants. Additive mapping identified 748 locus-trait associations across 239 regions. Multivariate fine-mapping revealed that 96% of signals showed effects across multiple cardiac chambers, while resolving independent signals with distinct anatomical profiles, including predominantly atrial and ventricular signals at RBM20. Fine-mapped signal sharing formed three robust communities corresponding broadly to atrial function, chamber remodeling and ventricular systolic function. Variance mapping identified 19 associations, 13 without a nearby additive signal. Individual variance loci and polygenic effects generalized to clinical echocardiographic phenotypes and outcomes in the Penn Medicine Biobank. These findings reveal cardiac common-variant architecture as broadly shared yet locally heterogeneous and identify phenotypic variability as a partly distinct genetic dimension.

Introduction

Cardiomyopathies and heart failure are characterized by remodeling of cardiac structure and function 1 . Recent studies leveraging cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR) in large cohorts have demonstrated that cardiac structure and function are heritable and have identified common variants influencing CMR-derived phenotypes, including loci near genes implicated in cardiac development and cardiomyopathy [2] [3] [4] [5] . Polygenic variation associated with CMR traits has also been associated with incident cardiomyopathy and with heart failure, supporting a link between common genetic influences on quantitative cardiac phenotypes and clinical cardiovascular disease 2- 5.

Prior CMR GWAS have identified genetic determinants of cardiac phenotypes and demonstrated substantial genetic correlation and pleiotropy across cardiac traits [2] [3] [4] . However, overlap at the locus level does not establish whether the same underlying genetic signal influences multiple phenotypes or whether distinct effects coexist within the same region 6 . The extent to which distinct signals within cardiac loci contribute to single or multiple phenotypes, the genes underlying these associations, and their relationships with cardiomyopathy and heart failure remain incompletely understood.

These studies have primarily characterized genetic effects on mean phenotype levels, whereas genetic influences on phenotypic variability remain largely unexplored [2] [3] [4] , limiting our understanding of context-dependent genetic effects. Although cardiac phenotypes can show substantial heterogeneity among individuals carrying pathogenic cardiomyopathy variants 7 , exhaustively testing all possible gene-gene or gene-environment interactions requires the interacting factors to be specified and is impractical at the genome scale. Variance quantitative trait locus (vQTL) mapping instead identifies variants associated with differences in phenotypic variance and can therefore prioritize loci at which genetic effects may depend on genetic or environmental context 8 . To our knowledge, vQTL mapping has not previously been applied to CMR-derived traits.

We performed genome-wide additive and variance association analyses of 22 CMR-derived phenotypes spanning all four cardiac chambers in up to 77,271 UK Biobank participants. We sought to determine whether common genetic effects on cardiac structure and function are broadly shared across phenotypes and chambers or exhibit anatomically distinct patterns; whether loci underlying quantitative CMR traits converge on genes and genetic signals implicated in cardiomyopathy and heart failure; and whether common variants also influence phenotypic variability beyond their additive effects on trait levels. To validate our findings, we further evaluated whether additive and variance-associated polygenic effects generalized to clinically acquired cardiac phenotypes in Penn Medicine Biobank.

Results

Genetic associations are extensively shared across cardiac phenotypes

In total, 77,271 UK Biobank participants were included. For these individuals, chamber measurements were derived by deep-learning segmentation 9 , yielding measurements across the cardiac chambers. These comprised left atrial (LA) ejection fraction (LAEF), minimum volume (LAMIV), maximum volume (LAMV) and stroke volume (LASV); left ventricular (LV) ejection fraction (LVEF), end-diastolic volume (LVEDV), end-systolic volume (LVESV), stroke volume (LVSV), cardiac output (LVCO), myocardial mass (LVM), global mean myocardial wall thickness (LVWTG), global longitudinal strain (LVLSG), global circumferential strain (LVCSG) and global radial strain (LVRSG); right atrial (RA) ejection fraction (RAEF), minimum volume (RAMIV), maximum volume (RAMV) and stroke volume (RASV); and right ventricular (RV) end-diastolic volume (RVEDV), ejection fraction (RVEF), end-systolic volume (RVESV) and stroke volume (RVSV).

We performed additive GWAS for each phenotype for 10.7 million variants with minor allele frequency (MAF) ≥ 0.005 and imputation score (INFO) > 0.8. Single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP)-based heritability estimates (h 2 ) ranged from 6.7% (LAEF) to 24.0% (RVESV), with a median of 13.5% (Supplementary Table 1). The highest estimates were observed for ventricular phenotypes, including RVESV (24.0%), RVEDV (23.6%), LVEDV (22.2%), LVM (21.7%), LVWTG (19.4%), and LVESV (19.3%). Across 22 CMR phenotypes, we identified 748 genome-wide significant locus-trait associations, corresponding to 239 unique genomic regions (Fig. 1a-c; Supplementary Table 2).

To determine which genomic regions had not been previously identified, we mapped our traits to findings from prior CMR GWAS. We identified 13 phenotypes corresponding to recent large CMR GWAS [2] [3] [4] . Among those 13 traits, we identified 501 genome-wide significant locustrait associations. A locus was classified as novel relative to the included studies when no previously reported genome-wide significant variant was located within 2 Mb of either locus boundary. Among these associations, 230 (45.9%) were novel relative to the same phenotype, and 105 (21.0%) were novel across all of the 13 comparable phenotypes (Fig. 1d; Supplementary Table 2). Notably, 125 (25.0%) loci had previously been associated with a different CMR phenotype, highlighting extensive locus-level overlap across cardiac traits. However, such overlap does not establish whether the same underlying genetic signal influences multiple phenotypes or whether distinct effects coexist within the same region.

Multivariate analyses resolve genetic effects across cardiac phenotypes

To evaluate the degree of shared common genetic variation influencing CMR traits, we examined the genetic signals at both the global-and locus-levels. Pairwise LD score regression (LDSC) 10 revealed extensive genetic correlation between phenotypes (Fig. 1e; Supplementary Table 3). Among 231 pairs (median |rg| = 0.341), 181 (78.4%) were significant (FDR < 0.05; Supplementary Table 3), and the strongest genetic correlations reflected biologically related phenotypes, including traits within the same chamber (RAMIV-RAMV), homologous traits across chambers (LVSV-RVSV), and related structural measures (LVM-LVWTG). These genome-wide correlations establish widespread genetic sharing but do not determine whether associations within individual loci arise from the same or distinct underlying genetic effects.

To resolve the individual genetic signals influencing the CMR traits, we then performed multivariate fine-mapping using mvSuSiE 6 across the additive regions for 18 cardiac traits. Because the four stroke-volume traits (LASV, LVSV, RASV and RVSV) are derived from the corresponding volumetric measures, we excluded them to avoid including highly correlated traits. We identified 669 credible sets (CS) (Supplementary Table 4), all of which demonstrated effects across multiple phenotypes (Supplementary Fig. 1), with 644 showing effects across multiple cardiac chambers. Nearly half of the fine-mapped signals showed effects across all four chambers (330, 49.3%), while 141 (21.1%) showed effects restricted to the left and right ventricles (Supplementary Fig. 2). Most fine-mapped signals therefore showed effects across multiple chambers, although the breadth of sharing at each locus varied substantially (Supplementary Table 5).

To examine higher-order organization of this sharing, we represented mvSuSiE credible sets and the phenotypes they supported as a bipartite network and applied community detection directly to the observed network. Three phenotype communities emerged (Barber bipartite modularity 11 Q = 0.181; Supplementary Table 6). One community comprised the atrial ejectionfraction traits LAEF and RAEF; a second community grouped atrial volume measures with LVEDV, RVEDV, LVCO, LVM and LVWTG, corresponding broadly to chamber size and remodeling; and a third community comprised ventricular ejection fraction, end-systolic volumes and LV strain measures, corresponding broadly to ventricular systolic function (Supplementary Table 6). The observed modularity exceeded that of 10,000 randomized networks preserving both credible-set and phenotype degree distributions (empirical P = 1.0 × 10 -4 ), and the same phenotype partition was recovered in all 10,000 random reorderings of the observed network (Supplementary Table 7).

Complementing this data-driven organization, we tested whether credible-set sharing followed prespecified anatomical groupings (Supplementary Table 8). Phenotypes within the same atrial or ventricular compartment shared a greater fraction of credible sets than atrial-ventricular phenotype pairs (mean Jaccard similarity, 47.2% versus 35.4%; permutation P = 0.0028), whereas no corresponding organization was observed by left-right laterality or individual cardiac chamber (P = 0.69 and P = 0.13, respectively; Supplementary Table 9 and Supplementary Fig. 3). Sharing was particularly extensive between corresponding ventricular measures, including LVEDV-RVEDV (83.6%), LVESV-RVESV (81.5%) and LVEF-RVEF (67.0%) (Supplementary Fig. 4).

At individual loci, shared and anatomically restricted effects could coexist. A single-variant signal at the IGF1R locus showed effects across all 18 analyzed phenotypes, illustrating the broad phenotypic effects observed at some loci (Fig. 2a). At the RBM20 locus, mvSuSiE resolved six distinct signals with differing patterns of phenotypic sharing (Fig. 2b). A single-variant signal led by 10:112544125:C:T (rs189569984; PIP = 1.00) showed effects across 10 left-and rightventricular phenotypes; the same missense variant was previously associated with LVESV, RVESV and LVEF in a UK Biobank exome-imaging analysis 12 . An independent single-variant signal led by 10:112576695:T:C (rs10749053; PIP = 0.977) showed a predominantly atrial pattern; rs10749053 was previously identified as an atrial-fibrillation risk variant 13 . The region encompassing TTN and CCDC141 showed the greatest signal complexity, with eight credible sets, including four single-variant signals. One single-variant signal showed effects across 16 phenotypes spanning all four cardiac chambers, whereas another showed effects only in 10 left-and rightventricular phenotypes (Fig. 2c). Two of the signals were led by missense variants in CCDC141: p.Arg935Trp (2:179721046:G:A; PIP = 0.512; two-variant credible set) and p.Ala141Val (2:179839888:G:A; PIP = 1.00), both showing effects across all four chambers. These same variants were previously identified as independent genome-wide significant risk variants for accessory atrioventricular pathways and were also associated with paroxysmal supraventricular tachycardia 14 . Together, these examples illustrate that extensive cardiac pleiotropy can coexist with marked signal-level specificity within the same genomic region.

Cardiac genetic signals converge on developmental and cardiomyocyte biology

We next investigated the biological context of these associations through gene prioritization and enrichment analyses. Single-trait fine-mapping of the 748 additive locus-trait associations yielded 650 credible sets, with at least one credible set identified for 594 associations. Of these, 36 were single-variant credible sets and 135 contained ≤ 5 variants. We integrated fine-mapping and genelevel evidence using FLAMES 15 to prioritize candidate genes for each credible set. High-confidence genes (cumulative precision > 75%) were prioritized at 304 credible sets, corresponding to 149 unique genes (Supplementary Table 10). Recurrently prioritized genes included TTN (11 CS across 6 traits), TBX3 (6 CS across 6 traits), GJA1 and RBM20 (4 CS across 4 traits each), and PITX2 (5 CS across 3 traits).

Gene Ontology enrichment 16, 17 of pooled CMR-prioritized genes highlighted biological processes related to cardiac development and morphogenesis, including heart morphogenesis, ventricular septum morphogenesis, and ventricular cardiac muscle tissue morphogenesis (Fig. 3a; Supplementary Table 11).

Separately, stratified LD Score Regression 18 using GTEx annotations 19 showed false discovery rate (FDR)-significant enrichment in atrial appendage and left ventricular tissue for 9 and 7 traits, respectively, accounting for 16 of 21 significant trait-tissue enrichments. Additional signals were found in several non-cardiac tissues, including skeletal muscle and uterus (Fig. 3b; Supplementary Table 12). HeartMap annotations derived from an adult human cardiac singlenucleus RNA-sequencing atlas 20 further localized this signal to cardiomyocytes, with atrial and ventricular cardiomyocyte enrichment across 11 and 8 traits, respectively, and no other cell type significant after FDR correction (Fig. 3c; Supplementary Table 13).

We also examined regulatory elements active during cardiac development using stratified LD Score Regression of additive GWAS summary statistics and stage-specific enhancer annotations from embryonic human hearts in the Heart Hub 21 . We observed broad heritability enrichment across active enhancer states and multiple stages of cardiac organogenesis. In total, 16 of 22 traits showed FDR-significant enrichment, with signals spanning all nine assayed Carnegie stages and all three active enhancer states. Enrichment was most prominent in the EnhA1 activeenhancer state and was particularly widespread for LVEDV and LVESV, each showing FDRsignificant enrichment in 51 of 54 developmental enhancer annotations (Fig. 3d; Supplementary Table 14). Together, these analyses linked common genetic variation in adult cardiac structure and function to both mature cardiomyocyte biology and regulatory programs active during cardiac development.

Cardiac genetic signals converge on cardiomyopathy and heart failure

We next examined whether quantitative CMR traits shared genetic determinants with pathological cardiac remodeling. CMR-prioritized genes were enriched for established dilated cardiomyopathy (DCM) genes 7 (3 overlaps: RBM20, SCN5A, and TTN; 7.49-fold enrichment, P = 0.0061) and for the combined cardiomyopathy gene set of established hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM) 7 , DCM 7 , and arrhythmogenic cardiomyopathy 22 genes (5.88-fold enrichment, P = 0.0039; Supplementary Table 15). Enrichment was not significant for HCM alone, and no overlap was observed with the curated arrhythmogenic cardiomyopathy gene set.

At the signal level, we tested colocalization between CMR associations and genetic signals for HCM 23 , DCM 24 , and heart failure 25 established in prior GWAS. Across 239 merged regions, 9 (3.8%) contained at least one high-probability (HyPrColoc 26 posterior probability > 0.7) CMRdisease colocalization cluster, including 3 regions with HCM, 5 with DCM and 4 with heart failure (Supplementary Table 16). One region, at the BAG3 locus, contained a cluster involving all three diseases; variation at BAG3 has previously been reported to modify risk in opposite directions for DCM and HCM 27 . These findings indicate that a subset of loci influencing quantitative cardiac structure and function also harbor shared genetic signals across the cardiomyopathy disease spectrum.

Several loci showed convergence across multivariate fine-mapping, gene prioritization and disease colocalization. For example, at the TMEM182 locus, mvSuSiE resolved 2:103426177:C:T (rs2540277) as a single-variant credible set (PIP > 0.999), showing effects across nine left-and right-ventricular phenotypes (Fig. 4a). The variant lies within an intron of the MANE Select TMEM182 transcript and was also identified by HyPrColoc for a cluster comprising LVCSG, LVEF, LVESV and LVRSG together with HCM and DCM (posterior probability = 0.915). TMEM182 was also prioritized for both LVCSG and LVRSG. Notably, rs2540277 was itself the lead variant at this locus in the prior HCM GWAS (OR = 1.32, P = 2.31 × 10 -8 ) 23 and prior DCM genetic analyses implicated TMEM182 through LV eQTL colocalization, with lower genetically predicted LV expression associated with increased DCM risk 28.

At the FHOD3 locus, mvSuSiE resolved two distinct signals. The top variant of a twovariant credible set (18:34213453:A:G; PIP = 0.827) was intronic in FHOD3 and was also the candidate variant for a DCM colocalization cluster spanning multiple left-and right-ventricular phenotypes (posterior probability = 0.857). This variant was in strong linkage disequilibrium with rs2644262 (Pan-UKB EUR r² = 0.89), the lead FHOD3/TPGS2 variant in the prior HCM GWAS 23 . An independent single-variant signal (18:34289285:G:T; PIP > 0.999) was a missense variant in the MANE Select FHOD3 transcript (p.Val822Phe; rs61735998) and showed effects across 11 phenotypes spanning all four chambers (Fig. 4b). The same missense variant was previously assigned 100% posterior probability of driving a resting-heart-rate association signal in annotation-informed fine-mapping 29 . FHOD3 was independently prioritized across three singletrait credible sets from two traits.

At a chromosome 10 locus, 10:121426884:C:T (rs17617337) was resolved as a single-variant credible set (PIP = 0.958) and was the candidate variant for a colocalization cluster spanning 13 CMR phenotypes together with HCM, DCM and heart failure (posterior probability = 0.776) (Fig. 4c). rs17617337 was previously identified as a heart-failure sentinel variant and is in nearperfect linkage disequilibrium with the BAG3 missense variant p.Cys151Arg (rs2234962; r² = 0.99) 30 , which was the lead BAG3 variant in the prior HCM GWAS 23 . The variant lies within an intron of the MANE Select BAG3 transcript, whereas integrated gene prioritization independently implicated INPP5F across three single-trait credible sets for LVEF and LVCSG. These loci illustrate how genetic effects on quantitative cardiac structure and function can converge with disease-associated signals while retaining substantial allelic and phenotypic complexity.

Variance QTLs identify genetic effects on cardiac phenotypic variability

All analyses thus far characterized additive genetic effects on CMR phenotypes. We next asked whether common variants also influence interindividual phenotypic variability. Genome-wide variance quantitative trait locus mapping analyses using QUAIL 8 identified 19 significant locustrait associations across 14 CMR phenotypes (Fig. 5a-c; Supplementary Table 17). Of these, 13 (68.4%) had no genome-wide significant additive signal within 500 kb, indicating that most variance-associated loci were not captured by nearby additive-trait associations.

All 19 variance-associated loci yielded a single credible set by single-trait fine-mapping (Supplementary Table 18), and integrated gene prioritization identified high-confidence candidate genes at 13 credible sets, corresponding to 9 unique genes. PITX2 was recurrently prioritized across five traits, while additional prioritized genes included SLC6A15, NAALADL2, ASXL2 and RXRA (Supplementary Table 17).

Genome-wide genetic effects on phenotypic variability were substantially smaller than additive genetic effects on trait levels, with median SNP heritability of 1.36% for variance effects compared with 13.5% for additive effects (Fig. 5d; Supplementary Table 19). Additive and variance heritability were uncorrelated (Pearson r = 0.11, P = 0.63).

Consistent with the lower variance heritability, stratified LD score regression showed a more limited and distinct annotation pattern than for additive effects. Among 11 FDR-significant GTEx trait-tissue enrichments, 9 involved nervous-system annotations and were concentrated in right-ventricular phenotypes, with seven annotations enriched for RVEF and two for RVEDV (Supplementary Fig. 5 and Supplementary Table 20). HeartMap analysis identified only two FDRsignificant variance enrichments, involving fibroblasts for RVEF and ventricular cardiomyocytes for LVSV (Supplementary Fig. 6 and Supplementary Table 21). Developmental cardiac enhancer analysis identified one FDR-significant variance enrichment, involving LVWTG in a CS17 EnhA1 annotation (Supplementary Fig. 7 and Supplementary Table 22). Together, these findings identified phenotypic variability as a smaller and partly distinct component of cardiac genetic architecture.

CMR-derived genetic effects generalize to echocardiographic phenotypes and cardiovascular disease

We evaluated whether genetic effects identified from the UK Biobank CMR analyses generalized across cohorts and imaging modalities in Penn Medicine Biobank (PMBB) 31 . Additive polygenic scores showed consistent associations with corresponding echocardiographic phenotypes. The LVEDV polygenic score was associated with left ventricular internal diameter in diastole (LVIDd) (standardized β = 0.107, 95% CI 0.094-0.119, P = 5.6 × 10 -66 ), the LVESV polygenic score with LV systolic volume (β = 0.052, 95% CI 0.033-0.071, P = 9.9 × 10 -8 ), the LVM polygenic score with LV mass (β = 0.065, 95% CI 0.049-0.080, P = 1.3 × 10 -16 ), the LAMV polygenic score with LA maximum volume (β = 0.057, 95% CI 0.044-0.071, P = 2.3 × 10 -17 ), and the LVEF polygenic score with echocardiographic LVEF (β = 0.037, 95% CI 0.024-0.050, P = 1.2 × 10 -8 ) (Fig. 5e; Supplementary Table 23).

Additive CMR-derived polygenic scores were also associated with related cardiovascular diagnoses in PMBB, with 20 of 25 tested associations remaining significant after FDR correction. For example, a higher LVEDV polygenic score was associated with dilated cardiomyopathy (OR = 1.15, 95% CI 1.09-1.21, P = 2.2 × 10 -7 ), any cardiomyopathy (OR = 1.07, 95% CI 1.04-1.09, P = 6.9 × 10 -8 ), heart failure (OR = 1.03, 95% CI 1.01-1.05, P = 0.0047), atrial fibrillation (OR = 1.10, 95% CI 1.08-1.13, P = 9.0 × 10 -19 ) and ventricular tachycardia (OR = 1.06, 95% CI 1.03-1.09, P = 1.2 × 10 -4 ). Conversely, a higher LVEF polygenic score was associated with lower odds of dilated cardiomyopathy (OR = 0.91, 95% CI 0.86-0.96, P = 3.9 × 10 -4 ), heart failure (OR = 0.97, 95% CI 0.95-0.99, P = 0.0015) and ventricular tachycardia (OR = 0.95, 95% CI 0.93-0.98, P = 6.2 × 10 -4 ). Variance polygenic scores showed more limited disease associations, with 7 of 25 tests remaining significant after FDR correction, including LVEDV vPGS with heart failure (OR = 1.06, 95% CI 1.03-1.08, P = 5.5 × 10 -7 ), LVESV vPGS with heart failure (OR = 1.05, 95% CI 1.02-1.07, P = 5.9 × 10 -5 ) and dilated cardiomyopathy (OR = 1.08, 95% CI 1.03-1.14, P = 0.0042), and LVEF vPGS with ventricular tachycardia (OR = 1.05, 95% CI 1.02-1.08, P = 0.0011) (Fig. 5f; Supplementary Table 24).

Additive and variance polygenic scores were modestly correlated across all 22 CMR traits in PMBB (Pearson r = -0.159 to 0.327; N = 59,687), suggesting that they capture partly distinct genetic information. We then tested whether variance polygenic scores generalized specifically to interindividual variability in corresponding echocardiographic phenotypes using QUAIL. Variance polygenic scores for LVEF and LVESV were associated with variability in minimum observed LVEF (β = 0.324, 95% CI 0.080-0.569, P = 0.0093) and maximum LV systolic volume (β = 1.54, 95% CI 0.34-2.74, P = 0.012), respectively, after adjustment for the corresponding additive polygenic score. No corresponding associations were observed for the other three traits (Supplementary Table 25).

We also tested whether individual vQTLs replicated in clinically acquired echocardiographic phenotypes. Among six genome-wide significant lead variants with corresponding phenotypes available in PMBB, three were significant after Bonferroni correction in participants of European genetic ancestry: 4:111677041:T:A (rs2634074) for LAMV with left atrial volume (β = 0.0155, 95% CI 0.0068-0.0243, P = 5.0 × 10 -4 ), 7:128469760:G:A (rs3807133) for LVESV with LV systolic volume (β = -0.0381, 95% CI -0.0644 to -0.0119, P = 0.0044), and 2:172414462:G:A (rs528) for LVEDV with LVIDd as a proxy phenotype (β = -2.02 × 10 -4 , 95% CI -3.49 × 10 -4 to -5.61 × 10 -5 , P = 0.0067; Supplementary Table 26). Notably, rs528, a 3′-UTR variant in CYBRD1, was associated with CYBRD1 expression in atrial appendage tissue in GTEx v8 (normalized effect size = -0.097, P = 7.1 × 10 -5 ) 19 . The rs528 variance association was not accompanied by a significant additive effect in PMBB and occurred at a UKB vQTL lacking a nearby additive locus or evidence of additive-variance colocalization (Supplementary Table 27), consistent with a variance-specific genetic effect. Together, these findings demonstrate that variance-associated genetic effects can generalize across cohorts and imaging modalities at both individual loci and the polygenic level.

Discussion

We characterized common genetic effects on cardiac structure and function across 22 CMR phenotypes and found extensive genetic sharing across the heart alongside marked heterogeneity among independent signals within individual loci. These signals converged on cardiac developmental and disease biology, while variance mapping identified a smaller, partly distinct component influencing phenotypic variability. Together, these findings distinguish broadly shared cardiac genetic effects from signal-level anatomical specificity and extend the genetic study of cardiac phenotypes beyond additive genetic effects.

Genetic sharing across the heart was particularly apparent at the level of individual finemapped signals. Although prior studies have demonstrated substantial genetic correlation and locus-level pleiotropy among cardiac imaging traits [2] [3] [4] , shared loci can contain multiple independent genetic effects and therefore do not necessarily imply a common underlying signal. In our multivariate analysis, nearly all fine-mapped signals showed effects across multiple phenotypes and 96% across multiple chambers, with approximately half spanning all four chambers. This sharing nevertheless showed biological organization. Corresponding ventricular measures were particularly strongly connected, and credible-set sharing was greater within atrial or ventricular compartments than between them. Independently, community detection separated phenotypes into groups corresponding broadly to atrial function, chamber remodeling and ventricular systolic function. These patterns were not captured by simple left-right laterality or individual chamber identity, suggesting that common genetic effects follow coordinated aspects of cardiac physiology rather than anatomical boundaries alone. At the same time, RBM20 and the TTN-CCDC141 region demonstrated that independent signals within the same genomic region can have markedly different phenotypic profiles. This pattern was particularly striking at RBM20. The ventricular-patterned single-variant signal was led by rs189569984, a missense variant previously associated with LVESV, RVESV and LVEF 12 , whereas the independent predominantly atrial signal was led by rs10749053, a previously identified atrial-fibrillation susceptibility variant 13 . Thus, distinct signals at the same locus showed previously reported cardiac associations concordant with the anatomical profiles resolved by multivariate fine-mapping. This signal-level differentiation parallels phenotypic heterogeneity reported for rare RBM20 variants, where different amino-acid substitutions at the same position in the protein have been associated with DCM with or without left ventricular noncompaction and with distinct cellular phenotypes 32 . A cardiac locus therefore cannot necessarily be classified as simply pleiotropic or trait-specific; allelic heterogeneity can produce distinct patterns of phenotypic sharing within the same genomic region. This signal-level heterogeneity complements prior evidence that shared genetic pathways can produce divergent phenotypic effects across cardiac traits and cardiomyopathies 33.

Our findings also connect common genetic variation in adult cardiac structure and function to both cardiac development and mature cardiomyocyte biology. Genes prioritized from finemapped associations were enriched for developmental processes including heart and ventricular morphogenesis. Genome-wide heritability enrichment localized predominantly to cardiac tissues and atrial and ventricular cardiomyocytes. These findings were complemented by genome-wide heritability enrichment in regulatory elements active during human cardiac development. Significant enrichment was observed for 16 of 22 CMR traits, spanning all nine assayed Carnegie stages and all three active enhancer states. Notably, LVEDV and LVESV each showed significant enrichment in 51 of 54 developmental enhancer annotations, suggesting particularly widespread overlap between genetic determinants of adult ventricular dimensions and regulatory programs active during cardiac organogenesis. These findings extend prior CMR genetic studies implicating cardiac developmental pathways and congenital heart disease genes in variation in adult cardiac structure and function 2, 4 . Together, these observations suggest that common variation influencing adult cardiac phenotypes acts in part through developmental programs that establish cardiac structure, while enrichment in mature cardiomyocytes points to additional effects on the cellular processes that maintain cardiac form and function after development.

Our findings also connect genetic determinants of quantitative cardiac phenotypes with susceptibility to cardiomyopathy and heart failure. Prior studies have linked common genetic variation in CMR-derived ventricular traits to cardiomyopathy and heart failure through polygenic prediction, genetic correlation and shared genetic signals [2] [3] [4] 34 . CMR-prioritized genes were enriched for established cardiomyopathy genes, and a subset of CMR-associated regions contained shared genetic signals with HCM, DCM or heart failure. This extends prior work showing that genetic analyses of quantitative cardiac endophenotypes in largely disease-free populations can recover genes and pathways implicated in clinical cardiovascular disease 34 . At TMEM182, the ventricular-patterned single-variant signal was itself a previously identified HCM lead variant, while prior LV eQTL colocalization implicated lower LV TMEM182 expression in DCM risk 23, 28 . At FHOD3, independent intronic and coding signals showed different patterns of quantitative cardiac involvement; the intronic signal was led by a variant in strong LD with a prior HCM lead and colocalized with DCM in our analysis, whereas the coding p.Val822Phe signal was previously fine-mapped to resting heart rate 29 . Together with the distinct signals at RBM20, these findings show that different variants within the same cardiac locus can map to different cardiac phenotypes and anatomical patterns. At BAG3, the lead variant of a broad CMR signal colocalizing with HCM, DCM and heart failure was in near-perfect LD with the BAG3 missense variant p.Cys151Arg (rs2234962). p.Cys151Arg has opposing associations with cardiomyopathy, being associated with lower DCM risk but higher HCM risk, and has also been associated with lower DCM risk among carriers of TTN truncating variants in highly expressed exons 27 . Together, these findings support partial continuity between common genetic effects underlying quantitative cardiac variation and those contributing to pathological cardiac remodeling.

A second distinct feature of the study was the identification of common genetic effects on phenotypic variability rather than additive trait effects. Variance QTLs can prioritize loci at which genetic effects may depend on genetic or environmental context, providing information complementary to conventional mean-based association testing 8 . Variance-associated heritability was substantially lower than additive heritability, and most vQTLs lacked a nearby genome-wide significant additive association, indicating that variance mapping captures information not readily recovered from conventional GWAS. The recurrent prioritization of PITX2 across additive and variance analyses implicates the same locus in genetic effects on both phenotype level and variability, although the underlying mechanisms remain uncertain. Importantly, variance effects showed evidence of generalization beyond UK Biobank: variance polygenic scores for LVEF and LVESV were associated with variability in corresponding clinical echocardiographic measurements, and several individual vQTLs replicated across cohorts and imaging modalities. These findings identify phenotypic variability as a smaller and partly distinct component of cardiac genetics. However, vQTL association alone does not identify the genetic or environmental factor responsible for the variance effect, and the relevant modifying contexts remain to be determined 8.

Several limitations should be considered. First, the UK Biobank imaging cohort is predominantly of European genetic ancestry and exhibits healthy-volunteer selection relative to the broader UK Biobank cohort, which may limit generalizability 35 . Second, multivariate finemapping depends on model assumptions regarding the number and sharing of genetic effects, and the extensive cross-phenotype support observed here should therefore be interpreted in the context of those assumptions 6 . Third, HyPrColoc assumes at most one causal variant for each trait within a region, and colocalization supports a shared genetic signal but does not by itself establish the underlying biological mechanism 26 . Fourth, vQTL mapping identifies loci associated with phenotypic variance but does not identify the genetic or environmental context responsible for that variance 8 . Finally, PMBB provided an independent clinically ascertained cohort, but echocardiographic phenotypes were not always direct analogues of the CMR measurements and only a subset of vQTLs could be evaluated. Nevertheless, convergence across fine-mapping, functional annotation, disease genetics and independent clinical phenotypes supports the principal patterns observed.

In summary, common genetic effects on cardiac structure and function are broadly shared across the heart, yet individual loci can contain independent signals with distinct anatomical and phenotypic profiles. Variance-associated genetics adds a smaller, partly distinct dimension, extending the genetic study of cardiac phenotypes beyond additive genetic effects.

Methods

Study population and relatedness

We analyzed cardiac magnetic resonance imaging-derived phenotypes from UK Biobank Category 157, generated using the automated image-segmentation pipeline developed by Bai et al. (2020) 9 . The discovery analyses included up to 77,271 participants and up to 10,662,572 variants. Participants were restricted to an unrelated analysis set using UK Biobank KING kinship estimates 36 . Pairs with kinship coefficients greater than 0.0884 were represented as edges in a relatedness graph. All isolated participants were retained, and a maximum independent vertex set was selected within each connected component using igraph::largest_ivs, thereby retaining the largest possible unrelated subset within each component.

Cardiac phenotypes and covariates

We analyzed a panel of 22 cardiac imaging traits. These comprised left atrial ejection fraction (LAEF), minimum volume (LAMIV), maximum volume (LAMV) and stroke volume (LASV); left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF), end-diastolic volume (LVEDV), end-systolic volume (LVESV), stroke volume (LVSV), cardiac output (LVCO), myocardial mass (LVM), global mean myocardial wall thickness (LVWTG), global longitudinal strain (LVLSG), global circumferential strain (LVCSG) and global radial strain (LVRSG); right atrial ejection fraction (RAEF), minimum volume (RAMIV), maximum volume (RAMV) and stroke volume (RASV); and right ventricular end-diastolic volume (RVEDV), ejection fraction (RVEF), end-systolic volume (RVESV) and stroke volume (RVSV).

Each trait was analyzed among participants with complete phenotype and covariate data. Covariates comprised age at recruitment, genetic sex and the first five genetic principal components.

Genotype data preparation

UK Biobank imputed BGEN v3 genotype data for autosomes 1-22 were converted to chromosomespecific PLINK files for the retained participant set. BGEN alleles were interpreted using the reference-first convention, and variants with minor allele frequency below 0.005 were excluded.

Genome-wide additive association analysis

Genome-wide additive association analysis was performed separately for each of the 22 CMR phenotypes using PLINK2 linear regression 37 . For each trait, the original CMR phenotype was regressed on genotype under an additive genetic model, adjusting for age at recruitment, genetic sex and the first five genetic principal components.

Summary-statistic quality control and coordinate conversion

Summary statistics were matched to UK Biobank MFI records by chromosome position and unordered allele pair. MFI records with conflicting duplicate annotations were excluded. Variants were required to have nonmissing INFO, a minor allele frequency between 0 and 0.5 and an MFI minor allele matching one summary-statistic allele. We retained variants with INFO greater than 0.8 and minor allele count greater than 20, where minor allele count was calculated as 2N × MAF. GRCh38 coordinates were generated with GWASLab v4.1.4, retaining unique mappings that remained on the same chromosome.

Locus definition and cross-locus merging

Loci were defined separately for additive and variance statistics using 50-kb genomic tiles. Tiles containing at least one variant with P ≤ 1 × 10 -5 were marked as occupied, directly adjacent occupied tiles were merged and each resulting interval was padded by one 50-kb tile on either side. A padded interval was retained when it contained at least one genome-wide significant variant with P ≤ 5 × 10 -8 ; the most significant variant in the interval was designated the lead variant. Within each trait, association model and chromosome, intervals were merged when they overlapped or when their lead variants had Pan-UKB European ancestry linkage disequilibrium 38 r 2 > 0.001. Merging was applied transitively through connected components, with final boundaries defined by the minimum start and maximum end. Pan-UKB variants were matched by GRCh37 position and unordered allele pair, requiring a unique reference match. Variance loci were annotated by the nearest genome-wide significant additive variant and whether it was located within 500 kb.

Locus coordinate conversion

Final locus boundaries and lead-variant positions were converted from GRCh37 to GRCh38 using rtracklayer 39 with an hg19-to-hg38 chain file. Lower boundary, upper boundary and lead position were first lifted as separate single-base coordinates; the conversion was accepted when all three mapped exactly once to the same chromosome. When this criterion failed but the lead position mapped uniquely, the complete GRCh37 interval was lifted as a fallback. Mapped fragments were restricted to the chromosome containing the lifted lead variant, and GRCh38 boundaries were defined by the outermost retained fragment coordinates. Converted loci were retained only when both boundaries and the lead position were valid and chromosome-consistent.

Comparison with previous cardiac imaging GWAS

Additive loci were compared with genome-wide significant variants from three previous cardiac imaging GWAS [2] [3] [4] . Variants with P ≤ 5 × 10 -8 were retained from each study. For each locus, prior variants were classified as directly overlapping the interval, within 500 kb of an interval edge, between 500 kb and 2 Mb or more than 2 Mb away. Comparisons were conducted using traits present in the Pirruccello 2020, Pirruccello 2022 and Nicholls 2026 datasets, both within the same trait and across any mapped trait, comprising LAEF, LAMV, LAMIV, LASV, LVEF, LVEDV, LVESV, LVSV, LVM, RVEF, RVEDV, RVESV, RVSV. A locus was classified as novel relative to the included studies only when every expected comparison was evaluable and no previously reported genome-wide significant variant was located within 2 Mb of either locus boundary.

Multivariate fine-mapping

Multivariate fine-mapping was performed for an 18-trait cardiac panel comprising LAEF, LAMIV, LAMV, LVCO, LVCSG, LVEDV, LVEF, LVESV, LVLSG, LVM, LVRSG, LVWTG, RAEF, RAMIV, RAMV, RVEDV, RVEF and RVESV 6 . After finding substantial linear dependence and numerical instability in the multivariate correlation matrices, the four stroke-volume traits (LASV, LVSV, RASV and RVSV) were excluded because they are directly derived from the corresponding volumetric measures. Unique additive loci from the 22-trait source panel were merged transitively when their intervals overlapped within a chromosome. One region overlapping the major histocompatibility complex was excluded from multivariate fine-mapping because a stable fine-mapping solution could not be obtained. Regions were required to contain complete (Z) statistics across all 18 traits. European-ancestry linkage disequilibrium matrices were used for each region. A residual-correlation matrix was estimated from two putatively null variants per region, selected as the variants with the smallest maximum absolute Z statistic across the 18 traits among variants with maximum Z < 2. The uncentered second moment of the selected Z-score vectors was calculated and scaled to a correlation matrix.

An empirical covariance prior was learned from one lead variant per region, defined as the complete variant with the largest maximum absolute (Z) statistic. Regions were included in prior training when the selected variant reached P ≤ 5 × 10 -8 in at least one trait. Candidate covariance matrices included an empirical second-moment covariance, FLASH-derived factors, principalcomponent covariances and canonical covariance matrices, which were combined using Bovy extreme deconvolution 40 . For each region, trait-specific sample size was defined as the median finite per-variant (N), and regional sample size as the median across traits.

mvSuSiE was fit with L = 10, 95% credible-set coverage, minimum absolute credible-set correlation of 0.5 and a trait-support local false sign rate threshold of 0.01. Prior variance was estimated by expectation-maximization, whereas residual variance and prior mixture weights were fixed.

Bipartite network analysis of cross-trait genetic sharing

Cross-trait genetic sharing was represented as a binary bipartite network linking mvSuSiE credible sets to supported cardiac phenotypes. Rows of the incidence matrix represented credible sets and columns represented the 18 analyzed phenotypes. An edge connected a credible set to a phenotype when the corresponding single-effect local false sign rate was ≤0.01 and the conditional effect was finite and nonzero.

Community structure was identified directly from the bipartite network by maximizing Barber bipartite modularity 11 . The observed network was analyzed using 100 optimization iterations, and phenotype assignments from the maximum-modularity solution were retained. Barber bipartite modularity is denoted Q.

To determine whether the observed modular organization exceeded that expected from differences in node connectivity alone, we generated 10,000 randomized binary networks preserving both row and column degree sequences. Thus, each randomized network retained the number of phenotypes supported by each credible set, the number of credible sets supporting each phenotype and the total number of edges, while randomizing the identities of individual connections. Preservation of both degree sequences and the total edge count was verified for every randomized network, and community detection was repeated using the same procedure. The one-sided empirical P value was calculated as #1 + ∑ 𝐼 ! "#$ [𝑄 " ≥ 𝑄 %&' ]+/(𝐵 + 1), where B = 10,000. Robustness of the phenotype partition to arbitrary node ordering was evaluated by independently permuting the credible-set rows and phenotype columns of the unchanged observed incidence matrix 10,000 times. Community detection was repeated after each permutation, and phenotype assignments were mapped back to their original identities. Agreement with the reference partition was quantified by the proportion of exact partition recoveries and the adjusted Rand index.

Anatomical organization of cross-trait genetic sharing

To test whether genetic sharing followed prespecified cardiac anatomy, pairwise credible-set sharing was compared according to atrial versus ventricular compartment, left versus right laterality and individual cardiac chamber. For each phenotype pair, normalized sharing was calculated as the number of credible sets supporting both phenotypes divided by the number supporting either phenotype, corresponding to the Jaccard index 𝐽 () = 2𝐶 ( ∩ 𝐶 ) 2/2𝐶 ( ∪ 𝐶 ) 2.

For each anatomical classification, mean normalized sharing among phenotype pairs assigned to the same group was compared with that among phenotype pairs assigned to different groups. Statistical significance was evaluated using 10,000 permutations of the anatomical labels across phenotype nodes while preserving the observed credible-set sharing network and the number of phenotypes assigned to each group. One-sided empirical P values were calculated as 𝑃 = #1 + ∑ 𝐼 ! "#$ [𝐷 " ≥ 𝐷 %&' ]+/(𝐵 + 1), where D denotes the difference between mean within-group and between-group sharing and B = 10,000.

Functional annotation of multivariate credible-set variants

Unique GRCh37 variants from mvSuSiE credible sets were submitted in VCF-like representation to the Ensembl GRCh37 regional Variant Effect Predictor 41 REST endpoint. Transcript annotations were restricted to official MANE Select release 1.5. 42 When more than one MANE Select annotation remained for a variant, annotations were ranked by VEP consequence severity, impact, gene symbol and transcript identifier. Coding, splice and VEP HIGH, MODERATE or LOW impact annotations were retained for downstream displays.

Single-trait fine-mapping

Additive and variance loci were fine-mapped with SuSiE-RSS 43 using the Pan-UKB European ancestry linkage disequilibrium reference panel. Variants were required to have finite effect estimates, standard errors and P values, with standard errors greater than zero; Z statistics were calculated as β/SE. Alleles were aligned to the Pan-UKB reference. Loci with fewer than 20 variants after harmonization were excluded. The effective sample size was the median per-variant N within each harmonized locus. SuSiE-RSS was fit with a maximum of L = 10 effects, 99% credible-set coverage, minimum absolute credible-set correlation of 0.5 and fixed residual variance.

Gene-level association and PoPS prioritization

Gene-level association testing was performed with MAGMA v1.07a 44 using the snp-wise=mean model, GRCh38 1000 Genomes 45 reference genotypes and per-variant SNP identifiers, P values and sample sizes. PoPS gene prioritization 46 was performed from the MAGMA gene results using the supplied full feature collection and control-feature matrix. MAGMA gene-property analyses used GTEx v8 tissue-expression covariates.

Integrated gene prioritization

FLAMES 15 was used to integrate single-trait fine-mapping, MAGMA gene-level associations, PoPS scores and GTEx tissue-property results. Annotation and scoring were performed separately for each cardiac trait and association model (additive and variance).

Gene Ontology biological-process enrichment

Biological-process enrichment was evaluated among high-confidence genes prioritized by FLAMES from additive analyses. Genes with estimated cumulative precision greater than 0.75 were pooled across the 22 CMR traits and deduplicated by gene symbol. Enrichment analysis was performed using Enrichr and the GO_Biological_Process_2025 database 16, 17 . Gene Ontology terms with Benjamini-Hochberg-adjusted P < 0.05 were considered significantly enriched.

Enrichment of curated cardiac disease genes

Additive FLAMES-prioritized genes were tested for overrepresentation of curated hypertrophic cardiomyopathy 7 , dilated cardiomyopathy 7 and arrhythmogenic cardiomyopathy gene sets 22 . Ensembl gene-version suffixes were removed before matching genes by Ensembl gene ID. The gene universe comprised unique genes in the additive FLAMES raw table, and the selected set comprised unique additive FLAMES-predicted genes present in that universe. For each curated gene set, enrichment was tested using a one-sided hypergeometric test, where N was the background size, n the selected-set size, K the number of curated genes in the background and x their overlap with the selected set. A one-sided Fisher exact test was used as a cross-check and its odds ratio was reported. Benjamini-Hochberg correction was applied across the three diseasespecific tests. The union of the three curated sets was additionally analyzed with shared genes counted once.

GTEx tissue-specific stratified LD Score Regression

Tissue-specific heritability enrichment was evaluated using GTEx 19 entries from the LDSC multitissue gene-expression annotation manifest. Stratified LD Score Regression 18 incorporated baselineLD v2.2 47 , HapMap3 weights excluding the major histocompatibility complex, European frequency files and overlapping annotations. Additive and variance-association summary statistics were analyzed separately. Benjamini-Hochberg correction was applied across GTEx tissue annotations within each trait and association model.

Cardiac cell-type annotations

Cardiac cell-type annotations were constructed from HeartMap 20 global-AUC marker genes. One gene set was defined for each of 14 HeartMap cell types together with an all-genes control set. Marker genes were mapped through Ensembl gene identifiers, and binary annotations were created from 100-kb windows around selected genes. Annotation LD scores were calculated using 1000 Genomes Phase 3 European genotypes, a 1-cM window and thin-annotation format.

Cardiac cell-type stratified LD Score Regression

Cardiac trait summary statistics were munged against the HapMap3 SNP list while retaining pervariant sample sizes. Cell-type-specific LD Score Regression was performed for 22 cardiac traits across the 14 HeartMap annotations using baselineLD v2.2, HapMap3 weights excluding the major histocompatibility complex, European frequency files and overlapping-annotation calculations. Additive and variance-association summary statistics were analyzed separately. Benjamini-Hochberg correction was applied across the 14 cell-type annotations within each trait and association model.

Developmental cardiac enhancer enrichment

Developmental cardiac enhancer annotations were derived from Heart Hub hg19 ChromHMM 25state data 21 . The enhancer states 13_EnhA1, 14_EnhA2 and 15_EnhAF were retained across 18 cardiac developmental samples, yielding 54 sample-by-state annotations. Intervals were converted to autosomal BED annotations and then to binary SNP annotations. Annotation LD scores were computed using 1000 Genomes Phase 3 European genotypes, a 1-cM window and thin-annotation format. Each annotation was fit jointly with baselineLD v2.2 using HapMap3 regression variants excluding the major histocompatibility complex, European frequency files and overlappingannotation coefficients. Enrichment was assessed from the positive coefficient tail, and Benjamini-Hochberg correction was applied across the 54 annotations within each trait. Additive and variance-association summary statistics were analyzed separately for each trait.

Cross-trait colocalization with cardiomyopathy and heart failure

Cross-trait colocalization was evaluated between the 22 additive CMR traits and hypertrophic cardiomyopathy 23 , dilated cardiomyopathy 24 and heart failure 25 using HyPrColoc 26 . Additive loci were merged by overlapping GRCh37 intervals. Within each region, summary statistics for all 25 traits were matched by GRCh37 chromosome position and unordered allele pair, and effect estimates were aligned to a common allele. Variants were required to have finite effect estimates and standard errors greater than zero and to be present with concordant alleles across all 25 traits; regions with fewer than 10 shared variants were excluded. HyPrColoc was applied using the default priors (P1 = 1 × 10 -4 , Pc = 0.02) and regional association and alignment probability thresholds of 0.5, without an external linkage-disequilibrium matrix. Trait clusters, candidate variants and colocalization posterior probabilities were retained. For highlighted comparisons with previously reported disease-associated variants, pairwise linkage disequilibrium was calculated from the corresponding Pan-UKB European-ancestry regional LD matrix used for multivariate fine-mapping.

Genome-wide variance QTL association analysis

Genome-wide variance QTL analyses were performed separately for each of the 22 CMR phenotypes using QUAIL 8 . For each trait, QUAIL Step 1 was applied among participants with complete phenotype and covariate data across 2,000 quantile levels to construct a quantileintegrated rank-score phenotype. Models included age at recruitment, genetic sex and the first five genetic principal components, and trait datasets were required to contain unique participant identifiers and at least 50 complete observations.

Genome-wide variance association testing was then performed in QUAIL Step 2. For each variant, PLINK2 linear regression was used to test the association between genotype and the trait-specific quantile-integrated rank score, adjusting for the same covariates. Variance effect estimates, standard errors, test statistics, P values and per-variant sample sizes were retained for downstream analyses.

SNP heritability and genetic correlation

LD Score Regression 48 was used to estimate SNP heritability separately for additive and variance association statistics, and cross-trait LD Score Regression 10 was used to estimate pairwise genetic correlations from additive association statistics. Across the 22 phenotypes, the relationship between additive and variance SNP heritability estimates was assessed using Pearson correlation.

Polygenic-score weight estimation

Additive and variance polygenic-score weights were estimated separately with PRS-CSx 49 from GRCh37 summary statistics. Variants were restricted to HapMap3 rsIDs represented in the UK Biobank European linkage disequilibrium reference and were supplied with SNP identifier, effect allele, other allele, effect estimate and standard error. PRS-CSx was run separately by chromosome with European target population, chromosome-specific median per-variant sample size, automatic global shrinkage (φ = auto) and prior parameter b = 0.5.

Polygenic-score calculation in the Penn Medicine Biobank

Additive and variance polygenic scores for all 22 CMR traits were calculated for Penn Medicine Biobank 31 participants with available genotypes (release 2024-3.0) using pgsc_calc v2.2.0 50 . The pgsc_HGDP+1kGP_v1 ancestry reference was used, a minimum score-target variant overlap of 70% was required and the ancestry-normalized Znorm2 value was used in downstream analyses. Pearson correlations between matched additive and variance polygenic scores were calculated across all 22 traits in 59,687 PMBB participants.

Echocardiographic phenotype processing in PMBB

Echocardiographic measurements were harmonized from CUPID and ProSolv extracts. CUPID stress, transesophageal, intracardiac, congenital transesophageal and research echocardiography procedures were excluded, whereas ProSolv records were restricted to complete or limited transthoracic echocardiograms. Participant identifier, examination identifier and date, procedure, measurement name, value and unit were standardized across sources. Numeric values were parsed after unit removal, and reported numeric ranges were converted to their midpoint. Trait-specific exact measurement-name mappings and physiological bounds were applied. Within each examination, a prespecified preferred measurement was selected. Participant-level summaries included the median, minimum, maximum, interquartile range and number of measurements, together with dates corresponding to the median-nearest, minimum and maximum values.

Imaging validation of PGS and vPGS

Additive and variance polygenic scores were evaluated against corresponding echocardiographic phenotypes in Penn Medicine Biobank. Five CMR-to-echocardiography mappings were evaluated: LVEF with LVEF, LVEDV with left ventricular internal diameter in diastole, LVESV with LV systolic volume, LVM with LV mass, and LAMV with LA maximum volume. Additive PGS portability was evaluated using participant-level median echocardiographic measurements. Associations were tested by linear regression of each phenotype on the ancestry-normalized Znorm2 score, adjusting for enrollment age and sex.

Variance PGS validation was performed for the same five CMR traits using the QUAIL vPGS framework. Participant-level minimum LVEF was used for LVEF and maximum measurements were used for the remaining phenotypes. Models used 2,000 quantile levels and included enrollment age, sex and the corresponding additive polygenic score as covariates. Benjamini-Hochberg correction was applied across the five variance-PGS imaging-validation tests.

Replication of individual variance QTLs

Genome-wide significant vQTL lead variants were tested in PMBB when a corresponding echocardiographic phenotype was available. Six variants were evaluated across four CMR traits: LAMV with left atrial volume, LVESV with LV systolic volume, LVM with LV mass, and LVEDV with LVIDd. For each phenotype, QUAIL Step 1 was applied across 2,000 quantile levels with age, genetic sex, the first six genetic principal components and sequencing batch as covariates. Lead variants were then tested for association with the resulting QUAIL variance phenotype by linear regression of imputed effect-allele dosage, adjusting for the same covariates. Analyses were performed in participants of European genetic ancestry. Statistical significance was defined using Bonferroni correction for six prespecified tests (P < 0.0083), with replication additionally requiring the same direction of effect as in UK Biobank.

To assess colocalization between variance-and additive-associated signals in UK Biobank, overlapping vQTL loci were merged into variance regions. Within each region, HyPrColoc was applied jointly to variance-effect estimates for the variance-associated trait(s) and additive-effect estimates across all 22 CMR phenotypes using variants shared across the included traits. Clusters containing at least one variance and one additive trait were retained as mixed additive-variance colocalization signals.

Cardiovascular disease associations of polygenic scores

Additive and variance polygenic scores for LVEF, LVM, LVEDV, LVESV and LAMV were tested for association with cardiovascular diagnoses in the Penn Medicine Biobank. Diagnoses were defined from PheCodeX 51 records as dilated cardiomyopathy (CV_414.2), any cardiomyopathy (CV_414), heart failure (CV_424), atrial fibrillation (CV_416.21) and ventricular tachycardia (CV_416.12). Scores were standardized within the analysis sample, and the corresponding additive and variance scores were included simultaneously in logistic regression models adjusting for enrollment age and sex. Benjamini-Hochberg correction was applied separately across the 25 additive-score and 25 variance-score associations.
