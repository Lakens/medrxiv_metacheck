Reporting p values imprecisely (e.g., p < .05) reduces transparency, reproducibility, and re-use (e.g., in p value meta-analyses). Best practice is to report exact p-values with three decimal places (e.g., p = .032) unless p values are smaller than 0.001, in which case you can use p < .001.

To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 13 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Full text

Abstract

Digital health technology (DHT) is increasingly being integrated into healthcare delivery; however, limited research exists exploring the pre-acceptability of DHT among ageing respiratory patients. This study aimed to identify the factors influencing the pre-acceptability of digital health technology in individuals with chronic respiratory disease in Ireland.

A quantitative study was conducted among respiratory service users attending two respiratory centres in the Midlands and West of Ireland. A convenience-based questionnaire was developed and distributed, which was informed by prior Phase 1 qualitative findings that adopted the Technology Acceptance Model and the Health Belief Model.Data were analysed using non-parametric statistical methods, including the Mann-Whitney U test, to explore differences between gender and geographical location (urban/rural).

Fifty-eight participants completed the study. Overall, participants demonstrated positive perceptions toward the use of DHT, particularly regarding support for self-management, hospital admission avoidance, increased symptom awareness, and enhanced engagement with healthcare professionals. Perceived usefulness emerged as a significant factor influencing acceptability. Concerns regarding privacy, data security, and digital literacy were less significant than anticipated. Statistical differences were identified between urban and rural participants in relation to perceived usefulness, technology design preferences, feedback.

Introduction Introduction

The global healthcare sector has witnessed accelerating interest in digital technologies to support and enhance clinical service delivery. The COVID-19 pandemic served as a catalyst, rapidly exposing both the potential and necessity of remote healthcare solutions. Digital health, encompassing e-health, m-health, and telehealth, offers opportunities to strengthen clinical engagement through advanced monitoring capabilities aimed at improving patient outcomes [1]. These technologies enable healthcare professionals to make more informed clinical decisions and elevate care quality, predicated on the assumption that service users possess both the capability and willingness to engage with such innovations. Consequently, digital health technologies (DHT) represent a promising paradigm for symptom monitoring, treatment adherence support, and enhanced healthcare access across diverse patient populations [2].

Relevance to Respiratory Care

For respiratory conditions including chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) and asthma, DHT present particularly compelling opportunities to advance disease management and enable personalised care pathways [3]. The clinical evaluation schedule for this patient cohort, typically involving assessments at six-to-twelve-month intervals, may contribute to under-recognition of disease progression. While increasing consultation frequency could address this gap, practical constraints including cost, time, and geographical accessibility often limit healthcare access. Digital health technologies offer a potential solution by facilitating remote symptom self-management and ongoing monitoring.

Digital Literacy and Adoption

Müller et al. [4] established that successful DHT utilisation is intrinsically linked to internet access and digital competence. These authors observed higher DHT adoption rates among younger, more educated, and higher-income populations, suggesting that effective engagement requires concurrent health literacy and information technology proficiency. Supporting this perspective, Neter and Brainin [5] documented that individuals with limited e-health literacy demonstrate reduced capacity to utilise available online health resources. Conversely, Smith [6] challenged assumptions regarding older adults' technological capabilities, arguing that media portrayals have perpetuated misconceptions about this demographic's willingness and ability to adopt technology. Perrin and Duggan [7] further contested this narrative, identifying older adults as one of the fastest-growing populations in terms of internet engagement. These conflicting perspectives underscore a critical research question: "What factors influence successful DHT adoption among ageing populations?"

Evidence from Clinical Trials

Clinical investigations have examined DHT applications across various healthcare contexts.

Steel et al. [8] evaluated remote patient monitoring for asthmatic patients aged 17-50, reporting mean compliance of 80%. Notably, 96% of participants indicated willingness to reuse the equipment and rated it as easy or very easy to use. During the study period, 48% received medical intervention, suggesting remote monitoring's potential to reduce asthma morbidity.

Ierodiakonou et al. [9] explored remote care for COPD and congestive heart failure, identifying significant reductions in emergency department presentations. While numerous studies have evaluated purpose-designed devices for chronic diseases, highlighting age, gender, and physiological measurement influences, the specific factors affecting service user engagement or disengagement remain unclear from existing research.

The Adoption Gap

Global DHT adoption patterns reveal significant variation, with wearable technologies lagging considerably behind established technologies such as smartphones and tablets [10]. Cheung et al. [11] identified a concerning knowledge gap regarding service users' adoption intentions in healthcare contexts. Much existing research has prioritised technical development over understanding diffusion processes, contrasting sharply with commercial technology marketing research, which focuses predominantly on consumer adoption and user experience, thereby accelerating diffusion. Lycett et al. [12] advocated for integrating psychological theoriesincluding social cognitive theory, the health belief model, and self-efficacy theory-into digital intervention development to enhance effectiveness and adoption rates.

.

Acceptance and Sustained Engagement

Nadal et al. [13] highlighted that user acceptance varies across pre-acceptance, initial acceptance, and sustained use phases, each presenting distinct design challenges that influence compliance and long-term engagement. The literature reveals considerable definitional variability regarding technology acceptance. Perski and Short [14] conceptualised acceptability as an individual's attitude or willingness to engage with novel therapies or digital devices, encompassing pre-engagement willingness, actual usage, and post-engagement satisfaction.

Recent recommendations by Nadal et al. [15] identified a gap in understanding pre-acceptance of technologies and emphasised the need to capture user perspectives post-usage. While postusage evaluation may assess effectiveness, it fails to illuminate factors necessary for sustained engagement. As healthcare continues its digital evolution, understanding service user perceptions becomes imperative.

Study Aim

This study therefore aims to identify and describe the factors influencing DHT preacceptability among respiratory patients as a specific end-user population.

Materials and Methods

Quantitative research is often referred to as a "confirmatory" or "deductive" approach as its main goal is to test theories and hypotheses by examining the relationships among variables [16, 17]. These variables in turn can be measured so that numbered data can be analysed using . statistical procedures [17]. The questionnaire was developed not only using qualitative data from phase 1 which was previously published [18], but also by the amalgamation of the Technology Acceptance Model and the Health Belief Model. The Technology Acceptance Model was developed in the 1980s in an attempt to help researchers explore the rationale as to why individuals were not using technology that was made available to them [19]. The use of validated frameworks in conjunction with the data collected from phase 1 enhanced the rigor of the questionnaire, and ultimately resulted in a study that was well-founded. External persons from the study were acquired for face and validity testing who reviewed the developed questionnaire independently.

Participants

The participants for the second phase of the study were recruited in two different Institutions by means of purposeful sampling. A cohort of respiratory patients attending Portiuncula University Hospital and Galway University Hospital were recruited from 02/02/2022 to 13/04/2022. They were invited to complete a questionnaire. Written information was provided to each participant; consent was obtained and assurance that their decision not to participate would not have any impact on the care provided at that clinic.

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

Participants in this study were women and men resident in Ireland. Inclusion criteria included service users with known respiratory disease attending respiratory services in the midlands/west of Ireland, English-speaking, and ≥60 years of age. Exclusion criteria included non-English speaking individuals and those on active palliative pathways.

Ethics

Ethical approval is important for all types of research. This is to ensure the protection of participants, the researcher and the organisation they represent. It aims to minimise the risk of harm to all involved. Participants were provided written information in the form of a handout about the study, its pros and cons along with the benefits of participating in the study. Ethical approval was first sought from the Technological University of the Shannon Research Ethics Committee, and upon gaining approval then ethical permission was sought from clinical sites.

Applications for ethical approval contained a copy of detailed information sheets and consent forms which was also attached for consideration. The completed submissions were made on 12th December 2021 and approval was granted on January 15th, 2022. The researcher carried out data collection from March 2022 to May 2022 (Ethical approval number C.A.2734).

Sample Size

The average number of patients attending both clinics over a 10-week period was estimated to be 300. The assumption was of an average non-attendance rate of 15% [21] and an overall expected completion rate of 33% [22], with the estimated sample size for this study being 84.

The duration of this study was dictated by the secondment period agreed for the researcher and the permission of access to clinics by management in the HSE.

Data Collection

Participants could choose to complete the questionnaire at the time of the clinic or return via a supplied stamped addressed envelope provided.

Data Analysis

Data was collected in the form of a questionnaire utilising a Likert Scale, which is ordinal data and cannot be considered normally distributed as the values are bound on the left and right. To confirm that data were indeed not distributed normally, the researcher conducted a Shapiro Wilk Test, which did further confirm with a significance of .000 across both variables that the data was not normally distributed; therefore, for the purpose of this study non-parametric tests were considered for statistical analysis. The non-parametric test selected was the Mann-Whitney U Test.

Results

Study Population

This study was conducted over a ten-week period collecting data from two separate institutions in the Midland and West of Ireland, with fifty-eight (58) individuals participating. Of the fiftyeight participants, 44.8% (n=26) were male and 53.4% (n=32) were female. The mean average of those who disclosed their age was 69.5. Geographically, 32.8% (n=19) self-reported to be from an urban area and 55.2% (n=32) from a rural location. In respect to technology skills, 52% (n=30) claimed to have self-taught skills, with no prior formal education. Over 300 questionnaires were distributed with only 58 respondents, despite the provision of stamped addressed envelopes.

Key Themes

The key themes yielded from this study were: • Support of self-management of respiratory disease; • Engagement with healthcare professionals; • Design and ability of the technology to provide data feedback; • Security; • Digital literacy; • Perceived usefulness and awareness of digital health technology.

Support for Self-Management of Respiratory Disease

When participants were asked whether DHT would assist them in managing their respiratory disease, 26.5% (n=13) strongly agreed and 32.6% (n=16) agreed, while 32.6% (n=16) were unsure and 4% (n=2) disagreed. Regarding hospital admission avoidance, 18.5% (n=10) strongly agreed and 37% (n=20) agreed that DHT would help reduce admissions, with 38.2% (n=21) uncertain and 5.5% (n=3) in disagreement. Concerning feelings of security between healthcare appointments, 8.4% (n=4) strongly agreed and 46.9% (n=23) agreed that DHT would increase their sense of security, while 34.6% (n=17) were unsure and 10.2% (n=5) disagreed. Notably, 17.3% (n=9) strongly agreed and 38.4% (n=20) agreed that DHT would empower them to take control of their chronic disease, with 40.8% (n=21) uncertain and 17.3% (n=9) disagreeing.

Mann-Whitney U tests comparing male versus female and urban versus rural perspectives revealed no statistically significant differences across any self-management or admission avoidance variables (all p > 0.05).

Engagement with Healthcare Professionals

When asked whether they derived more value from face-to-face appointments, 33.3% (n=18) strongly agreed and 37% (n=20) agreed, with 14.8% (n=8) uncertain and an equal proportion disagreeing. Regarding whether DHT would reduce required clinic appointments, 12% (n=6) strongly agreed and 48% (n=24) agreed, while 34% (n=17) were unsure and 6% (n=3) disagreed. Interestingly, 9.5% (n=4) strongly agreed and 40.4% (n=17) agreed that they would prefer a reduction in appointments, with 26.1% (n=11) uncertain and 23.8% (n=10) disagreeing.Finally, when asked if DHT would increase overall contact with healthcare providers, 10.8% (n=5) strongly agreed, 39.1% (n=18) agreed, 39.1% (n=18) were unsure, and 10.8% (n=5) disagreed. Mann-Whitney U tests demonstrated no significant differences between genders regarding any engagement variable (all p > 0.05). Similarly, no significant differences emerged between urban and rural participants regarding value placed on face-toface clinics or perceived increased contact with healthcare professionals (p = 0.60 and p = 0.81, respectively). However, a statistically significant difference was observed between urban and rural cohorts regarding preference for reduction in face-to-face clinics (p = 0.01). Rural participants demonstrated less preference for reduced clinic attendance compared to their urban counterparts.

Technology Design and Feedback Capabilities

Regarding technology size as a factor influencing adoption, 8.5% (n=4) strongly agreed and 36.1% (n=17) agreed that size would affect their decision, while 36.1% (n=17) were unsure and 19.1% (n=9) disagreed. Visual acuity was considered important by 4.3% (n=2) who strongly agreed and 47.8% (n=22) who agreed, with 17.39% (n=8) uncertain and 30.4% (n=14) disagreeing. Notably, 37.2% (n=19) strongly agreed and 50.9% (n=26) agreed that they would prefer the device to have the capability to provide health data feedback, with 11.7% (n=6) uncertain and no participants disagreeing.Mann-Whitney U tests revealed no significant differences between males and females regarding technology size, design features, or feedback capabilities (p > 0.05). Conversely, statistically significant differences emerged between urban and rural participants regarding design features (p = 0.01) and the ability to provide health information feedback (p = 0.01). Rural participants demonstrated less favourable attitudes toward DHT design features and expressed less interest in feedback capabilities compared to urban participants. No significant difference was observed regarding the impact of technology size (p = 0.08).

Security and Privacy

When asked whether DHT would invade their privacy, 7.3% (n=3) strongly agreed, 29.2% (n=12) were unsure, and 63.4% (n=26) disagreed. Regarding concerns about who would have access to their health data, 14.6% (n=6) strongly agreed, 21.9% (n=9) agreed, 21.9% (n=9) were unsure, and 41.4% (n=17) disagreed. Concerning technology security, 17.02% (n=8) strongly agreed, 10.6% (n=5) agreed, 31.9% (n=15) were unsure, and 40.02% (n=19) disagreed. Mann-Whitney U tests confirmed no statistically significant differences across gender or geographical variables (p > 0.05).

Digital Literacy

When asked to consider the statement "I do not have the IT skills to use technology," 17.6% (n=9) strongly disagreed and 41.1% (n=21) disagreed, while 15.6% (n=8) strongly agreed, 9.8% (n=5) agreed, and 15.6% (n=8) were unsure. Mann-Whitney U tests revealed no significant differences between genders (p = 0.43) or geographical locations (p = 0.46).

Perceived Usefulness

When presented with the statement "Digital health technology is something that I feel would be useful to me," 18.8% (n=10) strongly agreed, 49% (n=26) agreed, 30% (n=16) were unsure, and 1.8% (n=1) disagreed. Mann-Whitney U tests demonstrated no significant difference between male and female perspectives (p = 0.52). However, a statistically significant difference emerged between urban and rural cohorts (p = 0.04), with rural participants expressing fewer positive perceptions of DHT usefulness.

Awareness of Digital Health Technology

When asked to consider the statement "I have never heard of digital health technology prior to participating in this study," 23.9% (n=11) strongly agreed, 30.4% (n=14) agreed, 30.4% (n=14) disagreed, and 15.2% (n=7) were unsure. Mann-Whitney U tests confirmed no significant differences between genders (p = 0.47) or geographical cohorts (p = 0.23).

Discussion

Perceived Usefulness

Perceived usefulness is one of the most important factors discussed in the literature when discussing acceptability and DHT. People's understanding of why they are using DHT and what benefits it has for them significantly influences adoption. This is evident in studies such as Shinoda et al. [10] whose research shows that wearable technology is accepted when it facilitates goal setting, provides reminders with useful feedback, and has the ability to share data with HCPs. Cerdán de las Heras et al. [29], Hamine et al. [30] and Shinoda et al. [10] identify that the acceptability of technology is profoundly influenced by the service users' belief in why they are using the technology, what benefits it will have for them and how easy the technology is to use. In this study, most participants strongly agreed or agreed that DHT would be useful to them. Statistically, males and females' perspectives do not differ (p=0.528); however, there is an evident statistical difference between urban and rural cohorts (p=0.048).

There is a lack of clarity as to why there is a statistical difference between those from rural and urban areas. One possible reason is simply a lack of awareness. As previously discussed, the literature shows no evidence of exploration of the awareness of DHT. This study found that 54.3% of participants had never heard of DHT prior to the study. While there was no statistical difference between males and females or urban and rural areas, it is most definitely a concern.

There is an expectation or even worse, an assumption by those developing technology and HCPs that service users will engage with DHT. Service users need to understand clearly why they need to use DHT and what benefits it will have for them. Future research into the perspectives of urban and rural geographical location is required to gain a deeper understanding. Those involved in the development of DHT, policy makers and clinicians need to be aware that there is a statistical difference, and remain cautious if assuming what works for urban areas will work for rural areas.

Digital Literacy

Digital literacy is defined as a person's ability to find, evaluate, and communicate via a technology device. Digital health literacy is the ability to find, understand, and appraise health information from electronic sources and apply the knowledge gained to address or solve health problems [5]. The literature clearly shows that empowering service users to engage with DHT is heavily dependent on their digital literacy skills [4, 28]. In the same studies, digital literacy was reported to be a significant adoption barrier; often individuals did not possess any digital literacy skills, resulting in an inability to interpret and act upon the readings of the technology device.Contrary to the argument made by Neter and Brainin [5], Smith [6] is confident that communities such as the elderly fall victim to misconceptions concerning their use of technology. Perrin and Duggan [7] discredit this and commend the elderly population as being one of the fastest growing populations in engaging in internet use. Participants in this study were asked to consider the statement "I do not have the IT skills to use technology." A majority (58.7%) strongly disagreed or disagreed with this statement. There were no statistical differences between males and females or those from rural and urban areas. Digital literacy skills are an important contributing factor to the pre-acceptability of DHT, and this study shows that a majority of participants feel they have the skills required to engage.

Data Privacy and Security

The concepts of privacy and accessibility of personal health data were addressed with service users. When asked if they felt DHT would invade their privacy, 63.4% (n=26) disagreed. When asked if they had concerns as to who would have access to their health data, 41.4% (n=17) disagreed. Finally, when asked if they would have concerns about the security of the technology, 40.02% (n=19) disagreed. There was no statistical difference between the variables measured and the null hypothesis was retained. The perspectives of participants in this study appear contrary to other published studies [25, 32]. While it is important to safeguard the privacy and health data of service users, this was not a major concern for the ageing respiratory patients and, therefore, it can be argued that data privacy is not a significant contributing factor to the pre-acceptability of DHT in this cohort.

Support of Self-Management of Respiratory Disease

Self-management of chronic respiratory diseases is widely discussed in the literature and is strongly advocated for within appropriate guidelines [33]. There is evidence to suggest that DHT increases patients' awareness of their condition, reduces hospital admissions, and improves communication with healthcare professionals [12]. Slevin et al. [28] and Korpershoek et al. [25] report that participants viewed DHT as extremely useful in helping them to independently manage their chronic disease.This study found that 59.1% of participants agreed or strongly agreed that DHT would assist them in managing their respiratory disease, and 55.5% agreed it would help reduce hospital admissions. Additionally, 55.7% agreed that DHT would empower them to take control of their chronic disease. There was no noted statistical difference between perspectives of males and females or those from urban and rural locations.

These findings have the potential to have a massive impact on the healthcare system. The growth of the ageing population in the Republic of Ireland has accelerated compared to other EU countries. In 2019, the estimated population of individuals greater than 65 was 696,300 (14% of total population), estimated to reach 1.6 million by 2051 [36]. The number of patients waiting for a Respiratory Consultant appointment is estimated to be 19,200 [37]. The use of DHT has the potential to support self-management, reduce the need for hospital admission, and create awareness of symptoms prompting early medical advice.

Engagement with Healthcare Professionals

Participants were asked if they felt that they get more value from face-to-face appointments with HCPs, if they felt DHT would reduce the number of face-to-face clinics required, if they would prefer a reduction in clinic appointments, and if they felt DHT would give them more contact with their HCPs. The majority (70.3%) strongly agreed or agreed that they get more value from face-to-face clinics. When asked if DHT would reduce the amount of clinic appointments required, 60% strongly agreed or agreed.Interestingly, 49.9% strongly agreed or agreed that they would prefer a reduction in appointments, with 23.8% disagreeing. This cohort appears to value face-to-face appointments, in line with findings from the Netherlands [25].

There is a lack of clarity around the rationale; one reason may be an overall lack of awareness of DHT. Finally, when asked if DHT would increase overall contact with their HCP, 49.9% strongly agreed or agreed.There are no differences in perspectives between males and females or those from different geographical regions regarding value on face-to-face clinics and contact with HCPs. However, there is a noted statistical difference (p=0.017) among those from urban and rural locations on their preference for a reduction in face-to-face clinics.

Design and Ability to Provide Data Feedback

Nadal et al. [13] explain that our perceptions of technology evolve over time, and people may take up a new device only to discontinue its use a week later. User acceptance is frequently overlooked at the design phase, even though it is essential that the design accounts for the users' experience from pre-acceptability to sustained acceptability [13].

This study aimed to address this gap by exploring preferences regarding technology design and capabilities. Results showed that 44.6% agreed that technology size would influence their decision, 52.1% agreed visual acuity was important, and an overwhelming 88.1% agreed they would prefer the device to have the ability to feedback their health data. It is clear that design and functionality are important contributing factors to the pre-acceptability of DHT.

Statistically, there are differences between the variables evaluated. While males and females share similar perspectives, there appears to be a statistical difference between urban and rural areas regarding design features (p=0.018) and the ability to provide health information feedback (p=0.016). It is imperative this difference is noted and taken into consideration during the design phase of DHT.

Implications for Policy

There is an urgency both at National and International level to develop policy documents in retrospect of the introduction of DHT. Findings from this study will advance Digital Health Strategies in Ireland and Europe. While digital health policies are in their infancy, successive National policies have used limited retrospective enquiry from patient perspective. From an overarching system or national policy perspective, this use of bottom-up patient perspective will provide critical insight into understanding and eventual compliance with important new policies.Indicative outputs from this study indicate the following factors may be considered to inform key performance indicators: reliability, ease of use, numbers of patients supported expressing satisfaction, reduction in negative health outcomes, numbers up-taking new technology, cost savings to healthcare, adoption by healthcare service providers transregionally and internationally, policies informed, and adoption into education and training. To date there are no KPIs identified for the measurement of DHT, ultimately there are no measurements of risk.

Implications for Future Research

This study highlights multiple areas requiring future research. Firstly, there is an apparent difference in perspectives of people from urban and rural geographical locations. There is a lack of clarity on the reasoning behind these differences, which needs further investigation. It is imperative that technology design is founded on research findings indicative of what service users want. There will be potential exclusion of cohorts if these differences are not addressed. Risk measurement and risk assessment are to the fore of any healthcare intervention; however, there is a heightened awareness when referencing DHT. None of the studies in the scoping review referred to risk. There were no quantifiable measures of risk identified nor explanation of protective strategies embedded in the study protocols. There is an urgency both at National and International level to develop such documents.

Finally, to date, there is no evidence of standardisation of digital health technology. In healthcare, the introduction of a new medication is processed via a rigorous protocol supported by research data. There is a significant lack of policy and protocol to standardise the use of DHT in Ireland at present. This is most definitely an area in need of urgent investigation.

Conclusion

This study is novel and the first to explore the pre-acceptability of digital health technology in the ageing respiratory patient. This study shows that there is overall a lack of awareness of digital health technology and as a result poor trust. It is evident that respiratory patients are indeed very open minded and willing to embrace the use of DHT. A majority felt that they have the skills to engage and seemed open minded. There were some differences noted in the perspectives of those from urban and rural geographical locations, and the rationale for this is somewhat unclear.The results indicate strong viewpoints of service users' preferences for what they would like DHT to look like and what functionality they would like it to have. There is a positive role for DHT in the management of chronic disease, and participants do believe its use has merit pending the design. Service users would also need encouragement that DHT is not intended to replace the HCP but to complement a service. The skills required to use the technology and the overall security of the data were less of a concern than anticipated for this cohort.It is hoped that the findings of this study will contribute to policy and KPI development for digital health technology on a national and international level. If consideration is given to the factors that contribute to the pre-acceptability of DHT, then future technology and implementation into practice will be based on a foundation of fact, not assumption..
