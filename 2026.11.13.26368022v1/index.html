Power Analysis Check

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. Full text

Abstract

Spatial epidemic models estimate disease transmission counts between populations using human mobility data, but location-to-location transmission is rarely observed directly. To address this gap, we develop mutation-counting methods to infer transmission structure from viral genetic variation measured in wastewater. In epidemic simulations with known transmission networks, our primary estimator robustly recovers spatial structure under realistic mutation-detection limits. Applied to spatiotemporal SARS-CoV-2 wastewater data from Austria, the method reveals strong seasonal reorganization, with inferred transmission becoming more geographically localized in winter and more dispersed in summer. We further identify locations with systematically higher or lower connectivity than expected from commuting, with temporal changes coinciding with regional restrictions, reopening and tourism. We then test whether such inference remains possible across national borders, recovering coherent spatial structure and temporal variation in connectivity between Austria, Switzerland and Liechtenstein. These results establish wastewater sequence data as a promising complement to mobility data for monitoring spatial transmission and detecting changes in epidemic spread associated with seasonality and interventions.

Introduction

Network epidemic models provide a natural framework for representing the spatial spread of infectious diseases, with mobility, commuting and contact data commonly used to define connections between locations [1, 2, 3, 4]. However, such data capture opportunities for movement and interaction rather than the transmission events realized during an epidemic. The network of realized transmissions may therefore differ substantially from that implied by mobility and contact data, as transmission also depends on the nature and intensity of interactions, the infection and immunity status of individuals, and stochastic infection events [5].

Since location-to-location transmission is rarely observed directly, these differences remain difficult to quantify empirically [6]. Here, we show how wastewater sequence data can be used to estimate spatial transmission structure from the genetic variation of a pathogen.

A growing body of research has used genomic data to estimate spatial transmission patterns. Phylodynamic approaches reconstruct phylogenetic relationships between sampled genomes and use geographic annotations to infer migration or transmission between populations [7, 8, 9]. More recently, direct methods have been developed to infer transmission patterns without phylogenetic reconstruction. Spatial transmission between populations has been inferred from identical or closely related clinical consensus sequences [10, 11], while machine-learning approaches have been used to identify the spatial scales of interactions associated with viral genetic structure [12]. At the individual level, clinical sequences have been combined with observed or modelled contact-network information to reconstruct transmission structure [13, 14]. Genetic variation has also been used to identify superspreading events and infer subsequent changes in epidemic behaviour that can alter spatial transmission patterns [15]. However, these approaches generally rely on individual-level genomes obtained through clinical surveillance, which can introduce sampling biases arising from factors such as socioeconomic inequalities in testing uptake and geographic disparities in sequencing capacity [16, 17].

Wastewater surveillance offers a complementary population-level approach that can mitigate sampling biases associated with clinical testing by providing broad coverage at relatively low per-capita costs [18, 19]. Contact patterns between locations give rise to realized transmission through epidemic spread, which is reflected in pathogen genetic variation. Wastewater sequencing provides an incomplete observation of this variation because of withincatchment mixing, incomplete sampling and detection limits. We infer estimated transmission matrices (ETMs) from these wastewater mutation observations. In synthetic experiments, ETMs can be compared directly with the known realized transmission matrix and underlying contact structure. In empirical data, ETMs are compared with independent mobility and commuting data to identify both broad similarities in spatial transmission structure and departures from mobility-based expectations.

During the COVID-19 pandemic, wastewater monitoring expanded rapidly, with national and regional programs established in many countries to monitor SARS-CoV-2 circulation at the population level [20]. While many surveillance programs initially focused on measuring viral concentrations to track community prevalence, wastewater sequencing was also integrated into several programs to monitor SARS-CoV-2 lineage dynamics over time and across locations [19, 21, 22, 23, 24]. In addition to monitoring circulating lineages, wastewater sequencing can capture viral genetic diversity from multiple infections within a catchment, including those missed by clinical surveillance [19, 22]. This diversity can also provide information about local epidemic activity beyond viral concentration measurements, as demonstrated by its association with community COVID-19 incidence [25].

At the same time, wastewater sequence data pose several challenges [26]. Each sample contains genetic material shed by many infected individuals, making it difficult to determine which mutations occur together on the same viral genome. Low-frequency observations may be affected by amplification, sequencing errors, and read depth, while sampling frequency and quality may vary across locations and time. Methods developed for complete individual-level genomes therefore cannot be applied directly to wastewater sequencing data without additional genome reconstruction steps [27, 28], which introduce considerable uncertainty and computational complexity.

A simpler approach to analyzing wastewater sequence data is to compare mutation profiles without reconstructing linkage between mutations. Related mutation-based approaches have previously been used in clinical genomic epidemiology to estimate epidemiological parameters [29] and infer transmission routes [30].

More recently, mutation-based approaches have been applied to wastewater sequencing data to characterize genetic diversity and spatiotemporal patterns of viral variation [31, 32, 33]. Although these studies demonstrate that mutation profiles capture meaningful temporal and geographic patterns, they do not directly evaluate how accurately mutation-based similarity recovers underlying transmission structure. Furthermore, while genetic similarity has been shown to correlate with geographic proximity [33], its relationship to independent measures of epidemiological connectivity, such as human mobility data, remains underexplored.

To address this gap, we develop interpretable mutation-counting methods that translate spatiotemporal patterns of wastewater mutations into estimated transmission matrices (ETMs, see Fig. 1). We establish using epidemic simulations that these estimated matrices recover spatial transmission structure under realistic 2 . observation limits. We then apply the approach to wastewater surveillance data from Austria, Switzerland and Liechtenstein, revealing seasonal reorganization of spatial transmission patterns in agreement with expectations from mobility data. By focusing on the differences between the estimated matrices and mobility, we detect location-specific deviations coinciding with regional interventions, and temporal variation in crossborder connectivity. Through these results, we demonstrate the untapped potential of wastewater sequence data as a complementary tool for monitoring realized patterns of spatial disease transmission.

Results

Method overview

Wastewater data from Austria, Switzerland and Liechtenstein were harmonized by wastewater treatment plant (WWTP) catchment and sampling week (Methods 1.1). The Austrian dataset, which also included samples from Liechtenstein, comprised 6,534 samples collected between January 2021 and December 2024 from 48 catchments. The Swiss dataset included 1,371 samples collected between May 2021 and August 2024 from 15 catchments. We also developed an end-to-end synthetic validation pipeline to reproduce the main steps of the data generation and observation process (Fig. 1). The pipeline combined spatial contact network generation, an individual-based epidemic and mutation simulation, and a synthetic wastewater observation model with a soft allele-frequency detection limit (Methods 1.3). Both the empirical and the synthetic pipelines produced the same type of output: whether a particular substitution was observed at a given time and location, allowing the same inference methods to be evaluated in simulations and applied to empirical data.

These observations were converted into ETMs, which aim to approximate the true transmission matrix between locations, up to scaling factors. We propose two complementary methods for inferring ETMs, illustrated in Fig. 2a. We call the first approach First-Occurrence Propagation (FOP) counting, as it identifies mutations that are first observed in a single location and records their subsequent appearance at other locations at the next observation time as directed, one-step propagation events. FOP counting can be shown theoretically to be proportional to the true directed transmission counts under idealized transmission and observation models (Supplementary Section G). Its main limitation is that newly arising mutations may initially be carried by only a single infected individual. FOP counting therefore requires highly sensitive mutation detection and becomes less reliable when low-frequency mutations are missed.

We propose Co-occurrence Adjusted for Prevalence (CAP) counting as a more robust alternative to FOP counting. At each time point, it measures the mutations shared by each pair of locations and weights each mutation inversely by the number of locations in which it is observed. Mutations confined to a small number of locations therefore contribute more than widespread mutations, which provide little information about specific connections. CAP counting is a heuristic and does not infer direction, but it uses all informative shared mutations rather than only first-observation events. We evaluated alternative parameterizations of this prevalence weighting and compared both methods with temporally resolved Jaccard similarity as a simpler baseline (Methods 1.2) in synthetic experiments.

Cross-border connectivity

We next extended the ETM analysis to wastewater data from Switzerland and Liechtenstein to examine whether transmission structure could also be inferred across national surveillance systems. Combining independently generated datasets is a more challenging setting than the within-country analyses because differences in sampling density, laboratory procedures and sequencing protocols may introduce systematic differences in the resulting ETM values. To assess whether such cross-dataset misalignments were present, we first analysed the connectivity of the main wastewater treatment plant in Liechtenstein, a smaller country geographically situated between Austria and Switzerland. Liechtenstein has strong commuting links to both countries, while its wastewater data were generated as part of the Austrian surveillance system. This provides a useful comparison, as a dataset-specific effect could make Liechtenstein appear more similar to Austrian catchments, whereas commuting patterns suggest somewhat stronger connectivity to Switzerland: in 2021, 58.9% of cross-border commuters into Liechtenstein resided in Switzerland, compared with 36.9% in Austria [44].

As in the within-Austria analyses, the combined networks retained a clear spatial organization, with more geographically localized connections in winter and more long-distance connections in summer (Fig. 4a-c).

In winter 2021-2022, Liechtenstein appeared particularly strongly connected to neighbouring catchments in Vorarlberg (Fig. 4c,d), and summarizing ETM values by regional pair class showed that Liechtenstein-Vorarlberg connectivity was strongest in this first winter and decreased in subsequent years (Fig. 4e). Although this temporal correspondence does not establish causality, it suggests that temporal changes in cross-border connectivity can remain informative despite systematic differences between the national datasets. Together with the preserved spatial structure, these results demonstrate the potential of ETMs for cross-border transmission analysis, particularly as wastewater surveillance protocols become more standardized across countries.

Discussion

We showed that spatiotemporal mutation observations from pooled wastewater samples can be used to infer estimated transmission matrices that retain information about the spatial structure of an epidemic.

To estimate these matrices we introduced two interpretable mutation-counting approaches, FOP and CAP counting, and evaluated them extensively in simulation analyses. FOP counting uses the sequential appearance of mutations to estimate directed connections and has a direct theoretical relationship to transmission under idealized conditions. CAP counting measures prevalence-adjusted mutation co-occurrence and is symmetric, but recovered the known transmission matrices more reliably under realistic observation limits. We therefore used CAP counting for the main empirical analyses to compare transmission structure inferred from genetic sequences with independent mobility data, examining both broad similarities and location-specific differences.

Several of our results point to similarities between the inferred transmission structure and independent measures of population movement. In Austria, the effective distance-decay exponent varied seasonally and was correlated with temporal Google and Meta mobility indicators. We note that while the correlation of the mobility measures and the distance-decay exponent is reassuring, their comparison is inherently indirect.

A more direct comparison was possible in the United States, where spatiotemporal SafeGraph mobility matrices were available, and the distance-decay exponent of the mobility matrices could also be computed.

Although this analysis used clinical sequences aggregated to resemble wastewater observations, the high correlation between the distance-decay exponents of the two datasets indicates similar temporal variation in their spatial localization. At the international scale, the Austria-Switzerland cross-border connectivity multiplier also varied similarly to bilateral overnight stays, including a marked decrease during winter and spring 2021-2022. These comparisons show that ETMs recover spatial and temporal patterns that are also present in independent mobility data.

Differences between ETMs and mobility networks may be particularly informative, as they can identify locations where routine movement does not translate into transmission as expected. Their interpretation, however, depends on the mobility dataset used for comparison. For Austria, we compared the ETMs with a static post-pandemic commuting matrix, which captures routine work-related mobility but not the temporal changes in movement and behaviour observed during the study period. The patterns involving Vienna, Vorarlberg and Innsbruck coincided with documented changes in restrictions, reopening and seasonal tourism, although these associations do not establish a causal explanation. Spatiotemporal mobility networks would allow finer comparisons and could help distinguish changes in movement from other factors affecting transmission, such as regional immunity, contact behaviour or susceptibility. However, even spatiotemporal mobility datasets have important limitations. Mobile-device and platform data may represent only selected population groups or types of movement, and even detailed movement records do not measure the specific encounters through which infections spread. ETMs and mobility networks should therefore be viewed as complementary: mobility data describe selected opportunities for movement and contact, whereas ETMs summarize the spatial structure reflected in the similarity of pathogen genetic variation.

The population-pooled nature of wastewater surveillance also creates opportunities for cross-border analyses. Wastewater sampling is less dependent than clinical surveillance on individual testing behaviour and access to healthcare, and similar measurements can be collected independently in neighbouring countries.

By combining Austrian and Swiss wastewater data, we constructed a joint ETM whose temporal variation was consistent with bilateral overnight-stay data. Such analyses are nevertheless difficult because national surveillance programs can differ in sampling density, laboratory procedures, sequencing equipment and detection sensitivity. Interlaboratory comparisons have similarly shown that wastewater variant detection depends on adequate sequencing coverage and continued quality control [47]. These differences may have contributed to Liechtenstein appearing more closely connected to Austrian than to Swiss catchments in our analysis. Despite these limitations, the recovery of coherent cross-border structure from independently generated national datasets shows the potential of coordinated international wastewater surveillance. Several additional limitations affect the interpretation of the inferred matrices. CAP counting is symmetric and cannot determine the direction of movement or transmission. Its performance also depends on dense, repeated and sufficiently comparable sequencing across locations and time. Although we assessed sensitivity to missing catchment-time reports (Supplementary Fig. A.3h), repeated introductions from outside the sampled region or a systematic lack of observations from highly connected catchments could still bias inferred connectivity. The simulations also showed that the distance-decay exponent fitted to an ETM was attenuated relative to the exponent of the generating contact matrix. The fitted β eff should therefore be interpreted as a comparative measure of spatial localization rather than as an unbiased estimate of the underlying contact process. Furthermore, broad transmission structure was recovered more reliably than local deviations, with FOP counting sometimes outperforming CAP counting for detecting localized changes. Future estimators could improve CAP counting by incorporating some of the temporal information recovered by FOP counting.

The interpretation of the empirical results has additional limitations: comparisons depend on the mobility data available, and associations with policies or travel activity remain observational. Finally, the current analyses are largely restricted to SARS-CoV-2, for which unusually extensive longitudinal sequencing data are available.

Future work should apply these methods to additional pathogens, geographic settings and intervention periods. In particular, combining harmonized wastewater sequencing with detailed spatiotemporal mobility networks could reveal new biological and public-health insights into when transmission follows standard mobility patterns and when it is altered by interventions, behavioural changes, immunity or administrative borders. Our results therefore support extending coordinated wastewater sequencing within and across countries as a new source of information on spatial epidemic spread, especially when direct observations of transmission are unavailable.

1.1 Wastewater data and preprocessing

SARS-CoV-2 wastewater sequencing data from Austria and Liechtenstein were generated and processed as described in [21]. Raw wastewater sequencing data from Switzerland were generated using a different sequencing protocol [22] and subsequently processed with the same computational pipeline for mutation calling as the Austrian dataset [21]. Briefly, reads were trimmed with BBDUK and overlapping read pairs were corrected with BBMERGE from the BBTools suite (v. 38.90) [48]. Reads were mapped to a combined human GRCh38 and SARS-CoV-2 reference genome (RefSeq: NC 045512.2) using BWA-MEM (v. 0.7.17) [49]. Primer sequences were masked with iVar (v. 1.2.3) [50], and mutations were called using LoFreq (v. 2.1.2) [51], as described previously [21].

Mutations were represented by genomic position, reference allele, and alternative allele. Sample dates were assigned to weekly bins starting on Monday. We retained single-nucleotide substitutions and excluded samples with a low fraction of reads mapping to SARS-CoV-2. If multiple samples were available for the same catchment and week, the sample with the highest fraction of mapped viral reads was retained. An allele-frequency threshold of 0.01 was applied to the Austrian dataset. For the joint Austria-Switzerland analysis, the datasets were harmonized to a common set of columns and concatenated, and an allele-frequency threshold of 0.02 was applied to account for differences in sequencing protocols.

WWTP catchment coordinates and connected populations were taken from the associated metadata.

Geographic distances were calculated as geodesic distances on the WGS84 ellipsoid. Further source-specific preprocessing and the processing of the United States clinical-sequence data are described in the Supplementary Materials.
