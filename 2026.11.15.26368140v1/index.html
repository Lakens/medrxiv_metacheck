How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

The marginal module searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The list of terms is a subset of those listed in a blog post by Matthew Hankins. The module returns all sentences that match terms describing ‘marginally significant’ results.

Some of the terms identified might not be problematic in some contexts, and there are ways to describe ‘marginal significance’ that are not detected by the module.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Background Risk of pregnancy complications is reassessed at each antenatal visit, but whether earlier predictions add information beyond the most recent one is unclear. Classifying every prediction as low or high risk can leave uncertainty unexpressed. Whether reliability per visit holds across visits is also unclear.

Methods This secondary analysis included 9,289 nulliparous women from the nuMoM2b cohort (eight US centres, 2010-13). Logistic regression models predicted gestational diabetes, pre-eclampsia, preterm birth, suspected fetal growth restriction and large for gestational age at 6-13, 16-21 and 22-29 weeks (Visits 1-3). Mondrian conformal prediction reported 'no' (low risk), 'yes' (high risk), or both (review).

To test whether prediction history adds information beyond the Visit 3 prediction, we compared, among women classified as high risk at Visit 3, those first classified as high risk at Visit 2 or 3 (emerging high) with those classified as high risk at all three visits (persistent high), adjusting for Visit 3 predicted probability.

The adjusted proportion with the outcome was higher in the emerging-high than the persistenthigh group, by 6.1 percentage points for large for gestational age (95% CI -0.4 to 12.2) and 3.8 percentage points for suspected fetal growth restriction (-0.9 to 8.1). Other outcomes showed adjusted differences close to zero. Coverage decreased when each woman's three visits were considered together (79.0-85.4%).

Interpretation For fetal growth outcomes, how a woman's predicted risk evolved during pregnancy may add information beyond her Visit 3 prediction. For repeated clinical use, the target coverage level should reflect the acceptable risk of incorrect low-or high-risk classifications over a woman's follow-up and the clinical decisions supported at each visit.

Introduction

Adverse pregnancy outcomes are important causes of maternal and neonatal morbidity and mortality [1] [2] [3] . These outcomes include gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM), pre-eclampsia (PE), preterm birth (PTB), fetal growth restriction (FGR) and large for gestational age (LGA). Assessing the risk of these outcomes is part of routine antenatal care. Antenatal guidelines recommend repeating the assessment at every routine appointment and reviewing the plan of care each time, because a woman's risk of developing a pregnancy complication can change as pregnancy progresses 4, 5 . Later visits provide additional maternal measurements and information about fetal development, allowing risk estimates to be updated 6.

Prediction models estimate the risk of specific adverse pregnancy outcomes at defined gestational ages 7, 8.

A model developed for a later visit can combine information collected at earlier visits with newly available measurements, and dynamic prediction methods can update risk using repeated measurements 9- 12 . Repeated assessment therefore produces a sequence of predictions for each woman. Two women with similar predicted risks later in pregnancy may have different prediction histories: one may have had an elevated risk estimate from early pregnancy, whereas the other may have had lower risk estimates at earlier visits. Because a later model can already incorporate earlier measurements, it remains unclear whether a woman's earlier predictions add information beyond her current predicted risk.

To interpret these repeated predictions, it is also necessary to consider how each prediction is reported.

Prediction models provide estimated probabilities 7, 8 , which are often converted into low-or high-risk classifications using a single cut-off. This forces a classification on every woman, including those whose predicted probabilities fall in a range where women who will and will not develop the complication are difficult to tell apart 13 . Conformal prediction uses these predicted probabilities to report a set of possible answers rather than always a single category 14 . For example, for PE, this prediction set may contain 'no PE', 'PE', or both ('no PE' and 'PE'). A set containing only 'no PE' is a low-risk prediction, and a set containing only 'PE' is a high-risk prediction. A set containing both is labelled review, meaning that conformal prediction did not give a low-or high-risk classification at that visit.

The conformal prediction method uses a separate group of women with known outcomes to decide which answers to include in each prediction set. This step is called calibration. Calibration aims to ensure that, for a specified proportion of women, such as 90%, the prediction set includes the actual outcome, provided that the women being assessed are similar to those used for calibration 14 . This proportion is called coverage. When the complication occurs in only a small proportion of women, coverage across all women may be close to the target even if coverage among women who develop the complication is much lower. To address this, Mondrian conformal prediction applies the coverage target separately to women who develop the complication and to those who do not 15 . Applied at each antenatal visit, Mondrian conformal prediction gives a low-risk, high-risk or review prediction, so that clinicians can follow how a woman's predicted risk changes across pregnancy. The coverage target applies to each visit separately, so it does not establish how often the prediction sets include the actual outcome at every visit for the same woman.

A woman may also receive predictions for several different outcomes at the same visit. Because separate outcome-specific models can use the same clinical measurements, a change in those measurements may shift several predictions in the same or opposite directions. Examining the contributions of these shared predictors may help clinicians understand why predictions for different outcomes change together.

In this study, we developed models to predict five adverse pregnancy outcomes at three antenatal visits (6-13, 16-21 and 22-29 weeks of gestation) and used Mondrian conformal prediction to express each prediction as low risk, high risk or review. Among women classified the same way at Visit 3, we compared the proportion with the complication between groups with different prediction histories, after accounting for the Visit 3 predicted probability. We also examined whether predictions for different outcomes changed together, and which shared predictors contributed to these changes. Finally, we assessed how often the prediction sets included the actual outcome at each visit, and how often they did so at all three visits for the same woman.

Methods

Study population and data source

The Nulliparous Pregnancy Outcomes Study: Monitoring Mothers-to-Be (nuMoM2b) recruited nulliparous women with singleton pregnancies at eight US clinical centres between September 2010 and September 2013 16 . This secondary analysis used data from all 9,289 participants who consented to data sharing. The study included three scheduled antenatal research visits: Visit 1 at 6-13 weeks, Visit 2 at 16-21 weeks and Visit 3 at 22-29 weeks of gestation, followed by a delivery assessment 16 . An overview of the study design is shown in Fig. 1. Participants were enrolled at Visit 1 and followed to delivery; no participants entered the cohort after enrolment. The present analysis was approved by the Eunice Kennedy Shriver National Institute of Child Health and Human Development review board (15 December 2021; request ID 11961) and the Eindhoven University of Technology ethical review board (1 November 2021; reference ERB2021BME4). The study was conducted in accordance with relevant ethical regulations. Baseline characteristics of the cohort are reported in Table 1.

Adverse pregnancy outcomes

Outcome data were abstracted from medical records by trained and certified research staff after delivery 16.

Gestational age, used in the definitions below, was established at enrolment (6-13 weeks) using standardised ultrasonography performed by certified sonographers 16 . GDM was ascertained by oral glucose tolerance testing at 24-28 weeks according to site protocols, using the nuMoM2b diagnostic criteria 17 detailed in Supplementary Method S1. PE comprised mild, severe or superimposed preeclampsia and eclampsia, classified according to nuMoM2b study definitions after detailed chart review, with atypical cases classified by consensus of the principal investigators 16, 17 . PTB was defined as delivery between 20 and 36 completed weeks of gestation, irrespective of indication 16 . FGR was identified using the clinical abstraction variable for suspected fetal growth restriction before delivery (nuMoM2b variable CMAE08) 16 . LGA was defined as birthweight at or above the 90th percentile according to sex-specific, first-born birthweight-for-gestational-age values from the 2017 US reference by Aris et al. 18.

The number of eligible participants varied across outcomes, depending on the data needed to determine each outcome and on outcome-specific exclusions. Outcomes were modelled separately, and the same participant could be included in more than one outcome analysis. GDM required an evaluable pregnancy diabetes outcome, with pre-existing diabetes excluded; PTB required a valid gestational age at delivery;

LGA required gestational age at delivery, birthweight, and fetal sex; and FGR required an available clinical abstraction label. PE status was available for all participants. Visit 3 analyses were restricted to pregnancies with delivery at or after 30+0 weeks, corresponding to the end of the Visit 3 assessment window. For FGR, Visit 3 predictions were excluded for participants whose suspected FGR was recorded on or before the date of their Visit 3 assessment (n = 28); their Visit 1 and Visit 2 predictions were retained. Routine GDM screening and diagnosis at 24-28 weeks overlap the Visit 3 window, so Visit 3 discrimination for GDM does not represent prediction ahead of diagnosis.

Prediction models and conformal prediction

Candidate predictors were derived from variables used in previous nuMoM2b studies 10, 19, 20 and were organised according to their availability at each antenatal visit. Three predictor sets were constructed for Visit 1 (6-13 weeks), Visit 2 (16-21 weeks), and Visit 3 (22-29 weeks), with information from earlier visits retained at subsequent visits. The predictor sets included maternal demographic and clinical characteristics, medical and obstetric history, anthropometric and blood-pressure measurements, lifestyle and dietary variables, psychological measures, routine laboratory measurements, and ultrasound-derived fetal measurements. Variables were excluded if they were not available by the corresponding visit or had more than 20% missing values. The same visit-specific candidate predictor set was used for all five outcomes. Detailed predictor definitions and availability are provided in Supplementary Table S1.

Separate prediction models were developed for each outcome at each antenatal visit using elastic-net logistic regression 21 , with hyperparameters selected within the training data of each iteration. Models were evaluated by participant-level stratified 10-fold cross-validation, with each fold held out in turn so that every participant received a prediction from a model fitted without their data. The same fold assignment was used at Visits 1 and 2, whereas Visit 3 was partitioned separately within the Visit 3 analysis set restricted to deliveries at or after 30+0 weeks. Discrimination, the ability of the predicted probabilities to separate participants with and without the outcome, was estimated from held-out predictions of models fitted to the full training portion of each outer fold. For conformal prediction and the longitudinal analyses, separate models were developed after 20% of the outer training portion had been set aside for calibration. Hyperparameters were selected by fivefold internal cross-validation within the remaining proper training subset, and the model was then fitted to that subset. The calibration set was used to determine the conformal thresholds. These models then produced the held-out predictions used for conformal prediction and the longitudinal analyses.

Missing continuous values were imputed using five-nearest-neighbour imputation (KNN, k = 5). Continuous predictors were scaled using the 5th and 95th percentiles of the training data, with values below the 5th percentile truncated to 0, values above the 95th percentile truncated to 1, and intermediate values linearly scaled between 0 and 1. Missing categorical values were treated as a separate category before one-hot encoding. No feature selection was performed beyond excluding variables with more than 20% missingness. Participants with the outcome formed the positive class, and those without it formed the negative class. To account for the smaller positive class, its weight was set to the square root of the negative-to-positive sample ratio in the training data; the negative-class weight was set to 1. Elastic-net hyperparameters were selected by fivefold internal cross-validation using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) as the selection criterion, with l1_ratio ∈ {0.1, 0.3, 0.5, 0.7, 0.9} and C ∈ {0.001, 0.01, 0.1, 0.5, 1}. Preprocessing parameters and class weights were estimated from the training data used for each model: the full outer training portion for discrimination models and the proper training subset for conformal models. The fitted preprocessing transformations were applied unchanged to the corresponding evaluation data. Model performance was summarised by the AUROC. The area under the precision-recall curve, Brier score, and calibration intercept and slope for the predicted probabilities are reported in Supplementary Table S7.

Conformal prediction was applied separately for each outcome and antenatal visit using predicted probabilities from the corresponding visit-specific model 14 . Conformal calibration was performed separately for participants with and without the outcome, using a Mondrian split-conformal procedure 15.

For a calibration participant with outcome class 𝑦 ∈ {0,1}, the nonconformity score was defined as 𝑠 = 1 -𝑝 ˆ!, where 𝑝 ˆ! denotes the predicted probability assigned to the observed class. For binary outcomes, the resulting prediction set could contain the negative class only {0}, both classes {0,1}, the positive class only {1}, or neither class ∅. These sets were referred to as the low-risk (L), review (R), high-risk (H) and empty (E) prediction states, respectively. For longitudinal analyses, E was grouped with R because neither gave a low-or high-risk classification; the original prediction sets were retained when evaluating coverage. Predictions that were not evaluable at a given visit were not assigned a conformal prediction state.

For the binary outcomes considered here, these states could also be expressed as thresholds on the predicted event probability 𝑝 ˆ: class 0 was included when 𝑝 ˆ≤ 𝑞 " , whereas class 1 was included when 𝑝 ˆ≥ 1 -𝑞 # , where 𝑞 " and 𝑞 # denote the corresponding class-specific conformal thresholds. The two thresholds therefore partitioned the predicted-probability axis into the four prediction-set states defined above. Numerical agreement between the set-based and probability-threshold representations was evaluated at each visit and for complete three-visit trajectories (Supplementary Method S2).

The primary analysis used a 90% confidence level, corresponding to a 90% coverage target, with 80% examined in a sensitivity analysis. Under exchangeability within the Mondrian partitions, the procedure targets finite-sample coverage within each outcome class at each visit 15 . Empirical coverage was evaluated at each visit overall and separately within the two outcome classes. A prediction was counted as covered when the prediction set contained the observed class; empty sets were counted as not covered.

Visits for which no evaluable conformal prediction set was produced were excluded from the visitspecific denominator. Among participants with evaluable prediction sets at all three visits, we calculated simultaneous coverage as the proportion whose observed outcome was included in the set at every visit.

Longitudinal prediction trajectories

For each outcome, the sequence of prediction states across the three visits defined a participant's prediction trajectory, representing her prediction history. A trajectory was included only when an evaluable state was available at all three visits.

Trajectories were grouped according to the Visit 3 state and prediction history (Table 2). Among participants in the high-risk state at Visit 3, persistent high was defined as H-H-H. Emerging high comprised trajectories that first entered the high-risk state at Visit 2 and remained high risk at Visit 3, or first entered the high-risk state at Visit 3. Intermittent high comprised trajectories that were high risk at Visit 1, moved to a review or low-risk state at Visit 2, and returned to high risk at Visit 3. Among participants in the low-risk state at Visit 3, stable low was defined as L-L-L, whereas resolved-to-low comprised trajectories with at least one previous review state but no previous high-risk state. The individual three-visit sequences within each group are reported in Supplementary Table S3.

We compared trajectory groups that shared the same Visit 3 state to examine whether the proportion with the outcome differed according to prediction history. Among participants in the high-risk state at Visit 3, the comparison was persistent high versus emerging high; among those in the low-risk state, stable low versus resolved-to-low. To account for differences in predicted probability within the same Visit 3 state, these contrasts were estimated both before and after accounting for the Visit 3 predicted probability. For each outcome, observed outcome status was modelled using logistic regression with trajectory group and the Visit 3 predicted probability, represented by a natural cubic spline with four degrees of freedom. For each contrast, adjusted risk differences were estimated by g-computation 22 , standardising predicted risks over the combined sample of the two trajectory groups being compared. Confidence intervals were obtained by participant-level bootstrap resampling.

We performed two additional analyses to compare persistent-high and emerging-high participants within a shared range of Visit 3 predicted probability. First, we restricted both groups to the overlap of their respective 5th-95th percentile ranges of Visit 3 predicted probability (common support). We then refitted the spline-adjusted model and re-estimated the risk difference in this restricted sample.

Second, we formed matched pairs containing one participant from each group using nearest-neighbour matching on the logit-transformed Visit 3 predicted probability. Matching was performed without replacement, with a caliper of 0.2 pooled standard deviations. We assessed balance in the matching variable using the standardised mean difference and calculated the risk difference from the proportions with the outcome in the matched groups. Further details are provided in Supplementary Method S3 and Supplementary Fig. S1.

Cross-outcome associations in prediction changes

The preceding analyses followed each outcome separately. We next examined whether predictions for different outcomes changed together within the same participant, and whether such associations could be traced to predictors entering more than one model.

For each pair of outcomes, we compared how their prediction states changed between adjacent visits.

States were ordered as L < R < H, and a move to a higher state was defined as an upward transition. Each outcome was classified as having an upward or non-upward transition, and the association between the two classifications was summarised using an odds ratio. Analyses were restricted to participants with evaluable states for both outcomes at both visits and were performed separately for Visit 1 to Visit 2 and Visit 2 to Visit 3. Complete pairwise upward-transition associations are reported in Supplementary Table S4.

The same question was then examined without discretising predictions into states. For each outcome we calculated the change in held-out predicted probability between adjacent visits, and summarised pairwise associations between these changes using Spearman correlation coefficients.

Where predictions for two outcomes changed together, we examined the contribution of predictors entering both models. We expressed each model's predicted probability on the logit scale. On this scale, the model output is called the linear predictor and equals the intercept plus the sum of individual predictor contributions. A predictor was treated as shared when it entered both outcome-specific fold models for that participant at that visit. The contributions of these predictors were summed to obtain the shared component. The remaining component comprised the intercept and contributions from all other predictors, such that the shared and remaining components summed to the complete linear predictor. For the complete linear predictor and each component, we calculated the change between adjacent visits. We then correlated these changes between the two outcome models across participants using Spearman correlation coefficients.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were summarised as counts and proportions. Differences between trajectory groups in the proportion with the outcome were reported as absolute risk differences (percentage points) with 95% confidence intervals. For comparisons accounting for the Visit 3 predicted probability, adjusted risk differences were estimated by g-computation. Associations between upward transitions across outcomes were summarised using odds ratios and evaluated using two-sided Fisher's exact tests. Associations between changes in predicted probabilities or model components were summarised using Spearman correlation coefficients.

AUROC 95% confidence intervals were estimated from 2,000 participant-level bootstrap resamples of the held-out predictions using percentile intervals. Wilson intervals were used for visit-specific and three-visit simultaneous empirical coverage; the latter was evaluated among participants with evaluable prediction sets at all three visits. The three-visit independence benchmark was calculated for each outcome as the product of the three observed visit-specific marginal coverage rates among participants evaluable at all three visits. The Bonferroni lower-bound reference was calculated as 1 -3(1 -𝑐), where c was the nominal single-visit confidence level. Confidence intervals for adjusted trajectory-group risk differences and the common-support-restricted analysis used 2,000 participant-level bootstrap resamples, with heldout predicted probabilities and trajectory classifications kept fixed. The adjustment model was refitted within each resample. The matching analysis used 500 bootstrap resamples of the original matched pairs.

Discussion

Among women classified as high risk at Visit 3, we compared those first classified as high risk at Visit 2 or 3 (emerging high) with those classified as high risk at all three visits (persistent high). After accounting for Visit 3 predicted probability, the estimated proportion with the outcome was higher in the emerginghigh group for both LGA and FGR. The estimated differences were 6.1 percentage points (95% CI -0.4 to 12.2) for LGA and 3.8 (-0.9 to 8.1) for FGR. For GDM, PE and PTB, the adjusted point estimates were close to zero. Changes in predictions were also associated across outcomes, with the strongest associations reflected mainly in shared predictor contributions. Prediction sets included the actual outcome in close to 90% of women at each visit, as targeted. Among women with predictions at all three visits, the set included the actual outcome at every visit in 79.0-85.4%, depending on the outcome.

At the 90% coverage target, conformal prediction separated low-and high-risk predictions from review predictions. A review state meant that conformal prediction did not give a low-or high-risk classification at the chosen coverage target. The boundaries of this state were set by conformal calibration rather than by clinical thresholds for intermediate risk. In our study, the gains in discrimination for FGR and LGA coincided with fewer review predictions and more low-risk predictions, while empirical coverage remained close to the target. For antenatal assessment, the practical question is how to present this review state alongside other clinical information and whether and when it should prompt reassessment.

Discrimination improved most for FGR and LGA as measurements accumulated across visits, and less for GDM, PE and PTB. Studies of individual markers help put the smaller PTB and PE gains in context. In nuMoM2b, serial cervical length and fetal fibronectin measurements had limited predictive accuracy for spontaneous PTB 24 . Uterine artery Doppler measurements were associated with PE but had limited predictive performance in a separate low-risk nulliparous cohort 25.

In our study, women classified the same way at Visit 3 differed in their earlier predictions. For fetal growth, the adjusted comparisons suggest that prediction history may help interpret the Visit 3 predicted probability. The LGA estimate changed little after restricting the comparison to the shared probability range or matching on Visit 3 predicted probability. The two fetal growth outcomes also differed in ascertainment:

LGA was determined from birthweight, whereas FGR reflected a clinical suspicion recorded during pregnancy, so the FGR comparison also depends on when that suspicion was recorded.

For GDM, PE and PTB, adjustment for Visit 3 predicted probability brought the estimated history-group differences close to zero. Adjusted differences between stable-low and resolved-to-low histories were also close to zero. These findings raise the question of what information distinguished emerging-high from persistent-high histories for fetal growth.

For fetal growth, change over time may provide information beyond current size 26 . In the POP study, the association between low estimated fetal weight and neonatal morbidity varied with abdominal circumference growth velocity 27 . In our study, similar Visit 3 predicted probabilities may have arisen from different combinations of early maternal characteristics and later fetal measurements. Earlier predictions may help distinguish these combinations. A next step would be to test whether explicitly modelling fetal growth over time changes the adjusted history-group differences.

Information shared across models also helped explain how predictions for different outcomes changed together. For the most strongly associated outcome pairs, correlations between changes in shared predictor contributions had the same direction as correlations between changes in the complete linear predictor, whereas correlations between changes in the remaining contributions were close to zero. A shared predictor's contribution depends on both its value and its coefficient in each visit-specific model.

Shared predictors can therefore contribute to changes in the same or opposite directions across outcomes, as observed for PTB and FGR, and for LGA and FGR. Presenting related predictions alongside their shared predictor contributions could help clinicians understand why several risk estimates change together.

Across the complete visit sequence, 15-21% of women with prediction sets at all three visits had at least one visit at which the set did not include the actual outcome, compared with 26-28% expected if coverage at the three visits had been independent. Conformal calibration at each visit targets the prespecified coverage for that visit; coverage across all three visits also depends on whether prediction sets exclude the actual outcome repeatedly in the same women. Repeated use of earlier measurements in later models may help explain why the same women were affected at several visits. Conformal methods developed for timeseries prediction address related coverage problems 28 . Temporal Quantile Adjustment addresses crosssectional and longitudinal coverage in time-series regression 29 . CopulaCPTS addresses coverage for forecasts of multiple future responses from a common origin, using dependence among forecast errors 30.

In our study, successive models predicted the same final binary outcome as new information became available. The next methodological question is how to target coverage across this visit sequence while keeping prediction sets informative.

The clinical role of a prediction depends on when it is made. Early preventive interventions, as in the ASPRE trial, require early risk identification 31 ; later predictions may inform surveillance and assessment.

The handling of a review state would therefore need to reflect the decisions available at that visit. For fetal growth outcomes, a high-risk prediction at Visit 3 could be displayed alongside earlier predictions, including the visit at which the woman was first classified as high risk. Clinical evaluation could then examine whether these histories should influence the choice or timing of reassessment. Once a complication has been diagnosed, predicting its first occurrence is no longer the relevant task, although other complications may still require risk assessment.

Before these prediction states are used in another population, their coverage and the proportions of lowrisk, review and high-risk predictions would need to be evaluated there. In an external validation of a conformal sepsis model, discrimination declined and prediction sets containing more than one possible answer became more frequent 32 . Independent, labelled calibration data representative of the local population can support recalibration to a locally specified coverage target.

When several outcomes are assessed together, their predictions may lead to one shared assessment or to separate clinical actions. Changes in the same measurements can raise two risk estimates that call for different interventions. If predictions are used to generate flags, the number of flags, the number of women flagged and the number of resulting clinical actions describe different aspects of workload. In a study of clinical decision support, more alerts, particularly repeated alerts, were associated with lower acceptance rates 33 . Combining predictions into clinical actions would therefore require considering outcome priorities, the timing of available actions and local resources.

The study used one cohort of nulliparous women with singleton pregnancies and had no independent external validation. Model performance, history-group differences and coverage remain to be evaluated in other populations, and the history comparisons may also depend on the predictors and model structure used. Outcome definitions and timing also affect interpretation. For all FGR cases included at Visit 3, suspicion was recorded after the Visit 3 assessment; because the database did not record the examinations on which each suspicion was based, the extent of any overlap with information already available at Visit 3 could not be determined. Routine GDM diagnosis overlapped Visit 3, and some PE diagnoses may also have been made by that visit. Because deliveries before 30+0 weeks were excluded, Visit 3 results, including PTB results and comparisons of prediction histories, apply to pregnancies continuing to at least that gestational age. We did not evaluate how using these predictions changed clinical decisions, resource use or patient outcomes.

Among women classified as high risk at Visit 3, the adjusted proportion with the outcome was higher in the emerging-high group than in the persistent-high group for both LGA and FGR. The contribution analysis traced linked changes across outcomes to shared model inputs. The coverage results show that repeated predictions need to be evaluated across the complete visit sequence as well as at each visit. This approach can inform other longitudinal, multi-outcome classification studies, with contribution analysis adapted to the model structure. Shared predictor contributions help explain linked changes across outcomes

The preceding analyses considered each outcome separately, although predictions for several outcomes were generated for the same participants at each visit. We therefore examined whether prediction changes between consecutive visits were associated across outcomes. We first considered whether a participant's prediction state moved to a higher level, from low risk to review or high risk, or from review to high risk (Fig. 4a). Between Visits 1 and 2, upward changes in PTB and FGR tended to occur together (OR, 3.26), whereas upward changes in LGA were less likely to coincide with those in FGR (OR, 0.08) or PTB (OR, 0.27). Between Visits 2 and 3, the association remained positive for PTB and FGR (OR, 1.88) and negative for LGA and FGR (OR, 0.05; Supplementary Table S4). No participant had both LGA and FGR.

For each participant and outcome, we next calculated the change in predicted probability between consecutive visits and examined whether these changes were correlated between pairs of outcomes across participants (Fig. 4b). Between Visits 1 and 2, changes in predicted PTB and FGR risk were positively correlated (Spearman ρ = 0.50): participants whose predicted PTB risk increased tended also to have an increase in predicted FGR risk. Changes in predicted LGA and FGR risk were negatively correlated (ρ = -0.66), as were changes in PTB and LGA (ρ = -0.45). Between Visits 2 and 3, the corresponding correlations were 0.23, -0.61 and -0.18, respectively. The association between LGA and FGR was similar across both intervals, whereas those involving PTB were weaker later in gestation (Supplementary Table S5).

We next examined which model components contributed to these cross-outcome patterns. On the logit scale, each model's linear predictor is the sum of an intercept and individual predictor contributions. For each outcome pair, we separated contributions from predictors shared by both models from a remainder containing the intercept and other predictors, then correlated changes in each component between the two outcome models (Fig. 4c; Supplementary Table S6). For LGA and FGR between Visits 1 and 2, changes in the complete linear predictor were correlated at -0.73, compared with -0.63 for the shared-predictor component and -0.02 for the remaining component. A similar pattern was observed for PTB and FGR (0.53 overall, 0.41 for the shared component and -0.02 for the remainder). For these pairs, changes in the shared-predictor component were correlated in the same direction and with a similar pattern as the complete linear predictor, whereas correlations for the remaining component were close to zero. Because a shared predictor could enter two models with coefficients of opposite sign, shared information could contribute to either concordant or discordant prediction changes.
