Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Full text

Abstract

Mild cognitive impairment (MCI) and mild behavioral impairment (MBI) are overlapping prodromal states associated with dementia risk. Circulating apolipoproteins (Apos) may be linked to cognitive and behavioral changes, but their relationships remain unclear.

Examine plasma concentrations of multiple Apos in prodromal dementia.

This cross-sectional study included 148 Thai participants aged 60-75 years: 56 cognitively normal controls, 47 with MCI without MBI, and 45 with MBI with or without MCI.

We measured ApoA1, ApoA2, ApoB, ApoC1, ApoC3, ApoD, ApoE, ApoH, and ApoJ, five lipids, and APOE genotype, and computed an atherogenic Apo composite (ApoB-ApoC1-ApoC3). Primary analyses used multivariable linear regression of quantitative cognitive and behavioral outcomes. Group comparisons were secondary and served to contextualize the dimensional regression findings.

Across Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) models, 20.7-28.2% of variance was explained by combinations of ApoH, ApoC3, APOE ε4 carrier status, age, and male sex (inverse associations), and free cholesterol and education (positive associations). The atherogenic composite was inversely associated with MoCA total, memory index, and delayed recall scores, . and with Consortium to Establish a Registry for Alzheimer's Disease (CERAD) verbal fluency and word list recall. No significant predictors emerged for Neuropsychiatric Inventory (NPI) scores or NPI caregiver distress. All nine Apos differed significantly between groups, with levels highest in MCI, intermediate in MBI, and lowest in controls.

Circulating Apo profiles differed across controls, MCI, and MBI groups and were more consistently associated with cognitive than behavioral outcomes. ApoH and the atherogenic Apo composite warrant further investigation as candidate biomarkers of early cognitive dysfunction.

Introduction

Global aging brings increased risk, prevalence, and public-health burden of dementia, a syndrome characterized by progressive cognitive decline and behavioral changes. As neurodegeneration involves complex interactions across multiple biological systems, current interventions cannot reliably reverse or halt the underlying pathology once dementia has manifested. 1 With this limitation, early diagnosis and prevention are key in dementia research.

Newer work has shifted towards enhancing the detection of prodromal stages of dementia, particularly mild cognitive impairment (MCI) and mild behavioral impairment (MBI). 2, 3 MCI describes an objective phase of cognitive decline that does not extensively compromise independent functioning, 4 whilst MBI describes the later-life emergence of persistent neuropsychiatric symptoms that often accompany or precede any cognitive symptoms. 5 Both syndromes may be associated with an elevated risk of subsequent dementia, with an annual conversion rate of approximately 10-20% from MCI 6, 7 and 14.7% from MBI, 8 although progression estimates vary.

Vascular risk factors are among the most well-established clinical risk factors for the diagnosis and pathogenesis of all-cause dementia. [9] [10] [11] [12] [13] [14] Modifiable vascular risk factors such as diabetes and hypertension contribute to neurodegeneration, whilst vascular ageing interferes with neuronal survival. 14 Although certain treatments or lifestyle modifications seem to effectively reduce dementia risk, [15] [16] [17] [18] [19] their success is contingent on factors such as age, and the interplay of other risk factors. An estimated 45% of dementia cases may be attributed to potentially modifiable risk factors, 20, 21 highlighting the importance of early biomarker detection during midlife.

Among the biomarkers, circulating apolipoproteins (Apos) are plausible candidate markers of dementia-related risk and pathology. Apos are a group of specialized, multifunctional amphipathic proteins responsible for the stabilization and regulated transport of lipoproteins by binding to lipid surfaces, with different Apo species playing distinct biological roles. 22, 23 Their relevance to dementia is supported by reported associations with neurodegeneration and cognitive function. [24] [25] [26] [27] To date, previous work has explored the role of circulating Apos in cognitive impairment; however, their relationship with MBI remains largely unexplored.

Circulating ApoE has been the most extensively studied species in dementia, and its concentrations may provide information beyond APOE genotype. In a population study of over 76,000 individuals, lower plasma ApoE was associated with AD and all-cause dementia independent of APOE genotype and cardiovascular risk factors. 28 Neuropathological work on amygdalae specimens from dementia cases has also demonstrated ApoE enrichment and colocalization with complement component C4 in amyloid plaques, including among individuals without APOE ε4. 29 Longitudinally, lower plasma ApoE in MCI was associated with subsequent progression to AD, poorer cognition, and adverse amyloid, tau, and neurofilament light chain profiles. 30 Research in other Apo species is more heterogeneous and varies by clinical population and biospecimen. Two meta-analyses reported lower circulating ApoA1 levels in AD than in controls, 27, 31 whereas an inverse relationship between ApoA1 and cognition was observed in a type 2 diabetes cohort with cognitive impairment. 32 Higher CSF ApoB has been associated with early tau dysregulation and subsequent visuospatial decline, 33 while higher plasma ApoC3 and ApoJ have been linked to higher baseline cognitive function or lower dementia risk in longitudinal studies. 25, 34 Multi-Apo profiling has also identified lower ApoA1, ApoA2, and ApoH, but higher ApoE, ApoJ, and ApoB/ApoA1 ratios in MCI individuals that seemed to correspond with cognitive performance, dementia risk, and brain structure changes. 26 Despite research interests in Apos and cognitive impairment, the available body of knowledge remains inconsistent in establishing circulating Apos as reliable biomarkers of dementia risk. 11 Direct evidence concerning circulating Apo profiles in MBI remains limited, as previous studies have focused primarily on APOE genotype. 35, 36 The present study therefore aimed to examine plasma concentrations of multiple Apos and conventional lipid measures in individuals with MCI without MBI and individuals with MBI with or without co-occurring MCI, compared to controls. By analyzing these profiles across participant groups, we sought to clarify whether specific Apo species or composites, along with other demographic factors, are associated with early cognitive or behavioral impairment that could potentially serve as additional candidate correlates of pre-dementia risk.

Methods

Participants

This cross-sectional study recruited 148 older adults aged 60-75 years from King Chulalongkorn Memorial Hospital (KCMH), the Thai Red Cross Society, and community-based senior groups in Thailand. The sample comprised 56 cognitively normal healthy controls who exhibited cognitive and behavioral performance within the normative range, 47 participants with MCI without MBI, and 45 individuals with MBI with (25) or without (20) co-occurring MCI.

Controls were included in the study if they did not meet the study criteria for MCI or Exclusion criteria included dementia; major neurological disorders other than the conditions under study, including multiple sclerosis and stroke; lifetime and current major depressive, bipolar, psychotic, or substance-use disorders; neurodevelopmental disorders; diagnosis of medical disorders such as chronic kidney disease, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, inflammatory bowel disease, rheumatoid arthritis, and thyroid disorders; abnormal liver and kidney blood tests; and cognitive training within the previous three months.

Data collection

Participant screening. Potential participants were first informed about the study scope and procedures. Those who remained interested then completed screening assessments, namely the TMSE, CDR-SB, IQCODE, and MBI-C. For individuals with existing hospital medical records, their physicians were contacted to discuss and confirm their neuropsychiatric diagnoses. For those without prior neuropsychiatric examination or hospital records, an appointment with an experienced neuropsychiatrist was arranged to confirm eligibility before enrollment. Data collection procedure. Data were collected between June 2024 and October 2025 at KCMH over one or two visits, depending on participant availability and ability. After written consent, sociodemographic, medical, medication, and supplement histories were recorded. Then, 40 mL of fasting (8 hours) blood was collected between 7 and 8 a.m., and participants were given one hour to rest. Participants then returned for neuropsychological and behavioral testing.

Participants could take breaks between each assessment as needed and were compensated after protocol completion. Informants were contacted and interviewed within one week of the participant visit.

Blood. Following collection, fasting blood samples were immediately transported to the hospital laboratory for processing. Samples were centrifuged and separated into whole blood, serum, and plasma fractions. Then, some samples were sent to the relevant laboratories for immediate analysis, whilst the rest were stored at -80 o C until thawed for further analysis. High-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), total cholesterol, and triglyceride concentrations were measured in the hospital laboratory using the Alinity C system (Abbott Laboratories, USA; Otawara-Shi, Tochigi-Ken, Japan) with enzymatic, accelerator-selective detergent, and glycerol phosphate oxidase methods, respectively. The coefficients of variation (CV) were 2.6% for HDL-C, 2.3% for total cholesterol, and 2.3% for triglycerides. Low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) was estimated using the Friedewald equation. Plasma free cholesterol levels were determined using the Free Cholesterol Assay Kit (Catalog #E-BC-K004-M, Elabscience, Houston, TX, USA) according to the manufacturer's protocol.

Measures

Based on prespecified biological classifications, ApoA1, ApoE, and ApoJ were grouped as the reputedly protective composite because of their reported antioxidant and antiinflammatory functions. [37] [38] [39] ApoB, ApoC1, and ApoC3 were grouped as the reputedly atherogenic Apo composite due to their reported proatherogenic or inflammatory associations. 40- 42 The remaining species, ApoA2, ApoD, and ApoH, were deemed to have more contextdependent biological roles and were considered separately. The ApoB-ApoA1, atherogenicprotective Apo, LDL-C-HDL-C, and triglyceride-HDL-C indices were calculated as differences between the standardized Z-scores of their respective components. APOE genotyping. Genomic DNA was extracted from peripheral blood samples using the TIANamp Genomic DNA Kit (TIANGEN Biotech, Beijing, China), according to the manufacturer's instructions. DNA concentration and purity were assessed before downstream analysis. The target region of the APOE gene was amplified by polymerase chain reaction (PCR), and the PCR products were examined by agarose gel electrophoresis. Participants were subsequently classified as either having 'No ε4 allele' or having 'At least one ε4 allele' based on the identified sequence variants.

Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA).

The MoCA is a highly sensitive, widely validated tool for detecting MCI and early AD. 43 The test comprises 30 total points that determine the individual's performance across eight cognitive domains: visuospatial function, executive function, memory, naming, language, attention, abstraction, and orientation. In addition to the total score, MoCA subdomain scores can help further differentiate individuals into distinct cognitive syndromes, 44 whilst the Memory Index Score (MoCA-MIS), a recall score for the memory items, may help further discriminate between normal cognition and MCI 45 and indicate the extent of disease progression. In this study, we used the validated Thai translation of the MoCA (MoCA-Thai). 46

Neuropsychiatric Inventory (NPI).

Neuropsychiatric symptoms of dementia were comprehensively assessed using the NPI, a validated informant-based interview. 47 The tool assesses ten behavioral and two neurovegetative areas: delusions, hallucinations, agitation/aggression, depression/dysphoria, anxiety, elation/euphoria, apathy/indifference, disinhibition, irritability/lability, aberrant motor behavior, sleep and nighttime behavior disorders, and appetite and eating disorders. For each domain, informants first indicate whether symptoms are present. For any present symptom, frequency (1-4), severity (1-3), and associated caregiver distress (0-5) levels are rated. Frequency and severity scores are then multiplied and summed to produce a total NPI score, whilst caregiver distress scores are summed to give a total caregiver distress score.

The Consortium to Establish a Registry for Alzheimer's Disease (CERAD) neuropsychological battery. The CERAD neuropsychological battery comprises standardized cognitive tests designed to assess cognitive function and supports the evaluation of AD and dementia progression. [48] [49] [50] Our study focused on three subtests: verbal fluency (VF), word list learning (WLL), and word list recall (WLR). In the VF task, participants have to name as many animals as possible in one minute. For the WLL, participants are presented with a list of words to learn across multiple trials, and after a short delay of approximately 5 minutes, are asked to recall the word list for the WLR task.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 32 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and graphs were generated using MATLAB version R2026b (MathWorks, Natick, MA, USA). Continuous variables were examined for normality using histograms, Q-Q plots, and the Shapiro-Wilk test, and are presented as mean (± SD) or median (interquartile range), as appropriate. Categorical variables are presented as number (percentage). Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. There were no missing data for the variables included in the primary analyses. The study population was selected to encompass a broad spectrum of cognitive and behavioral functioning, including cognitively normal individuals and participants meeting conventional criteria for MCI or MBI. Rather than treating these diagnostic categories as the principal outcomes, we focused on continuous measures of the cognitive and behavioral phenome. 51, 52 Accordingly, multivariable linear regression was the primary analytical approach, while comparisons between diagnostic groups served a secondary purpose, providing clinical context and facilitating comparisons with existing literature.

For each analyte, Z-scores were calculated using SPSS based on the entire sample.

Composite values were calculated as the sum of the corresponding component Z-scores, with each component equally weighted. Z-score standardization eliminated differences in measurement units and concentration scales, ensuring each Apo or lipid contributed equally in terms of standardized variability and preventing analytes with larger absolute concentrations or variances from disproportionately influencing the composite indices. Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed on the nine Apos to determine whether a common underlying dimension could be extracted. Sampling adequacy and the suitability of the correlation matrix for PCA were assessed using the Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) measure and Bartlett's test of sphericity, respectively. The first principal component (PC1) was considered acceptable when the KMO value exceeded 0.70, Bartlett's test was significant (p < .05), PC1 explained more than 50% of the total variance, and all nine Apos showed loadings greater than .70. One-way Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) and Kruskal-Wallis tests were used to compare continuous variables among groups, while pairwise comparisons were examined with Bonferroni-adjusted contrasts.

When the homogeneity-of-variance assumption was not met, Welch's ANOVA with Games-Howell post hoc comparisons were used. Categorical variables were compared using Pearson's chi-square test, while individual NPI symptom frequencies were compared using two-sided Fisher-Freeman-Halton exact tests.

Apo and lipid concentration differences between groups were examined using general linear models adjusted for age, sex, BMI, and metabolic-syndrome status. Estimated marginal means, 95% confidence intervals, and omnibus tests were reported. To account for multiple testing across related biomarker outcomes, Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate (BH-FDR) p-value correction was applied separately within four prespecified families: nine individual apolipoproteins, five individual lipid measures, four derived Apo measures, and two derived lipid measures. As a sensitivity analysis, MBI participants were stratified according to the presence or absence of co-occurring MCI. Apo levels were compared between MBI-only and MBI with co-occurring MCI participants using general linear models with age and BMI as covariates and sex and metabolic-syndrome status as fixed factors. BH-FDR correction was applied across the nine apolipoprotein comparisons. Multivariable linear regression was used to examine associations with cognitive and global behavioral outcomes. For each outcome, one model evaluated individual Apo species together with lipid, demographic, and APOE variables, and an alternative model replaced individual Apo candidates with the prespecified composites (protective and atherogenic composites) together with the remaining candidate predictors.

The multivariable regression analyses constituted the primary outcome analyses, focusing on quantitative cognitive and behavioral measures rather than categorical diagnoses. Comparison among diagnostic groups was presented as supplementary descriptive analyses to facilitate clinical interpretation, particularly for readers less familiar with dimensional regression approaches. Standardized regression coefficients (β), t-statistics, p-values, and model-fit statistics were reported. Assumptions of the multivariable regression models were evaluated using regression diagnostics. Multicollinearity was assessed using tolerance and variance inflation factor (VIF) values. Normality of residuals was examined using residual histograms and normal probability (P-P) plots of standardized residuals, while homoscedasticity was visually assessed using plots of standardized residuals against standardized predicted values. An a priori power analysis for multivariable linear regression, assuming seven predictors, an anticipated explained variance of 15% (R² = .15), and α = .05, indicated that a minimum of 89 participants were required to achieve 80% power. Given the final sample of 148 participants, the estimated achieved post-hoc power was approximately 97.3%.

In the multivariable models, ApoH, ApoC3, and the atherogenic Apo composite were inversely associated with selected cognitive outcomes, whilst no candidate predictors were retained for the global neuropsychiatric symptom or caregiver distress measures.

These findings indicate that circulating Apo profiles may be more consistently associated with cognitive performance than with global behavioral burden, though this does not exclude possible associations with individual symptoms or changes over time.

Distinct and overlapping cognitive and behavioral phenotypes in MCI and MBI

Although the primary outcome variables in this study were continuous measures of cognitive and behavioral functioning, rather than the categorical diagnoses of MCI and MBI, it is notable that both MCI and MBI displayed distinct, but partially overlapping clinical profiles.

MCI was characterized predominantly by poorer cognitive performance, particularly in learning and delayed recall, concurrently with certain behavioral symptoms, whereas MBI was characterized by greater neuropsychiatric burden and caregiver distress, alongside accompanying cognitive deficits. However, unlike cognitive deficits that may more directly reflect underlying cognitive dysfunction, behavioral symptoms may also be influenced by psychosocial circumstances and environmental factors, 53 and in some cases, may be more closely related to these contextual influences than to the degree of cognitive impairment. Although the syndromes can coexist, 54 they are generally considered distinguishable but related manifestations of early clinical impairment, 3, 55, 56 reflecting different dimensions of cognitive and behavioral change in older adults. This distinction may also be reflected in their underlying biological correlates, as cognitive and behavioral abnormalities may have partly distinct biological associations early in the disease course, 56, 57 with increasing overlap as impairment progresses. 58

Apos and other metabolic correlates of behavioral impairment

We found that MCI and MBI share increased circulating Apo levels, although these alterations were associated with cognitive dysfunction rather than behavioral symptom severity or caregiver distress. Direct evidence relating circulating Apos to MBI is particularly sparse.

Available findings suggest that Apo-behavior associations may vary according to Apo species, symptom domain, and follow-up interval. In a longitudinal study with cognitive impairment, higher ApoA1 was associated with a lower likelihood of apathy, higher ApoA2 was related to higher prevalence of apathy, higher ApoB and ApoC1 predicted subsequent neuropsychiatric symptoms at two years, higher ApoE was related to fewer symptoms at two years, higher ApoH was associated with anxiety, and higher ApoJ was related to higher prevalence of neuropsychiatric symptoms at one year. 59 This heterogeneous pattern suggests that individual behavioral domains and longitudinal changes may be more informative than a single global symptom score. Evidence concerning APOE genotype is similarly mixed. Although one study observed that carrying an APOE ε4 allele increased MBI risk, 60 other studies suggest that APOE ε4 may be more related to specific domains, including affective dysregulation, 35 late-life depression, 61 and anxiety. 62 Associations with other symptoms remain inconsistent or insufficiently studied. 63, 64 Literature regarding lipid, metabolic, and demographic correlates of MBI is also limited and heterogeneous. Higher serum triglycerides have been reported in MBI, 65 while dysregulation in insulin and homocysteine 66 has also been linked with MBI-related outcomes. Correlations have been found between fasting glucose and the social inappropriateness domain of the MBI-C. 67 Indirect evidence from related psychiatric and dementia populations has linked lipid alterations to major depressive disorder and anxiety disorder; 68 fasting glucose to depression, anxiety, and poorer sleep quality; 67 and higher triglyceride concentrations to greater neuropsychiatric burden and psychosis. 69 Demographic findings are likewise inconsistent. An exhaustive systematic review found that MBI individuals often had fewer years of education and that some studies identified associations with older age or male sex; however, several studies reported no significant demographic associations. 70 Collectively, findings from the literature suggest that MBI correlates may depend on symptom domain, study population, disease stage, and analytical approach. Future studies should further examine individual MBI-C domains and NPI symptoms longitudinally and evaluate additional candidate biomarkers of behavioral impairment. 71

Coordinated alterations in circulating Apo profiles in MCI and MBI

Across groups, all nine Apo species and both Apo composites showed the highest values in MCI, intermediate in the MBI group, and the lowest values in controls. The coordinated pattern suggests that MCI was accompanied by a broad alteration in circulating Apos rather than an isolated change in a single species. Direct comparison with previous research is limited because multi-Apo profiles have not, to our knowledge, been examined across the same groups as this work. The closest comparable study reported lower ApoA1, ApoA2, and ApoH, and higher levels of ApoE, ApoJ, and ApoB/ApoA1 ratios in MCI than in controls, 26 contrasting with the broadly higher concentrations observed here. Indirect findings from related behavioral phenotypes have likewise shown heterogeneous changes in circulating ApoA and ApoB in depression, 72 ApoJ in relation to agitation/aggression, 73 ApoD in schizophrenia, 74 ApoA1 and ApoB before depression-, anxiety-, or stress-related diagnoses, 75 and ApoB in relation to short sleep duration. 76

ApoH and ApoC3 as correlates of cognitive impairment: potential metabolic and vascular mechanisms

ApoH was the most consistently associated analyte, showing inverse associations with global cognition, memory indexing, verbal learning, and delayed recall, independent of APOE ε4 genotype. Previous studies have reported associations in the opposite direction, with positive relationships between ApoH and global cognitive, memory, or visuospatial performance. 26, 77 Differences in population characteristics, cognitive measures, metabolic status, and laboratory procedures may contribute to this inconsistency.

Apart from ApoH, ApoC3 showed a more selective pattern, with negative associations with MoCA delayed recall and CERAD verbal fluency. As these outcomes involve partly different cognitive processes, these results do not establish specificity for a single cognitive domain. These findings also differ from earlier work linking higher ApoC3 concentrations to better cognitive performance, 77, 78 particularly in the memory and visuospatial domains, slower cognitive decline, 79 and slower CSF amyloid-β42 reduction. 80 On a broader level, higher concentrations of ApoH and ApoC3 have been suggested to indicate metabolic syndrome and increased cardiovascular risk 81, 82 due to associations with higher triglyceride levels. Therefore, the inverse associations between ApoH, ApoC3, and cognitive performance in this research may reflect shared metabolic and vascular dysregulation mechanisms that could affect cognition.

Other species, including ApoJ 83 and ApoA1, 84 have also been proposed as candidate correlates of neurocognitive decline, although they were not retained in the present models.

Atherogenic and protective Apo composites: associations with cognitive performance

We found that, against a background of generally elevated circulating Apo concentrations, higher levels of proatherogenic and proinflammatory Apos (composite based on ApoB, ApoC1, and ApoC3) were particularly associated with poorer cognitive performance.

Thus, the atherogenic Apo composite may be a potential correlate of general cognitive impairment, though further examination is required to determine the incremental value of the composite.

Regarding lipid profile, higher free cholesterol was associated with all three modeled MoCA outcomes. A previous study reported lower free cholesterol in peripheral blood mononuclear cells in MCI. 85 The wider lipid literature is similarly context-dependent. 20, [86] [87] [88] [89] [90] [91] [92]

Divergent associations of lipids and Apos with cognitive performance

The generalized increase in circulating Apos in MCI and MBI may reflect coordinated alterations in hepatic lipoprotein synthesis, secretion, and clearance, potentially associated with metabolic and vascular dysregulation. 93 Alternatively, the parallel increases across Apo species may reflect changes in circulating lipoprotein particle abundance or turnover rather than independent upregulation of individual Apos. 94 The positive association between plasma free cholesterol and cognitive performance, contrasting with the inverse associations observed for ApoH, ApoC3, and the atherogenic Apo composite, may reflect distinct aspects of lipid homeostasis. Unesterified cholesterol is essential for membrane integrity, myelination, and synaptic formation, including astrocyte-mediated cholesterol delivery to neurons. 95 However, cerebral cholesterol metabolism is largely independent of circulating cholesterol because of the blood-brain barrier. 96 Consequently, higher plasma free cholesterol may reflect more favorable systemic metabolic or nutritional conditions rather than directly increased cerebral cholesterol availability. Conversely, ApoC3 impairs triglyceride-rich lipoprotein clearance and promotes atherogenic remnant accumulation and vascular inflammation. 42, 97 ApoB and ApoC1 participate in lipoprotein transport and remodeling, 23 whereas ApoH has additional phospholipid-binding and immune-coagulation functions. 98 Thus, the divergent associations may reflect differences between systemic cholesterol status and lipoprotein-associated metabolic and vascular dysfunction, although the mechanisms underlying the observed cognitive associations remain to be established.

Limitations and future studies

Several limitations should be addressed. First, the cross-sectional design precludes insight into temporal sequence, causality, and prognostic value. Second, this Thai cohort was predominantly female and relatively highly educated, which may limit generalizability. The MBI group also included participants with and without co-occurring MCI, whereas the MCI group excluded MBI. Diagnostic classifications of MCI and MBI represent operationally defined, partly overlapping clinical constructs with potentially limited reliability and substantial withingroup heterogeneity. Therefore, our regression analyses deliberately focused on quantitative cognitive (MoCA and CERAD) and behavioral (NPI) measures rather than categorical diagnosis, thereby preserving dimensional information and avoiding potentially artificial diagnostic boundaries.

Third, the low frequency of several individual neuropsychiatric symptoms may limit domain-specific behavioral modeling.

Fourth, the multivariable analyses were conducted within the original sample; therefore, the reproducibility and generalizability of the findings require validation in independent cohorts across different countries and cultural settings.

Future studies should use longitudinal cohorts with repeated Apo measurements that are examined alongside established neurodegenerative biomarkers, metabolic and vascular measures, and neuroimaging. Further internal validation and external replication are needed to evaluate whether ApoH, ApoC3, and the atherogenic Apo composite provide reproducible information about cognitive performance beyond established demographic factors, APOE genotype, and conventional lipid measures. Emerging novel analytes such as ApoM may also extend the current panel and give rise to different patterns of association, as higher plasma ApoM has been reported in AD than in controls after adjustment for established risk factors. 99

Conclusion

In this cross-sectional case-control study of Thai older adults, MCI and MBI were characterized by distinct, but partially overlapping, cognitive and behavioral profiles. Adjusted circulating concentrations of all measured Apo species showed a consistent pattern of highest values in MCI, intermediate in the heterogeneous MBI group, and lowest values in controls, with significant diagnostic-group effects remaining after BH-FDR correction. ApoH, ApoC3, and the atherogenic Apo composite were inversely associated with certain cognitive measures. No candidate predictors were retained for global neuropsychiatric symptom or caregiver distress regression models. These exploratory findings suggest that Apo profiles may be more consistently related to cognitive performance than behavioral severity; however, further studies are required to validate these findings before diagnostic or prognostic utility of the notable Apos can be inferred.
