Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How StatCheck works

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Background: The Centiloid (CL) scale standardizes global amyloid PET quantification and is widely used to define amyloid positivity. As a global summary measure, however, CL may not fully reflect the regional distribution of amyloid deposition, which can carry additional prognostic information about the rate of cognitive decline.

Objective: To develop and externally validate a fixed, regional amyloid PET composite score that complements CL for predicting cognitive decline in Alzheimer's disease.

Methods: The Regional Amyloid PET Score (RAPS) was derived from 82 FreeSurfer regions using machine learning with bootstrap stability selection to predict the rate of change in CDR-Sum of Boxes (CDR-SB) in 433 amyloid-positive ADNI participants imaged with [ 18 F]florbetapir (n = 357) or [ 18 F]florbetaben (n = 76). The fixed nine-region weights were applied without retraining in a small ADNI [ 18 F]florbetaben subgroup (N = 71, 68 of whom were in the discovery cohort; sensitivity analysis) and two external validation cohorts, NACC SCAN (N = 1531; four tracers) and OASIS-3 (N = 428).

correlated more strongly with CDR-SB slope than CL and showed higher discrimination of rapid decliners (AUC 0.813 vs 0.708). RAPS remained associated with progression after CL adjustment in both external cohorts, although neither external AUC difference was significant. Cross-cohort meta-analysis including ADNI development and the two external cohorts favored RAPS alone over CL alone (pooled ΔAUC +0.069; I² = 1.6%).

Conclusions: RAPS, a fixed regional amyloid PET-derived score, may complement CL for prognostic stratification in Alzheimer's disease research.

Introduction

Amyloid positron emission tomography (PET) has transformed the diagnosis and management of Alzheimer's disease (AD) by enabling in vivo quantification of cerebral amyloid-β (Aβ) plaque burden. 1 With the clinical introduction of anti-Aβ immunotherapies, [2] [3] [4] confirmation of amyloid pathology has become essential for treatment eligibility; because these therapies carry a risk of amyloid-related imaging abnormalities (ARIA), accurate identification of individuals at greatest risk of near-term clinical progression is a pressing priority. Amyloid positivity defines Alzheimer's pathologic change, 5 yet not all amyloid-positive individuals show near-term cognitive progression, particularly at lower amyloid burdens.

The Centiloid (CL) scale standardized amyloid PET quantification across tracers and centers [6] [7] [8] and is widely used in clinical trials and practice as a measure of global amyloid burden; amyloid PET itself is used clinically under appropriate-use criteria. 9 However, the CL approach collapses the spatial distribution of amyloid into a single global metric, overlooking where amyloid is deposited-which may matter when global burden alone provides limited prognostic separation, including in the intermediate range.

Neuropathological studies have established distinct spatial progressions of neurofibrillary and amyloid pathology; Braak and Braak 10 described neurofibrillary staging, whereas Thal et al. 11 described five phases of Aβ spread from neocortical to allocortical, subcortical, and brainstem regions, and in vivo PET staging has confirmed analogous amyloid patterns. [12] [13] [14] Tau PET has demonstrated strong prognostic value; Ossenkoppele et al. 15 showed that baseline tau PET predicted subsequent cognitive decline and outperformed amyloid PET and structural MRI, raising the question of whether a region-specific approach could improve amyloid PET-based prognosis.

Despite growing evidence for regional amyloid quantification, existing studies have largely relied on global summary measures (CL or composite SUVRs) or individual a priori-selected ROIs. Few studies have systematically developed a sparse, interpretable regional amyloid PET composite optimized for longitudinal cognitive decline and externally evaluated across cohorts and tracers without retraining. Previous studies [16] [17] [18] have examined regional amyloid accumulation, staging, and spatial distribution, using approaches that differ from a fixed regional composite optimized for cognitive decline.

In this study, we developed the Regional Amyloid PET Score (RAPS), a data-driven regional amyloid composite built from the Alzheimer's Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI) cohort and externally evaluated in the National Alzheimer's Coordinating Center (NACC) Standardized Centralized Alzheimer's & Related Dementias Neuroimaging (SCAN) multi-tracer cohort and the Open Access Series of Imaging Studies (OASIS-3), with a sensitivity analysis in a small ADNI [ 18 F]florbetaben subgroup that largely overlaps the discovery cohort. We tested whether this data-driven regional composite provides prognostic information for longitudinal cognitive decline complementary to global Centiloid-across amyloid burden strata, APOE ε4 genotype, and survival outcomes-with secondary exploratory analyses in the CL 20-60 subgroup.

Methods

Study Participants

Discovery cohort (ADNI).

Data used in the preparation of this article were obtained from the ADNI database (adni.loni.usc.edu). [19] [20] The ADNI was launched in 2003 as a public-private partnership, led by Principal Investigator Michael W. Weiner, MD. The primary goal of ADNI has been to test whether serial magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), positron emission tomography (PET), other biological markers, and clinical and neuropsychological assessment can be combined to measure the progression of mild cognitive impairment (MCI) and early Alzheimer's disease (AD). Inclusion criteria were cognitively normal (CN) or MCI at ADNI enrollment, available amyloid PET with an [ 18 F]-labeled tracer ([ 18 F]florbetapir or [ 18 F]florbetaben), APOE genotyping, baseline (earliest PET) Centiloid ≥ 20, and ≥ 2 years of longitudinal Clinical Dementia Rating (CDR) follow-up. From 5062 participants, 433 amyloid-positive CN/MCI individuals were included. The baseline amyloid PET scan (the earliest scan with a Centiloid value) was [ 18 F]florbetapir in 357 participants and [ 18 F]florbetaben in 76; both tracers were processed through the same UC Berkeley pipeline (below), with global burden expressed on the Centiloid scale, whereas the regional model inputs were unharmonized SUVRs. Although the Centiloid scale harmonizes global amyloid burden across tracers, regional amyloid PET uptake patterns can differ between tracers; regional SUVRs from the two tracers were pooled, and tracer was not included as a covariate or stratification factor at any stage of derivation (residualization, feature selection, standardization, or refitting). Baseline Centiloid was similar in the two tracer groups (74.2 ± 40.4 vs 73.4 ± 34.9). Performance across tracers was examined in the multi-tracer NACC SCAN validation cohort. The CL ≥ 20 restriction targeted prognostic stratification after amyloid positivity was established; it was not intended to develop a score for distinguishing amyloid-negative from amyloidpositive individuals.

Overlapping florbetaben subgroup (ADNI; sensitivity analysis).

A subgroup of ADNI participants (N = 71) with [ 18 F]florbetaben (FBB) PET was used as a small-sample sensitivity analysis, applying the fixed RAPS weights without retraining. This subgroup was drawn from the same ADNI longitudinal dataset as the discovery cohort (participant-level CDR-SB slopes as in the discovery analysis) with Centiloid ≥ 20 at the FBB scan (source file N = 72); one participant with only a same-day CDR assessment and no subsequent assessment had zero follow-up time and was excluded (N = 71). The subgroup comprised largely the same individuals as the discovery cohort (68 of 71 overlapping, 67 of whom entered the discovery cohort through a florbetaben scan); it therefore provides only a descriptive check of whether the fixed weights remain informative in a small florbetaben-defined subgroup and, because 68 of 71 participants contributed to model development, provides no independent evidence of predictive performance or tracer transportability. For each participant, the imaging index was the earliest FBB scan with all nine RAPS regions available, and Centiloid was taken from that scan; for the Cox analyses, time zero was the date of that scan, whereas the CDR-SB slope used for the correlation and ROC analyses was the participant-level slope from the longitudinal CDR-SB series and was not recomputed relative to the FBB index date. The correlation and ROC analyses should therefore not be interpreted as prediction of decline exclusively after that scan.

Amyloid PET Acquisition and Processing

In ADNI, regional standardized uptake value ratios (SUVRs) were calculated by the UC Berkeley Jagust Laboratory [24] [25] using FreeSurfer (version 7.1) 26 parcellation (Desikan-Killiany cortical regions and subcortical regions from automated segmentation [aseg]), 27 with whole cerebellum as the reference region for normalization; 24 Centiloid was derived using tracer-and reference-region-specific conversions. 28 All 82 ROIs were retained (68 cortical, 14 subcortical). No partial volume effect (PVE) correction was applied. 29 Visualizations used MRIcroGL. 30 Amyloid positivity was defined as a global CL ≥ 20.

This threshold approximates the neuropathological level at which amyloid PET discriminates moderate-to-frequent neuritic plaques (≈19 CL for florbetaben 31 ) and equals the ADNI florbetapir positivity threshold of whole-cerebellum-normalized SUVR 1.11 (= 20 CL 28 ); comparable positivity cutoffs (≈19 CL) have been adopted in independent cohorts, 32 and CL ≤ 20 corresponds to the amyloid-negative boundary in a recent quantification study. 33

Cognitive Outcomes

In ADNI, the primary cognitive outcome was the individual rate of change in the Clinical Dementia Rating Sum of Boxes (CDR-SB), [34] [35] estimated by linear mixed-effects models (random intercepts and slopes per participant; details in Supplementary Methods). The decline rate was the per-participant slope (best linear unbiased predictor, BLUP), with ordinary least squares (OLS) slopes used in a sensitivity comparison. The original repeat-observation check counted post-origin data rows, including rows with missing CDR-SB, instead of counting nonmissing CDR-SB assessments as intended.

Thirteen of the 433 participants had one retained CDR-SB observation; their BLUP slopes were estimated using the population model rather than directly observed withinperson change. In the corrected evaluation, CDR dates were taken from the clinical assessment records and identical repeated observations were counted once; the fixed RAPS model and saved out-of-fold scores were retained. Model-development results (region selection, coefficients and internal cross-validation) are retained from the original development analysis; subsequent evaluations of the fixed scores use the corrected clinical data. The participant-level ADNI slopes were estimated from the retained longitudinal clinical series and do not necessarily represent decline exclusively after the index PET scan. External-cohort slope methods and observation windows are described in Supplementary Methods.

RAPS Construction and Post-selection Cross-Validation

The Regional Amyloid PET Score (RAPS) was constructed in the ADNI discovery cohort from 82 FreeSurfer regions (68 Desikan-Killiany cortical regions and 14 asegderived subcortical regions; Supplementary Table S1) using an outer-fold crossvalidation framework 36 to restrict model fitting and hyperparameter selection to the training data. Within each outer fold, covariate regression (age, sex, education, APOE ε4 count, baseline CDR-SB; Centiloid excluded to enable RAPS vs CL comparison) was fitted on the training set to residualize the CDR-SB slope and applied to the held-out test set. Training-set-standardized regional SUVRs were entered into an elastic net regression [37] [38] (ElasticNetCV, 5-fold inner CV; L1 ratio ∈ {0.1-0.9}; α auto-optimized).

Out-of-fold predictions were concatenated and Z-standardized to yield post-selection out-of-fold RAPS scores, and a final fixed-coefficient model was refitted on the complete dataset for external validation. The definitive RAPS was the intersection of non-zero ElasticNet coefficients and bootstrap selection frequency ≥ 80%, serialized without retraining (Supplementary Methods). Because the nine regions were selected on the full discovery sample, the 9-region out-of-fold metrics reflect a post-selection crossvalidation in which coefficients and hyperparameters-but not the choice of regionswere re-estimated within each fold, and may be optimistic for the feature-selection step; the comparison with the fully nested 82-region model should be interpreted with this asymmetry in mind.

ROI selection stability was assessed by 1000 bootstrap iterations (resampling with replacement, refitting elastic net de novo); ROIs selected in ≥80% were bootstrapstable. The final RAPS was the intersection of bootstrap-stable and non-zero-coefficient ROIs. OLS-derived and BLUP-derived CDR-SB slopes were highly correlated (Supplementary Figure S1).

As a sensitivity analysis, the original eligibility sequence was retained with baseline CL > 0, and the same 82-region, 5×5 cross-validation, 1000-bootstrap and 80% intersection procedure was repeated. The resulting model was compared directly with the locked nine-region RAPS in the same external participants without external retraining.

Corrected scan-level inputs and slopes re-estimated in an expanded LME pool were used for sensitivity retraining. A CL ≥ 20 model was also retrained using the same processing and the same slopes for shared participants, to assess the developmentrange difference. The original nine-region RAPS and corrected primary evaluations were retained (Supplementary Methods). We also examined exclusion of the 13 participants with one retained CDR-SB observation by refitting the original nine-region set and by repeating region selection in the remaining 420 participants (Supplementary Methods).

External Validation

The fixed 9-ROI RAPS weights were applied to the NACC 21 SCAN 22 cohort. The source sample comprised 1707 participants from 37 NIA-funded Alzheimer's Disease Research Centers, imaged with four tracers: [ 18 F]florbetapir (n = 619), [ 18 F]florbetaben (n = 280), [ 11 C]PiB (n = 709), or [ 18 F]NAV4694 (n = 99); demographics in Table 1. Regional SUVRs were used as supplied by SCAN, referenced to whole cerebellum. The primary analytic cohort was Centiloid-unrestricted (N = 1531; 178 visit-level Cox events; 117 rapid-decliner events at slope > 1.0/year; derivation in Results). Of these participants, 539 had Centiloid ≥ 20. Cox models, paired bootstrap AUC comparisons, and Pencinastyle reclassification metrics were used. The CL ≥ 20 subset was also evaluated using the existing Cox covariates, clinical definitions and full-cohort predictor standardization (Supplementary Methods).

RAPS was similarly applied to OASIS-3 without retraining (Supplementary Methods).

For three participants without a matching entry in the Centiloid table, Centiloid was calculated from the same-scan non-PVC mean cortical SUVR using the OASIS-3 conversion equation (163.6 × SUVR -181.0).

An exploratory ADNI tau-adjustment analysis required the original amyloid PET and the nearest available non-PVC meta-temporal flortaucipir PET measurement to be within one year. For this ancillary analysis, CDR-SB slopes were re-estimated, using at least two CDR observations after both scans and a final observation at least two years after the later scan. Linear models compared the association of fixed post-selection out-offold RAPS with these slopes before and after tau adjustment in identical complete cases, additionally adjusting for CL, age, sex, education, APOE ε4 count and the first CDR-SB after this ancillary index (Supplementary Methods).

Results

Cohort Characteristics

From 5062 ADNI participants, inclusion criteria yielded 433 amyloid-positive CN or MCI individuals for the discovery cohort (Figure 1). Demographic and clinical characteristics are summarized in Table 1 (mean baseline CL 74.1; 60.7% APOE ε4 carriers). The mean LME-estimated CDR-SB slope was 0.58 points per year (SD 0.73), and 96 participants (22.2%) were rapid decliners (slope > 1.0).

The OASIS-3 external validation cohort comprised 429 participants with [ 18 F]florbetapir PET and longitudinal CDR data; one was excluded for missing Centiloid, yielding N = 428. Demographic and clinical characteristics of the ADNI discovery, NACC SCAN, and OASIS-3 cohorts are presented in Table 1.

RAPS Construction and Internal Validation

In the original development analysis, post-selection internal validation used 5×5 crossvalidation predicting covariate-adjusted CDR-SB slope (mean fold R² = 0.090, mean fold Pearson r = 0.381; Supplementary Figure S2). Applied to all 82 ROIs (N = 433), ElasticNet selected 15 non-zero ROIs; 9 also met bootstrap stability ≥ 80%, constituting the final RAPS. In the 9-ROI refit, the left hippocampus had the largest absolute coefficient (β = -0.169); the remaining eight cortical and subcortical regions are shown in Figure 2A and Supplementary Table S3. OLS-derived and BLUP-derived slopes showed a strong correlation (r = 0.919; Supplementary Figure S1).

In the original post-selection analysis, fold-level R² ranged from -0.08 to 0.21, and the pooled out-of-fold correlation was 0.363 (P < .001; Supplementary Figure S2).

Bootstrap Stability and 9-ROI RAPS Definition

Bootstrap resampling (1000 iterations) identified the 9 ROIs meeting both criteria (selection ≥ 80% and non-zero ElasticNet coefficient; Figure 2B; Supplementary Figure S3). The left hippocampus was selected in 100% of iterations with perfect sign consistency. Several stable ROIs have 95% confidence intervals crossing zero (Supplementary Figure S3), indicating that RAPS's predictive value arises from the combined 9-ROI pattern rather than any single region.

The 9-ROI RAPS score-the intersection of ElasticNet-selected and bootstrap-stable

ROIs-was retained as the final model (Figure 2C). In the post-selection comparison, the fixed 9-ROI model showed slightly higher performance than the fully nested 82-ROI model (mean fold r = 0.381 vs 0.351; Supplementary Figure S4).

RAPS vs Centiloid

RAPS showed stronger zero-order correlation with CDR-SB slope than global CL (r = 0.545 vs 0.314; Steiger z = 5.27, P < .001; Figure 3A,B). The RAPS-CL correlation was moderate (r = 0.445; Supplementary Table S4). In discriminating rapid cognitive decliners, RAPS outperformed CL across all CDR-SB slope thresholds, with a significant gain in discrimination at the primary > 1.0/year threshold (bootstrap P = .001; Table 2, Panel A; Figure 3C-E). NRI and IDI were positive in exploratory descriptive analyses (Table 2, Panel B). RAPS also exceeded left hippocampus SUVR alone (AUC = 0.666), supporting multi-ROI weighting.

Subgroup Analyses

RAPS correlated with CDR-SB slope across all subgroups examined (Supplementary Figure S5A; Supplementary Table S5). However, performance was weaker in two clinically important subgroups: participants with baseline CDR-SB = 0 (r = 0.246, n = 136) and the high-amyloid stratum (CL ≥ 60; r = 0.473, n = 260). These zero-order values are not directly comparable to the covariate-adjusted out-of-fold r (0.363) because their outcomes were not residualized for covariates and were evaluated using the corrected, rather than original-development, clinical outcome data. The CN/MCI diagnostic strata were defined by diagnosis at ADNI enrollment.

A significant APOE ε4 × RAPS interaction was observed (β-interaction = 0.074, P = .047; Supplementary Table S6). CL-stratified analysis revealed distinct ROI weight patterns: the left hippocampus dominated in the late stage (β = -0.213), whereas in the early stage predictive information was distributed across broader cortical regions (Supplementary Figure S5B). Sensitivity analyses across CL thresholds (50, 60, 70) showed stable RAPS performance in both strata (early-stage r = 0.494-0.551). The APOE ε4 interaction was exploratory and was not adjusted across all interaction tests. Because cognitively normal and MCI participants may differ in their capacity for measurable decline, we analyzed the two diagnostic groups defined at ADNI enrollment separately. RAPS predicted CDR-SB slope in both CN (n = 169, r = 0.427 [95% CI 0.295-0.543]) and MCI (n = 264, r = 0.526 [0.433-0.608]) participants and significantly outperformed Centiloid in each stratum (Steiger P = .003 and P < .001, respectively). RAPS remained an independent predictor of clinical progression after Centiloid adjustment in both groups (CN: M3 hazard ratio per SD = 2.60 [1.51-4.47]; MCI: 1.36 [1.13-1.65]). Rapid decliners (CDR-SB slope > 1.0/year) were infrequent among CN participants (8 events), so the discrimination AUC in this subgroup is exploratory; RAPS nonetheless retained prognostic value in time-to-event analysis (42 events).

Time-to-event Prognostic Analysis

IPCW time-dependent AUC was numerically higher for RAPS than CL at early-to-mid horizons (mean 1-8 years: 0.774 vs 0.722; Supplementary Figure S5C), with the largest gap at 1-4 years. Median-split survival analysis showed survival separation for both RAPS and CL in the same analytic sample (N = 431) (RAPS hazard ratio [HR] = 3.86; CL HR = 2.58; both P < .001; Supplementary Figure S6), with median time to progression 2.1 years in RAPS-high versus 8.2 years in RAPS-low.

In Cox proportional hazards analysis (190 events in 431 participants), RAPS strongly predicted decline (HR = 2.12 per SD, 95% CI 1.81-2.47; concordance 0.705), outperforming CL alone (concordance 0.638). In the multivariable model with both scores plus demographic covariates, RAPS remained an independent predictor (HR = 1.61, 95% CI 1.35-1.92; Supplementary Figure S5D), as did baseline CDR-SB and CL (Table 2, Panel C; concordance 0.745; Supplementary Table S7). 45 Schoenfeld testing supported the proportional hazards assumption for RAPS (P = .70); no violation was detected for Centiloid (P = .16), whereas baseline CDR-SB showed evidence of nonproportional hazards (P = .03).

RAPS yielded larger incremental concordance than CL in nested Cox models (5-fold CV C-index: clinical 0.700, +CL 0.711, +RAPS 0.740, +CL+RAPS 0.734); adding CL to clinical + RAPS did not improve CV concordance.

In an exploratory ADNI CL 20-60 subgroup (n = 173; Supplementary Figure S7), RAPSbased median stratification yielded a large effect on cognitive decline (RAPS-high vs RAPS-low CDR-SB slope 0.528 vs 0.092; Cohen's d = 0.947), with higher discriminative AUC than Centiloid (0.877 vs 0.805 at > 1.0/yr). As a continuous variable, RAPS retained a strong partial correlation with slope after CL adjustment (partial r = 0.460, P < .001), and quartile stratification revealed a dose-response gradient (rapid decliners 0% Q1 → 30.2% Q4).

Sensitivity Analysis in the Overlapping ADNI Florbetaben Subgroup

As a small-sample sensitivity analysis, the fixed 9-ROI RAPS weights were applied without retraining to the ADNI florbetaben subgroup (N = 71; mean Centiloid 69.9), which largely overlaps the discovery cohort. RAPS showed numerically higher discrimination of rapid decliners (AUC 0.885 vs 0.781; bootstrap P = .325; Figure 4A; Supplementary Table S8) and a stronger correlation with CDR-SB slope than CL (r = 0.556 vs 0.390; Steiger P = .095). Because of the overlap with the discovery cohort, these estimates are not independent and are reported for completeness.

Multi-Tracer External Validation in NACC SCAN

The NACC SCAN source cohort comprised 1707 participants; excluding 167 without post-PET CDR follow-up and 9 same-day-only visits yielded the 1531-participant analytic cohort. RAPS correlated strongly with global Centiloid (r = 0.59). For rapid decline (slope > 1.0/yr; N = 1531; 117 events), RAPS AUC = 0.803 vs CL 0.750 (ΔAUC = +0.053; bootstrap P = .055; Figure 4B).

In the NACC SCAN analytic cohort (N = 1531; 178 visit-level events), RAPS and CL were each independent multivariable Cox predictors (M3, N = 1529 after excluding two missing education values: RAPS HR/SD = 1.20 [1.01-1.43], P = .043; CL HR = 1.31, P = .001; Figure 4C). Cross-tracer M1 HRs per SD were directionally consistent across four tracers (Supplementary Figure S8; tracer-stratified correlations with slope in Supplementary Table S9). RAPS improved reclassification at the primary threshold (categorical NRI = 0.230, IDI = 0.045; Supplementary Figure S9) and remained associated with progression in the CL 20-60 stratum (M1 HR = 1.49 [1.02-2.19], P = .041; 25 events/241).

Cross-Cohort Synthesis

Across cohorts, M3 Cox RAPS HRs were directionally consistent (range 1.20-1.61 per SD; Figure 4C). Among the three independent cohorts, heterogeneity was substantial (pooled HR = 1.38, 95% CI 1.14-1.67; I² = 63.3%), driven by the higher ADNI development-cohort estimate, based on post-selection out-of-fold RAPS scores and potentially retaining feature-selection optimism, whereas the two external cohort estimates were consistent (1.20-1.35). The difference in discrimination between RAPS alone and CL alone (ΔAUC at > 1.0/year) was directionally consistent across all four cohort datasets, significant in ADNI (+0.105, P = .001), not statistically significant in NACC (+0.053, P = .055), and nonsignificant in OASIS-3 (+0.031) and the overlapping ADNI FBB subgroup (+0.104; Figure 4D). Random-effects meta-analysis of the three independent cohorts yielded pooled ΔAUC = +0.069 (95% CI +0.030, +0.108; P < .001; I² = 1.6%; Supplementary Table S10).

External Validation in OASIS-3

OASIS-3 (N = 428; N = 425 in M3 Cox) supported RAPS's prognostic value (HR = 1.35 [1.05, 1.74], P = .018); RAPS yielded AUC = 0.864 vs CL 0.833 at slope > 1.0/year (bootstrap P = .569; Supplementary Figure S10; Supplementary Table S8).

The intermediate-range (CL 20-60) effect observed in ADNI was partially replicated in OASIS-3 (n = 89; Cohen's d = 0.560, P = .010; Supplementary Figure S7).

The CL > 0 sensitivity model included 715 participants and selected seven regions. In the same external participants, AUCs for the original nine-region RAPS and the CL > 0 model were 0.803 and 0.794 in NACC SCAN and 0.864 and 0.855 in OASIS-3. Paired differences for the CL > 0 model minus original RAPS were -0.009 (P = .067) and -0.010 (P = .147), respectively. When both models were retrained using the same corrected inputs and slope-estimation procedure, AUC differences for CL > 0 minus CL ≥ 20 were +0.005 (P = .107) in NACC SCAN and -0.009 (P = .037) in OASIS-3. These exploratory comparisons were unadjusted for multiplicity and conditional on the fitted models; nonsignificant differences do not establish equivalence. The primary nineregion RAPS was unchanged (Supplementary Table S11).

In the NACC SCAN CL ≥ 20 subset (539 participants; 110 Cox events), the M3 RAPS HR was 1.19 [0.97-1.46] (P = .097); AUCs were 0.725 for RAPS and 0.705 for CL (P = .559; Supplementary Table S12).

The ancillary tau analysis included 70 ADNI participants and 217 post-index CDR observations. The RAPS coefficient was 0.073 CDR-SB points/year per original discovery-cohort SD before tau adjustment (95% CI -0.051 to 0.196; P = .244) and 0.057 after adjustment (95% CI -0.063 to 0.177; P = .345). The tau coefficient was 0.133 per subset SD (P = .021). RAPS was not statistically significant either before or after tau adjustment in this small selected subset (Supplementary Table S13).

After excluding the 13 participants with one retained CDR-SB observation, the original nine-region set was refitted using corrected inputs and slopes re-estimated in the remaining 420 participants, yielding AUCs of 0.799 in NACC SCAN and 0.876 in OASIS-3, compared with 0.803 and 0.864 for the original RAPS. The NACC M3 HR was 1.19 (95% CI 0.99-1.44; P = .061). Repeating region selection in the corrected 420participant dataset yielded five regions under the original seed and selection criterion, with external AUCs of 0.777 in NACC SCAN and 0.886 in OASIS-3. CL-adjusted associations after region reselection varied across cohorts. The original model was retained for the primary analyses; coefficient, region-reselection and pooled comparisons are reported in Supplementary Table S14.

Discussion

Principal findings

This study shows that regional amyloid PET patterns contain prognostic information beyond global Centiloid. Using ElasticNet with bootstrap stability assessment in the ADNI discovery cohort (N = 433), we derived RAPS, a compact 9-region score from 82 FreeSurfer ROIs. In ADNI post-selection evaluation, RAPS correlated more strongly with CDR-SB slope than Centiloid (r = 0.545 vs 0.314; Steiger P < .001) and discriminated rapid decliners more accurately (AUC 0.813 vs 0.708 at >1.0/yr), with positive net reclassification and integrated discrimination improvement estimates in exploratory descriptive analyses, though absolute magnitudes were modest. In the principal external validation-the NACC SCAN multi-tracer cohort (N = 1531, four tracers)-RAPS remained an independent predictor of decline alongside Centiloid (M3 Cox HR = 1.20 per SD, P = .043) and showed numerically higher discrimination of rapid decliners (AUC 0.803 vs 0.750). RAPS remained associated with clinical progression after adjustment for Centiloid in both external cohorts. AUC differences favored RAPS numerically, but were not statistically significant within either external cohort (NACC SCAN, P = .055; OASIS-3, P = .569). Across the three independent cohorts, randomeffects meta-analysis favored RAPS alone over Centiloid alone (pooled ΔAUC = +0.069; 95% CI +0.030, +0.108; I² = 1.6%). In the ADNI development cohort, RAPS was associated with decline and showed higher discrimination than Centiloid in both enrollment-defined CN and MCI strata.

Beyond Centiloid

In the NACC SCAN cohort (N = 1531, four tracers), RAPS and Centiloid each retained independent prognostic value in multivariable Cox models (M3; Figure 4C). Across the four dataset-specific M3 analyses (including the overlapping ADNI FBB subgroup), RAPS hazard ratios ranged from 1.20 to 1.61 per SD. Cross-cohort meta-analysis favored RAPS alone over Centiloid alone (Figure 4D); this comparison does not estimate the AUC gain from adding RAPS to a Centiloid model. This is consistent with the established temporal lag between amyloid pathology and cognitive change.

Prognostic stratification of amyloid-positive individuals is an unmet clinical need in the era of disease-modifying therapy. The CL 20-60 'intermediate-range' effect (ADNI Cohen's d = 0.947) was partially replicated in OASIS-3. Across the overall cohorts, higher discrimination of RAPS alone relative to Centiloid alone was supported by pooled cross-cohort analysis (Figure 4D), supporting prognostic stratification over multi-year follow-up. The CL 20-60 prognostic stratum is distinct from the diagnostic 'gray zone' of 11-26 CL reported around data-driven and visual positivity thresholds of 18 and 27 CL. 46 Our subgroup analysis examined prognostic differences within the amyloidpositive range. Interpretation should be guided by the clinical context. 47 Whether RAPS can further identify at-risk individuals below conventional positivity thresholds is an important future direction.

Interpretation

Among the 9 regions, the left hippocampus had the largest absolute coefficient (β = -0.169) with 100% bootstrap selection. Higher hippocampal SUVR was associated with slower decline; hippocampal SUVR alone had a lower AUC than the nine-region RAPS (0.666 vs 0.813), supporting the combined regional score over hippocampal SUVR alone. The inverse association between hippocampal SUVR and CDR-SB slope persisted after hippocampal volume adjustment (partial r = -0.231, P < .001; Supplementary Methods) and was accompanied by a lower hippocampal-to-cortical SUVR ratio in rapid than slow decliners (0.72 vs 0.84); this observational contrast does not identify its biological cause. Positive fusiform and transverse temporal coefficients and negative entorhinal and pallidal coefficients describe a multivariable prediction pattern and do not establish region-specific harmful or protective effects. ElasticNet coefficients represent predictive weights, not direct mechanisms; without PVEcorrection, 29 hippocampal SUVR may partly reflect atrophy-related signal loss. RAPS should be viewed as a prognostic composite, not a mechanistic amyloid map; replication with PVE-corrected PET is needed. Whereas the Centiloid target region is a large, fixed cortical volume designed to summarize global burden, RAPS uses a small set of FreeSurfer cortical and subcortical regions with signed, data-driven weights, so the two quantify different aspects of the amyloid signal. Whether the left hippocampal contribution reflects atrophy rather than amyloid is examined in Supplementary Methods; partial-volume effects were not corrected, and incorporating MR-based partialvolume correction or atrophy adjustment is a natural extension of RAPS. The regional score may partly reflect correlates of tau pathology or neurodegeneration. The limited tau-adjusted analysis did not establish a tau-independent RAPS association, and these data do not support a mechanistic claim that regional amyloid topology itself drives decline.

Model development focused on amyloid-positive CN/MCI participants, whereas external validation included broader cohorts (NACC included dementia cases). CDR-SB slope captured the dynamic trajectory of decline, more clinically relevant than single-visit status. Regional approaches are gaining traction across molecular imaging: standardized tau PET scales and in vivo Braak staging capture the spatial distribution of tau and predict cognitive decline. [48] [49] RAPS applies an analogous spatial logic to amyloid, deriving a data-driven regional weighting optimized for prognosis rather than for cross-tracer harmonization. Amyloid accumulation follows a sigmoidal trajectory that plateaus at high burden, [50] [51] which may limit the prognostic resolution of global amyloid metrics once positivity is established; downstream markers such as FDG-PET add complementary predictive information. 52 RAPS summarizes regional amyloid PET measurements for prognosis; their association with decline may also reflect other disease processes. Disease stage and amyloid burden also differ across cohorts. Mean CL was 74.1 in ADNI, 23.6 in NACC SCAN and 25.4 in OASIS-3; the external cohorts had larger proportions with baseline CDR-SB = 0 (Table 1). These differences in case mix and outcome range may contribute to attenuated external effects, alongside tracer and processing differences. The CL 20-60 subgroup defines amyloid burden rather than cognitive severity. In the NACC CL ≥ 20 subset, the M3 HR was similar to that in the full cohort, but its confidence interval included 1 and the AUC difference was not significant.

Clinical implications

Mattsson et al. 16 developed a regional amyloid PET staging approach, but systematic data-driven multi-cohort validation has been limited. RAPS extends this through ADNI training and multi-cohort, multi-tracer external validation, and may complement Centiloid for prognostic stratification, pending prospective validation. RAPS was optimized for CDR-SB slope whereas Centiloid quantifies total amyloid burden, an asymmetry relevant to comparative performance. In settings where Centiloid quantification is available, RAPS can use regional measurements from the same amyloid PET scan. CL is required to identify a CL-defined subgroup such as CL 20-60, but is not an input to the RAPS formula. Supervised optimization against the outcome and post-selection evaluation can favor RAPS in ADNI; the external CL-adjusted associations and the nonsignificant external AUC comparisons should therefore be distinguished.

The 9-ROI model simplifies RAPS computation to a subset of standard FreeSurfer regions, facilitating integration into existing pipelines. Fixed-weight performance was evaluated without retraining in the multi-tracer NACC SCAN cohort, with directionally consistent tracer-stratified associations, but formal equivalence across tracers was not established; the ADNI florbetaben subgroup was small and largely overlapped the discovery cohort, so validation in larger, independent florbetaben cohorts is needed.

Regionally weighted quantification may also complement visual reads, which can be challenging in cases with focal or low-level deposition; quantitative support improves reader agreement and confidence in such cases. 53

Limitations

Limitations include: (i) potential informative dropout bias; (ii) all cohorts are predominantly white, and OASIS-3 differed from ADNI in composition and reference regions; (iii) post-selection CV showed fold-level variability (Supplementary Figure S2); [54] [55] [56] (iv) PVE was not corrected; 29 (v) external analyses used the ADNI-fitted StandardScaler; (vi) results may depend on the parcellation 26 and hyperparameter grid; (vii) the NACC analytic cohort included dementia cases (n = 171) whereas ADNI was restricted to CN/MCI; (viii) RAPS was derived from two [ 18 F] tracers (florbetapir and florbetaben) whose regional SUVRs were pooled without tracer harmonization or tracer adjustment, and although tracer-stratified associations in the multi-tracer NACC SCAN cohort (florbetapir, florbetaben, PiB, and NAV4694) were directionally consistent, regional uptake patterns can differ across tracers, formal tracer equivalence was not established, and residual tracer-specific bias cannot be excluded; (ix) the nine RAPS regions were selected on the full discovery sample, so the internal out-of-fold performance is a post-selection cross-validation that may be optimistic for the featureselection step, with generalizability supported primarily by the fixed-weight external cohorts; and (x) the ADNI [ 18 F]florbetaben subgroup largely overlapped the discovery cohort (a small-sample sensitivity analysis rather than independent validation), and participant overlap between NACC/OASIS-3 and ADNI cannot be fully excluded owing to differing identifier systems.

Caudate values from different PET scans in the original inputs were examined in corrected-input sensitivity analyses, alongside analyses excluding the 13 single-observation participants (Supplementary Tables S11 and S14).

Refitting the original nine regions produced small changes in external AUCs, whereas de novo selection yielded different region sets. The predictive performance of the fixed score should therefore be distinguished from the stability of its selected region set.

RAPS was developed in a single ADNI cohort. Larger, multicohort development studies could further assess the stability of regional selection, with independent validation of any updated model. Future directions include validation in Japanese (JADNI) cohorts, tau PET integration, 57-58 and voxel-level analysis.
