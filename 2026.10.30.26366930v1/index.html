To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 6 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Metacheck does not yet analyze automatically whether sentences which include non-significant p-values are correct, but we recommend manually checking the sentences below for possible misinterpreted non-significant p values.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Physical activity (PA) supports health across the life course, yet the biological pathways linking habitual PA to long-term health remain incompletely understood. Metabolomics provides a powerful tool to study PA-related metabolic adaptations, but how habitual PA shapes the metabolome in childhood remains poorly characterized. Addressing this gap, we examined cross-sectional associations between accelerometer-measured habitual PA and the plasma metabolome in 515 children aged 5.5 to 11 years from the European Childhood Obesity Project (CHOP) and additionally determined whether associations differed between sexes and across the body mass index (BMI) range. Using targeted metabolomics, we quantified 315 metabolites and derived 91 metabolite sums and ratios. Associations with PA were assessed using linear mixed-effects models. Replication was performed in an independent pediatric cohort. Among the 406 metabolite variables, 73 were associated (p < 0.05) with at least one PA variable, of which 12 replicated, including lysophosphatidylcholine (LPC) a C14:0 and LPCs/phosphatidylcholines (PCs) with higher levels in more active children, and PC aa C40:6, monounsaturated PCs/saturated PCs, and valine/LPC a C18:1 with lower levels in more active children. At the metabolite-class level, intensity-dependent association patterns emerged. Amino acids and acylcarnitines were lower in more active children predominantly for time spent in higher-intensity PA, whereas LPCs were higher mainly with more total PA (80% light PA) and less sedentary time. Associations were more pronounced at older ages and differed (p < 0.05) by BMI for 42 metabolite variables and by sex for 45 variables in the interaction analyses. These findings provide insights into PA-related metabolic adaptations early in life and may help elucidate biological pathways linking childhood PA to long-term health.

Introduction

Lack of physical activity (PA) is one of the leading risk factors for many noncommunicable diseases and chronic conditions such as insulin resistance [1] [2] [3] , metabolic syndrome 4, 5 , and cardiometabolic diseases [6] [7] [8] across the entire lifespan. Various metabolic mechanisms have been implicated in conveying the health benefits of PA. These include mitochondrial biogenesis and adaptations 9 , improved insulin sensitivity through exerciseinduced 5' adenosine monophosphate-activated protein kinase (AMPK) activation that promotes glucose transporter type 4 (GLUT4) translocation and glucose uptake in skeletal muscles 3 , and enhanced metabolic flexibility reflected by increased capacity for fuel storage and substrate utilization 10 . Nonetheless, molecular mechanisms underlying the systemic health benefits of PA are not yet fully understood 11, 12 . Addressing this gap, plasma metabolomics in large cohorts enables the identification of metabolic signatures of PA and can highlight candidate biological pathways underlying these adaptations. While acute metabolic responses to exercise are typically examined under tightly controlled conditions and are well characterized for the blood metabolome 13- 15 , the long-term metabolic adaptations to habitual PA have been studied less extensively, with studies typically relying on self-reported activity assessed using questionnaires, which yield less precise and potentially biased PA data 16 . In contrast, accelerometers provide more objective measures of movement intensity and duration but are less commonly applied in observational studies of free-living populations. The most consistent finding across these studies is that higher habitual PA is associated with lower circulating levels of branched-chain amino acids (BCAAs) and glutamate [17] [18] [19] [20] [21] [22] [23] [24] [25] . The majority of metabolomics studies on habitual PA have been conducted in adults 17- 21,26,27 , with limited data in children. In a Finnish cohort of girls, higher reported leisuretime PA at 11 years was associated with lower circulating isoleucine and leucine 24 . A similar inverse association with isoleucine was observed in another independent Finnish cohort of adolescents aged 13-19 years, where higher PA was also associated with lower lactate and higher levels of lipid-related metabolites, including phosphatidylcholines, sphingomyelins, and polyunsaturated fatty acids 23 . In a UK-based study, higher accelerometer-measured PA between 12 and 15 years was associated with lower pyruvate and alanine levels, and higher citrate and phenylalanine levels 25 . In this study, we aimed to deeply characterize the link between habitual PA, sedentary behavior, and the plasma metabolome in children. For this purpose, we investigated the associations between plasma metabolite levels, quantified through targeted metabolomics, and five PA variables, assessed by accelerometry, in children aged 5.5-11 years from the European Childhood Obesity Project (CHOP) cohort. We validated our findings in the Toddler Milk Intervention trial (ToMI) cohort, with both cohorts comprising participants from different European countries. We further examined whether PA-metabolite associations vary according to age, sex and body mass index (BMI), three factors that themselves associate with PA and with metabolite profiles independently of PA.

Methods

Study design and population

The present study investigated cross-sectional associations between PA and the plasma metabolome at multiple childhood ages within CHOP 28, 29 , a longitudinal cohort, and replicated the analyses in the independent longitudinal cohort of the ToMI trial 30 . Both studies were originally initiated as randomized controlled trials to investigate the effects of different protein levels in infant formula during the first (CHOP) and second (ToMI) year of life on body composition later in life. After the intervention, the children of both studies underwent multiple follow-up assessments. Detailed descriptions of the two studies are provided in Supplementary Material.

In the present study, we used anthropometric, plasma metabolomic, and PA assessment data from 515 CHOP children, collected at 5.5 (or 6), 8, and 11 years of age. Each child contributed one to three complete observations. For our validation analysis in ToMI, we used anthropometric, plasma metabolomic, and PA data obtained for 683 children at 2 and/or 6 years. In both cohorts, information on participants' sex and country of residence was available. BMI was calculated from weight and height measurements at 6, 8, and 11 years in CHOP and at 2 and 6 years in ToMI. To account for sex-and age-specific differences in body composition, zBMI scores were calculated using AnthroPlus software 31 . All research was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and the standards of good clinical practice. Ethics committees at each study center approved the BiomarKid study protocol (IISPV 095/2022, LMU Ethik-Kommission-Nr: 02070 & Project Nr: 555-15 UMIL 0009763, CHC Liege 13/48/671, CHMI n°682). Written informed consent from parents or legal guardians was obtained for children in both studies. In CHOP, the children's consent was additionally obtained at 8 and 11 years.

PA assessment

Habitual PA in CHOP was assessed at 6, 8, and 11 years using the SenseWear Armband 2 (Body Media Inc., Pittsburgh, PA). At each age, the device was worn over the right triceps muscle for at least 20 hours per day on three consecutive days. To be included in the study, the participants needed measurements from at least one weekday and one weekend day. Movement was recorded in 1-minute epochs. Data preprocessing was performed with Professional InnerView Software 6.1 32 . PA measurements were categorized into four intensity levels using metabolic equivalents of task (METs). Light PA (1.5 to < 4 METs), moderate PA (4 to < 6 METs), and vigorous PA (6 to < 9 METs) were defined according to recommendations by Trost et al 33 , while very vigorous PA was defined as ≥ 9 METs. Additionally, total PA was defined as duration of all activities ≥ 1.5 METs and moderateto-vigorous PA as duration in activities ≥ 4 METs. Sedentary behavior was defined as time spent in activities < 1.5 METs, after excluding periods recorded as lying and sleeping. Additional details and the rationale for the PA assessment steps have been published previously 34, 35 . For this study, PA levels [min/day] were normalized for device-wear-time by dividing each child's activity time by their device-wear-time and multiplying it with the mean device-wear-time across all children at all ages. Due to the presence of zero and identical values for vigorous and very vigorous PA across participants, these two PA parameters were categorized into tertiles and further analyzed as numerical variables (1, 2, 3). In ToMI, PA was assessed at 2 and 6 years with the ActiGraph wGT3X-BT accelerometer (ActiGraph LLC, Pensacola FL, USA). The ActiGraph was used with idle sleep mode and a sampling rate of 30 Hz. It was worn on the right hip for 7 consecutive days and taken off only for sleeping or water-based activities. If the device was not worn for at least 4 days, with a minimum of 6 hours of wear time per day, then the individual was excluded. Data from the ActiGraph device were imported into R with the read.gt3x package 36 and converted into activity counts (a measure of accelerometer-derived movement intensity) using the agcounts package 37 . Non-wear periods were identified and removed using the Choi algorithm 38 . Activity count data were categorized separately for measurements at 2 and 6 years of age, using the cut-points suggested by

Trost et al. (sedentary behavior: ≤ 25 ActiGraph acceleration intensity counts; total PA: > 25 counts; moderate-to-vigorous PA: ≥ 420 counts; epoch = 15s) 39 and Evenson et al. (sedentary: ≤ 25 counts; light PA: 26-573 counts; moderate PA: 574-1002 counts; vigorous PA: ≥ 1003 counts, moderate-tovigorous PA: ≥ 574 counts, epoch = 15s) 40 , respectively. Lastly, data was normalized for possible differences in device-wear-time, by dividing each child's duration of PA by their wear-time and multiplying it with average wear-time of all children. Further details on PA data preprocessing in ToMI were described previously 41 . For the present study, we used only sedentary behavior, total PA, and moderate-to-vigorous PA from ToMI, to ensure comparability between ages.

Blood sample collection

At each study site, blood samples for metabolite profiling were collected in EDTA tubes at 5.5, 8, and 11 years in CHOP and at 2 and 6 years in ToMI. After centrifugation (1500g for 10 min, at 4 -8 °C), plasma samples were stored at -80 ºC. Fasting status was recorded at blood collection. In CHOP, samples were collected in the fasted state (> 6 hours after the last meal), with the exception of samples from German participants at ages 5.5 and 8 years. In ToMI, the time since last meal was recorded at both time points and categorized as: < 3 hours, 3-6 hours, or > 6 hours. At age 2 years, most samples were obtained after fasting for either 3-6 hours (45%) or more than 6 hours (45%), whereas at age 6 years, most samples were collected after > 6 hours of fasting (74%). Besides fasting status, season at blood collection (summer, autumn, winter, spring) was recorded.

Metabolite profiling

Metabolite profiling of plasma samples (CHOP and ToMI, from all study centers) using tandem mass spectrometry coupled with liquid chromatography (LC-MS/MS) was conducted at the MS-Core Facility of the LMU Hospital Munich (Dr. von Hauner Children's Hospital). Following the longitudinal study design, CHOP samples were measured after completion of each study visit. For ToMI, 2-and 6-year samples were collected and analyzed together in one sequence. For both, CHOP and ToMI, quality control (QC) samples were co-analyzed in six wells of each 96-well plate batch. For CHOP, adult human plasma was used as QC, whereas for ToMI, a pooled sample comprising plasma from 2-and 6-year samples was used as QC. For both studies, two commercial adult human control plasma samples (CP-I and CP-II; Recipe) were also co-analyzed in duplicate on each analytical well plate. Below, we briefly summarize the applied analysis steps, which were similar for the two cohorts. Detailed information on protocols is provided in the Supplementary Material. An extended description of calibration standards, internal standards, MS-parameters, Q1/Q3 transition, chromatographic parameters and quantitation can be found in the Supporting Information of Hao et al. 42.

Sample preparation:

For metabolite extraction, 50 µL of thawed plasma samples were added to 450 µL of internal standard mixture in methanol. The 96-well plates with the clear metabolic extracts were stored at -20 °C (CHOP) or -30 °C (ToMI) before LC-MS/MS.

HPLC-ESI-QTRAP-MS/MS:

Different analytical methods were used for (i) targeted amino acid analysis after derivatization with n-butanol, (ii) targeted organic acid analysis, including α-aminoadipic acid and taurine, (iii) flow-injection-analysis (FIA) of phospholipids (phosphatidylcholines (PCs), lysophosphatidylcholines (LPCs), sphingomyelins (SMs)), in ToMI and CHOP, and acylcarnitines and hexoses in CHOP samples, (iv) targeted acylcarnitine analysis of ToMI samples after derivatization with nbutanol, (v) targeted homocysteine and cysteine analysis in CHOP, and (vi) free fatty acids (FFAs) analysis in CHOP. Data acquisition and data processing: For CHOP samples, Analyst 1.5.1 (Sciex) was used for data acquisition as well as quantitation. For ToMI samples, Analyst 1.6.3 (Sciex) was used for acquisition and MultiQuant 3.0 (Sciex) and Excel (Microsoft Office) were used for quantitation. Peak integration, background subtraction, isotope correction, and quantitation of FIA-MS/MS results were performed with an in-house R-script using R statistical software (version 4.1.0). For lipids, in particular, PCs, LPCs, and SMs, only the neutral loss corresponding to the head group and the total mass-to-charge ratio (m/z) of the molecule were determined, i.e., the length, desaturation, site and bond type of the linked fatty acid residues were not directly characterized. The 'a' and 'e' in the labels of these lipids describe acyl and ether bonds, respectively, and were chosen under the assumption that even-chain fatty acids and lower degrees of desaturation are more common than oddchain fatty acids or highly unsaturated species. The x:y after the lipid abbreviations denotes the total number of carbons (x) and double bonds (y) of all fatty acid chains in the lipid. Consequently, a variety of lipid species with the same m/z and neutral loss are measured under any specific label as has been shown previously 43 . As an example, the analyte labeled PC ae C40:1 is a sum measure of all isobaric PCs, which, among other PCs, include acylalkyl (ae) PCs with 41 carbons and 8 double bonds (41:8), diacyl PCs (aa) with 39 carbons and 1 double bond (39:1) and diacyl PCs (aa) with 40 carbons and 8 double bonds (40:8) when considering a mass resolution of < 0.7 Da). Metabolites that could not be confidently annotated were labeled as unknown, followed by the precursor ion m/z (e.g., Unknown_458.3).

Metabolomics data preprocessing and harmonization

A multi-step QC procedure has been applied to metabolomics data from CHOP and ToMI separately. Detailed descriptions of all steps are provided in Supplementary Material. Briefly, we checked the data set for sample outliers, performed batch correction using QC samples and estimated the remaining technical variation. Metabolites were excluded if they met the following criteria: (i) a coefficient of variation > 30% in CP-II samples (or in QC samples for acylcarnitines and phospholipids), (ii) a dispersion ratio, defined as the standard deviation of QC samples divided by that of study samples, greater than 50%. Metabolites with missingness exceeding 30% were excluded for the present analysis. Finally, missing values were imputed using a random forest approach. In CHOP, the final data set included 24 amino acids (including homocysteine and cysteine (on 2 different platforms)), 18 organic acids, 50 free fatty acids (FFAs), hexoses, 26 acylcarnitines (including free carnitine), 28 LPCs, 106 PCs, 37 SMs, as well as 25 unidentified lipid metabolites labelled in this study by their m/z. In ToMI, the final data set included 22 amino acids, 15 organic acids, 60 acylcarnitines (including free carnitine), and 167 phospholipids (91 PCs, 25 LPCs, and 51 SMs).

Statistical analysis

Before statistical analysis, metabolite outlier values were winsorized using a threshold of five times the interquartile range (Q1 -5×IQR and Q3 + 5×IQR). On the winsorized data, 49 biologically relevant metabolite sums and 42 metabolite ratios were calculated. Lastly, all metabolite values were log2-transformed and scaled separately for each age group and cohort. No outliers were identified for any PA variables based on the 5×IQR criterion. Correlations between PA variables in CHOP were assessed using Spearman correlation. Numerical PA variables and zBMI were scaled before statistical modelling. All statistical analyses were performed using R (version 4.4.0).

PA association analyses

We examined the association between metabolite levels and PA variables in the CHOP cohort by fitting linear mixed-effects models using the lmerTest R package (version 3.1-3) with restricted maximum likelihood estimation (REML = TRUE) and Satterthwaite approximation for degrees of freedom. To assess the assumption of linearity between metabolite levels and PA, we compared linear mixed-effects models with and without an additional quadratic term for continuous PA variables. Models were compared using likelihood ratio tests and differences in Akaike information criterion (ΔAIC), with false discovery rate (FDR) correction applied to account for multiple testing. Linearity of associations was assessed and confirmed for all models. In CHOP, the analysis was performed for 315 individual metabolites, 49 metabolite sums, and 42 metabolite ratios as outcomes (Supplementary Table 1). Each metabolite was modelled against five PA measures: sedentary behavior, total, moderate, vigorous, and very vigorous PA. Light PA was not included in the main analysis as we considered total PA as a proxy for predominantly low-intensity PA (Supplementary Fig. 1A). Sex, zBMI, and season were included as fixed effects, and visit, subject, and country as random effects. Country was included as a random effect, as it has previously been shown to explain substantial metabolomic variation in CHOP 44 . For 41 metabolites with reliable measurements (i.e., passing QC) at only one visit, subject and visit random effects were omitted from the models. The FDR across 2,030 tests in CHOP (5 PA variables × 406 metabolite measures) was controlled using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure, and FDR-adjusted p-values (qvalues) < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Metabolite associations were considered informative if (nominal) p values were < 0.05. For metabolite sums and ratios, an association was considered informative if p < 0.05 and the association of the ratio or sum had a lower p-value than the associations for their constituent metabolites (p-gain = min(p of constituents)/p of ratio or sum > 1) 45.

Sensitivity analyses

For sensitivity analysis, information on puberty status (prepubertal and pubertal) was added as an additional fixed effect in the linear mixed-effects models in CHOP. Pubertal status was assessed at age 11 years using the Pubertal Development Scale 46 . Children at 5.5 and 8 years were considered prepubertal, whereas at 11 years, 97 children were recorded as prepubertal and 258 as pubertal (including early, mid, and late pubertal). Subjects with no puberty information at 11 years (n = 6) were excluded from this sensitivity analysis. As an additional sensitivity analysis, fish intake was added as a fixed effect in models for metabolites presumably containing docosahexaenoic acid (DHA, 22:6), which is highly abundant in fish. These metabolites were LPC a C22:6, PC aa C38:6, PC aa C40:6, and PC aa C42:6 43 . Fish intake was assessed at ages 5.5 and 8 years using 3-day dietary records and at age 11 years using a food frequency questionnaire. Analysis was restricted to the subset of samples with dietary data (n = 775), and models without fish adjustment were refitted in the same subset for comparison.

Replication analysis in ToMI

For validation of our findings in the independent ToMI cohort, we performed the association analysis for all metabolites that were associated in CHOP with the corresponding PA variable (for moderate-to-vigorous PA: with moderate, vigorous or very vigorous PA) and that were also reliably measured in ToMI. Metabolites were modelled against three PA measures (sedentary behavior, total, and moderate-to-vigorous PA) using the same linear mixed-effects models as in CHOP with the exception that fasting duration (< 3 h, 3-6 h, > 6 h) was included as an additional fixed effect. The levels of the three PA measures did not differ significantly between the two ToMI intervention groups (all p > 0.05). In total, 67 association tests were conducted (11 for sedentary behavior, 16 for total PA, 40 for moderate-to-vigorous PA). Associations that were nominally significant and showed concordant directions of effect in CHOP and ToMI were considered replicated. Pvalues were adjusted (q values) for multiple testing across 67 tests using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure. To assess overall concordance between PA effect estimates in ToMI and CHOP cohorts, we additionally applied linear mixed-effects models to model the association of all reliably measured metabolites available in both cohorts (n = 177) with sedentary behavior, total PA, and moderate-to-vigorous PA, respectively. Metabolite sums and ratios were not included in this comparison. The correlation between β effect estimates derived from the ToMI and CHOP cohorts was quantified using Pearson's correlation coefficient (r). Statistical significance of the correlation was evaluated using stats::cor.test (R version 4.4.0), with p-values calculated based on the Student's t-distribution.

Interaction and stratified analyses

To assess whether associations between PA and metabolite levels differed by sex and BMI, we fitted interaction models including PA × sex and PA × zBMI terms in CHOP. For BMI interaction analysis, significant (p < 0.05) interactions were visualized using the interactions::interact_plot (version 1.2.0) function in R, displaying predicted metabolite levels across tertiles of the moderator (modx.values = "terciles") with 80% confidence bands (int.width = 0.80).

Additionally, linear mixed-effects model analyses were performed stratified by sex and by age/visit. For sex-stratified analysis, sex was removed as fixed effect, while for visitstratified analysis, visit and subject were removed as random effects. Sex-stratified results were visualized as dot plots displaying metabolite-specific effect estimates separately for males and females, generated using the ggplot2 (version 4.0.2) and patchwork (version 1.2.0) packages in R. Metabolites reliably measured only at one visit and metabolite sums and ratios were not included in the plot. For age-stratified analyses, only (single) metabolites measured at all three time points were included. To account for unequal sample sizes across visits, analyses were standardized to 280 samples, corresponding to the sample size of the 5.5-year visit. For the 5.5-year visit, the model was run once. For the 8-and 11year visits, which had larger sample sizes, we conducted 100 down-sampling iterations, each time randomly selecting 280 samples. Mean and SD of the number of significantly associated metabolites (p < 0.05) across 100 iterations and per visit were calculated. The difference in the number of significant hits at 8 and 11 years was tested with a paired Wilcoxon signed-rank test across 100 matched resampling iterations. To identify sex-and BMI-associated metabolites, we fitted linear mixed-effects models without PA variables using sex and zBMI as fixed effects and otherwise including the same covariates as in the PA association models.

Metabolite set enrichment analysis

To obtain an overview of metabolite associations with PA on the level of metabolite classes, we performed a metabolite set enrichment analysis for association tests performed above, i.e., β estimates for PA from each of the main models, β estimates for PA × sex from each interaction model, and β estimates for sex or zBMI from the corresponding models without PA. These β estimates were used to rank metabolites for the enrichment analysis. Metabolite sets were defined based on metabolite classes (see Supplementary Table 1). Metabolites with reliable measurements at only one visit were excluded from the analysis, as β estimates from such models were not comparable to those obtained from models including visit and subject as random effects. The analysis was performed using fgsea::fgseaMultilevel function (version 1.30.0), with parameters set to minSize = 2, maxSize = 500, and nPermSimple = 10000.

Associations between metabolites and habitual physical activity

To assess the relationship between metabolite levels and habitual PA, we applied linear mixed-effects models separately for each of the five PA measures (adjusted for sex, zBMI and season as fixed effects, and including subject, visit, and country as random effects). Of the 315 single metabolites tested, 62 were associated with at least one PA measure (p < 0.05). In addition, three of 49 metabolite sums and eight of 42 metabolite ratios met the significance criteria (p < 0.05 and p-gain > 1), resulting in a total of 73 metabolite variables associated with PA (Fig. 1, Supplementary Table 2). After correction for multiple testing, none of the associations remained significant at a false discovery rate (FDR) of q < 0.05. Results were very similar when we added puberty status as an additional covariate (Supplementary Table 2). Of the 73 associated metabolite variables, 46 (63%) were lower in children with more PA (Fig. 1). For metabolites associated with multiple PA measures, the direction of effect was consistent across total PA and the three PA intensities and opposite to that observed for sedentary behavior. BMI modulates the association between metabolites and physical activity

To investigate BMI-dependent differences in metabolite associations with PA, we used linear mixed-effects models of metabolite levels with a PA × zBMI interaction term (and sex added as one of the covariates). Nominally significant (p < 0.05, p-gain > 1) interactions were identified for 42 metabolites with sedentary behavior, 25 with total PA, 21 with moderate PA, 18 with vigorous PA, and 13 with very vigorous PA (Supplementary Table 11). β-Hydroxybutyric acid was the only metabolite showing a zBMI interaction for all PA variables, with a positive interaction effect for all variables except sedentary time. A positive interaction indicates that the PA-metabolite association becomes more positive with increasing zBMI. Depending on the magnitude of the interaction, this may reflect a stronger, weaker, or opposite-direction association at different levels of zBMI. To facilitate interpretation, we stratified zBMI per tertiles and plotted predicted PA-metabolite association for each tertile (Fig. 3A). For βhydroxybutyric acid, the interaction results indicate that higher levels were observed with more activity in children with higher zBMI, whereas the opposite trend (i.e., lower levels with higher activity) was seen in leaner children (Fig. 3A). For several metabolite variables, we observed nominally significant BMI interactions for multiple PA variables: Valine, leucine, and phenylalanine showed negative interaction effects with total and moderate PA, suggesting that lower levels of these amino acids associate with higher activity in children with higher zBMI, while this effect is weaker or even in opposite direction in leaner children (Fig. 3A). Similarly, multiple carnitines, including free carnitine, CPT1 ratio (free carnitine/[CAR 16:0 + CAR 18:0]), and CAR 3:0, showed negative interaction effects with total and/or very vigorous PA in children with greatest zBMI whereas associations were weaker or positive for leaner children. Conversely, CAR 18:2 demonstrated the opposite pattern, being inversely associated with moderate and vigorous PA in leaner children but not in those with higher zBMI. Among phospholipids, SM C36:3 showed zBMI interactions for total, moderate, and very vigorous PA, exhibiting more positive associations with PA at higher zBMI. Similar as for sex, we additionally assessed the associations between metabolites and zBMI for context: 84 metabolite variables (q < 0.05) were found associated with zBMI. Among others, DHA-containing lipids (e.g., PC aa C42:6, PC aa C38:6, and LPC a C22:6 43 ), PC ae C34:3 and LPC a C18:2 were generally lower in children with higher zBMI. While Val/LPC a C18:1, CPT1 ratio and SM C34:2 were higher in those with higher zBMI (Supplementary Fig. 5A, Supplementary Table 12). Discussion

In this metabolome-wide analysis, we show that device-measured habitual PA is associated with a metabolic imprint implicating various pathways, including lipid and BCAA metabolism, already in childhood. Associations for 12 metabolite variables replicated in an independent cohort, suggesting the consistency of these associations from toddlerhood to early puberty. The identified associations varied depending on PA intensity and became more numerous with age. Interestingly, PA associations partially differed between the two sexes and across the BMI range.

Metabolite associations with habitual physical activity span various pathways

Carnitines were lower in children who exercised more: Children who spent more time in higher intensity activities, which include exercise, showed lower levels of free carnitine and multiple acylcarnitines. These metabolites are involved in transporting fatty acids from the cytosol into mitochondria for β-oxidation 50 . Their circulating levels are commonly used as markers of mitochondrial fatty acid oxidation, with higher levels often reflecting incomplete β-oxidation and lower levels suggesting greater metabolic efficiency [51] [52] [53].

Given that exercise is known to improve β-oxidation and mitochondrial capacity 9 , these associations may therefore reflect greater oxidative efficiency in children who exercise more. Amino acids, including BCAAs and their degradation products, were lower in more active children: Similar to acylcarnitines, amino acids, including the BCAA isoleucine, were lower in children who spent more time in higher intensity activities. Moreover, two branched-chain α-keto acids, which are products of BCAA catabolism 54 , were inversely associated with total PA. In line with our results, multiple PA observational studies have reported lower BCAA levels in more active individuals in adulthood 18, 21, 22 and childhood or adolescence [23] [24] [25] . Enhanced uptake and oxidation of BCAAs in contracting skeletal muscle during prolonged or extensive activity could explain these observations. Accordingly, activation of the branched-chain α-keto acid dehydrogenase complex, the second and rate-limiting step in BCAA oxidation, has been proposed as a potential mechanism 55 . Supporting this hypothesis, a twin study found that more active individuals exhibited higher expression of genes involved in BCAA degradation in both skeletal muscle and adipose tissue compared with their less active co-twins 56 . Also, the reason for lower plasma glutamate levels in individuals who exercise more, as observed in CHOP and seen consistently in adults [17] [18] [19] [20] , could be connected to BCAA catabolism, as glutamate serves as a nitrogen acceptor in this process and in ammonia detoxification more generally 57 . In addition to higher BCAA oxidation, lower circulating BCAA levels in children who exercise more may reflect enhanced protein synthesis in muscle [58] [59] [60] [61].

LPCs, the products of PC hydrolysis, were higher in more active children: Various LPCs, including LPC a C14:0, were robustly associated with total PA and (inversely) with sedentary behavior. LPCs are generated from PCs -the most abundant phospholipids in mammalian cell membranes and lipoproteins -either through lecithin-cholesterol acyltransferase (LCAT), which transfers a fatty acid from PC to free cholesterol, producing cholesterol esters and LPC, or via hydrolysis of PC primarily by phospholipase A2 (PLA2), which removes one fatty acyl chain and releases an LPC 62 . Muscular PLA2 activity has been shown to increase after exercise in rats 63 , supporting a role for exercise-induced PLA2 activity. Consistent with this, we observed lower LPCs/PCs ratios in more sedentary children. In contrast to LPCs, we did not see a consistent pattern for PCs. We therefore hypothesize that in sedentary children, lipolytic activity is potentially reduced due to lower demand for lipid oxidation, potentially resulting in decreased PC hydrolysis and consequently lower circulating LPC levels and a reduced LPCs/PCs ratio. Our findings are consistent with prior literature in adults. In intervention studies, LPCs, including LPC a C14:0, have been reported to increase with the exercise intervention 20, 64 . Furthermore, in observational studies, higher plasma LPC levels have been reported in adults with more habitual PA after BMI adjustment 19 . Higher plasma LPC levels have also been associated with better mitochondrial function in skeletal muscle cross-sectionally 65 , and faster decline in plasma LPCs has been associated with faster decline in skeletal muscle mitochondrial function 66 . While it is important to keep in mind that the relationship between mitochondrial function and LPC levels is complex and not fully understood yet 67 , we speculate that the observed PA-related increases in circulating LPCs may contribute to the maintenance of mitochondrial function in active children. DHA-containing PCs were lower in more active children: Some of the most consistent PA associations involved DHA-containing PCs, which were lower in more active children independent of fish intake, with PC aa C40:6 also replicating in ToMI. The same direction has been reported in adults, where lower levels of the corresponding phosphatidylethanolamine PE(40:6) were found in individuals with higher habitual PA 19 . Moreover, PC aa C40:6 levels decreased in a 10-week exercise intervention 20 . These observations may reflect a redistribution rather than a depletion of DHA in more active individuals. While greater weekly running distance in non-elite adults has been associated with a gradual decrease in the omega-3 index in blood 68 , regular exercise training has been shown to increase DHA content in phospholipids of skeletal muscle membranes 69 . Omega-3 fatty acids, including DHA, exert beneficial effects on skeletal muscle primarily through incorporation into membrane phospholipids, where they are associated with enhanced muscle protein synthesis and mitochondrial function 70, 71 . A constant remodeling of fatty acid contents within PCs in relation to catabolic processes in skeletal muscle induced by fasting (transiently) or insulin deficiency has been shown in a mouse study. While these processes reduced DHA-containing PCs, the overall PUFA-containing PC pool remained stable 72 . This supports the hypothesis of a state-dependent redistribution of DHA that may explain the observed lower circulating DHA-containing PCs in more active children through PA-induced uptake of these PCs from circulation into muscle. Beyond individual lipid metabolites, we identified robust associations of the ratio Val/LPC a C18:1 with multiple PA measures. Given its reported associations with insulin resistance and prevalent and incident type 2 diabetes in adults 48 , this ratio is of particular interest in the context of PA and metabolic health. The lower Val/LPC a C18:1 ratios in more active children in our study may reflect better insulin sensitivity. Notably, neither valine nor LPC a C18:1 alone was associated with PA, suggesting that their ratio captures a combined metabolic signal from BCAA and PC metabolism that is not apparent in either metabolite individually. Val/LPC a C18:1 may therefore be a potential marker for capturing metabolic health that is modifiable through PA in children.

Metabolite association patterns vary by physical activity intensity

The overlap in metabolite associations across the five PA variables broadly reflected the correlations between the corresponding activity measures, indicating that similar PA intensities are accompanied by similar metabolic profiles. Sedentary behavior and total PA showed substantial overlap, with shared metabolite associations consistently displaying opposite effect directions, as reported previously 18, 21, 25 . Conversely, the relatively small overlap between the associations for moderate to very vigorous PA, capturing exerciserelated activity, and total PA, which constitutes mostly of light activity, suggests that activities of different intensities may engage partly distinct metabolic pathways: While TCA cycle metabolites and DHA-containing PCs were associated with activity across intensity levels, acylcarnitines and amino acids were associated predominantly with higher intensity activity and LPCs predominantly with total PA.

Metabolite associations with habitual physical activity depend on sex

Our findings extend previous evidence of sex differences in the childhood plasma metabolome 73, 74 , body composition 75 , and PA patterns, with boys generally spending less time in light PA but more time in moderate-to-vigorous PA than girls 76 . We further show that associations between habitual PA and several lipid metabolites, specifically PC ae metabolites, FFAs and LPCs, also differ between boys and girls, independently of BMI. For FFAs and LPCs, more active girls generally had higher metabolite levels, whereas more active boys generally had lower levels. In adults, lipid classes have been reported to respond to exercise in a sex-specific way, both acutely, with FFAs and LPCs among the metabolite classes showing the strongest sex differences 77 , and with habitual training, where, for example, 24 weeks of moderate-intensity exercise increased circulating triglyceride species more in women than in men 64 . One possible explanation for these findings is the known sex difference in substrate use. Females rely more on lipid metabolism during both exercise and fasting, whereas males generally utilize carbohydrates to a greater extent 78 . In adults, this difference is driven in part by estrogen, which promotes lipid oxidation, and is reflected in the higher intramuscular triglyceride content and greater fatty-acid uptake capacity (FAT/CD36) in females 79 , alongside higher glycolytic capacity, lower oxidative capacity, and lower skeletal-muscle insulin sensitivity in males 80 . Because in our study, blood samples were largely collected after fasting, circulating FFA levels likely reflect the balance between adipose lipolysis and tissue uptake. Within this framework, the higher FFAs in more active girls may reflect greater lipid mobilization and turnover, whereas the lower FFAs in more active boys may be linked to stronger suppression of fasting lipolysis due to improved insulin sensitivity. However, this interpretation is largely derived from adult studies and its relevance to childhood remains uncertain.

Metabolite associations with habitual physical activity depend on BMI

Various metabolite associations with PA, in particular those implicated in energy-related processes such as acylcarnitines, BCAAs, and the ketone body β-hydroxybutyric acid, differed depending on BMI, suggesting that habitual PA may be accompanied by different patterns of substrate utilization in children with different body weight status independent of sex. For example, among children with lower BMI, higher activity was associated with lower circulating levels of the long chain acylcarnitine CAR 18:2, whereas the model suggests no such association among children with higher BMI. Long-chain acylcarnitines are frequently higher in obesity, where they are thought to reflect impaired fatty acid utilization 52, 81 . Thus, their PA-associated reduction seen only in leaner children may indicate a greater capacity to mobilize and oxidize fatty acids in response to habitual activity. The absence of this association in children with higher BMI may indicate a reduced ability to adapt lipid oxidation pathways to increasing PA. However, BMI also modified the associations of PA with free carnitine and the CPT1 ratio (free carnitine/[CAR 16:0 + CAR 18:0]), a newborn screening marker with higher values indicating lower CPT1 enzyme activity 82 . With more PA, leaner children showed a higher ratio, whereas children with high BMI showed no or opposite associations. As reduced CPT1 activity has been linked to decreased lipid oxidation in skeletal muscles of obese adults 83 , this pattern does not align with the interpretation above, and the underlying mechanisms remain unclear. Evidence for BMI-related differences in lipid utilization for energy metabolism was also observed for β-hydroxybutyric acid. Although β-hydroxybutyric acid was not associated with sex, BMI or habitual PA in the pairwise analyses, the interaction analyses revealed that its levels tended to be higher in more active children with higher sex-and age-adjusted BMI and in boys but tended to be lower in more active children with lower BMI and in girls. β-Hydroxybutyric acid is a ketone body produced in the liver when dietary carbohydrate availability is low (e.g., during fasting or prolonged exercise) and energy production must rely on fatty acids. Its plasma levels typically reflect the balance between hepatic production and utilization in peripheral tissues such as skeletal muscle 84 . The positive association between PA and β-hydroxybutyric acid in children with higher BMI and in boys may indicate relatively greater ketone production or reduced utilization, whereas the inverse association in children with lower BMI and in girls may reflect lower production or more efficient peripheral uptake and utilization. The latter would be consistent with the increased capacity of exercise-trained skeletal muscle to utilize ketone bodies 85 , which could result in lower circulating concentrations despite higher metabolic flux. BMI-dependent association patterns were also observed for phenylalanine and the BCAAs valine and leucine. Among children with higher BMI, levels of these amino acids were lower in more active children, whereas, in leaner children, the effect was in the opposite direction. Higher circulating BCAA concentrations have been consistently observed in obese children 86 and adults 87 and are considered to reflect alterations in BCAA catabolism linked to mitochondrial dysfunction 88, 89 . Conversely, exercise has been linked to enhanced mitochondrial function and oxidative metabolism, specifically in skeletal muscle 9 . In this context, the lower amino acid levels observed with increasing PA may indicate enhanced amino acid oxidation or utilization among children with higher BMI. On the other hand, exercise enhances muscle mass and protein synthesis 58 and higher circulating BCAA levels have repeatedly been associated with greater lean mass in adults [59] [60] [61] , which may be explained by greater BCAA release from skeletal muscle during fasting. Therefore, in children with lower BMI, the higher BCAA levels observed in more active individuals may partly reflect relatively greater muscle mass compared with their less active (lean) peers. Broadly, these findings raise the possibility that children with higher BMI rely less on lipid oxidation and more on amino acid catabolism during PA. Although the underlying mechanisms cannot be established from our observational data, these results are consistent with the concept that obesity is associated with reduced metabolic flexibility and altered substrate partitioning, which may modify the metabolic response to habitual PA.

Metabolic imprint of habitual physical activity increases with age

In our study, the number of PA-associated metabolites increased with age, suggesting growing PA-related metabolic effects potentially reflecting the maturation of muscle systems, including anaerobic metabolism, sympathetic nervous system activation, and ability to recruit higher-threshold motor units from lower to higher capacities over time in childhood [90] [91] [92] . Furthermore, muscle glycogen and phosphocreatine stores 90, 93 and muscle fiber size increase with age 94 . Consistent with the age-dependent effect observed in our study, a longitudinal study reported an approximately 15% increase in leucine and isoleucine levels from 11 to 18 years of age among consistently low-active individuals. In contrast, no change was observed among high-active girls, potentially suggesting a stronger metabolic impact of sedentary behavior at older ages 24.

Strengths and Limitations

The rich phenotypic and metabolomic data from children at different ages, including accelerometer-assessed PA, its metabolome-wide analysis approach, and the replication of results in an independent cohort are particular strengths of our study. Nonetheless, it also comes with various notable limitations. First, PA was assessed only over a limited number of days, and short-term monitoring may not fully capture long-term behavior. Moreover, wearing an accelerometer may itself influence PA during the measurement period. Second, the applied metabolomics approach primarily targeted specific pathways and therefore does not provide a comprehensive coverage of the entire metabolome. Moreover, the measurements were performed in separate batches mostly following the times of sample collection. Thus, differences in metabolite profiles between study visits may partly reflect technical variability, rather than capturing purely age-related biological changes. Despite the longitudinal design of the study, this restricts the direct interpretation of intra-individual changes. To cope with this issue, we included visit as a random effect in the linear mixedeffects models. Third, the ToMI study used for replicating our results differed in various aspects from the CHOP cohort, including methodological differences (accelerometer devices and data processing methods, and, for certain metabolite classes, metabolomics platforms) and differences in age ranges as ToMI data was on toddlers and young children while CHOP data extended into puberty, representing developmental stages with markedly different PA patterns. Replicated PA-metabolite associations across these cohorts despite these differences therefore indicates effects that are conserved across distinct developmental stages in childhood. However, as both the discovery and the replication cohort were limited to participants from European countries, generalizability of the findings to populations with different ethnic and cultural backgrounds may still be limited. Lastly, none of the metabolite associations survived FDR correction, likely reflecting the modest effect sizes expected for habitual PA and the substantial multiple-testing burden inherent to metabolomics analyses. Also, the fewer associations at 5.5 years may partly reflect the six-month gap between PA assessment and blood sampling at this visit rather than age differences. Finally, due to the association-based nature of our analyses, no causal relationship between metabolite levels and habitual PA can be inferred from our results. Consequently, individual associations should be interpreted with caution. However, the convergence of findings at the metabolite-class level, together with replication of several associations in an independent cohort and consistency with previous observational studies, suggests that the reported patterns may reflect underlying biological relationships warranting further investigation.

Conclusion

The World Health Organization 2020 guidelines on PA and sedentary behavior for children and adolescents identified key research gaps to better inform future recommendations 95.

Our study addresses three of proposed priorities: the use of device-based measures to quantify habitual PA, the investigation of intensity-specific associations, and the evaluation of subgroup differences. Using a metabolome-wide approach with accelerometer-based PA assessment, we identified and replicated several metabolite signatures associated with habitual PA in children, suggesting that PA is reflected in the plasma metabolome already early in life. These associations varied by PA intensity and differed by age, sex, and BMI. Future studies in larger, longitudinal cohorts integrating health outcomes are needed to further investigate the clinical relevance of these findings and to inform effective prevention strategies early in life.
