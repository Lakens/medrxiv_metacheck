How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Full text

Abstract

Adolescent psychotic-like experiences, particularly those that are distressing and persistent (dpPLEs), may elevate risk for a range of psychiatric disorders, yet the relative predictive contributions of brain structure, genetics, and environment to dpPLEs remain unknown. Using four timepoints from 7,399 children in the Adolescent Brain Cognitive Development (ABCD) Study, we developed stacked ensemble machine-learning models integrating cortical morphometry and whole-brain microstructural properties, polygenic risk scores, family psychiatric history, and childhood environmental factors to predict adolescent dpPLEs. Environmental predictors alone, driven by area deprivation index, neighborhood drug-related crime, and cumulative early adversity, were the strongest predictive domain (ROC-AUC = 0.687 ± 0.003), outperforming genetic (0.619 ± 0.020) and all imaging models (0.588-0.650). Adding genetic risk to environmental features produced the best-performing model overall (ROC-AUC = 0.697 ± 0.007), whereas adding neuroimaging yielded no further incremental gain (ROC-AUC=0.683-0.696). Beyond environmental exposures, family history of psychosis, crossdisorder polygenic risk, and both globally distributed and regionally specific imaging components contributed to prediction. Childhood environmental factors thus capture a core predictive signal preceding dpPLEs, while genetic and neuroimaging features added comparatively modest discriminative prediction value, but may offer complementary biological insights.

INTRODUCTION

Psychotic-like experiences (PLEs), encompassing subclinical perceptual distortions, disorganized communication and unusual thought content, are reported in approximately 9% of U.S. adults, and 17-60% of youth experience some form of PLEs 1, 2 . Although PLEs are relatively common in the general population, experiences that are both distressing and persistent across time may capture a subgroup of youth at higher risk for later mental health concerns 3, 4 . This is supported by previous research identifying that youth with distressing persistent PLEs (dpPLEs) exhibit the most pronounced impairments across cognitive, neuroimaging, environmental, and functional domains compared to youth with either lower levels or nondistressing/transient PLEs 5 . DpPLEs also confer risk for outcomes in adulthood that span a transdiagnostic spectrum beyond psychotic disorders to encompass mood, anxiety, and behavioral conditions 4 , underscoring the broad clinical relevance of dpPLEs and the importance of studying premorbid factors early in development and before the emergence of dpPLEs.

Adolescent PLEs have been linked to genetic factors, with twin heritability estimates ranging from 15-59% 6 . Polygenic risk markers derived from large-scale genome-wide association studies (GWAS) for schizophrenia, bipolar disorder, and major depression have also been associated with PLEs in childhood 7 , adolescence 8 , and young adulthood 9 . This suggests that genetic vulnerability to adult-onset psychiatric disorders may already contribute to PLEs in youth 10, 11 . Given the transdiagnostic predictors 12 and outcomes 3, 4, 13, 14 associated with PLEs, recent GWAS highlighting shared genetic architecture across major psychiatric disorders [15] [16] [17] may be particularly relevant to integrate as early genetic risk markers for dpPLEs.

Many of the genetic variants that shape mental health risk also shape brain structure 18, 19 . Previous work has shown that brain structure is linked with polygenic risk for major psychiatric disorders and PLEs 7, 20 . Beyond volume, differences between youth with dpPLEs and low levels of PLEs have been found in cortical surface area and thickness 21 , consistent with independent samples showing widespread structural abnormalities across frontal, temporal, and parietal regions in youth with psychosis-spectrum symptoms [22] [23] [24] . However, these analyses have relied predominantly on predefined regions of interest and traditional morphometric measures, potentially obscuring signals that traverse regional boundaries or reside in developmentally sensitive microstructural features 25.

Neuroimaging-derived phenotypes that index microstructural tissue properties, particularly residing within deeper subcortical structures and peri-cortically, are dynamically changing during adolescence and may have developmental and theoretical relevance to dpPLE prediction [26] [27] [28] [29] . They also may provide complementary, as well as distinct, neuroanatomical information relative to cortical morphometric measures such as cortical thickness, surface area, and sulcal depth. For instance, cortical gray-white contrast, measured from commonly acquired T1-weighted acquisitions and capturing patterns of intracortical myelin 26, 30 , has been shown to capture case-control group differences in patients with psychotic disorders with greater sensitivity than other cortical metrics (e.g., cortical thickness) 26, 31 , and tracks psychosis-related longitudinal outcomes 32 . Multi-shell diffusion models, such as restriction spectrum imaging 33 , also offer a valuable framework to measure developmentally sensitive changes in brain tissue microstructure, such as putative markers of cellular density, myelin, neuroinflammation, and glial morphology, which have been shown to change substantially across late childhood and early adolescence 27, 28 . Measures of restricted or intracellular diffusion in particular can capture microstructural changes beyond commonly studied white matter tracts, including within subcortical structures and cortex, and may be meaningful predictors of early and dynamic brain structural changes in dpPLEs. These tissue microstructural properties have not yet been examined prior to emergence of adolescent dpPLEs.

Environmental context and adversity also substantially contribute to dpPLE emergence and play an important role in neurodevelopmental and mental health outcomes 14, 34, 35 . For example, urban residence is associated with a two-to three-fold increased risk of psychosis spectrum outcomes, likely driven by correlated exposures including deprivation, social fragmentation, and toxin exposure, and may also be relevant for dpPLEs 36, 37 . Measures of urbanicity, area deprivation, poverty rate, and lead exposure risk have also been associated with PLEs in youth 38, 39 . Cumulative stress, including discrimination and adverse childhood events, further compounds risk for PLE emergence 14 which may impact brain circuitry important for emotional regulation and cognitive development 40 . Indeed, prior work has shown that brain volume mediates 11-25% of the association between environmental adversity and PLEs 38 , highlighting the importance of both neural and environmental pathways in shaping PLE risk. In the same vein, findings from a twin adolescent sample from the UK found that environmental and genetic factors interacted to further elevate risk for PLEs, emphasizing that these domains do not act in isolation 41.

Despite evidence that brain structural patterns, genetic risk, and environmental factors are all associated with PLEs in adolescence, these three domains have not yet been examined together in a predictive framework. Large prospective studies, such as the Adolescent Brain Cognitive Development (ABCD) Study, offer a powerful lens to study such risk signatures prospectively across high-dimensional neuroimaging, genetic, and environmental factors. Here, we leverage baseline data from approximately 7000 participants in the ABCD Study (ages 9-10) to build an interpretable multimodal predictive framework for the emergence of later dpPLEs in adolescence through three aims: (1) to test whether later dpPLE risk is preceded by a detectable baseline vulnerability signature across multimodal measures; (2) to compare the predictive signal contributed by whole-brain structural MRI, diffusion MRI, genetic risk, and environmental exposures; (3) to identify imaging and non-imaging features that consistently drive model predictions. We hypothesized that complementary, multimodal integration would improve prediction beyond any single domain, and that the novel integration of high-dimensional cortical anatomy and whole-brain tissue microstructure may elucidate neurobiological patterns that may be most relevant for prediction of dpPLEs and other mental health concerns.

METHODS

Participants.

Data were drawn from the ABCD Study 42 , a prospective, multisite, longitudinal investigation of child brain development and health that enrolled 11,878 children aged 9-10 years across 21 U.S. sites. The ABCD Study includes data collection spanning neuroimaging, genetics, environmental and geocoded exposures, physical and mental health, cognition, and family history, with participants assessed at baseline and at regular follow-up visits. The design and recruitment details of the ABCD Study have been described previously 43, 44.

Identifying youth with dpPLEs

Psychotic-like experiences were assessed annually from baseline through 6-year followups using the Prodromal Questionnaire-Brief Child Version (PQ-BC), a 21-item self-report measure validated for use in this age range 2 . For each endorsed item, children reported associated distress, yielding both a total symptom score and a distress-weighted score at each visit. Consistent with prior ABCD work 5 , participants were classified into the dpPLE group (n=322) if their PQ-BC distress score was > 1.96 standard deviations (SDs) above the mean for at least two of their last three available waves of data. Individuals with low PQ-BC severity scores < 0.50 SDs below the mean across the three last available waves were classified into the low PLE group (n=7,077). Given that we were interested in evaluating features that were most predictive of dpPLEs compared to having a low level of PLEs, participants with transient or non-distressing PLEs (n=1731) were excluded from the binary outcome (see Supplementary Materials for details on participant inclusion/exclusion).

Predictor domains

Baseline predictors across neuroimaging, genetic, and environmental domains are described in Supplementary Table 1, with a schematic of all imaging features provided in Supplementary Figure 1.

Neuroimaging features

Imaging predictors comprised vertex-wise structural MRI (sMRI) measures and voxelwise diffusion MRI (dMRI) measures, processed through the standardized ABCD imaging pipeline 45 . Vertex-wise structural features included cortical surface area, sulcal depth, gray-white contrast (GWC), and cortical thickness. Voxel-wise diffusion features were derived from restriction spectrum imaging (RSI) model fit to diffusion weighted imaging and consisted of restricted normalized directional (RND) and restricted normalized isotropic (RNI) signal fractions 46 . Modality-specific quality control inclusion flags were applied before analysis as recommended by the ABCD Study Consortium 26.

Environmental features

Environmental predictors captured household economic hardship, neighborhood and residential context, and exposure to adverse life events. Financial adversity was operationalized as a summed score across seven caregiver-reported indicators of material and economic hardship. Neighborhood safety was indexed using three caregiver-reported items. Geocoded contextual measures were derived from external data linked to participants' residential locations, as described in previous work 47, 48 . These neighborhood-based factors included years at current residence, drug-related neighborhood crime, national percentile on the Area Deprivation Index (ADI), estimated lead-exposure risk, and neighborhood poverty rate. Adverse life events were summarized as a cumulative count across 33 candidate events, counting only events endorsed as experienced, appraised as negative, and reported to have affected the child at least somewhat. In total, eight environmental predictors were derived from 148 unique variables (Supplementary Table 1).

Genetic and family history features

The genetic domain comprised family history variables and polygenic scores (PGS). Family history of psychopathology was derived from caregiver reports of relevant behaviors in any blood relative. Included features were indicators of any psychiatric history regardless of degree of relatedness, and of having a first-degree relative that had experienced depression, mania, hallucinations, and/or delusions. GWAS summary statistics for schizophrenia 49 , major depression 50 , and bipolar disorder 51 , and a cross-disorder psychopathology score (P-factor) 15 aggregating shared liability across 14 psychiatric disorders were leveraged to calculate PGS using PGScs 52, 53 . GWAS sample details are included in Supplementary Table 2. PGS derived from predominantly European-ancestry GWAS show reduced portability to multi-ancestry samples; thus, we additionally compared prediction of dpPLEs from PGS derived in European and non-European subsamples separately. See Supplementary Materials for more details of PGS calculation and genomic ancestry estimation.

Overview of predictive modeling

We trained interpretable machine learning models to predict dpPLE or lowPLE outcomes from each predictor domain individually and from multimodal combinations, following recommended practices in machine learning models in psychiatry 54 . We used Shapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) 55 to quantify the contribution of individual features and modalities to model predictions. All analyses were implemented in Python 3 using scikit-learn 56 ; base and meta-level classifiers were regularized logistic regression models. Cross-validation framework.

Predictive performance was estimated using repeated, family-based nested crossvalidation (Figure 1A). We performed 20 repeats of 5-fold stratified cross-validation, yielding 100 outer splits, with hyperparameters selected in a 5-fold inner loop nested within each outer training fold, so that performance was always estimated on data unseen during both model fitting and model selection. Stratification preserved the proportion of dpPLE to lowPLE cases within folds. Family identifiers were used as grouping variables so that siblings were never split across training and test sets, preventing leakage from shared genetic and common environmental factors within a family 57, 58.

Preprocessing of input features.

All predictors were preprocessed within the cross-validation framework, with every parameter estimated on the training fold only and applied unchanged to the held-out test fold (Figure 1B). Vertex-wise sMRI and voxel-wise dMRI maps were residualized for baseline age, sex, study site, scanner, and the first ten genetic ancestry principal components using a generalized least-squares estimator implemented in the Fast and Efficient Mixed-effects Algorithm (FEMA) 55 , with family as a random effect, then standardized and reduced by principal component analysis (PCA) to retain 15 components per feature map. Non-imaging predictors were residualized by ordinary least squares regression: genetic and family history features accounted for baseline age, sex, study site, and the first ten genetic principal components, and environmental features accounted for baseline age, sex, and study site. Full preprocessing parameters are provided in Supplementary Materials.

Base-model development

Separate base models were trained for each predictor domain, including one model per imaging feature map (e.g., surface area, sulcal depth, GWC, cortical thickness for sMRI; RNI and RND for dMRI), each taking 15 principal components as input (Figure 1C). All base models were regularized logistic regression classifiers. The penalty type (L1, L2, or elastic net), regularization strength, L1 ratio (for elastic net) and class weighting were selected by grid search in the inner 5-fold cross-validation loop, and the best configuration was refit on the full training fold. Solver settings, the hyperparameter grid, and model-selection metrics are provided in Supplementary Materials.

Stacked multimodal models

To integrate information across domains, we used stacked generalization 59, 60 (Figure 1D). Stacked generalization is a powerful method that uses a meta-learner to combine the predictions of multiple base models, and has shown success as a classification tool in various biomedical signal processing contexts 61 . Each base model returned a single predicted probability of dpPLE group membership per participant, and these predicted probabilities (hereafter metafeatures) were treated as predictors in a further regularized logistic regression (the meta-learner) that learned how much weight each domain should carry. Stacking was preferred over fitting a single logistic regression to the pooled feature set because the domains differed substantially in dimensionality and signal density (15 imaging principal components against 8 genetic and 8 environmental predictors) and stacking allowed each domain to be tuned to its own optimal penalty type and strength. To prevent leakage, meta-features for training participants were generated out-of-fold using a further level of 5-fold family-grouped stratified cross-validation within each outer training fold. Meta-features for the outer test fold were generated from base models refitted on the full outer training fold. The meta-learner was tuned over the same grid as the base models.

Five configurations were evaluated: (i) imaging only; (ii) imaging plus genetic; (iii) imaging plus environmental; (iv) non-imaging only (genetic plus environmental); and (v) a full multimodal model combining all three domains. All configurations were fit on identical participants and evaluated on identical outer test folds, so differences in performance are attributable only to which domains were available. Configuration (i) supplied a single metafeature and therefore was equivalent to a recalibration of the imaging base model.

Prediction performance metrics

Performance was evaluated on the held-out outer test folds (Figure 1E). The receiver operating characteristic -area under the curve (ROC-AUC) and precision recall AUC (PR-AUC) were computed within each held-out test fold, averaged across the five folds of each repeat, and reported as the mean ± SD of the resulting 20 repeat-level estimates. Because the five test folds within a repeat are mutually exclusive and exhaustive, every participant contributed exactly once to each repeat-level estimate. The reported SD should therefore be interpreted as the stability of the performance estimate under repeated random partitioning of this sample, rather than as a confidence interval for generalization to a new sample.

Model interpretability (SHAP)

Feature contributions were quantified with SHAP 55 , computed within each outer split for held-out participants only (Figure 1F). For imaging-only models, fitting occurred in PCA space, so SHAP values indexed the contribution of the 15 principal components retained within each training fold rather than individual vertices or voxels. For stacked models, SHAP values quantified the contribution of each base-model prediction to the meta-model output, placing imaging, genetic, and environmental information on a common scale. Feature contributions are reported as the mean absolute SHAP value per feature, computed within each held-out test fold, averaged across the five folds of each repeat, and summarized as the mean + SD of the resulting 20 repeat-level estimates, matching the aggregation used for performance metrics. For each imaging feature map, the loadings of the component with the highest mean absolute SHAP value were displayed on the brain alongside its component-level SHAP values, reflecting descriptive maps of what the leading component encodes spatially rather than voxelwise importance maps.

Single-domain prediction performance

The environmental model alone achieved the highest prediction performance, with an ROC-AUC of 0.687 ± 0.003 (PR-AUC = 0.091 ± 0.001) (Figure 2A; Supplementary Table 3). This exceeded every individual imaging modality by an AUC of 0.037-0.099, and exceeded the genetic model (ROC-AUC = 0.619 ± 0.020; PR-AUC = 0.080 ± 0.006) by 0.068. Notably, the genetic domain, comprising polygenic scores and family psychiatric history, performed comparably to the best-performing vertex-wise cortical measure (both ROC-AUC = 0.619). Note that performance of non-imaging domains was driven largely by participants of predominantly European ancestry (environment: ROC-AUC= 0.712 ± 0.044; genetics: 0.679 ± 0.054) compared to participants of non-European-like ancestry (environment: 0.593 ± 0.046; genetics: 0.573 ± 0.050) (Supplementary Table 4).

Across the six imaging measures (e.g. four cortical sMRI measures and two dMRI measures), the prediction of adolescent dpPLEs was modest (ROC-AUC range 0.588-0.650; PR-AUC 0.065-0.087; Figure 2B; Supplementary Table 5). Voxel-wise microstructural measures outperformed vertex-wise cortical measures: restricted normalized directional diffusion (RND) was the single best-performing imaging modality (ROC-AUC = 0.650 ± 0.005; PR-AUC = 0.087 ± 0.004), followed by restricted normalized isotropic (RNI; ROC-AUC = 0.611 ± 0.006; PR-AUC = 0.082 ± 0.003). Among the vertex-wise cortical measures, surface area performed best (ROC-AUC = 0.619 ± 0.006), followed by sulcal depth (0.599 ± 0.011), T1-weighted gray-white matter contrast (0.595 ± 0.010) and cortical thickness (0.588 ± 0.008). Repeat-level variability was low for all imaging models (SD ≤ 0.011), indicating stable but limited discrimination. Stacked multimodal prediction performance

We next tested which combination of domains improved prediction through stacked ensembles. Combining the two non-imaging domains yielded the highest performance observed in any model configuration (Env + Gen: ROC-AUC = 0.697 ± 0.007; PR-AUC = 0.100 ± 0.006), a gain of 0.010 AUC and 0.009 PR-AUC over the environmental model alone (a ~10% relative improvement in PR-AUC) (Figure 2; Supplementary Table 6). Full multimodal models (Img + Env + Gen) achieved ROC-AUCs of 0.683-0.696 and PR-AUCs of 0.096-0.101. The bestdiscriminating configurations incorporated T1-weighted GWC (ROC-AUC = 0.696 ± 0.010) and cortical surface area (0.695 ± 0.012), while the highest PR-AUC was obtained with RNI (0.101 ± 0.007) (Figure 2; Supplementary Table 6).

No full multimodal configuration exceeded the non-imaging Env + Gen model (ROC-AUC = 0.697 ± 0.007; Supplementary Table 6), with differences ranging from -0.001 to -0.014 AUC. The same pattern held for two-domain stacks: Img + Env models (0.661-0.688) did not improve on the environmental model alone (0.687), and Img + Gen models (0.586-0.617) did not improve on the genetic model alone (0.619). Within every imaging modality, performance ordered consistently as Img + Env + Gen > Img + Env > Img + Gen, mirroring the standalone ordering of the non-imaging domains and indicating that ensemble performance was driven predominantly by the environmental domain, with a smaller genetic contribution and negligible incremental contribution from imaging.

Stacked models incorporating imaging also showed substantially greater variability across repeats (SD = 0.008-0.049) than non-imaging models (SD = 0.003-0.020), with the largest variability in Img + Gen configurations (e.g. sMRI area, SD = 0.049). This inflation was consistent with the smaller imaging QC subsample available for stacking and suggests that apparent differences between imaging-based stacks should be interpreted cautiously.

Feature importance: SHAP analysis

To characterize which predictors drove model output, we computed mean absolute SHAP values in log-odds units for each model. These quantify the magnitude of a feature's contribution to the predicted log-odds.

Environmental features

In the environmental model (Figure 3; Supplementary Table 7), the strongest contributors were indices of neighborhood disadvantage and early adversity: area deprivation index at the primary residential address (national percentile; 0.193 ± 0.013), neighborhood drug-related crime count (0.161 ± 0.013), and cumulative count of adverse life events (0.144 ± 0.005). Attributions were tightly distributed across repeats (SD ≤ 0.013), indicating a stable and consistently ranked set of environmental predictors. The two leading features index the socio-spatial context of the child's residence rather than individual-level exposure, suggesting that neighborhood-level deprivation and crime may carry predictive information beyond directly experienced adversity.

Genetic and family history features

In the genetic model (Figure 3; Supplementary Table 7), familial psychiatric history outweighed PGS predictors. First-degree family history of psychosis was the leading contributor (0.253 ± 0.071), followed by first-degree family history of depression (0.220 ± 0.042) and the pfactor PGS (0.217 ± 0.037). Attribution magnitudes for the top three features were closely spaced and their SDs comparatively wide, indicating that no single genetic-domain predictor dominated the predictive signal and that their relative ranking varied across repeats.

Within imaging-only models (Supplementary Table 8), attributions differed markedly in magnitude between voxel-wise diffusion (Figure 4) and vertex-wise cortical features (Figure 5). Models built on PCs of the voxel-wise dMRI measures yielded attributions an order of magnitude larger than those built on components of the vertex-wise cortical measures. Stacked model contributions

At the ensemble level, SHAP values were computed on the out-of-fold domain scores entering each stacked model, quantifying each domain's contribution to the ensemble's predicted log-odds (Figure 6). In the non-imaging stacked model, the environmental score contributed more than twice as much as the genetic score (ENV LR = 0.427 ± 0.124 vs GEN LR = 0.200 ± 0.107; Supplementary Table 9). This dominance persisted in stacks built on the vertex-wise cortical measures (Supplementary Table 9): in all four full multimodal sMRI configurations, the environmental score was the leading contributor (0.413-0.440), followed by the genetic score (0.209-0.249), with the imaging score contributing least (0.123-0.202). The single exception among these was the Img + Gen stack for cortical thickness, in which the imaging score marginally exceeded the genetic score (0.267 ± 0.151 vs 0.237 ± 0.075).

Stacked models built on the voxel-wise dMRI measures inverted this ordering. In full multimodal models, the imaging score was the largest contributor for both RND (0.482 ± 0.223 vs ENV = 0.332 ± 0.119; GEN = 0.272 ± 0.200) and RNI (0.711 ± 0.498 vs ENV = 0.396 ± 0.121; GEN = 0.319 ± 0.148). Importantly, this greater attribution did not translate into better discrimination: the two dMRI-based full multimodal stacks had the lowest ROC-AUCs of any full multimodal configuration (0.684 ± 0.027 and 0.683 ± 0.016), and their attributions carried the widest repeat-level SDs (up to ± 0.498). Attribution magnitude therefore reflects the influence of a domain score on the predicted log-odds rather than its accuracy.

Finally, imaging-only stacks produced near-zero attributions (0.002-0.063; Supplementary Table 9), which was lowest for RND (0.002 ± 0.008) and highest for cortical thickness (0.063 ± 0.098). DISCUSSION

We applied a multimodal machine-learning framework to predict distressing and persistent psychotic-like experiences in a large, population-based cohort of youth drawn from the ABCD Study. Across all imaging modalities and stacked model configurations, findings converged upon neighborhood socioeconomic disadvantage and cumulative early adversity as the most important predictive features for dpPLEs, consistently outperforming neuroimaging features and polygenic/familial risk. The full multimodal ensemble achieved ROC-AUCs of up to 0.696 (sMRI) and 0.684 (dMRI), representing meaningful discrimination above chance. Critically, the environmental module alone approached the performance of the full multimodal model (ROC-AUC ≈ 0.687), whereas adding neuroimaging and genetics yielded only modest incremental changes (ΔAUC ≈ -0.004 to 0.009), underscoring that childhood socioenvironmental context carries disproportionate predictive weight for dpPLE risk. These findings carry implications for both the conceptualization of the risk architecture of childhood psychosisspectrum phenomena and the design of scalable early identification tools.

Our findings that environmental features contributing most strongly to prediction of adolescent dpPLEs are aligned with a large body of literature, including recent emphasis in the ABCD Study, that socioeconomic context and exposome-related factors are critical for shaping youth mental health 34, 35, 47, 62 . The three environmental features that consistently ranked highest across all SHAP analyses (area deprivation index, drug-related neighborhood crime, and cumulative adverse life events) align closely with established theoretical and empirical accounts of environmental risk for psychosis-spectrum experiences. Neighborhood socioeconomic deprivation has been implicated in psychosis risk through proposed mechanisms of social fragmentation, chronic stress activation, and reduced access to protective social resources [63] [64] [65] . The prominence of area deprivation index is consistent with emerging evidence that area-level structural disadvantage is a meaningful correlate of dpPLEs in the ABCD cohort 38, 66, 67 , and aligns with a recent meta-analysis demonstrating that neighborhood deprivation is associated with a 79% increase in rates of psychotic disorder incidence 64.

The stable predictive contribution of cumulative adverse life events extends prior work demonstrating that childhood adversity is among the most potent environmental antecedents of psychotic-like experiences in population-based youth samples 38 . These findings also converge with a recent predictive analysis of general psychopathology risk in the ABCD Study, which reported that caregiver-reported psychosocial measures, including adverse childhood experiences and family mental health history, outperformed neuroimaging features 68 . Although that study targeted broad psychopathology risk rather than dpPLEs, the convergence of findings across independent analyses and distinct outcomes strengthens the conclusion that environmental and psychosocial measures carry disproportionate predictive weight in child and adolescent mental health prediction.

Within the genetic and family-history predictor domain, a family history of psychosis and/or depression was the strongest contributor, with the cross-disorder P-factor PGS ranking third. This ordering suggests a transdiagnostic genetic liability dimension, where dpPLEs may be capturing broader risk for psychopathology rather than risk that is more specific to psychosisrelated outcomes. Although family history measures were grouped within the genetic module, these features also capture shared environmental exposures within the family unit, which may partly explain their greater predictive weight relative to PGS in this sample.

The modest contribution of disorder-specific PGS (schizophrenia, depression, bipolar disorder) is consistent with previous evidence of these scores showing limited and inconsistent associations with PLEs and related phenotypes in general population samples 69, 70 . In another large youth cohort (the Philadelphia Neurodevelopmental Cohort; N = 7,225), multi-PGS models classified psychosis spectrum symptoms only marginally better than chance, with any predictive signal driven predominantly by participants of European ancestry 71 . The limited portability of PGS derived from predominantly European discovery GWAS to participants of non-European ancestry is a well-recognized limitation 30 . Ancestry-stratified sensitivity analyses confirmed that genetic predictive performance was driven by European-ancestry participants (Supplementary Tables 9 and 10). Although we prioritized retention of as many participants as possible to reflect the ABCD sample's diversity, it is acknowledged that interpretation of PGS prediction in participants with non-European ancestry remains limited. Multi-ancestry PGS approaches thus represent a critical direction for future work.

The interplay between genetic liability and neighborhood environment should also be considered. A recent study found a significant negative interaction between schizophrenia PGS and neighborhood exposome scores on dpPLEs, such that the association between adverse neighborhood environments and dpPLEs was strongest among those with lower genetic risk 72 . This suggests that neighborhood exposures may exert a dominant influence on dpPLEs in the absence of a strong genetic predisposition, potentially attenuating the observable main effect of PGS 72 . This intersectional framing helps contextualize our findings of combined environmental and genetic features (ENV + GEN: ROC-AUC ≈ 0.697), which offered substantially greater discriminative value than genetic features alone. Across all imaging features and stacked configurations, the incremental change in ROC-AUC from adding neuroimaging to the non-imaging ensemble was modest (ΔAUC ≈ -0.014 to -0.001). This pattern replicates recent findings 68 that similarly reported limited predictive performance of neuroimaging measures for general psychopathology risk in ABCD. However, the neuroimaging patterns contributing to dpPLE prediction hold important information about prospective neuroanatomical signatures of dpPLEs. For example, while global cortical surface area and RND across white matter tracts were found to contribute most in identifying youth with dpPLEs, other neuroimaging modalities showed more regionally specific patterns. RNI patterns, reflecting distribution diffusion among cell bodies, highlighted the basal ganglia and adjacent white matter as the most predictive features, while sensorimotor regions characterized by higher intracortical myelin drove prediction of GWC features. The latter findings are consistent with prior work noting potential alterations in peri-cortical myelin within sensory-motor pathways in patients at various clinical stages of psychotic disorders 26, 73 . Broader patterns of cortical thickness and subcortical morphometry in youth with dpPLEs have also been found to be similar to adult neuropsychiatric illness signatures 74 . Our findings specifically suggest that such neuroanatomical changes, including measures of tissue microstructure and peri-cortical myelin, may already precede onset of observable PLE symptoms. These models were developed in a population-based, non-help-seeking cohort and are best understood as a framework for characterizing premorbid risk signatures and informing future prediction efforts. Given that dpPLEs capture an emerging, non-diagnostic phenotype in the ABCD Study sample rather than an established clinical outcome, the predictive performance reported here (with peak performance of an AUC ≈ 0.7) should not be benchmarked against diagnostic models or expected to mirror clinical performance thresholds. Although we leveraged up to four waves of data from the ABCD Study dataset, predictors were limited to cross-sectional baseline measures only. Longitudinal trajectories, particularly for neuroimaging features, may elucidate useful temporal and developmentally grounded factors for dpPLE prediction in future work. We also acknowledge that some of the environmental risk measures, particularly adverse life events, relied on retrospective caregiver-report, which could introduce potential recall and appraisal bias; however, our environmental predictive features were strengthened by the use of geocoded measures (e.g., ADI, neighborhood crime).

CONCLUSION

Using a large multimodal and prospective machine-learning framework, we demonstrate that neighborhood socioeconomic disadvantage and cumulative early adversity in childhood are the primary measurable risk substrates for persistent distressing psychotic-like experiences in adolescence, and are further informed by biological risk factors through family psychiatric history, polygenic influences, and broad patterns of morphometric and microstructural neuroimaging. These findings converge with evidence from broader psychiatric prediction analyses in ABCD that psychosocial and contextual features carry disproportionate predictive weight relative to neuroimaging in population-based youth cohorts, and they reinforce the importance of prevention strategies that address the structural social determinants of mental health risk early in development. Longitudinal extension and external validation represent critical next steps toward translating these findings into actionable clinical and public health frameworks.
