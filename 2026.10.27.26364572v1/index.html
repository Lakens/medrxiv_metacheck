Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. Some differences come from the ground truth: it lacks p-values with a correction name (“p Bonf = .061”), and counts partial eta squared written with its subscript after it (“η 2 p = .05”), “p = .05”, and some table notes and figure legends (“† p < .10, *p < .05”), which this module leaves out, as non-significant p-values. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Background: Mental health problems are highly prevalent among university students globally and in Indonesia, yet the vast majority do not receive adequate care. Whilst existing digital mental health interventions in Indonesia have primarily targeted students with clinically elevated symptoms, no prior study has evaluated an internet-based intervention designed to promote positive mental health among Indonesian university students. LifeHack is an internet-based guided self-help intervention developed to promote positive mental health as part of the Caring Universities initiative within the WHO World.

Introduction

Mental health problems are common among young people worldwide. Global Burden of Disease estimates indicate that a substantial proportion of individuals aged 20-24 live with a mental disorder, approximately 16% (95% UI 13.9-19.4) (1). This pattern suggests that young adults-particularly university-aged populations-experience a higher burden of mental health problems compared to the general population, reinforcing their heightened vulnerability to conditions such as depression and anxiety (1). Consistent with this, around a third of students arrive at university with symptoms of a common mental disorder (2), and roughly 38% screen positive for at least one 12-month disorder (3).

Another systematic review also reports prevalence rates of 25% for depression and 14% for suicidal ideation among university students (4). Similar patterns are observed in Indonesia, where studies during the COVID-19 pandemic reported high levels of stress (44.6%), anxiety (47.8%), and depression (18.6%) among undergraduate students (5), alongside substantial levels of psychological distress in other student populations (6).

Despite this high burden, access to adequate mental health care among university students remains limited. Although effective treatments exist, only one in five students with mental disorders receive minimally adequate treatment (7). This gap is also evident in Indonesia, where most students with mental health problems do not receive adequate care (8). In low-and middle-income countries such as Indonesia, structural constraints -including an acute shortage of mental health professionals relative to the student population -further exacerbate this gap (9). Critically, treatment-oriented approaches alone are insufficient to address population-level mental health burden: they reach only those who already present with elevated symptoms, often after significant distress has accumulated (10). What is needed alongside treatment capacity is a shift toward promotive approachesinterventions that target the general student population not merely to prevent or reduce symptoms but to actively build students' psychological strengths and enhance positive functioning and well-being (11, 12). A recent mega-analysis of 198 meta-analyses found small-to-medium effects of positive psychology interventions on well-being and psychopathology (13). Digital mental health interventions offer a scalable and cost-effective pathway for delivering both treatment and promotive intervention at scale (14, 15), with meta-analytic evidence supporting their effectiveness in enhancing well-being (16) and reducing symptoms of anxiety, depression, and stress in university populations (17). In Indonesia, a growing body of research has begun to evaluate culturally adapted digital interventions for students, including programs targeting depression (18), transdiagnostic symptoms of anxiety and depression (19), stress (20), emotional distress (21), and psychological distress (22). Across these studies, eligibility was determined by elevated symptom or distress scores, and outcomes centered on reducing depression, anxiety, stress, or distress-even where well-being was assessed (22), it remained a secondary measure within an illness-reduction framework. This literature has thus been oriented predominantly toward treatment and indicated prevention (23), designed to alleviate symptoms once distress has already emerged. This study extends this literature toward a promotive approach, one designed to build resilience and positive mental health as its primary aim, accessible to students regardless of their current symptom status. Such an approach targets a distinct outcome from the illness-reduction paradigm that has so far dominated digital mental health research in Indonesia. It complements existing treatment and indicated-prevention services by reaching students regardless of symptom level -including those without elevated symptoms -who can benefit from strengthening their psychological resources and positive mental health (12, 24).

LifeHack is an internet-based intervention developed within the Caring Universities initiativea collaborative project embedded within the World Health Organization World Mental Health-International College Student (WMH-ICS) framework in the Netherlands. The intervention was developed using a co-creation approach involving university students and mental health professionals; co-creation as a participatory design strategy leverages the experiential knowledge of end-users to enhance relevance and acceptability (25). LifeHack is a guided internet-based self-help programme grounded in cognitive behavioural techniques with an evidence base spanning multiple meta-analyses demonstrating effectiveness in improving mood and reducing stress in the general and student population (7, (26) (27) (28). Its primary goal is to improve mental well-being by increasing resilience, building life skills, and strengthening positive mental health. The program was developed based on the existing empirical literature and further refined through direct collaboration with university students via interviews and co-creation, ensuring that the content and format reflect the specific needs and preferences of the student population (25). LifeHack comprises 12 modules across four thematic domains-emotional well-being, study skills, social life, and self-esteem-each taking approximately 30 minutes. Content is delivered via text, images, and infographics, with some modules including video and audio elements; each module includes evidence-based information, exercises, and homework assignments. The program is delivered via guided internet-based self-help, with asynchronous written personalized feedback provided by trained e-coaches within 48 working hours of module completion. The study addresses the following hypotheses: 1.

H1: The recruitment process will be considered feasible if at least 70% of eligible students who submit an Expression of Interest (EOI) enrol within the planned recruitment period.

2. H2: The LifeHack: Menjadi Mahasiswa Tangguh platform will demonstrate acceptable usability, with at least 70% of participants achieving a System Usability Scale (SUS) score of 68 or above.

3. H3: Participant adherence will be considered acceptable if at least 70% of active participants complete at least one module, click "finish". 4. H4: Preliminary signals of improvement will be observed in resilience (RES) and positive mental health (MHC-SF) scores from baseline (T0) to post-intervention (T1), defined as a within-group effect size of at least Cohen's d = 0.2 (small effect) (32).

Methods

Study Design

This study is an open-label, single-arm pre-post feasibility study. This non-randomised pre-post design with the primary aim of evaluating feasibility will generate evidence to inform the design and procedures of a future fully powered RCT (33). The study is best characterised as a quantitative feasibility study with embedded qualitative feedback. Its primary aim is to generate quantitative feasibility metrics-recruitment, retention, adherence, module completion, and the variability of preliminary outcome measures-while qualitative data serve a secondary, supportive role. Qualitative feedback will be collected through open-ended questions administered at the end of each module and, where participants are contacted by the researcher or an e-coach following disengagement, from the reasons given for discontinuing; these data will be analysed using qualitative content analysis to help interpret the quantitative findings and to refine the intervention content and study procedures prior to a definitive trial. In addition, preliminary effect estimates on secondary psychological outcomes will be reported to guide sample size calculations for future trials.

The reporting of this protocol follows the Standard Protocol Items: Recommendations for Interventional Trials (SPIRIT) expanded checklist 2025 (34), supplemented by relevant items from the CONSORT 2025 statement (35). The SPIRIT and CONSORT expanded checklist is provided as a supplementary file.

Three assessment time points are employed: baseline (T0), post-intervention (T1), and follow-up (T2). T1 will be administered immediately after participants indicate completion of the intervention by clicking the "Finish" button, or at the end of the six-week intervention period if they have not previously indicated completion. T2 will be administered three months after T1. The assessment schedule, following the recommended SPIRIT format, is presented in Fig 1.

Currently enrolled as an active undergraduate student at Universitas Padjadjaran or Universitas Jambi; c. Have access to a digital device (smartphone or computer) with reliable internet connectivity; d. Score at or below one standard deviation above the mean (Mean + 1 SD) of the MHC-SF total score in the screening sample, calculated after the screening period closes by pooling all students screened at both universities. This criterion ensures that students with already optimal mental wellbeing, who would have little room for improvement (ceiling effect), are not included e. Provide informed consent to participate. Individuals will be excluded if they meet any of the following exclusion criteria: a. Report a current risk of suicidal behaviour, operationally defined as: PHQ-9 item 9 score ≥ 1, AND positive endorsement of having thought about or worked out a plan to kill themselves in the past 12 months, AND reporting that this is at least somewhat likely they will act on this plan in the next 12 months; b. These additional questions reflect established distinctions between suicidal ideation, suicide plans, and suicidal behaviour used in suicide-risk assessment research (36, 37). The number of individuals excluded for each reason will be recorded as part of the recruitment feasibility evaluation.

Procedures

Eligible participants-those who pass the online screening-will be presented with detailed study information and an electronic informed consent form. Those who provide informed consent will immediately proceed to the baseline assessment (T0) within the same form, administered via Google Forms. Participants will be given one week to complete the consent and baseline form, with a reminder email sent to those who have not yet completed it. At the end of this period, on 1 November 2026, participants who have completed T0 will receive access credentials to the LifeHack: Menjadi Mahasiswa Tangguh platform by email, so that all participants begin the intervention on the same date. Data collected at different stages will be linked using a unique participant identification code and stored without direct personal identifiers, with access restricted to named members of the research team.

Prior to commencing the intervention modules, all participants will be invited to view a brief onboarding video. The video will cover (1) a concise overview of the study; (2) how to navigate the intervention platform; (3) the module structure and completion requirements; (4) an introduction to the e-coaches, and (5) how to contact the e-coaches and access support if needed.

Viewing the onboarding video is encouraged to support engagement but is not a condition of eligibility or continued participation.

Participants will be given six weeks to engage with the intervention. Participants are required to complete at least one intervention module. Once they consider their use of the intervention complete, they will click the "finish" button to indicate completion and proceed directly to the post-intervention assessment. Ongoing monitoring of engagement will be conducted: participants who have not logged into the platform for two consecutive weeks will be contacted via email for a welfare check-in and to encourage continued engagement. Reasons for non-adherence will be recorded where provided. Any reasons given for reduced engagement or discontinuation will be recorded and, where provided, used as qualitative feedback to help interpret the feasibility findings.

Participants who have completed the minimum module requirements and indicate that they have finished the intervention will proceed to the post-intervention assessment (T1). Participants who have not indicated completion by the end of Week 6 will be invited to complete T1 at that point regardless. A follow-up assessment (T2) will be administered three months after completion of the postintervention assessment (T1) to examine short-term maintenance of outcomes.

Participants who complete both T1 and T2 will receive compensation in the form of mobile data credit totaling IDR 150,000, distributed in two installments: IDR 100,000 upon completion of T1, and IDR 50,000 upon completion of T2. This compensation is framed as reimbursement for data (quota) costs rather than as a performance incentive. To reduce the likelihood that participation is motivated primarily by financial gain, the compensation will be disclosed only during the informed consent process and not in the recruitment materials or the study overview presented before screening.

Visual materials (e.g., images and memes) were evaluated through a student survey assessing comprehensibility, relatability, and engagement value, followed by a focus group discussion to refine culturally appropriate elements. Subsequently, an expanded expert and user review involving five clinical psychologists and 25 undergraduate students evaluated functional, conceptual, and linguistic equivalence relative to the original programs. Feedback from this review informed final refinements.

Structure and content of the adapted intervention

The adapted intervention, LifeHack: Menjadi Mahasiswa Tangguh, consists of 12 modules organised into four thematic domains: (1) emotional well-being, (2) study skills, (3) social life, and (4) self-esteem. The overall theoretical foundation and promotive objectives of the original LifeHack programme were retained to ensure conceptual fidelity. Module titles and theme names have been translated and contextually adapted into Indonesian, whilst the article uses English terminology throughout for consistency.

Each module is designed to take approximately 30 minutes to complete. Content is delivered via text, images, and infographics, with some modules including video and audio elements. Each module includes evidence-based psychoeducational information, structured exercises, and homework assignments. Participants engage in activities aimed at strengthening resilience-related capacities, adaptive coping strategies, and positive mental health processes. Several modules incorporate structured goal-setting exercises in which participants develop actionable plans. At the end of each module, participants complete a brief evaluation. All module content in the Indonesian version has been adapted into Indonesian, with contextual modifications applied to language, examples, and visual materials to reflect the lived experiences of Indonesian university students.

In the Indonesian adaptation, participants are required to complete a minimum of one module and indicate completion by clicking the "Finish" button at the end of the module. This minimum completion requirement is a contextual adaptation to support the feasibility evaluation, whereas the original LifeHack programme allows students to engage with the modules as standalone units based on their individual needs and preferences. Participants will retain access to the intervention during the waiting period before the T2 assessment. They may revisit previously completed modules or access any module at any time, consistent with the intervention's intended real-world implementation as an ondemand resource that students can use whenever support for a specific topic is needed.

The intervention is delivered entirely online via a dedicated web-based platform and is supported by asynchronous written e-coaching. Participants submit responses to module exercises and receive structured written feedback from trained e-coaches within 48 working hours of submission.

Coaching is guided by standardised templates and supervised by a licensed psychologist to maintain intervention fidelity. All coaching interactions are documented within the platform. A brief description and list of topics covered in each of the 12 modules is provided in Supplementary Materials 1.

In the Indonesian adaptation, e-coaching will be delivered by psychologist interns or master's students in psychology, all of whom hold a bachelor's degree in psychology. Psychologist interns are students enrolled in Indonesia's Professional Psychology Program who are completing their supervised professional internship as part of their pre-licensure training to become licensed psychologists. In the Indonesian context, both psychologist interns and psychology master's students have completed foundational training in psychology and possess competencies in applied psychological knowledge and skills. However, their role in the intervention will remain limited to e-coaching rather than psychological assessment, counseling, or treatment, to preserve the self-guided nature of the digital intervention and prevent excessive therapist-like input. E-coaches will provide asynchronous, written, personalized feedback within 48 working hours of module completion to support motivation, adherence, and understanding of module content. They will receive standardized training and ongoing supervision, and participants will receive email reminders in case of non-engagement. E-coaching fidelity will be assessed through regular review of a random sample of coaching exchanges by the supervising psychologist using a standardized fidelity checklist.

The e-coach training materials and manual will also be culturally adapted to the Indonesian context, including guidance on culturally appropriate communication, safety procedures, and responses to potential adverse or negative reactions to the intervention. Clear escalation procedures will be provided for concerns beyond the e-coach's role, with referral to the supervising licensed psychologist when needed. E-coaches will also receive training in psychological first aid to support appropriate initial responses in situations requiring urgent assistance. These adaptations aim to strengthen participant support while maintaining the intended intensity and boundaries of the digital intervention.

Outcomes Primary Feasibility Outcomes

The primary outcomes of this feasibility study are indicators of usability, satisfaction, adherence, and retention. A predetermined traffic light system will be used to inform whether proceeding to a fully powered randomized controlled trial (RCT) is warranted. The three-tiered progression criteria require pre-specified cutoffs for a green zone (proceed without modification), amber zone (proceed with amendments), and red zone (do not proceed) (38). Consistent with current guidance for pilot and feasibility studies, these criteria will be treated as guidelines rather than mechanical rules (39, 40).

Accordingly, rather than basing the decision on the single worst-performing criterion, the criteria will be interpreted together and mapped to their corresponding feasibility objectives, taking into account contextual factors and the embedded qualitative feedback. On this basis, the research team-361 comprising a clinical psychologist, a digital health researcher, and a methodologist-will make a holistic progression decision, distinguishing whether the trial procedures are feasible ("can we?") from whether progression is warranted ("will we?"), and the rationale for the final decision will be documented and reported transparently. As this guidance was developed primarily for randomized pilot trials, it is applied here with adaptation to the present single-arm feasibility design.

The pre-determined thresholds are presented in Table 1. Each threshold was derived from domain-specific evidence rather than applied uniformly across all criteria. Recruitment and retention thresholds were informed by published feasibility benchmarks from randomized pilot trials in psychological and digital health interventions (41) (42) (43), with additional grounding in pilot evidence from digital mental health interventions specifically targeting university student populations (7, (44) (45) (46). The thresholds were additionally informed by a systematic review of dropout, attrition, and adherence patterns in culturally adapted digital mental health interventions, which identified deep cultural adaptation as consistently associated with higher adherence and lower dropout rates (47). Usability thresholds were anchored to established System Usability Scale (SUS) benchmarks (48), and satisfaction thresholds were informed by published score classifications for the measure used in this study (49) Higher scores indicate greater satisfaction. A mean total score ≥24 will be considered indicative of satisfactory acceptability.

Adherence will be operationally defined as completing at least one intervention module, clicking the "finish" button to indicate that the participant perceived their intervention needs to have been met and considered their use of the intervention complete. The overall adherence rate will be calculated as the proportion of active participants who complete at least one module and click the "Finish" button. The overall adherence rate will be calculated as the proportion of active participants (i.e., those who started at least one module) meeting this definition. Additional engagement metrics will be collected from the platform, including the total number of modules completed, time spent on each module, number of coaching exchanges initiated, and number of logins during the six-week intervention period. To provide a more detailed descriptive profile of intervention engagement, participants will be categorised as: (1) complete users-participants who completed all 12 modules and clicked the "Finish" button; (2) partial users-participants who completed between 2 and 11 modules and clicked the "Finish" button; (3) minimum adherers-participants who completed exactly one module and clicked the "Finish" button; and (4) non-adherers-participants who did not meet the adherence criterion (i.e., completion of at least one module and clicking the "Finish" button).

Retention will be evaluated separately for post-intervention (T1) and follow-up (T2). Post-intervention retention will be defined as the proportion of active participants who complete the T1 assessment.

Follow-up retention will be defined as the proportion who complete T2. Reasons for dropout or discontinuation will be recorded where provided and will be reported descriptively.

Safety Outcome

Potential negative effects associated with participation in the intervention will be assessed at T1 using the Negative Effects Questionnaire (NEQ) (60, 61). The present study will use the 20-item version of NEQ. It employs a three-step conditional response format: for each of the 20 items, participants first indicate whether they have experienced the described effect (Yes/No). If they respond Yes, they then rate the severity of that effect on a 5-point Likert scale (0 = not at all distressing to4 = extremely distressing), and subsequently indicate whether they attribute the effect to the intervention itself or to other causes. As no validated Indonesian version was available, the NEQ underwent a structured translation and cultural adaptation process prior to use in this study, including forward translation, backward translation, and expert review to ensure linguistic and conceptual equivalence. The internal consistency of the adapted version will be evaluated (Cronbach's alpha) within the present feasibility study. Negative effects data will be reported descriptively: the proportion of participants reporting at least one negative effect, mean severity ratings among those endorsing each item, and the proportion attributing effects to the intervention will be reported across the six NEQ domains.

Sample size

As a single-arm feasibility study, a formal statistical power calculation was not conducted, as the primary aim is to evaluate feasibility parameters -including recruitment, retention, and acceptability -rather than to test intervention efficacy. This approach is consistent with established guidance for pilot and feasibility studies, which cautions against powering such studies for efficacy outcomes (62).

Sample size was instead informed by rule-of-thumb recommendations for feasibility studies, with a minimum of 12 participants considered sufficient to estimate key feasibility parameters (63). A target of 50 enrolled participants was selected to improve the precision of preliminary estimates, provide a buffer against anticipated attrition, and remain feasible within the dual-university recruitment context. This target falls within the range supported by empirical reviews reporting a median sample size of approximately 30-60 participants in internet-based intervention feasibility studies (64). A traffic light progression criteria framework (38) was adopted to guide decision-making regarding the feasibility of proceeding to a full-scale trial, consistent with its recommended application in behavioral intervention research (65).

Statistical analysis

All analyses will be conducted using JASP 0.95.2 (66). Descriptive statistics will be presented for all measures, including means, standard deviations, medians, and ranges for continuous variables, and frequencies and proportions for categorical variables.

Quantitative feasibility outcomes (recruitment rate, adherence rate, retention rates, and usability scores) will be calculated as proportions and compared against the predetermined traffic light progression criteria (Table 1). Usability progression criteria will be evaluated based on the proportion of participants achieving the predefined SUS benchmark (≥68). Mean SUS scores and standard deviations will also be reported descriptively. Satisfaction (CSQ-I) will additionally be summarized using means and standard deviations, with the proportion of participants meeting each benchmark reported. The internal consistency of the adapted CSQ-I and NEQ will be evaluated using Cronbach's alpha coefficients in the present sample. Negative-effects (NEQ) data will be reported within an adverse-events framework, as described in the Safety Outcome section.

Changes in the secondary psychological outcomes (resilience, positive mental health, psychological distress, and coping) across T0, T1, and T2 will be examined using linear mixed-effects models, with time as a fixed effect and participant as a random intercept. This approach uses all available data under the missing-at-random (MAR) assumption via maximum likelihood estimation; accordingly, the amount and pattern of missing data will be described for each outcome and the baseline characteristics of participants with complete versus incomplete data compared to assess the plausibility of the MAR assumption, whereas mean substitution, pairwise deletion, and decision rules based on the proportion of missing data will not be used (67, 68). Effect sizes (Cohen's d) and preliminary estimates of outcome variability (standard deviations) will be derived to characterise the magnitude of change and to inform sample-size calculation for a future trial. As this is a feasibility study, these analyses are exploratory and will be interpreted with caution: results will be reported with 75%, 85%, and 95% confidence intervals, with a focus on estimation rather than null-hypothesis significance testing (69, 70). Exploratory associations between feasibility indicators (e.g., module completion) and change in secondary outcomes will be examined using Pearson's or Spearman's correlations, as appropriate.

Responses to the open-ended questions will be analysed using conventional qualitative content analysis. The first author will independently code the responses and develop initial codes inductively from the data. Similar codes will be iteratively grouped into categories, with the coding framework and category development reviewed by the other authors to enhance the trustworthiness of the analysis.

During interpretation, the categories will be considered in relation to relevant cultural adaptation domains (e.g., Bernal's Ecological Validity Model) and key feasibility constructs, including acceptability, usability, and participant engagement, where appropriate. The qualitative findings will be used to complement and help interpret the quantitative feasibility outcomes and to identify areas for refinement of the intervention and study procedures prior to a definitive trial.

Changes to the original protocol version is available for the latter but not for the former (53, 71). Screening was separated from the baseline assessment so that eligibility could be determined before full informed consent, and the exclusion criteria were described in more detail to protect participant safety. The follow-up assessment was rescheduled from 8 weeks after baseline to three months after T1, the most common follow-up period in digital mental health and resilience interventions (71, 72). Because of this longer interval between T1 and T2, focus group discussions after the intervention would further prolong data collection; they were therefore replaced by open-ended questions at the end of each module, which will be analysed using conventional qualitative content analysis rather than thematic analysis. As participation now extends over a longer period, compensation for internet data (quota) costs was increased from IDR 100,000 to IDR 150,000 and is transferred to participants' e-wallets in two instalments, after T1 and after T2, rather than in a single payment. Finally, repeated measures ANOVA was replaced by linear mixed-effects models.

Safety Monitoring and Potential Adverse Events

Participant safety will be supported through both pre-enrollment screening and ongoing monitoring throughout the intervention. Prior to enrollment, all prospective participants will complete an online screening questionnaire. Individuals who meet the predefined suicide-risk exclusion criterion or report severe depressive symptoms (PHQ-9 ≥20) will not be enrolled in the study. These individuals will be contacted via email within 48 hours, informed that they do not meet the eligibility criteria, and provided with written information about accessible professional mental health resources, including university counseling services and the national crisis helpline. (e.g., www.healing119.id or by calling 119 ext. 8).

Throughout the intervention, participant safety will be monitored through routine monitoring by the e-coach via the intervention platform. At the end of each completed module, participants will be invited to respond to two brief safety check-in questions regarding (1) whether they experienced emotional discomfort or deterioration in their well-being during the module, and (2) whether they experienced any unpleasant or unexpected experiences while using the programme. Participants responding affirmatively will be invited to provide a brief description of their experience.

All participant responses will be reviewed by the assigned e-coach as part of routine monitoring.

Potential adverse events will be documented using a standardised monitoring log. Where participant responses indicate clinically significant psychological deterioration, fulfilment of the predefined suiciderisk criterion, or another concern requiring additional support, the case will be discussed with the principal investigator to determine the appropriate course of action, including referral to appropriate professional mental health services where indicated. Routine safety monitoring will complement, rather than replace, the systematic assessment of participant-reported negative effects using the Negative Effects Questionnaire (NEQ), which will be administered at T1.

Discussion

This protocol describes a single-arm pre-post feasibility study of LifeHack: Menjadi Mahasiswa Tangguh, a culturally adapted internet-based guided self-help promotive mental health intervention for Indonesian undergraduate students. This study will be among the first to systematically evaluate the feasibility, usability, and preliminary effects of this culturally adapted digital intervention, thereby addressing a significant gap in the evidence base.

Several methodological strengths of the present study are noteworthy. First, the present adaptation draws on an established, evidence-based program framework: the Caring Universities programs have demonstrated feasibility and preliminary efficacy in European university populations and have a documented evidence base in related populations. The current version of LifeHack: Menjadi Mahasiswa Tangguh has additionally undergone a rigorous and systematic cultural adaptation process informed by Bernal's Ecological Validity Framework, including expert review by clinical psychologists and direct input from the target population. This enhances the likelihood that the intervention will be perceived as relevant and acceptable by Indonesian students. Second, the study employs a comprehensive assessment of feasibility indicators-including usability, satisfaction, adherence, retention, and safety-thereby providing a multidimensional picture of the intervention's feasibility.

Third, the use of predetermined traffic light progression criteria (38) ensures that decision-making about proceeding to a future RCT will be transparent, systematic, and evidence-based. Fourth, the inclusion of a broad set of secondary psychological outcomes (resilience, positive mental health, psychological distress, and coping) will generate preliminary effect estimates to inform sample size calculations for a future RCT.

A potential limitation is the single-arm design, which precludes causal attribution of any observed changes. Without a control group, it is not possible to distinguish intervention effects from the passage of time, regression to the mean, or other contemporaneous influences. This limitation is inherent to the phase of intervention development represented by the present study and will be addressed in a future RCT. A second anticipated challenge is the commonly high dropout rates observed in internet-based interventions among university students (7). To mitigate this, the study incorporates several evidence-based engagement strategies, including asynchronous personalized e-coaching, automated email reminders for participants who have not engaged with the platform, and compensation for completing assessments. Additionally, the broad eligibility criteria-encompassing a wide age range, varying levels of psychological distress, and allowing concurrent use of other mental health resources-were intentionally designed to reflect real-world implementation conditions.

However, this heterogeneity may limit the interpretability of preliminary effect estimates.

Within the Heuristic Framework for Cultural Adaptation (31), the present study constitutes Stages 3 (Preliminary Adaptation Test) and 4 (Adaptation Refinement). Findings will directly inform whether further modifications to the adapted intervention are warranted prior to large-scale testing.

Promising feasibility results will support progression to a fully powered randomized controlled trial.

Unsatisfactory results will guide targeted refinement of the intervention content, delivery procedures, or study design prior to further evaluation.
