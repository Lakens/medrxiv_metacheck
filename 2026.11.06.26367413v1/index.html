Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How Marginal Significance works

The marginal module searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The list of terms is a subset of those listed in a blog post by Matthew Hankins. The module returns all sentences that match terms describing ‘marginally significant’ results.

Some of the terms identified might not be problematic in some contexts, and there are ways to describe ‘marginal significance’ that are not detected by the module.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 225 papers containing 405 instances of non-exact p-values, the module correctly detected 269 cases (true positives) and incorrectly identified 78 (false positives). It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Abstract

People with severe mental illness (SMI) experience poorer oral health than the general population. They are less likely to visit the dentist or brush their teeth and face unique barriers to accessing oral health support. There is a limited number of oral health interventions for people with SMI, and there is no conclusive evidence on their effectiveness. An integrated oral health intervention delivered by mental health professionals in a community mental healthcare setting could offer a scalable option to oral health support for people with SMI. In this paper we report on the process of co-producing such an intervention in England.

The intervention development process was informed by Hawkins' co-production framework. In this paper we report on stages 2 (co-production) and 3 (prototyping). Two online and two faceto-face co-production workshops took place (N=22) involving service users with SMI (n=6), mental health professionals (n=8) and dental health professionals (n=8). Two online patient and public involvement consultations, and a professional stakeholder meeting were held during the.

Background

Oral health is an integral part of health and wellbeing. Oral diseases are a common cause of pain, infection and tooth loss, leading to functional limitations such as eating difficulties which can affect nutrition (1). Poor oral health also has negative psychosocial impacts on self-esteem, social interaction, and overall quality of life and is related to other systematic diseases such as diabetes and cardiovascular disease (1). Oral diseases are a global public health concern with a significant economic burden. In 2019, UK households' expenditure on dental services was more than £5.96 billion (2).

Oral health among people with severe mental illness (SMI) is poorer than that of the general population. They are nearly three times more likely to lose all of their teeth (OR = 2.81, 95% CI [1.7 -4.6]), have higher rates of dental caries (mean difference 5.0, 95% CI 2.5 -7.4) and are less likely to visit a dentist for routine dental check-ups or treatment (3). These poor outcomes are due to several factors (4) including symptoms of mental illness being associated with decreased motivation for regular tooth brushing (5), high consumption of sugary food and drinks (6), and side effects of medications (e.g. dry mouth) (5). Support with the maintenance of good oral health and attending regular dental check-ups is scarce for people with SMI, which only exacerbates the poorer oral health outcomes among this population. This issue also contributes to serious complications such as hospital admissions due to physical health complications from untreated dental decay (7), accompanied by associated resource implications for the health service. People with SMI also often require specialist dental care and general anaesthesia, partly due to seeking treatment late. However, to improve oral health, people with SMI require interventions that are tailored to their needs. This is crucial given that this population faces both individual barriers, such as a lack of trust in dental health professionals, as well as mental health symptoms; and organisational level barriers, including limited staff capacity and resources to address their dental health concerns. Furthermore, these complex needs and barriers to oral health support among people with SMI require an approach that integrates oral health care into mental health services where possible. This need is also underlined by the calls for approaches which integrate physical and mental healthcare to reduce health inequalities (8). To synthesise and evaluate currently available oral health interventions for people with SMI, we previously conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis which identified 12 studies (9). We found that the interventions covered five broad categories: dental education, motivational interviewing, dental checklists, dietary change and incentives. Although there were some statistically significant improvements in dental plaque index and oral health-related behaviours, we found that the studies included in the review were of low to moderate quality; thus, the clinical effectiveness of oral health interventions for people with SMI remains unclear. Only one intervention was delivered by mental health professionals in a UK community mental health setting; no effects on service users' oral health outcomes were found after a one-year follow-up (10). Additionally, none of the interventions were co-produced or reported public and patient (PPI) involvement, meaning that the voices of service users, their carers, and healthcare professionals were not necessarily reflected in the respective intervention development processes. Given that co-production is a key aspect of evidencebased intervention development guidance (11), and the unique challenges of service users with SMI, it is crucial to involve them in the development of any future oral health interventions.

To explore potential avenues of addressing the limitations of existing interventions, we began the process of co-producing an intervention to improve oral health in people with SMI. This was informed by Hawkins' co-production framework which aims to provide guidance on co-producing and prototyping intervention materials with relevant stakeholders before proceeding with their evaluation (12). The framework therefore involves three stages: 1. Evidence review and stakeholder consultation, 2. Co-production and 3. Prototyping. Stage 1 included our systematic review on oral health interventions for people with SMI, and initial stakeholder consultations to identify preliminary key ingredients for a new oral health intervention (13). Here we conducted consultations with 23 stakeholders including service users, carers, dental and mental health professionals, and dental public health professionals. The potential intervention steps we identified were: 1) initiating the conversation around oral health; 2) delivering tailored oral health advice; 3) providing encouragement to reinforce the delivered advice; 4) including oral health in the service user's care plan; 5) facilitating visits to the dentist. Methods

Study design

We drew on Hawkins' (2017) three-stage intervention co-production model, based on recommendations for co-production approaches to intervention development whereby stakeholders are actively involved across planning, developing, and testing of interventions (12).

As described in the background section, we previously completed Stage 1 of Hawkins' model, comprising an evidence review and stakeholder consultations to establish the initial intervention components. In this paper we report on stages 2 (co-production) and 3 (prototyping) to refine the intervention and produce its related outputs. The intervention development process is reported in line with the GUIDED checklist (14)(Additional File 1), and the intervention content followed TidiER guidance to describing interventions (15).

Ethics

We obtained a favourable NHS ethical approval from the West Midlands -South Birmingham Research Ethics Committee for the co-production workshop component of the study (ref. no, 24/WM/0267).

Recruitment

We recruited participants with lived experience of severe mental health, dental, and mental healthcare professionals to take part in intervention co-production workshops. Recruitment channels included professional networks, including mental and dental healthcare colleagues and the two NHS trust sites involved with the SMILE project. All participants had to be aged 18 or over, have capacity to consent to participation and be willing to be audio recorded during the workshop. Additional eligibility criteria that applied to service users were having a verified diagnosis of an SMI such as schizophrenia, schizoaffective disorder, bipolar disorder or other non-organic psychosis, carers had to identify as supporting someone with an SMI, and healthcare professionals included mental health professionals providing support to people with SMI or professionals involved in the delivery of oral health care. Some PPIE group members also took part in the co-production workshops as participants, providing a link between the PPIE group and the workshops.

Procedure

To co-produce the intervention, we carried out four co-production workshops described below which were based on individual topic guides for each workshop (Additional File 2). The formal co-production process was accompanied by two PPIE meetings with people with SMI and their carers, and one meeting with mental health professionals. The two stakeholder groups were consulted separately to provide ongoing feedback on the iterative intervention development process and to inform workshop planning.

PPI group meetings

Two online PPIE meetings with service users were held alongside the workshops to guide the iterative process of refining the intervention (Figure 2). The meetings were attended by a total of nine service users and were conducted after the first and second workshop, respectively. Both meetings focused on refining the chosen outcome measures for the feasibility trial, the steps involved in delivering the intervention and aspects of oral health-related advice agreed on in the workshops. Input from the PPIE meetings directly informed the structure and topics of subsequent co-production workshops. Before the first workshop we held two PPIE meetings with n=3 service users, and n=2 mental health professionals in total. We held an additional mental health professional PPIE meeting after the final workshop (n=4). This was to seek guidance from this group on which suggested changes from the final workshop would be feasible to implement.

Co-production (Stage 2) Workshop 1

The first workshop was conducted online on MS Teams to maximise accessibility and reduce geographical barriers to participation. Participants were service users with SMI (n=3), a carer (n=1), mental health professionals (n=4), dental health professionals (n=6). The workshop was facilitated by three research team members, one of whom is also a dental health professional.

Here we presented the five-step intervention structure which was developed based on the initial stakeholder consultations from Stage 1. The initial five steps were: 1) mental health professionals initiating the conversation around oral health with the service user; 2) delivering tailored oral health advice; 3) providing encouragement to maintain good oral health habits; 4) including oral healthcare in the service user's care plan; 5) providing help to support the service user to visit a dentist if needed.

Research team members facilitated in-depth discussions for each intervention step in two breakout rooms, ensuring a balance of various stakeholders' perspectives in both rooms.

We received detailed input across the initial intervention steps. Participants discussed the various ways in which a conversation about oral health could be started, and all agreed on using non-stigmatising language to avoid causing feelings of shame and embarrassment among service users. The discussion included details on where the oral health conversation would take place; it was agreed that it was most fitting to incorporate it into routine conversations around physical health, rather than a standalone conversation which could make service users selfconscious about their oral health. When giving practical advice on maintaining good oral health (e.g. toothbrushing demonstration, dietary sugar intake, tobacco and alcohol), it was important that people delivering the intervention used an encouraging tone to avoid sounding patronising.

Suggested approaches included asking service users for permission to receive the advice, e.g.

'Would you like to see a toothbrushing demonstration?', rather than imposing the advice.

Participants suggested that people delivering the intervention should clearly explain the links between the importance of lifestyle habits (diet, tobacco and alcohol) and their impact on oral health specifically, rather than just the better-known links with general health.

Workshop 2

The main focus of the second workshop (online) was to discuss which outcome measures would be suitable for the upcoming feasibility study (Mishu et al., under review) to test the coproduced intervention. Participants were service users (N=2), a carer (N=1), mental health professionals (N=3), and dental health professionals (N=2). A summary of the discussion on outcome measures is provided here.

Emphasis was placed on simplifying the initially proposed outcome measures such as dental plaque index, and detailed measures of toothbrushing habits, dietary sugar intake, alcohol and tobacco consumption. Dental and mental health professionals agreed that collecting clinical outcome measures of oral health indicators were beyond the scope of mental health professionals. Service users felt that taking such measures using clinical instruments could be intimidating. Mental health professionals and service users suggested that asking for precise habit data may lead to recall bias or inaccurate information recall. The agreed measures which were deemed acceptable for a further feasibility evaluation covered information on oral health status, including broad toothbrushing habits, dental visits, dietary sugar intake, alcohol and tobacco use, a custom resource use questionnaire refined with stakeholders, and standardised outcome measures -oral health and health-related quality of life.

Workshop 3

The third workshop was conducted face-to-face. Participants were people with lived experience of SMI (n=2), one carer, mental health professionals (n=3), dental health professionals (n=4), and a health economist (n=1). Three researchers facilitated the workshop. Based on the findings from the first workshop, we developed a topic guide in which we focused the discussion on the second intervention component -delivering oral health advice, and the four advice categories within it: 1) oral hygiene; 2) dietary sugar intake; 3) tobacco and alcohol consumption; 4) side effects of psychotropic medication. We also discussed the practicalities of delivering the intervention, including who was best placed to initiate the conversation, and how the initial question should be phrased. We then sought participants' input on the images used in the intervention materials to ensure they were acceptable and relevant to the target population.

Following the first workshop we refined the initial intervention steps and drafted a document outlining a skeleton of the SMILE intervention leaflet. This draft version contained an overview of the intervention steps, as well as preliminary images and graphics which were to be developed further. Participants suggested using an image to facilitate initiating the conversation, which would be through the link between general and oral health.

While service users expressed the need for support with visiting a dentist, healthcare professionals agreed that it would not be feasible for people delivering the intervention or care co-ordinators to actively look for dentists for service users due to time and capacity constraints.

Therefore, we decided to include a step on encouraging and optionally supporting the service user to visit a dentist, should the person delivering the intervention have the capacity to do so.

Workshop 4

The fourth and final workshop was conducted face-to face. Here we presented the revised intervention components based on participants' previous input from workshops 1 and 3 and continued to discuss and refine any outstanding issues or areas of uncertainty. We also focused on the intervention flowchart to discuss the order in which the intervention components would be delivered. Participants were people with lived experience of SMI (n=3), one carer, mental health professionals (n=3), and dental health professionals (n=4). Four researchers facilitated the workshop in two breakout groups.

In this workshop we finalised the content of the intervention and leaflet, and the order in which the steps would be delivered. Participants suggested decreasing the amount of text to make it easier to follow. Participants agreed on which images were to be adapted by a graphic designer for the leaflet, and suggested modifications such as reductions in the amount of text in the images. We also made some changes to the wording based on service users' suggestions, e.g. using 'top advice' instead of 'top tips'. An issue regarding the intervention flowchart was that conversations with service users would not always follow a linear structure in real-life intervention delivery settings. Therefore, we decided to emphasise this fluidity of intervention step delivery to staff delivering the intervention in any subsequent intervention materials. We also agreed to remove the provision of a toothbrushing demonstration, as mental health professionals advocated that they would not feel comfortable providing this, given that it is outside their scope of practice. Therefore, we opted for a graphic to illustrate this in the leaflet with accompanying text instructions and a link/QR code to a video demonstration.

To ensure the tailored element to the intervention, we added free-text space under each intervention step where service users could note down their own barriers and goals relating to each step (e.g.

toothbrushing -what is currently getting in the way of regular toothbrushing?).

A key change introduced following the final workshop was that mental health professionals felt 1) the inclusion of the intervention in the service users' care plan, and 2) support with visits to a dentist or oral health professional, would not be implementable, as part of a feasibility study, due to staffing resources. Therefore, a compromise was agreed whereby those delivering the intervention were to record in the service users' care records the details of their personal oral health intervention plan and any concerns or advice given by oral health professionals or dentists where such visits had occurred as part of the intervention. The proposed step of mental health professionals supporting service users with visits to oral and dental health professionals was removed from the intervention.

The final intervention components as agreed after the final workshops were as follows: 1)

Initiating a conversation about oral health; 2) providing advice to service users on keeping their teeth and mouth healthy; 3) weekly text reminders for tooth brushing; and 4) updating the service user's care record to document the details of the intervention plan. SMILE Intervention

The final intervention prototype consisted of the following components: 1.

Initiating the conversation: the health professional delivering the intervention opens the conversation by highlighting the link between oral and general health, referring to a corresponding illustration in the leaflet. Additional prompts such as 'Do you feel you are satisfied with your oral health?' are offered to encourage a discussion. Service users with urgent dental health problems are advised to call 111.

2. Top advice to keep your teeth and mouth healthy: this is the main section of the intervention, covering advice on four areas of habits related to good oral health 1) tooth brushing, including advice on good technique through an illustration, a video link, and written instructions. Service users can note down their current toothbrushing habits and tooth brushing frequency goals based on a tiered system to tailor the recommendations offered to them, depending on their level of readiness to engage with the intervention; 2) managing dry mouth due to the side effects of psychotropic medication; 3) reducing dietary sugar intake through food and drinks -service users can note down their current barriers to reducing their intake; 4) stopping or reducing tobacco use, if ready and willing to, with advice on local cessation support services; 5) responsible alcohol drinking with NHS advice on weekly alcohol unit consumption and information on local alcohol support services; 6) visiting a dentist, explaining the importance of regular dental visits and discussing individual barriers to attending dental appointments.

3.

Sending reminder text messages: Service users can opt into receiving a weekly standardised text message reminder to encourage regular tooth brushing for the first four weeks from when the intervention delivery has commenced.

4. Recording the intervention in the care record:

A note is added to the service user's care record to document that they have received the SMILE intervention. If the health professional delivering the intervention, carer, or service user raises any concerns about their oral health, or barriers to receiving dental health support, these are also recorded here so that their mental health team are aware of them. A letter is sent to the service user's care co-ordinator to confirm the same information and bring their attention to any urgent dental health issues to ensure follow-up.

Discussion

This paper describes the process of co-production used to develop the SMILE intervention, a novel intervention to offer oral health advice to community mental health service users. Through an iterative co-production process consisting of four co-production workshops, concurrent PPIE meetings, and prototyping iterations with stakeholders, we developed an intervention consisting of four steps: 1) mental health professionals initiating a conversation with service users around 24 h E g . oral health, 2) mental health professionals providing top advice to service users on keeping their teeth and mouth healthy, 3) mental health professionals sending weekly text reminders for toothbrushing and 4) mental health professionals updating the care record to document the administration of this intervention. We co-produced three intervention components to support intervention delivery: 1) intervention flowchart, 2) intervention leaflet, and 3) training manual for mental health professionals. The overall structure of the intervention remained similar to the initial proposed intervention steps; however, several amendments and considerations were suggested during the iterative process of producing the intervention components.

Suggestions were made by participants to simplify the intervention content to avoid overwhelming service users with advice. The benefit of keeping oral health advice simple was considered to be greater than providing more comprehensive and lengthy oral advice; as such, habitual changes were often difficult to implement and the success of service users implementing one change was deemed more motivational and sustainable than attempting to implement many, with perhaps varying levels of success. This was further understood and substantiated through the tiered approach which we used to understand the 'readiness' and capacity of service users to engage with the intervention.

Mental health professional participants advised that we reduce the tailored aspect of the intervention to ensure the advice they were being asked to give service users on their oral health was within the scope of their expertise. The overarching intention of the SMILE intervention was to increase accessibility of oral health information and dental hygiene advice amongst service users with SMI. To do so, the intervention must be deemed usable and understandable by the mental health professionals tasked with delivering it. Therefore, we removed the originally proposed toothbrushing model demonstration, and scaled back the recording of the intervention from the service users' care plan to their care record, as a recommendation for care co-ordinators to support dentist visits where needed.

Our previous work on understanding the barriers to oral health access for people with SMI identified the impact of mental ill-health itself, lack of patient involvement, and a lack of accessibility and availability of dental services for this group. Barriers contributing to oral health inequities amongst people with SMI compared to the general population also included a low level of integration of dental and mental health services, and limited inter-disciplinary working.

The main facilitators of better access to oral health were more effective communication skills of service providers to service users, and further support through the involvement of carers (4).

The co-production of the SMILE intervention components (i.e. the intervention flowchart, intervention leaflet, and the training manual for interventionists) specifically aim to reduce the barriers and utilise the facilitators identified in this previous work.

As identified by a recent systematic review and meta-analysis on mental providers' attitudes, knowledge and practice relating to providing oral health support (16), there is unexplored potential for mental health professionals supporting service users with their oral health. Our intervention addresses this gap; importantly, by co-producing SMILE with key professional and service user stakeholder groups, we sought to ensure the intervention is simple to administer with minimal time resources, given the commonly identified workload challenges among mental health professionals (17).

Furthermore, this study aligns with Palmier-Claus' et al. 's (18) seminal randomized-control trial (RCT) assessing the impact of link-workers in supporting service users with SMI in accessing dental care and services, with the aim of improving their oral health. Link workers were support workers within NHS mental health services, and they were specifically tasked with providing support for people with SMI in attending a routine dental appointment during the intervention.

Findings from this study were promising in that the intervention was deemed acceptable, feasible and safe, and initial results showed promise in terms of clinical effectiveness. Our study adds to such findings in that it provides a framework for understanding the process behind coproduction of such an intervention, which engages stakeholders ranging from those receiving the care, and those implementing/delivering the care. Such insights are beneficial in modifying and developing the intervention which, if found to be effective, may eventually become integrated into standard care provision.

Strengths and limitations

The approach taken in this study presents several strengths in this field of research. Firstly, the tiered approach adopted during co-production was an important strength for developing an effective and practical intervention for service users with SMI. Given the range of complex needs that service users with SMI experience, such an approach is important as it bases evaluations of intervention components through hierarchical levels of 'readiness' and capacity of individuals to engage with the intervention. Secondly, this intervention signifies important progress in the field of oral health of people with SMI: the SMILE intervention is a novel systemlevel approach to both empowering service users to engage with preventive action to maintain and improve their oral health, while also working with MHPs and existing healthcare settings to provide systemic support for people with SMI. This builds upon our previous work which found that barriers to receiving dental care for people with SMI were generally cross-cutting across personal, interpersonal and system-levels (4) and specifically addresses both bottom-up and top-down approaches to improving the oral health of service users with SMI.

However, this study also presents with several limitations. Firstly, we recognise the lack of diversity in the stakeholder representation from different ethnic backgrounds and non-Englishspeaking groups. While we aimed to transparently report on a pragmatic, iterative intervention development approach with ongoing real-time feedback and refinements from multiple stakeholder groups, this also meant that we did not conduct formal qualitative analysis of workshop data. Therefore, it is possible that a more systemic, in-depth understanding of stakeholders' needs and preferences had been overlooked at times.

Future directions

The next step is to conduct a planned single-arm feasibility trial of the co-produced intervention via two NHS sites. If the intervention proves to be feasible and acceptable to service users and mental health professionals, a larger-scale pilot study with a control group would be the next step.

If the current study indicates the SMILE intervention could be feasible and acceptable, additional professional stakeholder consultations could include a broader range of stakeholder backgrounds, such as health commissioners and health economists, to explore how the intervention could be implemented at the wider system level. We realise the need to engage with broader groups of stakeholders to understand the acceptability and feasibility of integrating this intervention within a public healthcare system like the NHS.

Conclusions

In this paper we described the co-production process of SMILE, a novel systems-level oral health intervention for community mental health service users with SMI. We shed light on the needs and preferences of service users, mental and dental health professionals, and showcased an example of how an intervention can be developed pragmatically with real-time engagement and refinements from the target user group and professional groups involved in intervention delivery and content. The next step for the SMILE intervention will be to conduct feasibility and acceptability testing to evaluate whether the intervention can progress onto further effectiveness testing.
