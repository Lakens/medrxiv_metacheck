Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 225 papers containing 405 instances of non-exact p-values, the module correctly detected 269 cases (true positives) and incorrectly identified 78 (false positives). It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. Full text

Abstract

Objective: To develop and independently validate a decomposed, modality-specific large language model (LLM) pipeline that abstracts structured variables from the free-text neuroimaging reports of a multimodal stroke workup, and to validate the resulting structured dataset for reuse in downstream research.

A frozen pipeline (temperature 0) extracting 22 variables across CT head, CT angiography, CT perfusion, and MRI brain was applied to 1,669 ED encounters for dizziness or vertigo; 11 variables were pre-specified as primary (Tier 1). Reproducibility used three same-session runs and a nineweek re-run; three frontier models (Claude Sonnet 4.6, GPT-5.4, Gemini 3.5 Flash) measured cross-model agreement (Cohen's/Fleiss' κ). Accuracy was assessed in an independent 200-encounter cohort (150 random, 50 enriched for rare findings); 100 were double-read by two blinded reviewers with discordances adjudicated.

Results: Test-retest reproducibility was high (Tier 1 Fleiss' κ=0.993; 99.8% exact) and stable nine weeks later (κ=0.992); inter-model agreement was κ=0.962. Against the adjudicated reference, pooled Tier 1 accuracy was 0.971 to 0.988 across models (Cohen's κ 0.959 to 0.982), comparable to inter-reviewer agreement (κ=0.932). Accuracy was 0.980 to 0.983 in random and 0.955 to 0.977 in enriched encounters (agreement on model-flagged findings, not sensitivity).

Discussion: A decomposed LLM pipeline produced reproducible, model-portable, stable, and accurate multimodal abstraction. Because schema design and validation are up-front costs, such pipelines scale to larger research cohorts.

The quantified per-field error rates support reuse of this dataset with field-specific review, and such pipelines offer a practical path to large-scale, reproducible research datasets.

Background and Significance

Turning free-text radiology reports into structured, analyzable variables is a persistent bottleneck in clinical research data curation. Manual abstraction is accurate but slow, requires domain expertise, and is reviewer-dependent, which in practice caps the size of cohorts that can be assembled and the number of questions that can be asked of them. This constraint is acute in the emergency evaluation of dizziness and vertigo, where distinguishing benign vestibular causes from posterior-circulation stroke frequently requires a multimodal workup: non-contrast CT head (CTH), CT angiography (CTA) of the head and neck, CT perfusion (CTP), and MRI brain. Each study generates a separate narrative report whose vessel-grading and posterior-fossa terminology must be interpreted and encoded. 1, 2, 3, 4, 5 Dizziness and vertigo are among the most common emergency department presentations, at roughly 3 to 4% of visits, and a recognized source of missed posterior-circulation stroke, which makes accurate, scalable capture of this workup valuable. 6, 7, 8 Classical natural-language-processing (NLP) systems automated parts of this task but remained narrow, single-task classifiers that required annotated training data and did not generalize across modalities. 9, 10 Large language models (LLMs) now extract structured variables from unstructured clinical text with accuracy that can match or exceed specialized tools, and prompt specificity, not model choice alone, largely governs quality, and open-source models achieve high agreement extracting from emergency neuroimaging reports specifically. 11, 12, 13, 14, 15 LLM-based abstraction is accordingly being adopted for largescale cohort and registry curation, with validation frameworks emerging to certify machine-extracted data. 16, 17, 18, 19, 20 Three gaps recur, however: prompt and schema design are rarely standardized or reported; reproducibility, meaning whether a model returns the same answer on re-execution, and crossdeveloper agreement are seldom quantified, despite being prerequisites for trusting machine-extracted data; and most studies address a single modality, usually CT head alone. We are not aware of a prior pipeline that abstracts the full CTH, CTA, CTP, and MRI stroke workup at a scale approaching 1,669 encounters with reproducibility, cross-model, and independently adjudicated accuracy reporting in one system.

Objective

We developed a decomposed, modality-specific LLM pipeline for structured abstraction of the multimodal stroke-workup neuroimaging cascade, and evaluated three properties required of a research-grade extraction tool: (1) reproducibility, both within a session and across a multi-week interval; (2) agreement across independently developed frontier models; and (3) field-level accuracy against an independent, two-reviewer, adjudicated human reference. We pre-specified eleven high-confidence variables as the primary (Tier 1) endpoints. The overarching aim was to validate the resulting dataset for reuse by quantifying, per field, the error a future study would inherit. Although instantiated for stroke imaging, the architecture (modality-specific decomposition with fixed schemas, deterministic quality control, and prevalence-informed sampling) is intended as a generalizable template for multimodal report abstraction.

Materials and Methods

Study design and cohort

This retrospective study at Staten Island University Hospital (Northwell Health) covered January 1, 2022-October 31, 2025 and was approved by the Northwell Health IRB (#26-0151; Category 5 expedited), with a waiver of consent and HIPAA authorization; all patient identifiers were removed before analysis.

Reporting follows STARD 2015, adapted for automated report abstraction (checklist provided as a separate supplementary file). Adults (≥18 years) presenting to the ED with an initial complaint of dizziness or vertigo were eligible; all consecutive eligible encounters over the study period were extracted by the institutional quantitative-intelligence team, defining 1,669 encounters. Imaging reflects the institution's usual stroke-workup pathway, not a study-imposed requirement. MRI completion was not an inclusion requirement; 719 encounters (43.1%) completed MRI and constitute the subset for MRI-dependent fields. Calls 1-4 are independent of one another and each reads only source report text; the only LLM output passed to another LLM call is the infarct evidence phrase that the MRI core call supplies to the localization calls (together with the MRI report). No call uses tools, retrieval, memory, planning, or messaging between calls, and cross-modal information is combined only by the deterministic layers described next (Figure 1B).

A per-case confidence score is computed deterministically from cross-modal concordance rules (for example, reconciling an indeterminate CT-head finding against a confirmatory MRI). A deterministic quality-control layer then scans report text with rule-based regular expressions (not a learned model) for unnegated abnormality language inconsistent with the structured output; 80 of 1,669 encounters (4.8%) in Claude run 1 (50 for GPT-5.4, 57 for Gemini) triggered at least one such flag; most applied a valuechanging correction, and the remainder were advisory flags that recorded a possible inconsistency (for example, unnegated abnormality wording, or a vessel grade lacking a supporting phrase) without altering the value. A JSON-repair and exponential-backoff layer ensured near-complete processing. All prompts, rules, and thresholds were frozen in v14.0.3 (July 22, 2026) before any production run and before validation sampling. Models were called as claude-sonnet-4.6, gpt-5.4, and gemini-3.5-flash; no dated snapshot identifier was available, so run dates are reported (production runs August 1-5, 2026; GPT-5.4 re-run October 8, 2026). Prompts and code are provided (Data availability).

Variable tiers

The 22 variables were pre-specified into eleven primary (Tier 1) and eleven secondary (Tier 2) fields before validation (finalized before validation, not preregistered). Tier 1 comprised the CTH acute finding and microvascular disease; the seven CTA vessel grades; whether CTP was performed; and acute infarct on MRI, which are the findings most consequential to the dizziness/stroke decision and most cleanly defined by the categorical schema. Each vessel was graded on a 0-4 stenosis scale; a value of 9 is assigned only when no CTA report text is available (11 encounters) and was treated as not evaluable (excluded from numerators and denominators); within a reported CTA, a vessel that is not described is graded 0.

Agreement and accuracy were computed on the full 0-4 categorical grade; finding prevalence is reported as the proportion of assessable vessels with any stenosis (grade ≥1).

Iterative development and the 155-case development cohort

The pipeline was refined over four phases. Phase 1 began with 30 cases, sampled at random from the MRI cohort, for prompt design; these 30 are the first cases of the 155-case development cohort described below and, like all development cases, were excluded from the validation sample. Phase 2 performed prompt engineering across a four-model panel (GPT-5, GPT-5-mini, Gemini 2.5 Pro, and Gemini 2.5 Flash), driven by multi-model concordance rather than human review. A first batch of 75 cases was then reviewed once by the development reviewer (a general neurologist and director of a Joint Commission-certified primary stroke program, separate from the three validation reviewers), confirming high accuracy on common findings but sparse coverage of rare endpoints. In Phase 3, multi-model consensus over the 719 MRI encounters was treated as a proxy for prevalence to size a targeted, prevalence-informed enrichment (target ≥14 human-confirmed positives per endpoint, for an approximately ±15-point 95% Wilson-score interval), producing a 155-case development cohort (112 random, which include the 30 initial promptdesign cases, plus 43 curated) drawn from the MRI cohort. This exhausted the model-flagged hemorrhages in the MRI cohort (all 18 hemorrhage encounters flagged by Claude among the 719 were in the 155), so the subsequent enrichment of the validation sample (below) drew on all 1,669 encounters. This multi-model consensus was only a sampling signal; no accuracy estimate used it, all being computed against the human-adjudicated reference. In Phase 4 the frozen architecture was run across the full cohort with three frontier models. The development reviewer coded the 155 development cases and none of the validation cases. The 155 development cases were coded blinded to model output and to case-selection origin; they were excluded from the validation sample.

Independent validation and reference-standard construction

The validation cohort comprised 200 encounters drawn, after the pipeline was frozen, from the 1,514 encounters outside the 155-case development cohort (no overlap, confirmed by accession and patient identifier). Using the same model-consensus enrichment strategy as in development, 50 encounters were selected from the frozen pipeline's output (never human labels) to over-sample rare findings, prioritizing hemorrhage (including the three remaining model-flagged hemorrhages, all outside the MRI cohort) and basilar stenosis, then CT-head acute findings, MRI acute infarct, and MCA stenosis; 17 of the 50 came from outside the MRI cohort. The other 150 were randomly sampled from the remaining non-development encounters. One hundred encounters (the 50 enriched and 50 random) were independently abstracted by two neurology residents (Reviewer 1 and Reviewer 2), each blinded to the other and to model output; the remaining 100 random encounters were single-read (50 per reviewer). Reviewers were blinded to the origin of each case: random and enriched cases were interleaved in randomized order, and only the principal investigator held the origin key, which was opened after review and adjudication were complete.

A single reference standard was built per field: concordant reviewer values were taken as-is, and disagreements were resolved by a blinded vascular neurologist (Reviewer 3). The adjudicator resolved disagreements only and never overrode a concordant cell, so the reference is independent of model output. Accuracy was exact categorical agreement between each model and the adjudicated reference over evaluable cases; not-applicable cells were excluded from numerator and denominator. Because the reference standard is expert abstraction of the same free-text reports, these figures quantify agreement with expert report abstraction (abstraction fidelity) rather than accuracy against imaging ground truth.

Statistical analysis

Test-retest reproducibility used three same-session runs of Claude Sonnet 4.6 (Fleiss' κ, exact three-way agreement); temporal reproducibility used a nine-weeks-later GPT-5.4 re-run on the identical cohort (Cohen's κ, exact agreement). Pairwise inter-model agreement (Cohen's κ) was computed across the three frontier models, aligned to one encounter order (identifiers 1-1,669). Reproducibility and inter-model agreement are summarized as the mean of field-level κ. For the independent validation, accuracy is the pooled proportion of correctly abstracted Tier 1 cells with 95% Wilson score intervals, and overall agreement is Cohen's κ with 95% confidence intervals from a case-clustered bootstrap (2,000 resamples); pooled-accuracy Wilson intervals treat cells as independent and do not account for within-encounter clustering, whereas the κ intervals do. Cells with a not-applicable reference or a vessel grade of 9 were excluded; a missing model value against an applicable reference was scored as incorrect. Accuracy is reported for the 150 random encounters (the estimate for the non-enriched remainder), the 50 enriched encounters (rare-finding performance), and the 100 double-read encounters against the adjudicated reference (the comparison benchmarked against inter-reviewer agreement). As a sensitivity analysis, an approximate design-weighted estimate for the 1,514 non-development encounters weighted each random encounter by 1,464/150 and each enriched encounter by 1. Vessel grades were treated as unweighted categorical values across the 0-4 scale; a quadratic-weighted κ sensitivity analysis and pervessel confusion matrices are provided in the Supplement (Supplementary Tables S2 and S3). Wilson score intervals were also used for prevalence and for sample-size targets. 21

Reproducibility and temporal stability

Across three same-session runs, Tier 1 mean Fleiss' κ was 0.993 (99.8% exact three-way agreement) and secondary-field κ was 0.991. Re-running the GPT-5.4 configuration on the identical cohort nine weeks later reproduced the output almost exactly (Tier 1 mean κ=0.992, 99.9% exact; secondary κ=0.974), indicating stability across a multi-week interval rather than only within a session; because the models are accessed through a hosted gateway that could in principle be updated between runs, this reflects observed stability of the deployment rather than a guarantee (Table 2, Figure 2).

Inter-model concordance

Run with three independently developed frontier models, mean pairwise agreement was κ=0.962 (Tier 1) and κ=0.932 (secondary), consistent across all three model pairs (Table 2, Figure 2). Because the models were built by different developers, their convergence indicates the structured outputs are reproducible across systems rather than idiosyncratic to one model; because such models may nonetheless share pretraining data and failure modes, this convergence reflects cross-system reproducibility rather than correctness, which is assessed separately against the adjudicated reference.

Discussion

A decomposed, modality-specific LLM pipeline abstracted the full four-modality stroke workup across 1,669 ED encounters with very high same-session reproducibility (Tier 1 κ=0.993), stability on a nineweeks-later re-run (κ=0.992), high cross-developer concordance (κ=0.962), and accuracy against an independent adjudicated reference (0.97-0.99 across three models) at the level of inter-reviewer agreement. Three independent lines of evidence (the same model repeated, three vendors, and models versus humans) converge to within a few percentage points field by field. Model-reference agreement was high and similar in magnitude to the agreement between the two reviewers; because the two comparisons use different reference constructions and evaluable-cell sets, this does not establish human equivalence; residual disagreement likely reflects both report ambiguity and reference-standard noise.

These figures quantify the measurement error a downstream analysis would inherit: pooled Tier 1 disagreement with the adjudicated expert abstraction was roughly 1 to 3% in the double-read sample, though error rates varied by field (about 6% for the CT-head acute finding), a modest, now-quantified error budget for future studies to account for. These figures reflect fidelity to expert abstraction of the reports rather than correctness against imaging ground truth; prior work likewise reported high agreement with report abstraction but only moderate agreement with research-grade image scoring. 25 Reproducibility and robustness under repeated runs are increasingly recognized as under-reported dimensions of clinical LLM extraction 22 ; the near-ceiling test-retest, temporal, and cross-model agreement reported here address that gap directly.

We attribute this to three compounding design choices rather than any single innovation: decomposition into narrow, modality-specific LLM calls with fixed categorical schemas and explicit decision rules; iterative, disagreement-driven prompt refinement; and conditional calls with deterministic qualitycontrol overlays. This pattern is consistent with evidence that decomposing a task into narrow sub-tasks reduces cross-field contamination relative to monolithic prompting, though the calls here are fixed and non-interacting rather than autonomous agents. A field-by-field account of successes and struggles (Supplementary Table S5) maps onto the architecture: explicit presence/absence findings, discrete vessel grades, and study ascertainment were captured most reliably, whereas open-vocabulary localization and interpretation-dependent fields (infarct territory, perfusion deficit, carotid grade) remained hardest. 23, 24 A second methodological contribution is the triple-duty use of one modest human-adjudicated cohort: as a development signal that localized ambiguous fields, as a prevalence anchor enriched for rare findings, and as the scarce gold-standard sample in a prevalence-informed enrichment that sized sampling before review. The reviewer coded blinded to model output and to selection origin, so enrichment concentrated expert effort where it was most informative. Because the full cohort was much larger than the development sample, the independent validation could be drawn from genuinely held-out cases; because part of that sample was itself enriched, its accuracy is reported separately for random and enriched encounters.

Toward population-scale clinical research

These results fit a broader shift in which decomposed LLM systems are moving clinical information extraction from narrow single-task classifiers toward general pipelines that read, reason over, and structure free-text records at scale. 11, 16, 17, 18, 19 The decisive feature for research infrastructure is cost structure: schema design, prompt engineering, and validation are substantial up-front costs (with periodic or change-triggered revalidation), after which the marginal cost per additional case is small, fixed, and dominated by inference tokens rather than expert time. After external multi-site validation, a pipeline of this kind could be applied to cohorts an order of magnitude larger at a low, predictable per-case cost, making previously impractical population-level questions (prevalence, imaging-utilization and practice variation, temporal trends, and the epidemiology of rare but high-consequence findings) tractable, and a cohort feasible to refresh as practice and models evolve. We frame this as a complement to, not a replacement for, expert review: the reproducibility, cross-model, and adjudicated-accuracy reporting shown here, with external validation, are prerequisites for trusting such pipelines as the engine for large research datasets.

Limitations

This is a single-center, retrospective study; external validation across institutions and reporting conventions remains to be done. The reference standard is expert abstraction of the reports by two neurology residents with vascular-neurology adjudication rather than a neuroradiologist re-read of the source images; agreement therefore reflects fidelity to the documented report, and vessel-grade agreement in particular should be read as agreement with the report rather than with the underlying images. Because MRI was completed in 43.1% of encounters, the CTH acute-finding field is a cross-modally resolved construct in encounters with MRI and a CT-only construct otherwise; on the double-read set, Claude's accuracy for this field was 97.7% (43/44) without MRI and 90.7% (49/54) with MRI, in small subgroups. CTP and MRA utilization were ascertained from report text rather than structured order fields (CTP from the CTA reports, full cohort; MRA only from MRI reports, 719 MRI encounters). The 50 enriched validation encounters were selected from model-flagged output, so accuracy in that stratum reflects agreement on model-flagged findings and cannot measure how often the model misses a finding. Because the enriched set included every remaining model-flagged basilar stenosis, CT-head acute finding, and hemorrhage, the random stratum contains none of these model-flagged cases; it represents the non-enriched encounters, and the design-weighted estimate approximates the full non-development population. The MRI chronic-infarct location field is free text and not directly comparable across models.

The secondary fields that lagged most, chronic-infarct localization and CT-perfusion deficit, are those most exposed to narrative hedging and non-standardized phrasing, where a controlled vocabulary and targeted human review would most help. The study was not designed to validate detection of individual rare acute pathologies. Intracranial hemorrhage in particular was infrequent, and its targeted enrichment drew on the limited pool of model-flagged cases, so pooled accuracy should not be interpreted as validated hemorrhage detection; any hemorrhage-related discordances are retained within the CT-head acutefinding and acute-type fields. Establishing detection performance for rare acute pathologies would require a dedicated, adequately powered study. Finally, the pipeline abstracts report content rather than images and inherits any error in the underlying report.

Conclusion

A decomposed, modality-specific LLM pipeline produced structured multimodal stroke-workup abstraction that was highly reproducible, temporally stable, portable across the three tested models, and accurate against a rigorously adjudicated reference, at a scale and cost relevant to research data curation.

The quantified per-field error rates support research reuse of this dataset with field-specific quality assessment and additional review for weaker or rare fields, and, with external validation, pipelines of this design offer a practical path to large-scale, reproducible clinical research datasets.

Pipeline architecture (v14.0.3 )

Each encounter passes through up to six single-turn LLM calls at temperature 0 (top_p 0.95 for GPT-5.4 and Gemini; Claude temperature 0 only; no fixed seed) via an institutional enterprise generative API:(1) CT head (acute-finding status, an indeterminate flag, acute-finding type, microvascular disease), which also receives the CTA head report so that non-contrast head findings described within a combined CTA report are captured; (2) CTA head/neck, grading seven vessels (bilateral vertebral, basilar, bilateral internal carotid, bilateral middle cerebral) on a fixed 0-4 stenosis scale (0 = none,1 = <50%,2 = 50-69%,3 = 70-99%/near-occlusion,4 = occlusion;9 = not evaluable when no CTA text); (3) CT perfusion (technical quality, perfusion deficit), called only when a deterministic pattern match finds CT-perfusion terminology in the CTA reports (the same match sets CT-perfusion-performed status); (4) MRI core (acute infarct, chronic infarct, other abnormality and type, technical quality); and (5-6) acute-and chronic-infarct localization (territory, bilaterality), called only when the MRI core call returns a positive acute or chronic infarct. MRA inclusion is determined deterministically from MRI report text rather than by an LLM call.
