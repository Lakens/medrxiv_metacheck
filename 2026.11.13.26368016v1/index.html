Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 128 papers with 246 instances of power statements, 203 were correctly detected (true positives), 22 were missed (false negatives) and 21 were incorrectly detected (false positives). Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

How Marginal Significance works

List all sentences that describe an effect as ‘marginally significant’.

The marginal module searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The list of terms is a subset of those listed in a blog post by Matthew Hankins. The module returns all sentences that match terms describing ‘marginally significant’ results.

Some of the terms identified might not be problematic in some contexts, and there are ways to describe ‘marginal significance’ that are not detected by the module.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 51 papers with 87 statements, this module correctly identified 38 statements (true positives) and incorrectly flagged 22 statements (false positives). It failed to detect 27 statements. Thus, among all statements flagged by the module, 63% were genuine cases (positive predictive value). However, the module missed 42% of all true statements (false negative rate).

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. Some differences come from the ground truth: it lacks p-values with a correction name (“p Bonf = .061”), and counts partial eta squared written with its subscript after it (“η 2 p = .05”), “p = .05”, and some table notes and figure legends (“† p < .10, *p < .05”), which this module leaves out, as non-significant p-values. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Effect Sizes in t-tests and F-tests works

The Effect Size module checks if effect sizes are correctly reported in t-tests and F-tests.

The Effect Size check searches for regular expressions that match typical ways in which effect sizes are reported. It subsequently checks different ways in which Cohen’s d, g, ηp2, and ωp2 can be computed against the reported value. If effects are missing, or might be incorrect, you the module provides a warning. The module was validated on APA reported statistical tests, and might miss effect sizes that were reported in other reporting styles. It was validated by the Metacheck team on papers published in Psychological Science.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download files from additional data repositories reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Raphael Merz

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Levi Baruch

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Full text

Abstract

Background. Smoking is a prevalent and preventable risk factor for poor brain health. These habits often establish in early adulthood and have lasting consequences, yet most research focuses on later-life smoking, overlooking important prevention windows. We examined smoking trajectories from early adulthood in relation to midlife brain health.

Methods. In 1,900 adults (65.6% female, mean age 32.5 years) from the Dutch population-based Generation R Study (ORACLE subset), smoking was assessed five times over 15 years, from before pregnancy to midlife. Kmeans clustering identified smoking trajectories, alongside measures of current smoking, pack-years, and abstinence years. At mean age 47.7 years, MRI assessed gray and white matter volumes, white-matter hyperintensity volume, cerebrovascular markers, and vertex-wise cortical thickness and surface area.

Findings. Five trajectories were identified: non-smoking (63%), quitting before pregnancy (12%), quitting during/after pregnancy (11%), relapsing (9%), and persistent smoking (4%). All compared with non-smoking, quitting before pregnancy was associated with 6.57 cm³ larger gray matter (95%CI[0.27,12.87],p=.041), 7.71 cm³ larger white matter (95%CI[0.77,14.65],p=.030), and larger cortical surface area in bilateral middle/inferior temporal regions. Quitting during/after pregnancy was associated with more lacunes (OR=2.39; 95%CI[1.23,4.68],p=.011), but similar brain volumes. Relapsing was associated with 0.15 standard deviations more white matter hyperintensity volume (95%CI[0.00,0.30],p=.045) and thinner regions in the right cortex. Persistent smoking had double white matter hyperintensities (0.31 standard deviations; 95%CI[0.10,0.50], p=.003) and widespread bilateral thinner cortices. Similarly, single-timepoint measures like current smoking (vs never smoking) were linked to 9.72 cm³ smaller gray matter volume (95%CI[-16.53,-2.19],p=.005), 0.17 standard deviations more hyperintensity volume (95%CI[0.03,0.32],p=.015), and widespread thinner cortices. Each additional pack-year was associated with 0.45 cm³ smaller gray matter volume (95%CI[-0.85,-0.06],p=.025); each additional abstinence year was associated with 0.01 standard deviations less hyperintensity volume (95%CI[-0.02,0.00],p=.022).

Interpretation. Smoking trajectories from early adulthood and midlife are associated with midlife brain health, supporting early and sustained smoking cessation.

Introduction

Worldwide, smoking is a significant public health concern. In 2020, an estimated 22.3% of individuals aged above 15 years used tobacco, with the prevalence following an inverted U-shaped pattern across age and peaking at around 50 years. 1 Smoking cessation is difficult but achievable, and evidence-based interventions can result in sustained abstinence in a substantial proportion of individuals. 2 Beyond its established detrimental effects on physical and vascular health, 3 smoking has been linked to poorer mental health, including more depression, anxiety, and cognitive decline. 4 These findings underscore the importance of understanding how smoking affects the brain.

Previous studies have demonstrated robust associations between smoking and adverse brain outcomes across midand late life. In the UK Biobank (>32,000 participants), both current and lifetime smoking were associated with smaller gray and white matter volumes, with dose-response associations with pack-years. 5 Lifetime smoking has also been associated with greater white matter hyperintensity burden, 6 higher prevalence of lacunes and cortical infarcts, 7 and higher prevalence of cerebral microbleeds. 8 However, several gaps remain. First, smoking is a dynamic behavior rather than a stable trait: quitting commonly requires repeated attempts, and relapse is frequent. 9 Longitudinal studies have identified distinct adult smoking trajectories, including persistent smoking, quitting, relapse, and decreasing use, 10 which differ in their associations with lung disease and mortality. 11 Yet, whether such trajectories relate to brain structure has rarely been examined. These associations are biologically plausible: experimental animal studies indicate that repeated nicotine exposure can induce persistent structural and synaptic plasticity in brain regions involved in reward and behavioral control, which may contribute to the development and maintenance of nicotine dependence. 12 Second, smoking patterns established and modified earlier in adulthood may have consequences extending into later life, with earlier cessation associated with greater long-term health benefits. 13 However, much of the literature associating smoking with brain health has focused on smoking measured in mid-or later life, potentially overlooking both the contribution of earlier changes in smoking behavior and opportunities for prevention before exposure cumulates. Third, it remains unclear whether trajectories add information beyond the conventional measures used in most neuroimaging studies, such as ever versus never smoking, which can merge behaviorally distinct groups. In adolescents, trajectory-based approaches have captured meaningful heterogeneity in smoking exposure and may be more informative than simple indicators for predicting later mental health. 14 Whether trajectory-based modeling adds information for adult brain health remains unclear.

We therefore investigated smoking behavior and brain health in middle-aged adults from a population-based cohort. Smoking was assessed at five time points over approximately 15 years, allowing us to derive longitudinal smoking trajectories. Brain health was subsequently assessed using global brain volumes, cerebrovascular markers, and hypothesis-free vertex-wise measures of cortical thickness and surface area. We aimed to (1) identify smoking trajectories; (2) examine their associations with midlife brain health; and (3) evaluate whether trajectorybased approaches capture associations missed or obscured by single-timepoint measures.

Methods

Participants

This study was conducted within the Origins of Alzheimer's Disease Across the Life Course (ORACLE) Study, 15 embedded in the population-based Generation R Study. 16 Generation R enrolled pregnant females and their partners living in Rotterdam, the Netherlands, with expected delivery dates between April 2002 and January 2006. Baseline therefore reflects the period when participants were expecting a child, with intake during the first trimester of pregnancy. Approximately 15 years later, a selected group was invited for an ORACLE follow-up focused on adult brain health. This group comprised Generation R (i) Focus cohort participants, who had undergone extensive earlier assessments; (ii) a random sample of other participants; and (iii) females with a history of hypertensive disorders of pregnancy.

Of 2,083 ORACLE participants, we excluded those missing three or more of five smoking assessments (n=116), those without any usable neuroimaging sequence (n=56), and those with major incidental neuroimaging findings that could distort structural measures (e.g., meningiomas >3 cm; n=11). The final analytical sample included 1,900 participants. Generation R and ORACLE were approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Erasmus Medical Center and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. All participants provided written informed consent.

Smoking variables.

Self-reported smoking behavior was assessed using postal questionnaires and operationalized as (1) repeated smoking status and (2) retrospective single-timepoint measures of smoking exposure.

Repeated smoking status.

Smoking was measured over approximately 15 years. At intake, participants retrospectively reported smoking before pregnancy and current smoking during pregnancy (or until pregnancy was known). Current smoking was subsequently assessed at 6, 10, and 14 years after childbirth. Females reported their own smoking at all waves and their partner's smoking before pregnancy and at 6 and 14 years postpartum. Males reported their own smoking at intake and 10 years postpartum. Across waves, smoking was harmonized into a binary indicator of current smoking (yes/no); details are provided in Supplementary Table S1.

Single-timepoint measures of smoking exposure. At 14 years after childbirth, detailed retrospective smoking information was collected, from which we derived three single-timepoint measures: smoking status, pack-years, and abstinence years.

Smoking status was assessed using the question "Did you ever smoke?" and categorized into never smoking, quit smoking, and current smoking.

Pack-years were calculated for those who indicated they had quit or were currently smoking at 14 years after childbirth, by multiplying smoking duration (in years) by average packs smoked per day. Smoking duration was calculated as age at smoking cessation minus age at smoking initiation minus any reported periods of temporary cessation for quitting, and as current age minus age at initiation minus temporary cessation when currently smoking. Average packs per day were derived from a categorical question on daily cigarette consumption. Daily cigarette categories were converted to packs using category midpoints (<1/day [0. Abstinence years were calculated for those who indicated they had quit at 14 years after childbirth and were defined as the number of years since smoking cessation.

Brain health assessment

Neuroimaging was performed approximately 15 years after inclusion using a 3 Tesla GE Discovery MR750w scanner (General Electric, Milwaukee, WI, USA) with an 8-channel head coil. The scanning protocol and qualitycontrol procedures have been described previously. 15 Brain health was assessed across three domains: (1) total gray matter, white matter, and white matter hyperintensity volumes; (2) cerebrovascular markers; and (3) hypothesis-free vertex-wise cortical thickness and surface area.

Brain volumes.

Total gray matter, total white matter, and white matter hyperintensity volumes were derived from T1-weighted images (inversion recovery GRE; 3D resolution 1x1x1 mm voxels; two-fold accelerated ARC). Images were processed using FreeSurfer version 6.0 with default setting, 17 which provides segmentation of gray matter, white matter, and cerebrospinal fluid. Surface-based models were generated and anatomically labeled using the Desikan-Killiany atlas. 18 Outcomes from low-quality scans were set to missing and imputed.

Cerebrovascular markers.

Lacunes and cortical infarcts were assessed on FLAIR images (2D acquisition; voxel size 0.8x1.1x2.5 mm) by trained researchers. Cerebral microbleeds were assessed on T2*-weighted images (3D acquisition; voxel size 0.8x1.1x1 mm; echo time >30 ms), defined as hypointense foci ≤10 mm, and identified through visual inspection by trained raters. Outcomes from low-quality scans were set to missing and imputed.

Hypothesis-free, vertex-wise analyses of cortical thickness and surface area. Cortical thickness and surface area were derived from T1-weighted images (inversion recovery GRE; 1x1x1 mm voxels) processed with FreeSurfer version 6.0. Outcomes from low-quality scans were excluded in vertex-wise analyses.

Covariates

Covariates were assessed at intake by postal self-report questionnaire and included age at neuroimaging, sex, country of origin, educational level, household income, body mass index (BMI), and alcohol use before pregnancy. Age at neuroimaging was calculated from date of birth and imaging date. Country of origin was based on participants' parents' countries of birth 16 and categorized as (i) Europe excluding Turkey, North America, Oceania, and Indonesia, or (ii) Africa, Asia excluding Indonesia, South America, and Turkey. Education was categorized as low (none/primary), intermediate (vocational/higher secondary), or high (Bachelor, Master, or PhD). Household income was dichotomized at €2,200/month, the modal salary in the Netherlands at baseline. Because females were pregnant at baseline, female BMI used retrospectively reported pre-pregnancy weight; male BMI used self-reported weight at intake. Alcohol use before pregnancy was categorized as <1 glass/week, ≥1 glass/week but <1 glass/day, or ≥1 glass/day. For sensitivity analyses, we additionally assessed hypertensive disorders of pregnancy, specifically gestational hypertension and preeclampsia, defined as systolic blood pressure. ≥140 mmHg or diastolic blood pressure ≥90 mmHg after 20 weeks of gestation, without or with proteinuria, respectively.

Statistical analysis

Analyses were conducted in R version 4.5.1. White matter hyperintensity volume was natural log-transformed because of skewness and standardized to facilitate interpretation. Missing data were handled using multiple imputation by chained equations with 10 imputed datasets and 10 iterations per dataset; estimates were pooled using Rubin's rules. 19, 20 Smoking trajectories and brain health. Smoking trajectories were identified using k-means clustering of repeated smoking-status measurements. This unsupervised approach iteratively assigns participants to groups without imposing trajectory shapes. 21 The number of trajectories was selected using (i) the Calinski-Harabasz criterion, 22 (ii) consistency with prior literature, and (iii) retention of meaningful between-group heterogeneity.

Associations between trajectories and brain health were examined using linear regression for continuous outcomes (gray matter, white matter, and white matter hyperintensity volumes), logistic regression for binary cerebrovascular outcomes (lacunar infarcts, cortical infarcts, and cerebral microbleeds), and vertex-wise analyses for local cortical thickness and surface area. Vertex-wise data were registered to standard stereotaxic space and smoothed with a 10-mm full-width at half-maximum Gaussian kernel, allowing identification of spatially distributed cortical clusters without restricting analyses to predefined regions. The lowest-exposure trajectory (non-smoking) served as reference. Effect estimates are reported as adjusted mean differences for continuous outcomes and odds ratios for binary outcomes.

Comparison with single-timepoint smoking measures.

Association analyses were repeated using smoking status (never [reference], quit, current) in the full sample, pack-years among former and current smoking participants, and abstinence years among former smoking participants. The same outcome-specific analytical framework was used.

Sensitivity analyses.

Three sensitivity analyses were restricted to brain volumes (gray matter, white matter and white matter hyperintensity volume), as cerebrovascular markers were too sparse. We tested sex-by-smoking interactions, excluded women with a history of hypertensive disorders of pregnancy, and added a random intercept for family to account for clustering of partners within families.

Covariate adjustment.

Minimally adjusted models included age at neuroimaging and sex; fully adjusted models additionally included country of origin, education, household income, BMI, and alcohol use before pregnancy. Fully adjusted models were considered the primary analyses. Vertex-wise analyses were conducted only in fully adjusted models.

Multiple-test correction. Vertex-wise analyses were cluster-wise corrected for multiple testing. 23

Results

Study population characteristics

The analytical sample included 1,900 participants, of whom 65.6% were female. Mean age was 32.5 years (SD 4.8) at intake and 47.7 years (SD 4.9) at neuroimaging (Table 1).

Smoking trajectories and subsequent brain health

The optimal solution comprised five trajectories (Figure 1A): non-smoking (n=1,203; 63.3%), quit before pregnancy (n=234; 12.3%), quit during/after pregnancy (n=216; 11.4%), relapsing (n=163; 8.6%), and persistent smoking (n=84; 4.4%). Characteristics by trajectory are shown in Table S2. Trajectories characterized by more smoking included fewer highly educated participants and more participants with lower household income.

Brain volumes.

In fully adjusted models, compared with the non-smoking trajectory, participants who quit before pregnancy had 6.57 cm³ greater total gray matter volume (95%CI[0.27,12.87]; p=.041) and 7.71 cm³ greater total white matter volume (95%CI[0.77,14.65]; p=.030), with similar white matter hyperintensity volume (Figure 2). Participants who quit during/after pregnancy had similar gray matter, white matter, and white matter hyperintensity volumes to non-smoking. The relapsing trajectory had similar total gray and white matter volumes but 0.15 standard deviations more white matter hyperintensity volume (95%CI[0.00,0.30]; p=.045). The persistent smoking trajectory had 0.30 standard deviations more white matter hyperintensity volume (95%CI[0.10,0.50]; p=.003).

Cerebrovascular markers.

In fully adjusted models, participants in the quitting during/after pregnancy trajectory had 2.39 times the odds of lacunar infarcts compared with non-smoking (95%CI[1.23,4.68], p=.011). No other associations were observed for lacunes, cortical infarcts, or cerebral microbleeds (Table S3).

Vertex-wise cortical thickness and surface area. In fully adjusted analyses (n=1,847), persistent smoking was associated with thinner cortex in bilateral frontal and parietal regions, as well as left cingulate, fusiform/parahippocampal, and supramarginal and right lateral occipital and medial orbitofrontal regions (mean differences 0.07-0.10 mm; Figure 3A; Table S5). Relapsing smoking showed more restricted thinning in right frontal, medial orbitofrontal, and paracentral regions (mean differences 0.06-0.08 mm; Figure 3B; Table S5). For cortical surface area, quitting before pregnancy was associated with larger bilateral middle and inferior temporal surface area (mean difference 0.04 mm² in both hemispheres; Figure 3C; Table S6).

Single-timepoint markers of smoking and subsequent brain health

During the 14-years after childbirth assessment, 54% of participants had never smoked, 36% had quit, and 10% currently smoked (Figure 1B). Among participants with a smoking history (n=831), mean pack-years were 8.0 (SD 8.2), with lower exposure among former than current smoking participants (6.6 [SD 7.0] vs 12.5 [SD 10.1]; Figure 1C). Among former smoking participants (n=644), mean abstinence duration was 15.9 years (SD 7.9; Figure 1D).

Volume of total white matter, gray matter, and white matter hyperintensities. Results are presented in Table 2. In fully adjusted models and compared with never smoking, current smoking was associated with 9.72 cm³ smaller total gray matter volume (95%CI[-16.53,-2.91], p=.005) and 0.17 standard deviations more white matter hyperintensity volume (95%CI[0.03,0.32], p=.015), but not with white matter volume. In contrast, participants who quit smoking had similar volumes to those who never smoked. Yet, among those with a history of smoking, each additional pack-year was associated with 0.45 cm³ smaller total gray matter volume (95%CI[-0.85,-0.06], p=.025). Among those who quit smoking, each additional year of abstinence was associated with 0.01 standard deviations less white matter hyperintensity volume per year (95%CI[-0.02,0.00], p=.022).

Cerebrovascular markers.

No associations were observed for cortical infarcts, lacunar infarcts, or cerebral microbleeds (Table S4).

Vertex-wise cortical thickness and surface area. Compared with never smoking, current smoking was associated with widespread cortical thinning across both hemispheres (Table S5; Figure S2A, n=1,847), involving bilateral frontal, cingulate, temporal, and paracentral regions, as well as left fusiform, supramarginal, and parietal and right orbitofrontal, pre-/postcentral, and insular regions (mean differences 0.04-0.10 mm). For cortical surface area, higher pack-years were associated with smaller surface area in the left fusiform and inferior temporal regions (mean difference -0.002 mm² per pack-year; Table S6, Figure S2B, n=807).

Sensitivity analyses.

Results were largely consistent across sexes, although the association between quitting during/after pregnancy and white matter volume was more negative in females than in males (-16.60 cm³, 95%CI[-30.44,-2.76], p=.019), without a significant main effect. After excluding women with hypertensive disorders of pregnancy (11.6%), effect sizes were similar, but associations of before-pregnancy quitting with white matter and of relapsing and current smoking with white matter hyperintensity volume were less or no longer significant, likely reflecting reduced power. Accounting for family clustering did not change results; only relapsing smoking lost significance for white matter hyperintensity volume, with an unchanged estimate.

Discussion

In this population-based study of middle-aged adults, smoking trajectories across approximately 15 years of prospective measurements were differentially associated with brain health. We highlight three findings. First, repeated smoking assessments identified substantial heterogeneity beyond conventional never, former, and current smoking categories, distinguishing former smoking by the timing of cessation and current smoking by patterns of relapse versus continued smoking. Second, trajectories involving cessation generally showed more favorable brain-health profiles than continued smoking, consistent with the association between longer abstinence and lower white matter hyperintensity burden. Third, trajectories reflecting greater cumulative smoking exposure were associated with less favorable brain health, including greater white matter hyperintensity burden and widespread thinner cortices, a pattern further supported by dose-response associations between pack-years and brain health outcomes.

Our findings underscore that smoking patterns are more heterogeneous than can be captured with classifications of never, former, and current smoking. The repeated data separated participants who stopped smoking before pregnancy from those who stopped later, and distinguished relapsing from persistently smoking participants despite both groups contributing to the broad category of ever or current smoking at later assessments; broadly resembling trajectories identified in a previous general-population study of >3,000 adults followed for 25 years. 10 Differences from other longitudinal studies likely reflect variation in cohort composition, historical changes in smoking policy and social norms, developmental stage, and follow-up duration. 14, 24 For example, studies beginning in adolescence more often capture smoking initiation and experimentation, 25 whereas our prospective assessments assessed specifically pregnancy, a period when cessation and relapse are particularly common, with retrospective information on smoking before pregnancy which allowed us to distinguish earlier quitting patterns.

Examining these smoking patterns in relation to brain health showed that the level of detail in smoking history mattered. When former smoking was treated as one category, former smoking participants had similar total gray matter, white matter, and white matter hyperintensity volumes to never-smoking participants. Trajectory analyses, however, separated cessation by timing and revealed meaningful heterogeneity. Those who quit before pregnancy had larger total brain volume and biliteral temporal surface area than non-smoking participants, whereas those who quit during/after pregnancy had similar volumes. Longer abstinence was associated with less white matter hyperintensity volume. These findings are broadly similar to prior evidence of partial brain recovery after smoking cessation, 26 which could suggest that smoking accelerates age-related volume loss via microvascular damage or direct neurotoxicity (e.g., oxidative stress, reduced oxygen from carbon monoxide, mitochondrial dysfunction), while after smoking cessation, surviving neurons may undergo temporary compensatory structural changes through neuroplasticity, potentially contributing to partial recovery of brain volume. Yet, recovery would not explain the larger volumes we observed, pointing towards residual confounding explaining these effects: participants who quit before pregnancy had more favorable socioeconomic profiles, which may suggest that part of the observed associations may reflect broader health and lifestyle differences. Notably, for cerebrovascular outcomes, the trajectory analysis suggested higher odds of lacunar infarcts among participants who had quit during/after pregnancy, although this finding should be interpreted cautiously given the sparse binary outcomes and the absence of a broader pattern across cerebrovascular markers. More generally, because the timing of infarcts relative to smoking cessation is unknown, reverse causation or confounding by indication remains possible if vascular events prompted cessation. 27 Repeated brain imaging would be required to establish the temporal sequence more clearly.

Greater smoking exposure showed a consistent adverse pattern. Each additional pack-year was associated with smaller gray matter volume, while relapsing and persistent trajectories had greater white matter hyperintensity burden than non-smoking, with the largest difference for persistent smoking. Vertex-wise analyses further differentiated these trajectories. Persistent smoking was associated with widespread thinner cortex across frontal, parietal, temporal, occipital and cingulate regions, whereas relapsing smoking showed a restricted pattern in right superior frontal, rostral middle frontal, medial orbitofrontal, and paracentral regions. The right paracentral lobule has shown withdrawal-related changes in task response, particularly increased response variability during placebo/abstinence conditions, 28 although the functional implications of regional patterns remain uncertain. Notably, there were substantially fewer associations with surface area, suggesting that cortical thickness may be the more sensitive brain marker of smoking exposure in midlife.

Strengths include prospective and repeated smoking assessments over 15 years together with detailed MRI phenotyping. Assessments around pregnancy provided a natural point at which smoking patterns often change: smoking prevalence and intensity decline during pregnancy because of fetal-health concerns, 29 after which some individuals maintain abstinence while others relapse. This offered meaningful heterogeneity in cessation timing and subsequent smoking behavior. The combination of global brain volumes, cerebrovascular markers, and hypothesis-free vertex-wise cortical analyses also allowed us to examine both global and spatially localized brain differences. Several limitations should be considered. First, the sample was predominantly female (>65%) and included couples. Because smoking and second-hand smoke exposure may be correlated within couples, 30 observations may not be independent. Adding a random effect for family in brain volume analyses did not,, change any results. Second, pre-pregnancy smoking was reported retrospectively, introducing recall bias. Moreover, questionnaire data might be susceptible to information bias due particularly to smoking individuals providing socially desirable answers about smoking in relation to pregnancy. This may have led to an underestimation of associations with continued smoking, while associations attributed to quitting may partly reflect the effects of continued smoking among participants who reported quitting but had continued to smoke. Third, midlife followup enabled brain health assessment before neurodegenerative disease onset, which reduces reverse causation. Yet, the timing of white matter hyperintensities, infarcts, and microbleeds relative to smoking trajectories remains unknown.

In conclusion, smoking trajectories across approximately 15 years were differentially associated with midlife brain health. Repeated assessments distinguished sustained cessation, later cessation, relapse, and persistent smoking patterns that were obscured by conventional single-timepoint categories. Greater and more persistent smoking . exposure was associated with higher white matter hyperintensity burden, smaller gray matter volume in doseresponse analyses, and widespread cortical thinning, whereas sustained cessation generally showed more favorable profiles. These findings indicate early-and mid-adult smoking behavior may shape cumulative exposure long before age-related brain disease becomes clinically apparent, creating a prevention window that is missed when studies assess smoking only near the time of neuroimaging. Findings also support the importance of early and sustained smoking cessation.

Research in context

Evidence before this study

Prior to this study, several population-based studies examined the relationship between smoking and brain health, amongst others focusing on total gray and white matter volume, white matter hyperintensity volume, and cerebrovascular markers. Notable studies include large cohort analyses from the UK Biobank, which showed associations between both current and lifetime smoking and reduced brain volume, particularly gray and white matter, as well as increased white matter hyperintensity volume and higher incidence of cerebral microbleeds. Research on smoking trajectories, however, remains limited, with few studies exploring how long-term smoking behavior, including repeated quit attempts and relapses, is associated with brain health. Furthermore, many studies relied on cross-sectional measures of smoking, overlooking its dynamic and changing nature over time, particularly across life stages such as pregnancy and post-pregnancy where individuals often reconsider smoking behaviors. Earlier studies also mostly categorized smoking behavior into broad groups (never, former, current smoking individuals), which likely missed important variations within smoking trajectories that might relate differently to brain health outcomes.

Added value of this study

This study offers new insights by identifying and characterizing five distinct smoking trajectories (i.e., nonsmoking, quitting before pregnancy, quitting during/after pregnancy, relapsing, and persistent smoking) over a 15-year period in a large cohort of middle-aged adults. By analyzing smoking behavior across multiple time points, we demonstrated that smoking trajectories are associated with nuanced differences in brain health, revealing a more detailed understanding of how smoking cessation timing and relapse influence neuroanatomy. Key findings include that quitting smoking before pregnancy was linked to larger gray and white matter volumes, while quitting during/after pregnancy was associated with higher odds of lacunes. Persistent smoking was associated with widespread thinner cortices, and relapsing smoking exhibited localized thinner cortices in the right hemisphere.

Implications of all the available evidence

These findings highlight that smoking behavior is not a static trait, and the timing of cessation plays a potential role in subsequent brain health. This study also underscores the importance of sustained smoking cessation in promoting better brain health, with more favorable outcomes for those who quit earlier. This could influence public health strategies and interventions, highlighting the importance of early cessation and the need for ongoing support to prevent relapse.
