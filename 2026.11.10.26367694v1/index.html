Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

I. INTRODUCTION

UTANEOUS sensation, the perception of touch, temperature, and pain, underpins safe and effective interaction with the environment and is essential to everyday hand function [8]. These sensory capabilities frequently deteriorate with ageing and, more abruptly, after neurological injury such as stroke [1], [9]. Depending on the assessment and the time since injury, discriminative or light-touch sensory loss in the hand has been reported in roughly 35-55% of stroke survivors, and discriminative loss has been observed in almost half of chronic and up to about 85% of acute stroke survivors [2], [23]. Such deficits impair dexterity, degrade grip-force regulation, increase injury risk, and reduce quality of life [5], [23]. Because they arise from disruption of the somatosensory pathways that encode and relay tactile information [1], there is a pressing need for rehabilitation strategies that target the neurophysiological basis of the impairment rather than its symptoms alone.

Conventional sensory-rehabilitation and pharmacological approaches have had limited success in restoring the underlying sensory processing. Pharmacological agents may offer symptomatic relief but do not reliably drive the neural reorganization required for lasting recovery [19], [20], and more invasive options such as peripheral nerve stimulation carry risks that limit their routine clinical use [21]. These limitations have motivated interest in non-invasive neuromodulation, and in particular in stochastic resonance (SR), a stimulation phenomenon in which adding an optimal level of random noise to a weak, (un-detectable) sub-threshold signal improves its detection and transmission in a non-linear system [3], [4].

In the somatosensory domain, sub-threshold mechanical noise has repeatedly been shown to enhance tactile perception. Collins and colleagues first demonstrated that low-level subthreshold vibrotactile noise improves tactile detection in healthy skin, whereas supra-threshold noise degrades it by masking the signal [4]. Liu et al. subsequently showed that adding sub-threshold mechanical noise significantly lowered vibrotactile detection thresholds at the fingertip in older adults, in patients with diabetic neuropathy, and in four of five stroke patients, establishing the clinical relevance of SR for populations with sensory loss [5]. More recently, Enders et al. reported that even remotely applied sub-threshold vibrotactile noise, i.e., delivered at the wrist or dorsal hand rather instead to the fingertip, instantaneously improved fingertip lighttouch sensation in stroke survivors, independent of noise location and intensity. The authors attributed this effect to increased excitability and synchronization within the central nervous system via inter-neuronal connections [23]. The optimal noise intensity, however, remains debated: levels from approximately 50% to 90% of the sensory threshold have been reported as effective, with no consensus for stroke specifically [5], [23], motivating the sub-threshold (80%) and supra-threshold (120%) levels examined here. Despite this consistent behavioral evidence, the cortical mechanisms that accompany noise-enhanced tactile perception remain poorly characterized. Prior work has largely inferred a central contribution indirectly, from psychophysics, evoked potentials, or the observation that remote noise can influence distant skin sites [5], [7], [23], however the frequency-specific (alpha, beta band) changes in cortical functional connectivity (FC) that underlie these perceptual gains have not been directly studied, particularly in stroke. FC, the temporal synchronization of activity between spatially separated brain regions [14], is a sensitive index of how sensory information is integrated across networks, and alpha-and beta-band synchronization in particular are implicated in attention, sensory processing, and sensorimotor integration [15], [18], [22]. Characterizing how sub-threshold stochastic vibration reshapes FC would therefore provide a mechanistic bridge between the peripheral stimulus and the perceptual outcome.

Therefore, this study combines quantitative psychophysics with source-localized, high-density EEG to answer two questions: (1) how sub-threshold stochastic vibration modulates cortical FC during tactile processing, and (2) whether these neural changes accompany measurable improvements in tactile detection thresholds. Using a withinparticipant design, we compared pure 30 Hz sinusoidal stimulation with sinusoidal stimulation combined with subthreshold (80%) or supra-threshold (120%) stochastic noise, in healthy adults and stroke survivors. We hypothesized that (1) sub-threshold stochastic vibration would lower tactile detection thresholds-that is, increase sensitivity, relative to sinusoidal stimulation alone in both groups; (2) these perceptual improvements would be accompanied by increased cortical FC, predominantly in the alpha and beta bands; and (3) supra-threshold noise, being itself perceptible, would not confer a sub-threshold-type benefit and would instead be consistent with signal masking.

By linking psychophysical thresholds to source-localized FC, this work aims to establish a neurophysiological rationale for SR-based sensory rehabilitation after stroke. The remainder of the paper describes the methods (Section II), results (Section III), discussion (Section IV), and conclusions and future directions (Section V).

II. METHODS

A. Participants

Nineteen healthy adults and five chronic stroke survivors participated. The stroke participants were recruited in Solapur, India. Healthy-participant inclusion criteria were: age 18 years or over, no history of neurological disorder, and no use of neuro-inhibitory medication in the preceding month. Strokeparticipant inclusion criteria were: age 18 years or over; stroke more than six months previously; impaired light touch on twopoint discrimination testing; ability to place and maintain the hand on the device plate; and absence of cognitive or severe language impairment that would preclude reliable psychophysical responding. Exclusion criteria were: an implanted electronic device such as a cardiac pacemaker; epilepsy or seizure history; a history of upper-extremity orthopedic conditions; and metalwork in the forearm. The stroke participants mean age was 60.66 ± 8.44 years; 3 male; mean time since stroke 20.75 years. All stroke participants had impaired light touch (monofilament testing: average 4.8 ± 1.0 in the middle digit of the affected hand vs 3.0 ± 0.3 in the unaffected hand).

The nineteen healthy (mean age 26.5 ± 6.84; 6 female) participants were recruited at the University of Hertfordshire (UH), UK. The stroke participants and the first healthy batch (n=9) completed vibration modes (a), (b), and (c); the healthy batch additionally underwent EEG recording. The second healthy batch (n=10) additionally completed mode (d), the super-threshold condition, allowing assessment of a superthreshold stochastic vibration. Experiments were conducted at the site of recruitment (UK or India) using a shared, harmonised protocol. Two researchers (MA and AP) conducted the experiments: MA and AP jointly in the UK, and AP alone in India. The Ethical Review Committee of the University of Hertfordshire approved the study (Reference: aSPECS/SF/UH/04703(1)). All procedures performed involving human participants followed the ethical standards of the institutional review committee and adhered to the declaration of Helsinki. An information sheet was given to the participants, and all the participants signed a 'written consent form' in presence of the researcher before the experiment.

B. Procedures

Apparatus and Positioning

Vibrotactile stimuli were delivered to the volar surface of the right middle fingertip using a custom research apparatus developed at the University of Hertfordshire. The participant sat in a supported seated position, their affected hand was placed palm down on the device plate, on a platform that held the forearm and hand in a fixed position with a foam pad resting on top for comfort and to help maintain the position during testing, with a vibration actuator sited beneath the volar surface of the middle fingertip. The device generated four experimental modes of vibration profiles, i.e. stimuli, described below.

Stimuli

Two classes of vibration were delivered at a single fingertip contact site, alone or superimposed: (i) a deterministic sinusoidal vibration at 30 Hz (Fig 1, upper trace), and (ii) a zeromean random (stochastic) vibration (Fig 1, lower trace). Four experimental modes were used: (a) stochastic vibration alone; (b) 30 Hz sinusoidal vibration alone; (c) sinusoidal vibration superimposed with sub-threshold stochastic vibration, fixed at 80% of the participant's individually determined stochastic detection threshold; and (d) sinusoidal vibration superimposed with supra-threshold stochastic vibration at 120% of that threshold. Each stimulus presentation lasted 1 s. Stimulus amplitude was adjustable in discrete, monotonically increasing steps spanning each participant's detection threshold; amplitudes are consequently reported throughout in arbitrary device units rather than absolute physical magnitudes. Representative sinusoidal and stochastic stimulus profiles are shown in Fig. 1.

Protocol

Participants rested the right hand on the device and kept it still throughout. Stimuli were delivered through the device, and participants signalled detection with the left index finger. For healthy participants, EEG was recorded continuously; to limit ocular and movement artefact, participants were asked to minimise head and eye movement and to wait 5 s after detecting a stimulus before signalling.

Testing began by determining each participant's minimum detectable amplitude of stochastic vibration alone, i.e. the stochastic (noise) detection threshold. A sub-threshold level was then set at 80% of this value (the nearest achievable level was used where device discretization prevented an exact setting), yielding an imperceptible noise. Participants were then tested, in randomised order, on mode (b) sinusoidal vibration alone, and mode (c) sinusoidal vibration summed with the 80% sub-threshold stochastic noise. The minimum detectable amplitude was determined for each condition; these were designated the sinusoidal (normal) detection threshold [mode (b)] and the sub-threshold stochastic detection threshold [mode (c)] and were compared to quantify the effect of imperceptible noise on sensitivity. The same protocol was used for the stroke participants. For the ten healthy participants recruited at NZCC, an additional super-threshold condition [mode (d)] combined sinusoidal vibration with stochastic noise fixed at 120% of the stochastic threshold was tested. The resulting threshold was designated the super-threshold stochastic detection threshold.

Thresholds were located using a 4-2-1 incremental stepping algorithm, following the approach of Liu et al. [5]: amplitude was first adjusted in steps of four levels until the undetectable range was reached, then in steps of two levels, and finally in single levels to pinpoint the threshold. The procedure was repeated until a consistent value was obtained; if no definitive threshold was reached within 25 observations, the value at the 25th observation was recorded. All 25 observations were retained for each participant. Fig. 2 summarises the protocol.

Electroencephalography (EEG) Assessments

EEG was recorded from healthy participants throughout. A 3minute eyes-open resting-state recording was obtained before the vibration tasks, followed by recordings during each of the first three vibration modes. For the ten NZCC participants, an additional recording was obtained during mode (d) together with a 3-minute post-experiment resting-state recording.

Participants were asked to keep the body, head, and eyes still to limit artefact. Data were acquired with a 64-channel cap and a TMSi REFA amplifier at 2,048 Hz using Polybench software. Electrodes followed the international 10-20 system, with the ground at AFz and both mastoids (M1, M2) as reference; impedances were kept below 10 kΩ.

C. Data Processing

Data Processing

Comparisons were performed across five conditions: (1) resting-state pre (eyes open, 3 min), ( 2) mode (b) sinusoidal vibration, (3) mode (c) sub-threshold combination, (4) mode (d) super-threshold combination, and (5) resting-state post (eyes open, 3 min). Two outcomes were assessed: (i) cutaneous detection thresholds across conditions, to establish the clinical effect, and (ii) EEG-based FC changes between conditions, to characterise the underlying neurophysiology. Comparisons involving all nineteen participants used the full sample; comparisons involving mode (d) used the ten NZCC participants. For the psychophysical outcome, thresholds for all applicable modes were determined for each participant and compared to quantify noise-induced changes in sensitivity. EEG preprocessing (Fig. 3) proceeded as follows: the 62 scalp electrodes (excluding the two mastoids) were re-referenced to a common average; signals were band-pass filtered at 0.5-40 Hz; artefact subspace reconstruction was applied to remove high-amplitude noise; and independent component analysis with ICLabel was used to reject artefactual components using pretrained classifiers. These steps were performed in MATLAB using EEGLAB. The pre-processed data were then imported into Brainstorm for source estimation.

D. Source Reconstruction and Functional Connectivity

In this study, we employed source-level EEG analysis to investigate FC changes induced by stochastic vibration. Sourcelevel analysis allows for the localization of neural activity to specific brain regions, providing a more precise assessment of connectivity patterns compared to sensor-level approaches. This helps us identify what brain regions are associated with the vibration stimuli and what effect does it have on the functional behaviour. Our methodology involved two key steps: forward modelling and inverse modelling. Forward modelling is essential for EEG source reconstruction, as it defines the relationship between cortical sources and EEG sensor measurements. We determined the location and orientation of EEG sensors relative to cortical sources by modelling current dipole fields on a voxel grid approximating the cortical space. Dipoles were oriented perpendicular to the cortex to ensure physiological accuracy. The symmetric boundary element method (Open MEEG BEM) was used to model dipoles for all subjects [10]. A default generic head model from Brainstorm (Dec 2024) was employed, featuring 15,000 vertices and a three-layer compartment (scalp, skull, and brain). Tissue conductivities were set based on established values: scalp = 1, skull = 0.0125, and brain = 1 [11]. Electrode locations were defined using the 10-20 electrode placement system, with 64 electrodes (including references M1 and M2) positioned on the scalp.

The inverse problem in EEG source reconstruction involves estimating cortical activity from sensor measurements, which is inherently underdetermined and ill-posed due to the limited number of sensors relative to the number of potential sources. To address this, we utilized the minimum-norm estimation (MNE) method, which minimizes the overall power of estimated sources while using an identity matrix as the noise covariance matrix [12]. However, MNE tends to bias sources toward superficial cortical regions, leading to inaccuracies. To mitigate this, we applied the standardized low-resolution brain electromagnetic tomography (sLORETA) method, which normalizes current density maps and provides more accurate localization of source dipoles [13]. This source reconstruction step was performed in Brainstorm software. Fig. 4 shows the source reconstruction pipeline. Functional connectivity (FC) refers to the temporal correlation or synchronization of neural activity between spatially separated brain regions, reflecting their interaction and information sharing within a functional network [14]. Unlike structural connectivity, which is defined by physical pathways, FC captures dynamic relationships between brain regions, even in the absence of direct anatomical connections. It is typically quantified using metrics such as coherence, phase-locking value, or mutual information, which assess the degree of neural synchronization across regions. For functional connectivity analysis, high-resolution sources were grouped into 68 regions of interest (ROIs) based on the Desikan-Killiany atlas. Time series within each ROI were averaged to form an [ROIs × time] matrix. To prevent activity cancellation, the sign of dipoles with opposite orientations was flipped before averaging. This approach enabled robust estimation of brain activity and facilitated the assessment of connectivity patterns between distinct brain regions. Triple network (salience, central, and default mode network) were then separated out from these regions to gain a specific understanding of the FC trends.

The reconstructed EEG source signals were filtered into narrow-band signals using a 4th-order Butterworth filter to extract frequency bands of interest: alpha (7.5-12.5 Hz) and beta (12.5-30 Hz) based on [15]. To quantify FC, we employed the Phase Lag Index (PLI), a measure that assesses the asymmetry in the phase difference distribution between two signals, denoted as x(t) and y(t). PLI is particularly useful for identifying consistent phase leads or lags between signals, which are indicative of directed functional interactions.

The PLI was calculated using the following equation: where ϕx(t) and ϕy(t) represent the instantaneous phase of signals x(t) and y(t), respectively.

The phase information was derived from the Hilbert transform of the signals. The resulting phase difference can be positive, negative, or zero, depending on the relative timing of the signals. The PLI was computed over a specific time window, with N representing the total number of samples within that window. This process enabled us to quantify the extent of connectivity and asymmetry between the signals, providing valuable insights into the functional interactions of brain regions. The PLI was computed between every pair of source signals within each band. PLI values range from 0 (no connectivity) to 1 (maximum connectivity), capturing phase asymmetry and functional interactions. PLI values were stored in adjacency matrices (diagonal zeros, symmetric) for each frequency band and experimental modes (mode (a), mode (b), mode (c), mode (d), resting-state pre and post). E. Statistical Analysis

To address the multiple comparison problem and control the familywise error rate, we employed a non-parametric clusterbased permutation test [16]. This test compared PLI values within adjacency matrices across experimental modes (restingstate pre vs. resting-state post, mode (b) vs. mode (c), and mode (b) vs. mode (d)) using a within-study design. The analysis was conducted using the FieldTrip toolbox (Donders Institute, Brain, Cognition, and Behavior, Nijmegen, Netherlands).

For each pairwise comparison, cluster-level statistics were computed. The largest cluster-level statistic served as the critical t-value for identifying significant clusters. A null distribution was generated by performing 5000 permutations, where data labels were randomly shuffled for each comparison. Cluster-level statistics were recalculated for each permutation to reflect the expected distribution under the null hypothesis of no connectivity differences between conditions. The p-value for each observed cluster was determined as the proportion of permutations where the cluster-level statistic exceeded the observed value. Significant clusters were classified as positive (+cluster) or negative (-cluster), indicating increased or decreased functional connectivity, respectively. This procedure identified regions with significant condition-dependent changes in connectivity while correcting for multiple comparisons.

IV. DISCUSSION

This study examined how sub-threshold stochastic vibration affects tactile detection thresholds and cortical FC in healthy adults and stroke survivors. Adding imperceptible stochastic noise to a sinusoidal stimulus lowered detection thresholds, that is, increased sensitivity, in every participant tested in healthy participants (individual sensitivity gains up to 1900%) and consistent reductions in all five stroke survivors (up to 400%). In parallel, source-localised EEG showed that sub-threshold vibration significantly increased cortical FC, most extensively in the alpha band. Together, these results indicate that subthreshold stochastic vibration amplifies weak tactile signals and reorganises the cortical networks that process them. These behavioural findings are consistent with, and extend, the established SR literature. Liu et al. reported that sub-threshold mechanical noise significantly lowered fingertip vibrotactile thresholds in older adults, in patients with diabetic neuropathy, and in four of five stroke patients, using the same 4-2-1 threshold-tracking procedure adopted here [5]; Enders et al. found that even remotely applied sub-threshold noise improved fingertip light-touch sensation in seven of ten stroke survivors, independent of location and intensity [23]. In the present cohort, all five stroke participants improved, and the direction and imperceptible-noise dependence of the effect reproduce these prior reports. Whereas earlier studies characterised the perceptual outcome and inferred a central contribution indirectly, the present study adds direct, source-localised evidence that the cortical networks supporting tactile processing are reorganised during sub-threshold stimulation.

The connectivity results constrain the mechanistic interpretation. Under sub-threshold noise, alpha-band phase synchrony increased between superior frontal, rostral middle frontal, anterior cingulate, insular, inferior parietal and precuneal regions, and no significant pair in any contrast involved precentral or postcentral cortex. The regions involved are the nodes of the salience and frontoparietal control networks [24], [25]. Their state is known to determine whether a nearthreshold somatosensory stimulus is perceived: baseline activity in this network predicts detection of an identical weak stimulus on a trial-by-trial basis [26], and pre-stimulus oscillatory activity over sensorimotor and parietal cortex influences detection of near-threshold tactile stimuli, while prestimulus alpha-band lateralisation over somatosensory cortex predicts tactile discrimination performance. [27], [28]. One account of alpha-band activity is that it gates sensory information by timed inhibition [29]. On that account, the present data are consistent with sub-threshold noise acting on the networks that gate perceptual access to a weak tactile signal, with the cortical representation of the signal itself unchanged at the resolution of this analysis. This reading fits the outcome measured, a lower detection threshold, and it fits the earlier finding that remote sub-threshold noise improves light-touch detection but not two-point discrimination [23]. It also fits the behavioural result, which is the expected signature of stochastic resonance in a threshold system [31], [32]. Because the phase lag index is insensitive to volume conduction [30], the increase in long-range synchrony reflects a change in inter-regional coupling rather than the spread of a single source. The same measure discards zero-lag coupling [30], so a zero-lag interaction between primary somatosensory and motor cortex would not have been detected, and the absence of a primary sensorimotor pair is not evidence that primary sensorimotor cortex is unaffected. The design is correlational and used one sub-threshold noise level, so whether the change in alpha-band phase consistency contributes to the lower threshold or accompanies the attentional state that produces it cannot be decided from these data. The contrast between sub-and super-threshold conditions reinforces the SR interpretation. Consistent with Collins et al., who showed that noise enhances tactile detection only below the perceptual threshold and degrades it above [4] (as also emphasised by Enders et al. [23]), the 120% super-threshold noise did not confer a sub-threshold-type benefit: it was itself always perceptible (thresholds recorded as 0) and was accompanied by markedly weaker, predominantly beta-band FC changes, with minimal alpha-band involvement (two region pairs). In other words, once the added noise is supra-threshold it masks rather than resonantly amplifies the target signal, engaging a different, and less extensive, connectivity pattern than sub-threshold stimulation. The stronger and more widespread FC changes under sub-threshold [mode (c)] than super-threshold [mode (d)] stimulation suggest that imperceptible noise more effectively engages latent sensory pathways. Again, understanding the detailed mechanistic action requires future studies.

For stroke survivors, the immediate improvement in tactile thresholds, reproducing Liu et al. [5] and Enders et al. [23] in an independent cohort, supports sub-threshold stochastic vibration as a non-invasive route to enhancing impaired cutaneous sensation. Because cutaneous feedback is essential to grip control and dexterity [23], restoring light-touch sensitivity could, in principle, support sensorimotor recovery, although this requires direct investigation. The accompanying FC changes are consistent with a capacity of stochastic noise to promote cortical reorganisation, a key substrate of post-stroke recovery. Several limitations should be noted. First, the stroke cohort was small (n = 5) and the super-threshold condition was limited to ten healthy participants, constraining the generalisability of the FC findings; larger, adequately powered cohorts are needed. Second, individual responses varied widely (sensitivity gains from 16% to 1900%), pointing to person-specific determinants of SR efficacy-such as age, baseline sensitivity, lesion characteristics, and neural plasticity-that warrant systematic study. Third, although we observed both threshold reductions and FC increases, we did not compute a formal brain-behaviour correlation between the two; establishing whether the magnitude of FC change predicts the magnitude of perceptual improvement is an important next step (cf. the correlation analyses used in prior work [23]). Fourth, we assessed detection thresholds only; Enders et al. found that sub-threshold noise improved light-touch detection but not two-point (spatial) discrimination [23], so future studies should include spatialacuity and functional (dexterity, grip-force) measures to determine which aspects of sensation and hand function are enhanced. Finally, we measured immediate effects; the durability of both perceptual and connectivity changes, and their response to repeated or longer-term exposure, remain to be established.

V. CONCLUSION

Sub-threshold stochastic vibration significantly enhanced tactile sensitivity and reorganised cortical FC in both healthy adults and stroke survivors. Combining imperceptible stochastic noise with sinusoidal stimulation lowered detection thresholds in every participant in healthy participants (individual sensitivity gains up to 1900%) and in all five stroke survivors (up to 400%), consistent with stochastic resonance amplifying weak sensory signals. Source-localised EEG revealed frequency-specific network reorganisation, with the most extensive increases in alpha-band connectivity during subthreshold stimulation and a shift toward beta-band involvement under super-threshold stimulation, the latter consistent with signal masking rather than resonance.

By pairing psychophysical thresholds with direct, sourcelevel connectivity evidence, this work links noise-enhanced perception to measurable cortical reorganisation, extending prior behavioural demonstrations of somatosensory stochastic resonance [5], [23] toward a mechanistic account. The main limitations are the small stroke cohort (n = 5) and the wide interindividual variability in response. Future work should pursue longitudinal designs, individualised noise parameters, formal brain-behaviour correlations, and combined sensory-motor outcomes, with the goal of translating sub-threshold stochastic vibration into practical, non-invasive sensory rehabilitation after stroke.
