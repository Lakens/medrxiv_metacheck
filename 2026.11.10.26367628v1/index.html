How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Abstract

Introduction: Although psilocybin has shown promise for treating depression, responses vary considerably, with some individuals experiencing limiting improvement and even adverse effects. Research has largely relied on assessments conducted at discrete time points, providing limited insight into how post-psilocybin change unfolds in everyday life. The Dial-In study aims to characterize daily-life processes associated with differential responses to psilocybin, including factors associated with improvement or worsening following challenging dosing experiences.

Dial-In is a prospective, observational, multi-method longitudinal study embedded within a clinical trial administering 25 mg of psilocybin to up to 141 adults with major depressive disorder. Ambulatory assessments combine ecological momentary assessment (EMA) and voice diaries for 14 days pre-and post-dosing, and passive audio sampling via the Electronically Activated Recorder (EAR) for three four-day periods to characterize everyday affect, behavior, reflections, and social interactions. Outcomes include depressive symptoms, psychological well-being, and psychedelic-related adverse effects assessed through eight weeks post-dose. Analyses will examine whether affective sudden gains and losses, social support, interpersonal conflict, conversational focus, reflection and rigidity predict differential outcomes. Additional analyses will test whether these factors moderate associations between challenging dosing experiences and clinical outcomes.

The project was approved by WCG Institutional Review Board, with Emory University relying on WCG as the reviewing board. All participants provide written informed consent. Findings will be disseminated through peer-reviewed publications and scientific presentations.

[div-01]

Psilocybin has been shown to reduce symptoms of depression in multiple studies [1] [2] [3] [4] [5] [6], yet responses to psilocybin vary considerably across individuals [2, 4, 7]. Many individuals experience sustained psychological benefits extending beyond symptom reduction [7] [8] [9] [10], whereas others experience limited improvement [2, 4] or, at times, worsening across emotional, interpersonal, or spiritual domains [7, 11, 12]. However, a notable absence of ambulatory assessment has made it difficult to ground such research in experiences and behaviors that occur outside the lab, where many of the enduring impacts of psilocybin are expected to unfold. Ambulatory assessment encompasses methods that collect data in the context of participants' everyday environment outside of the laboratory, including experience sampling or ecological momentary assessment (EMA), naturalistic observation, and passive behavioral or physiological measurement [13, 14]. These methods can capture data from participants' behaviors and environments, as well as fine-grained assessments of emotional and cognitive processes, in real or near-real time as they unfold in daily life. The collection of multiple daily assessments also generates intensive longitudinal data streams suitable for determining temporal dynamics of change [14, 15]. The collection of ambulatory data is nascent in psychedelic research, with only one published study to our knowledge having used EMA for full-dose psychedelics [16] (although see [17] for an EMA study of microdosing, and also [18], and clinical trial registrations NCT06355414; NCT06442423 for current protocols that use EMA).

Nevertheless, published clinical psilocybin research continues to rely primarily on laboratory assessments or occasional at-home self-report surveys [2] [3] [4] 6]. Naturalistic observational studies likewise often rely on waves of surveys delivered either retrospectively [19] or prospectively [7, 20]. Though useful in many ways, these yield limited information about daily and momentary experiences and behaviors, leaving the temporal and contextual dynamics comparatively understudied in psychedelics.

Nonetheless, processes and outcomes captured through ambulatory assessment are highly germane to psychedelics research. These include the assessment of patterns in moment-tomoment emotional dynamics, and changes in those patterns; changes in perspective, wellbeing, and phenomenology, which might unfold either gradually or suddenly; the contributions of individuals' daily environments and interactions to therapeutic change; and observed behavior change. Such data may help characterize the dynamics of change after psilocybin, identify dailylife factors associated with differential response, and detect early indicators of improvement or deterioration. This protocol paper describes the Dial-In Study, which incorporates ambulatory assessment into an existing clinical trial of psilocybin for major depression (OPTIMIZE; ClinicalTrials.gov NCT06512194) to generate novel ambulatory data on daily-life processes surrounding psilocybin treatment and identify predictors of differential response. The Dial-In study will use multi-method prospective longitudinal assessment to generate fine-grained timeseries data across four weeks of the clinical trial. The study integrates three complementary modalities of daily-life assessment: intensive longitudinal self-report via EMA, repeated daily open-ended voice diaries, and naturalistic behavioral observation by means of recorded ambient audio. Together, these methods allow us to characterize participants' affective dynamics, observed behaviors and social interactions, and participants' reflections of their experiences, while also examining whether these processes are associated with differential response to psilocybin.

Known predictors of differential response to psilocybin

Growing evidence has identified several correlates of differential response to psilocybin, spanning features of the acute psychedelic experience, baseline psychological and neurobiological characteristics, and aspects of the therapeutic context [21] [22] [23]. Subjective positive dosing experiences have been associated with greater benefits, while challenging experiences have been linked with less benefit and even deterioration after psilocybin, albeit inconsistently [7, 20, 21, [24] [25] [26]. Importantly, challenging dosing experiences are not uniformly associated with poor outcomes: in a large retrospective survey, most individuals describing their most difficult psilocybin experience reported eventually benefiting from it [19]. More recent work similarly suggests that the outcomes associated with challenging experiences may depend on how those experiences are processed and supported [27] [28] [29] [30], underscoring the importance of understanding when such experiences lead to growth versus worsening. Beyond the dosing experience itself, individuals' daily environments and social contexts may also shape how psilocybin experiences are integrated over time. Although social and interpersonal contexts have been linked to longer-term psychedelic outcomes [20, [31] [32] [33], these factors have rarely been examined prospectively and at a fine-grained temporal level. Consequently, little is known about how individual's daily environments, experiences, and behaviors are related to post-acute trajectories as they unfold in everyday life [34, 35].

Contribution of Ambulatory Assessment and Dynamic Systems Modeling

The Dial-In Study uses a multi-method, ambulatory, intensive longitudinal assessment approach. Multi-method assessment includes subjective psychological assessment using EMA, daily voice diaries, and passive audio acquisition using the Electronically Activated Recorder (EAR). Ambulatory assessment involves tracking experiences that unfold in participants' everyday environments [13]. Because improvement or deterioration take place over time, and within the context of daily life, ambulatory assessment methods are well suited to capturing clinically relevant phenomena outside of the lab. Intensive longitudinal assessment involves frequent repeated measurements at relatively short intervals, and at a sufficiently granular time scale, to enable the characterization of processes that unfold dynamically over time [36, 37].

Using intensive longitudinal methods, psychotherapy research has found that improvement and worsening is often a discontinuous process, comprising sudden gains and losses, which are predictive of the ultimate trajectory of change [38, 39]. This may be especially true of psilocybin, which can produce marked and enduring shifts in subjective experience and perspective [40] [41] [42].

Because clinical change following psilocybin can emerge rapidly in the post-dosing window, sudden gains and losses may be especially likely and clinically meaningful for psilocybin assisted therapies [6, 43, 44]. Instantiated in day-to-day patterns of emotion, cognition, and behavior, sudden gains and losses are potential early indicators of subsequent clinical change and differential response.

Dynamic complexity theory offers a potentially useful framework for conceptualizing and evaluating the contribution of momentary dynamics to ultimate therapeutic change [45] [46] [47] and identifying early warning signs of differential response. From a complex dynamic systems perspective, psychological functioning may be conceptualized as a persistent system of interacting states in continuous exchange with the environment [48]. Psilocybin may disrupt individuals' habitual response patterns, creating a period of instability during which the system becomes more susceptible to reorganization [46]. Such reorganization may allow new and adaptive patterns of functioning to emerge but may also introduce vulnerability for maladaptive changes. Intensive longitudinal data collected in this study are suitable to complex dynamic systems modeling and thus offer an opportunity to study psilocybin's contributions to shifts in individuals' dynamic systems as part of therapeutic change.

Study Objectives

This study has two complementary objectives. The first is to generate novel data comprising observational metrics of behavior (via passively sensed audio), affective dynamics (via EMA), and longitudinal qualitative data (voice diary) within the context of a Phase II psilocybin trial. This makes possible the second aim, which consists of planned analyses that will evaluate determinants of differential response to psilocybin. For the planned analyses, differential responses to psilocybin are operationalized as differences in depression reduction, wellbeing enhancement, and adverse effects after the psilocybin intervention. Planned analyses will also examine determinants of differential response after challenging dosing experiences, specifically moderators of the relationship between challenging experiences and these outcomes.

First, we will examine sudden gains and losses in the time series as independent predictors of differential response, with the expectation that sudden gains will precede better responses and sudden losses will precede poorer responses (H1). Second, we will evaluate ecologically assessed predictors of differential response from participants' observed environments and behaviors, assessed via passive monitoring with the Electronically Activated Recorder (EAR), and naturalistic speech collected via daily voice diaries. Using composite indices derived from these methods, we expect more support, less conflict, and a shift in conversational focus from self to others (EAR; H2a), and greater personal reflection and lower rigidity (voice diary; H2b), to be associated with better outcomes. We expect to replicate previously observed associations between challenging experiences and poorer responses (H3a), but that the risk and protective variables identified in H1-2 will moderate this relationship, with protective factors exerting a buffering role and risk factors strengthening the relationship (H3b). Finally, the resulting longitudinal data will support exploratory analyses of additional temporal and dynamic predictors of differential treatment response using dynamic systems and other computational approaches.

Methods

The Dial-In study is embedded within a larger clinical trial administering a single 25 mg dose of psilocybin to adults meeting criteria for Major Depressive Disorder. In the parent trial all participants receive 2 preparation sessions with trained therapists, a single dosing session, and 3 integration sessions, comprising 8 hours of interpersonal support. After psilocybin dosing, participants are randomized to receive either transcutaneous auricular vagus nerve stimulation (taVNS), sham-taVNS, or no taVNS. See Supplement A, "Parent Study Design and Procedure" for further detail on the parent study's design. Methods for the Dial-In Study are described below. The parent protocol and informed consent form were approved by WCG IRB, with Emory University relying on WCG as the reviewing IRB under an institutional reliance agreement. All participants provided written informed consent (ICF) for OPTIMIZE. A separate consent form was not used for Dial-In as its procedures were summarized in the parent ICF.

Emory University oversees the collection and processing of the EMA, voice-diary, and EAR data. Identifiable audio data are transferred to and stored on a secure SharePoint folder, with access restricted to authorized study personnel.

Participants

OPTIMIZE plans to enroll up to 141 adults aged 18-70. Participants must meet the DSM-5 criteria for Major Depressive Disorder (MDD) with a current major depressive episode of at least 60 days' duration and clinically significant depressive symptoms at screening (as indexed by a MADRS score of ≥ 28). Additional inclusion criteria include medical eligibility for psilocybin administration, willingness to comply with study procedures, and ability to complete assessments in either English or Spanish. The study began enrolling participants on 05/13/2025 and will continue enrolling participants through 05/2029. At the time of submission, the study has enrolled 41 subjects and the recruitment is ongoing.

Exclusion criteria include a history of psychotic or bipolar disorders; current or recent (within 12 months of screening) anorexia nervosa, bulimia nervosa, or antisocial personality disorder; and current or recent obsessive-compulsive disorder, posttraumatic stress disorder, panic disorder, agoraphobia, or binge eating disorder when judged to be primary relative to MDD. All participants recruited for the parent-study will also follow the Dial-In Study procedures and assessment.

Ambulatory Assessment Methods

Ecological Momentary Assessment (EMA) and Voice Diary.

Ecological momentary assessment (EMA) involves repeated self-reported assessments of individuals' current experiences, behaviors, and contexts in real time as they go about their daily lives, minimizing reliance on retrospective recall [15, 49]. In the Dial-In Study, a series of standardized EMA items and voice-diary data are collected using Fabla 1 [50], a smartphone-based platform for mixed method EMA developed and hosted at Emory University (See Figure 2). On enrollment into the study, participants are informed about the EMA assessment and voice diary, their frequency, content, duration, confidentiality, and compensation schedule. At their baseline visit, participants are instructed to download the Fabla app and to link it to their study ID. Participants are provided with a brief training and orientation to ensure comfort with the app, including a practice survey with the opportunity to ask questions. During this visit, a researcher also works with each participant to develop two personalized EMA items, which are added to the standardized EMA questionnaire, described below.

EMA and voice diaries are administered in intensive bursts aligned with key phases of the trial. EMA and voice-diary assessments begin 14 days prior to dosing, resume following Day 1 post-dose for another 14 days (yielding 28 days total). An additional, optional 14-day period of EMA and voice diary assessments is offered to participants who complete the first 28 days, extending intensive assessment through Week 4 post-dose. At the end of the daily assessment period, participants answer weekly questions through Week 8 (See Figure 1 and Table 1). In addition to compensation for the parent trial, participants are compensated up to $275 for participation related to the Dial-In Study, using a progressive payment schedule that rewards greater adherence and a bonus payment during each EMA/voice diary collection period for >70% adherence. 1 Fabla is a HIPAA-compliant smartphone application allowing real-time customization and study-specific tailoring without reliance on third-party platforms.

During intensive assessment periods, participants receive four EMA surveys per day via app notifications, customized to their self-reported sleep schedule, with assessments at approximately 2, 7, 11, and 13 hours after waking. Each survey is open for two hours. The final EMA assessment of each day includes a daily voice diary entry, in which participants are prompted to talk about their experience that day, as though sending a voice memo to a friend, recording for approximately five minutes. Participants who prefer to type rather than speak their daily diary are provided with a text-box response option.

EMA questionnaires are designed to be brief while covering multiple domains relevant to depression and recovery. The standardized item set includes measures of current context and activity; affective valence and activation, and connectedness to others, their surroundings, and something sacred, transcendent, or larger than themselves. See Table 1 for the full list of items.

Participants submit their answers on a slider in the application from 0 "strongly disagree"/ "very unpleasant"/ "very low" to 10 "strongly agree"/"very pleasant"/ "very high". The first assessment of each day additionally queries the number of hours of sleep during the previous night (drop-down using integers). The final assessment of each day queries substance use in the previous 24 hours (checklist), and whether participants discussed their upcoming or completed psilocybin session.

The pre-and post-dose EMA item sets are largely parallel, with several treatmentspecific additions following dosing. Post-dose assessments additionally assess participants' present-moment connection to their psilocybin experience. Two personalized EMA items are created for each participant, enabling idiographic assessment. Participants identify one personally meaningful target that they would like to increase (e.g., joy, motivation, relaxation) and one target that they would like to decrease (e.g., sadness, pain, loneliness). The research team reviews each target to ensure that they are sufficiently specific, likely to vary over the span of the day, readily reportable in the moment, and unlikely to produce discouraging or iatrogenic effects (see supplemental Table S1). The resulting items are incorporated into each participant's otherwise standardized EMA item set.

The final EMA assessment of each intensive-assessment day also includes a voice-diary component designed to capture participants' naturalistic reflections on their daily experiences.

Participants respond to two open-ended prompts: one asking them to describe their day and a second asking them to reflect on their psilocybin experience prospectively or retrospectively, allowing them to provide a brief stream-of-consciousness audio reflection on their daily experiences. An overview of the daily data collection schedule can be found in Table 1 and Table S2.

Recordings are automatically transcribed using AWS integrated into the Fabla app and subsequently reviewed and corrected by trained research assistants to ensure accuracy. Voicediary responses are coded using the Verbal Observation of Inner Coping and Experience framework [51], which characterizes affective, cognitive, interpersonal, behavioral, and contextual features of participants' reports. The individual audio recording serves as the primary unit of coding, and both the transcript and the original audio are used to assign codes to capture linguistic and paralinguistic features of speech that can increase interpretation accuracy. Coding categories capture affective, cognitive, interpersonal, behavioral, and contextual features of participants' reports. Categories are coded using binary or dimensional response formats, with many dimensional categories rated on a 0-5 scale. The coding system for the VOICE framework is publicly available on OSF (OSF link ) and further explained in a tutorial [51]. Recordings are each independently coded by two coders, with regular coder meetings and quality-control checks used to maintain consistent application of the coding framework and minimize coder drift.

Category-level interrater reliability is evaluated using Cohen's weighted kappa, with Cohen's weighted kappa of 0.41 or higher (corresponding with conventional thresholds for moderate agreement) used to evaluate acceptable agreement.

Electronically Activated Recorder (EAR).

The Electronically Activated Recorder (EAR) is an ambulatory assessment method that intermittently samples brief segments of ambient sound from participants' naturalistic environments, providing naturalistic observational data on daily behaviors, social interactions, and environmental context [52] [53] [54]. The EAR has been used widely over the last two decades in research on both clinical and nonclinical populations and has generally demonstrated high acceptability and low ratings of obtrusiveness in populations ranging in age from children to older adults [55, 56].

EAR data are collected for three 4-day periods, at Baseline 1 pre-dose, and Week 1 and

Week 4 post-dose, with each period comprising two weekdays and two weekend days (see Table S3). During each EAR collection period, participants are given an Android smartphone with the EAR app preloaded and are instructed to wear the device while going about their daily activities.

The EAR app records bursts of 30-second samples of ambient audio every 12 minutes silently and without participant awareness of when it is recording. A 6-hour blackout window is also programmed to coincide with each participant's typical sleep period. This sampling schedule captures a random sample of 5% of the day (approximately 300 audio recordings per four-day period). This recording schedule has been used widely in prior research using this method [52, 53, 57], and found to provide a representative sample of a person's day with acceptable base rates of key behaviors and features of the wearer's social environment, while leaving 95% of the participants' daily life unrecorded and private.

Participants are provided instructions for device use (e.g., not covering the device with clothing, allowing the "recording" symbol to be visible) and privacy, including use of a privacy button that prevents recording for 30 minutes and the option to leave behind the device during activities they do not wish to have recorded. Participants are also given an opportunity to review and delete recordings before returning the device. EAR data are stored locally on the smartphone and then downloaded to a secure server when the EAR devices are returned to the research team.

EAR recordings are coded by trained human coders using a study-specific adaptation of the Social Environment Coding of Sound Inventory (SECSI) [58], a behavioral coding framework designed to characterize acoustically detectable features of participants' everyday environments, activities, behaviors, and social interactions. Coding domains include physical and social context, interaction partners, conversation topics and types, daily activities, emotional expression and affect, and interpersonal behaviors. Categories characterize, among other features, whether participants are alone or with others, whom they are interacting with, whether conversations are practical or substantive, and whether interactions involve conflict or tension. Additional categories capture participants' emotional and interpersonal behavior, including affection and gratitude, self-criticism, frustration, support seeking, support giving, and support receiving, as well as study-specific questions. Behaviors expressed by others in participants' immediate environments are also coded, including affection, humor, praise and encouragement, validation, sharing of feelings, dismissiveness or minimizing, criticism, hostility, and emotional overinvolvement. All categories are binarily coded (0 = the feature is not present in the audio file,1 = the given feature is present). For more information about the EAR Coding System, see this OSF page. Participant speech captured in EAR recordings is additionally transcribed for linguistic analysis. Transcribed speech is analyzed using Linguistic Inquiry and Word Count (LIWC-22) [59] to derive count-based linguistic indicators, including self-and other-focused language.

Measures

Self-Report and Clinician Rated-Measures

Warwick-Edinburgh Mental Well-Being Scale (WEMWBS).

The WEMWBS is a 14item self-report measure of psychological well-being that captures both emotional and functional aspects of well-being [60]. Example items include "I've been feeling optimistic about the future," "I've been dealing with problems well," and "I've been feeling close to other people."

Items are positively worded and rated on a 5-point scale ranging from 1 (none of the time) to 5 (all of the time), with participants reporting on their experiences over the previous 2 weeks.

Higher scores indicate greater mental well-being. The WEMWBS is administered pre-dosing at Baseline 1, Baseline 2, and post-dosing at Weeks 1, 2, 4, and 8 as part of the OPTIMIZE parent study procedure. For our hypothesis testing, we will use participants' WEMWBS scores at Baseline 1 and Week 8 post-dosing.

Montgomery-Åsberg Depression Rating Scale (MADRS).

The MADRS is a 10-item clinician-rated measure of depressive symptom severity designed to be sensitive to changes in symptoms over the course of treatment [61]. The structured interview [62] assesses reported and apparent sadness, inner tension, reduced sleep and appetite, concentration difficulties, lassitude, inability to feel, pessimistic thoughts, and suicidal thoughts. Each item is rated from 0 to 6, yielding a total score ranging from 0 to 60, with higher scores indicating greater depressive symptom severity. MADRS assessments are administered by trained independent raters who are blinded to treatment allocation and visit number. For our hypothesis testing, we will use participants' MADRS scores at Baseline 1 and Week 8 post-dosing.

Challenging Experience Questionnaire (CEQ).

The CEQ is a 26-item self-report measure designed to assess challenging experiences associated with psilocybin use [63]. The measure comprises seven dimensions of challenging experience: fear, grief, insanity, death, isolation, physiological distress, and paranoia. Example items assess experiences such as fear, feelings of isolation, feeling as though one is dying, and concern about losing one's sanity. The CEQ is administered at Day 1 and Week 8 post-dose as part of the OPTIMIZE parent study procedure. For this study, we will use the Day 1 CEQ answers in the analyses.

Psychedelic-Assisted Therapy Harms and Side Effects Scale (PATHS).

The PATHS assesses potential adverse effects that may occur in psychedelic-assisted therapy [64]. The measure captures up to 54 discrete adverse effect criteria. Participants are instructed to rate the extent to which they are experiencing each potential adverse effect on a scale from 0 ("None/Does not apply") to 3 ("Quite a bit"). An example is "I have been struggling with the meaning or purpose of my life". Items predicated on experiences specific to psychedelic treatment, such as inappropriate physical contact during dosing, are omitted from the baseline measure. For our hypothesis testing, we will use participants' PATHS scores at Baseline 1 and

Week 8 post-dosing.

Analysis Plan

Analyses will be conducted using R 4.6. [65]. The analytic sample will include participants who complete pre-dose study activities, receive a psilocybin dose, and provide at least one post-dose assessment of the relevant outcome. Missing EMA time-series observations will be imputed using the imputeTS R package [66, 67]. Missing data on repeatedly assessed continuous outcome variables will be handled using full-information maximum likelihood under the assumption that data are missing at random. Variables will be transformed as appropriate when distributional violations of model assumptions can be addressed through transformation, with all transformations reported transparently. Participants contributing fewer than 50 codable EAR sound files at each given timepoint will be excluded from analyses involving EAR-derived variables. Parent-trial treatment conditions will be included as a covariate in planned analyses.

Demographic and baseline characteristics will be summarized using descriptive statistics.

Statistical tests will use a two-sided significance level of .05, and effect estimates will be reported with 95% confidence intervals.

Power Analysis

Statistical power was evaluated for the primary linear mixed-effects model testing the cross-level interaction between time (baseline and week 8) and a binary variable (e.g., sudden gain). Assuming a total sample size of N = 141 individuals, a standard moderate effect size (Cohen's f² = 0.06) for the interaction, a two-sided significance level of α = 0.005 (adjusting for 9 multiple tests in the most restrictive case), and a standard moderate-to-high baseline-to-followup correlation of r = 0.50 typical of longitudinal behavioral data, the study achieves approximately 99% statistical power to detect the interaction effect.

Outcome Construction

Across hypotheses, outcomes will be characterized using depressive symptoms, psychological well-being, and psychedelic-related adverse effects. MADRS and WEMWBS scores will characterize depressive-symptom and well-being outcomes, respectively, whereas the sum of worsening domains on the PATHS from pre-to post-dosing will characterize adverseeffect outcomes.

Construction of Risk and Protective Variables

EMA-Derived Sudden Gains and Losses. We will derive sudden gains and losses from each participant's EMA time series, using momentary affective valence as the primary state variable. The ts_levels() function in the casnet R package will identify abrupt shifts within each individual trajectory. We will specify a minimum data split of 12 observations, corresponding to approximately 3 days of EMA assessments; a minimum level duration of 28 observations, corresponding to approximately 1 week; and a change-sensitivity parameter of .05. We will classify upward abrupt shifts as sudden gains and downward abrupt shifts as sudden losses.

EAR-Derived Risk and Protective Factors.

We will aggregate dichotomous EAR behavioral codes to create continuous indicators of participants' everyday behaviors and social environments (i.e., for each timepoint, the percentage of the four-day period during which each feature of behavior and social environment occurred). Linguistic analyses of transcribed EAR speech will provide complementary indicators of self-and other-focused language.

The EAR-derived variables used in H2 will capture social support, interpersonal conflict, and conversational focus on the self-versus others. Social support will serve as a protective factor and interpersonal conflict as a risk factor. We will operationalize conversational focus using selfand other-focused language, with greater movement from self-focused toward other-focused language representing the hypothesized protective pattern.

Voice Diary Derived Risk and Protective Factors.

The voice-diary analyses will focus on personal reflection/perspectival flexibility and rigidity, indexed through thematic coding using the VOICE as well as linguistic analyses of speech features contained in the transcripts.

Indicators of curiosity, perspective-taking, and explorative language will contribute to the personal reflection/perspectival flexibility exploration construct, whereas ruminative and repetitive language will index rigidity. Hypothesis Testing

For H1, participant-level EMA time series will be analyzed using dynamic-systems methods implemented in the casnet R package. Sudden gains or losses will be estimated as binary variables indicating presence or absence. Across 6 mixed-effects models, one of the three endpoints (i.e., depressive symptoms, psychological well-being, and psychedelic-related adverse effects) will serve as the outcome variable, and predictors include a time variable (baseline vs. nested within individuals, an EAR predictor assessed at three times, interaction term of an EAR predictor and time, and other covariates. The interaction regression coefficients -EAR predictor X time -will constitute the primary tests of whether risk and protective factors are associated with change in outcomes.

For H2b, voice diary variables (rigidity, personal reflection/perspectival flexibility) aggregated at two time points (before dosing vs. after dosing) will be examined simultaneously as longitudinal predictors of depressive symptoms, psychological well-being, and psychedelic-related adverse effects in 6 separate mixed-effects models. The procedures will be similar to above.

For H3a, three linear regression models will examine associations between challenging experiences during psilocybin dosing, operationalized using CEQ scores assessed at Day 1 postdosing, and week-8 depressive symptom, psychological well-being, and psychedelic-related adverse-effect outcomes, controlling for baseline values. For H3b, moderation effects will be tested by including interaction terms, one at a time, between CEQ scores and the previously tested risk and protective factors, including sudden gains and losses and factors derived from EAR, EMA, and voice-diary data.

To account for multiple testing, we will control the false discovery rate (FDR) using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure within families of conceptually related hypothesis tests (i.e., EAR, EMA) [68]. Families of tests will be defined a priori based on the hypothesis being tested and the source of the predictor variable. In that vein, the six tests of EMA-derived sudden gains and losses corresponding to H1 will constitute one family; the nine tests of EAR-derived risk and protective factors in H2a will constitute a second family; the six tests of voice-diary derived factors in H2b will constitute a third family. For H3a, the three tests of the association between CEQ and outcomes will constitute a fourth family; for H3b, for each moderator, the three tests of moderation effects (one for each outcome) will constitute a separate family.

Exploratory analyses will examine time-varying effects of voice diary variables on each of the three outcomes by fitting models of change over time. For other exploratory aims, additional dynamic-systems indices will be examined as moderators of associations between risk and resilience variables derived from EMA, voice-diary, and EAR data and clinical outcomes.

Additional analyses will examine associations between the timing of dynamic changes relative to psilocybin dosing and subsequent clinical outcomes. Exploratory analyses will also examine additional EMA variables, including affective activation, activity prototypicality, social connectedness, and participant-specific items, as well as acoustic voice biomarkers derived from voice-diary and EAR recordings.

Discussion

The Dial-In Study is designed to characterize predictors of differential response to psilocybin, including both improvement and worsening following treatment. Its primary contribution is the use of multi-method, ambulatory, intensive longitudinal assessment to examine changes that unfold around psilocybin dosing and within participants' everyday lives.

By combining EMA, voice-diary, EAR, and repeated assessments of depression, well-being, and adverse effects, the study will examine subjective, behavioral, interpersonal, and clinical variables across different time scales rather than relying only on assessments conducted at discrete study visits.

This design makes it possible to examine post-acute change at a finer temporal level.

Intensive longitudinal assessment allows sudden gains and losses to be identified within individual affective trajectories, extending work showing that improvement and worsening in psychotherapy can occur discontinuously rather than gradually [38, 39]. Ambulatory assessments administered in the contexts of participants' daily lives also allow these changes to be considered alongside participants' everyday environments and behaviors. EAR data characterize social support, interpersonal conflict, and conversational focus, while voice-diary data capture personal reflection and rigidity. The inclusion of personalized EMA items further allows participants' own desired outcomes to be represented alongside standardized measures. Together, these methods allow the study to examine processes that would be difficult to capture through conventional baseline and follow-up assessments alone.

The resulting data may help clarify several sources of differential response to psilocybin.

First, they will allow examination of whether sudden gains and losses precede differences in depression, well-being, and adverse effects. Second, they will allow risk and protective factors observed in participants' daily lives to be examined as predictors of these same outcomes.

Finally, Dial-In may help clarify why challenging dosing experiences are followed by improvement in some individuals and worsening in others. Challenging experiences have been associated with poorer outcomes, but these associations are inconsistent, and difficult experiences can also be followed by perceived benefit [19, 20, 25]. By examining whether risk and protective factors occurring before and after dosing moderate this relationship, the study may provide a more detailed account of the conditions under which challenging experiences are followed by better versus worse outcomes.

Several strengths and limitations should be considered. The prospective design and repeated assessment surrounding dosing allow temporal ordering of predictors and subsequent outcomes, while the combination of self-report, naturalistic speech, and observed everyday behavior provides complementary information about participants' post-acute experiences. At the same time, Dial-In is observational, and associations between risk and protective factors and later outcomes cannot establish causality. Intensive EMA, voice-diary, and EAR assessment may also result in missingness or differential adherence, and the different assessment schedules of these methods limit the extent to which all data streams can be aligned at the same temporal scale. In addition, the study is embedded within a clinical trial of adults with major depressive disorder, which may limit generalizability beyond this treatment context.

More broadly, Dial-In will generate fine-grained longitudinal data that may support continued investigation of temporal and dynamic predictors of differential response to psilocybin. Identifying patterns that consistently precede improvement or worsening could inform future research on early indicators of vulnerability and resilience and on the periods in which additional support may be most relevant. The present study will not establish how such factors should be modified in treatment, but it may provide a basis for more targeted research on how post-acute experiences, behaviors, and environments contribute to outcomes following psilocybin.
