Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Metacheck does not yet analyze automatically whether sentences which include non-significant p-values are correct, but we recommend manually checking the sentences below for possible misinterpreted non-significant p values.

Text Sentence
p = 0.083 However, when controlling for cognitive performance, the Aware phenotype was no longer significantly associated with an increase in progression risk (HR ratio = 1.48, 95% CI: 0.95, 2.31; p = 0.083).

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). Statcheck might still miss one-sided tests that are described in another sentence, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. Abstract

Background: Altered memory self-awareness is common in mild cognitive impairment (MCI), but its relationship to Alzheimer's disease (AD) pathology and progression remains unclear.

Methods: Using 590 amnestic MCI ADNI participants, memory self-awareness was derived from participant-informant discrepancies on the memory subdomain of the Everyday Cognition questionnaire. Heightened awareness and unawareness subscores were calculated, and k-means clustering identified four phenotypes. Associations with cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) and positron emission tomography (PET) were examined, and clinical progression was assessed using Cox models.

Results: Aware and Unaware groups showed lower CSF amyloid-β, and higher CSF phosphorylated tau and PET amyloid. The Unaware phenotype was associated with increased progression risk (hazard ratio 2.41, 95% CI 1.48-3.92), whereas the Worried Well phenotype showed reduced risk (hazard ratio 0.58, 95% CI 0.34-0.99).

Conclusions: Multidimensional assessment of memory self-awareness identifies biologically and prognostically distinct subgroups within MCI and may improve risk stratification in prodromal AD.

BACKGROUND

Alzheimer's disease (AD) is the leading cause of cognitive impairment among older adults and is frequently accompanied by altered awareness of cognitive decline. Self-awareness of cognitive function is commonly assessed by examining discrepancies between participant-and study partner-reported cognitive complaints [1, 2]. Heightened awareness, self-perceived decline not corroborated by an informant [3], has often been observed in preclinical stages, whereas unawareness (anosognosia), defined as greater informant-reported than self-reported decline, is characteristic of AD dementia [4, 5]. In mild cognitive impairment (MCI), awareness is heterogeneous, with individuals ranging from intact insight to marked unawareness [4] [5] [6]. Estimates of anosognosia in MCI vary widely [1, [7] [8] [9] [10] [11] [12] [13] [14] [15], underscoring uncertainty regarding its clinical and biological significance. This heterogeneity has important implications. Reliance on participant-reported subjective cognitive decline alone may be misleading when awareness is impaired, highlighting the importance of incorporating study partner reports in early disease stages [5]. Because episodic memory impairment is the defining clinical feature of amnestic MCI, and one of the earliest cognitive manifestations of AD, memory self-awareness represents a clinically and biologically relevant construct for investigating heterogeneity in disease progression. As efforts intensify to identify individuals at highest risk of progression, particularly in the era of disease-modifying therapies, the pathological and prognostic relevance of altered self-awareness in MCI has become increasingly important. Despite these efforts, methods to effectively stratify individuals within the MCI stage remain limited -a gap that may be addressed by leveraging heterogeneity of memory self-awareness.

Traditional discrepancy scores average differences across questionnaire items, potentially obscuring distinct patterns of heightened awareness and unawareness within individuals. A recently proposed approach separates these dimensions by independently quantifying unawareness and heightened awareness based on item-level discrepancies [16]. For each questionnaire item, discrepancies are calculated between participant and study partner ratings; items on which the study partner reports greater decline contribute to an unawareness subscore, whereas items on which the participant reports greater decline contribute to a heightened awareness subscore. By isolating these directional discrepancies rather than averaging them into a single summary score, this approach yields a two-dimensional construct that distinguishes loss of awareness from increased concern. In cognitively normal older adults from the Alzheimer's Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI), only the unawareness subscore, rather than the traditional discrepancy score or the heightened awareness subscore, was associated with future clinical progression [16]. These findings are consistent with prior studies demonstrating that reduced awareness, but not heightened concern, is linked to greater clinical decline [17, 18], suggesting that directional measures of self-awareness may capture clinically meaningful risk. Importantly, these studies have primarily focused on memory self-awareness, reflecting the central role of episodic memory impairment across the AD continuum and providing the rationale for the present study's focus on memory selfawareness. However, whether these constructs reflect underlying AD pathology or predict progression once cognitive impairment is present remains unknown. Moreover, traditional discrepancy measures do not account for the overall magnitude of participant and informant complaints. For example, a discrepancy score of zero may reflect either low complaints from both individuals or high but concordant complaints (i.e. when the participant and informant report similar levels of decline), potentially obscuring clinically meaningful differences in risk [19, 20].

Data-driven approaches may offer greater sensitivity for identifying distinct self-awareness profiles.

Unsupervised clustering methods allow integration of participant-reported complaints, study partnerreported complaints, and independent measures of unawareness and heightened awareness to derive clinically interpretable phenotypes [21, 22].

In this study, we sought to characterize memory self-awareness phenotypes in individuals with amnestic MCI using a clustering approach and to examine their associations with cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) biomarkers of amyloid and tau and with longitudinal progression to AD dementia. Because amnestic MCI is defined by impairment in episodic memory, the cognitive domain most closely linked to the earliest stages of AD pathology, and previous studies of altered self-awareness in Alzheimer's disease have primarily focused on memory function, we examined memory self-awareness as our primary construct of interest. We hypothesized that phenotypes characterized by greater study partner-reported decline would demonstrate higher AD biomarker burden and increased risk of clinical progression [18].

METHODS

Participants

All participants (N = 590) used in the preparation of this article were MCI at baseline and were obtained from the ADNI database (adni.loni.usc.edu). ADNI was launched in 2003 as a public-private partnership, led by Principal Investigator Michael W. Weiner, MD, and includes the extensions ADNI-GO (launched 2009), ADNI-2 (2011), and ADNI-3 (2016). The original goal of ADNI was to test whether serial magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), positron emission tomography (PET), other biological markers, and clinical and neuropsychological assessments can be combined to measure the progression of MCI and AD dementia. The current goals include validating biomarkers for clinical trials, improving the generalizability of ADNI data by increasing diversity in the participant cohort, and providing data concerning the diagnosis and progression of AD to the scientific community. For up-to-date information, see adni.loni.usc.edu. Exclusion criteria included a history of alcoholism, drug abuse, and head trauma, as well as serious medical or psychiatric conditions. Cholinesterase inhibitors and memantine were only allowed in participants if they were stable for 3 months before screening. Moreover, antidepressants were allowed if participants were not significantly depressed at the time of screening and did not have a history of major depressive disorder within the past year. Institutional review board approvals and informed consent were obtained prior to all procedures. This manuscript conforms to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (http://www.equator-network.org/reporting-guidelines/strobe/) reporting guidelines for cohort studies.

The selection criteria for this study required both a participant and study partner response on the memory component of the ECog questionnaire from the same time point, as well as a clinical examination and a lumbar puncture to collect CSF occurring within a year of ECog completion. Their clinical diagnosis was used as a diagnostic criterion for amnestic MCI, determined by a Clinical Dementia Rating (CDR) Scale global score of 0.5, with a Memory Box score of at least 0.5, a Mini-Mental State Exam (MMSE) score between 24 and 30 inclusive, and performance below education-adjusted cutoffs on the Wechsler Logical Memory II subscale. For the survival analyses, a diagnosis of AD dementia was assigned to participants with a CDR global score of ≥ 0.5, an MMSE score between 20 and 24, performance below educationadjusted cutoffs on the Wechsler Logical Memory II subscale, and a probable AD label according to NINCDS/ADRDA criteria. The participants included individuals sampled from the ADNI-GO, ADNI-2, and ADNI-3 phases, spanning from 2010 to 2022. ADNI-1 was not included in the sample since the ECog questionnaire was administered starting in the ADNI-GO phase in 2010. Any individuals with missing baseline ECog questionnaire and CSF biomarker data were removed from the analysis, and any longitudinal observations without clinical diagnosis data were removed. For the survival analysis, we created a subset that included participants with at least two follow-up clinical diagnosis time points (N = 492). For more information on ADNI, see the ADNI database (adni.loni.usc.edu).

Self-awareness measure

Memory self-awareness was measured using the memory subsection of the ECog Questionnaire. The ECog is comprised of 39 items, 8 of which specifically focus on memory [23, 24]. The memory subsection primarily evaluates episodic memory, with participants and their study partners independently assessing the participant's ability to accomplish everyday memory tasks compared to 10 years earlier, such as remembering a few shopping items without a list, or if there has been any change in repeating stories and/or questions. Responses were recorded on a Likert scale, where 1 indicated improvement or no change, 2 reflected occasional or questionable decline, 3 denoted consistent mild decline, and 4 signified consistent significant decline. An "I do not know" option was also available and treated as missing data when selected.

A two-step approach was used to assess self-awareness. First, an unawareness sub-score and a heightened awareness sub-score for the ECog Questionnaire, originally published in [16] were calculated for each participant. The unawareness sub-score was calculated by subtracting the study partner's ratings from the participant's ratings for each item, setting all positive differences to zero, and averaging the results (including the zeros derived from the positive differences). Similarly, the heightened awareness sub-score was calculated by capping all negative differences at zero and taking the mean, again including the zeroes derived from negative differences in the mean computation. Second, k-means clustering (with Euclidian distance) using the kmeans package was used to categorize participants into four distinct clusters, with 25 random initializations to ensure stability and the seed set to 42. K-means was chosen due to the continuous nature of the ECog questionnaire. Additionally, k-means clustering is uniquely fast, scalable, and intuitive by assigning all individuals into discrete clusters (unlike probabilistic methods such as Latent Profile Analysis). These factors make k-means easy to integrate into clinical settings for risk stratification. Prior to clustering, all four features were z-scored, and nine outliers based on the four awareness measures were discovered using Mahalanobis distance and excluded from clustering.

Mahalanobis distance was used due to its ability to check for multivariate outliers. Assumptions of sphericity for k-means clustering were checked visually using principal component analysis. The choice of using four clusters was determined with the elbow method and the Davies-Bouldin Index (DBI; see eFigures 1 and 2), along with prior literature, which has used both participant and study partner scores from the ECog questionnaire at a cut off of 2.5 for both measures to classify individuals into the following awareness quadrant profiles: 1) Low Complaints, 2) Worried Well, 3) Aware, and 4) Unaware [25]. Four measures were used as clustering features: the unawareness sub-score, the heightened awareness sub-score, the mean study partner complaints, and the mean participant complaints. We evaluated the quality of clustering using the Davies-Bouldin Index (eFigure 2) and silhouette scores. These metrics were chosen as they are shown to be more informative and reliable compared to alternatives (e.g. Shannon entropy, Dunn index) in biomedical contexts [26]. The Davies-Bouldin Index (DBI) is a measure of the average ratio of within-cluster scatter and between-cluster separation. Scores on the DBI range from zero to positive infinity, with lower scores indicating more compact, well-separated clusters [27]. Meanwhile, the silhouette score measures how similar each point is in Euclidean space to other clusters. Silhouette scores range from -1 to +1, with scores closer to +1 indicating better clustering quality [28]. Silhouette scores were computed for each cluster to capture inter-cluster variability in cluster quality that can be masked when looking at a global silhouette score. Due to the multicollinearity of our features, we also used these four measures in principal component analysis (PCA) to create clusters with orthogonal features. This PCA found that two components accounted for 90.1% of the variance between our features.

CSF Measurements of AD Core Biomarkers

Lumbar punctures were performed as part of the ADNI protocols and CSF was collected, centrifuged, subpackaged, and stocked in polypropylene tubes in a refrigerator at -80 °C. ADNI used the Roche Elecsys immunoassay to assess AD biomarkers, including total tau (t-tau), phosphorylated tau (p-tau) (181P) and Aβ (1-42). A single lot of reagents for each of the three measured biomarkers was collected (provided in UPENNBIOMK9.csv file available at http://adni.loni.usc.edu). CSF was collected both at baseline and follow-up visits. The following procedure only used p-tau and Aβ1-42 as measures for tau and amyloid, respectively. CSF was chosen over other measures due to PET, blood-based, and plasma measures only becoming standard in ADNI, which would have limited statistical power and follow-up duration for many participants. However, supplemental analyses were performed in a subsample (N = 114) with PET inferior temporal tau standard uptake value ratios (SUVR), entorhinal tau SUVR, and amyloid centiloids.

Statistical Analyses

All analyses were conducted in R version 4.4.1 (R Project for Statistical Computing). The threshold for statistical significance was p < .05.

Given the non-linear distributions of both CSF biomarkers, we utilized Kruskal-Wallis tests to assess cross-sectional differences in CSF biomarkers among the four awareness phenotypes. Significant omnibus effects were followed by post hoc pairwise Wilcoxon rank sum tests to identify specific group differences. To control for multiple comparisons, p-values were adjusted using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure for false discovery rate correction where applicable. To account for age, sex, and education, we conducted confirmatory Rank-Based analyses of covariance (ANCOVAs) using the Rfit packages, using CSF measures in two separate tests as predictors and with cluster, age, sex, and education as covariates.

We used post hoc Wilcoxon rank sum tests on the residuals of the covariates to look for specific group differences. Supplemental analyses also performed these tests with PET tau (inferior temporal and entorhinal) and amyloid centiloids as predictors.

Survival analyses were run using Cox regression models using the survival package to examine the longitudinal associations of each awareness phenotype at baseline and time to progression (measured in years from baseline). Outcome variables were progression status and (1) time to progression, for progressors, and (2) total time observed, for stable (i.e., censored) individuals. Progression from MCI to AD dementia was determined using progression by clinical diagnosis. The model was run with phenotype membership of each participant as the primary predictor, and the model adjusted for baseline age, sex, and education. The Cox proportional-hazard model was used to find correlation among the factors, and hazard ratio (HR) and 95% confidence interval (CI) were calculated. To assess the fit of these progression analyses, Uno's C-index, time-dependent AUC values at times 1, 2, and 4 (corresponding to each quartile of the proportion of progression events), and the inverse probability of censoring weighted (IPCW) Brier scores were computed. To ensure that any clinical or biological differences in phenotypes were not due to underlying clinical impact severity, supplemental sensitivity analyses were also controlled for MMSE.

DISCUSSION

In this cohort of individuals with MCI, distinct memory awareness phenotypes were associated with differential Alzheimer's disease biomarker burden and markedly different risks of progression to dementia. Phenotypes characterized by greater study partner-reported decline, particularly the Unaware phenotype, demonstrated higher phosphorylated tau, lower amyloid-β concentrations, and substantially increased progression risk. In contrast, the Worried Well phenotype, marked by heightened self-reported concern without informant corroboration, was associated with lower risk of progression. These findings suggest that structured assessment of memory self-awareness using patient-informant discrepancies may provide clinically meaningful prognostic information during the prodromal stages of AD. Importantly, these findings should be interpreted within the context of memory awareness in amnestic MCI rather than as evidence that awareness operates uniformly across all cognitive domains.

Our results extend prior work demonstrating that unawareness predicts clinical decline in cognitively normal individuals [17, 18] by showing that directional measures of awareness remain informative once cognitive impairment is present. Notably, although the Unaware and Aware phenotypes did not significantly differ in cross-sectional biomarker burden, the Unaware phenotype conferred a substantially greater hazard of progression. This dissociation suggests that loss of awareness may capture aspects of disease vulnerability not fully reflected by baseline pathology alone. Importantly, informant reports alone would not distinguish between these two groups; rather, the interaction between self-and informantreported decline appears to convey additional prognostic value above and beyond only informant report, which would be unable to separate the Unaware and Aware phenotypes.

Interestingly, age as a covariate was not found to have an association with risk progression. While this goes against most literature suggesting older age as a risk factor, we believe that, given the Unaware phenotype had the oldest mean age as well as the highest risk of progression, the risk that would have come with age might have been already captured via our phenotypes. Additionally, there is some literature using age as a covariate to find predictors of risk progression in the MCI stage that has found similar null findings for age [29, 30].

Additionally, our findings support a growing body of research demonstrating an association between loss of memory self-awareness and increased AD biomarker burden [3, 18, [31] [32] [33] [34] [35] [36] [37] [38] [39], reinforcing the biological relevance of unawareness across the disease continuum. The present study focused on memory selfawareness because participants met criteria for amnestic MCI, a syndrome defined by impairment in episodic memory, which represents the earliest and most prominent cognitive manifestation of AD.

Consequently, memory self-awareness was selected as the primary construct of interest rather than multidomain awareness. Whether the observed prognostic associations reflect a particular relevance of memory awareness in amnestic MCI or broader differences across cognitive domains remains an important question for future investigation and will require direct comparisons of domain-specific awareness measures.

The dissociation between cross-sectional biomarker burden and longitudinal progression risk in the

Unaware phenotype raises important mechanistic considerations. Self-awareness of cognitive function depends on distributed neural networks involving medial prefrontal, anterior cingulate, and default mode regions [40] [41] [42], areas that are vulnerable in early AD [43] [44] [45] [46] [47] [48] [49] [50]. Loss of awareness may therefore reflect network-level dysfunction or impaired metacognitive processing that is not fully captured by global cerebrospinal fluid biomarker concentrations or clinical decline as shown via our sensitivity analyses. In this context, unawareness may represent a behavioral manifestation of emerging network disintegration, signaling reduced compensatory capacity and increased vulnerability to clinical decline.

From a clinical standpoint, these findings underscore the potential value of incorporating structured informant-based assessments into routine evaluation of MCI. As MCI-stage disease-modifying therapies, such as lecanemab and donanemab, increasingly require accurate prognostication and biomarker confirmation, scalable behavioral markers that help identify individuals at highest risk of progression are critically needed. Awareness phenotyping derived from brief questionnaires may provide complementary information alongside fluid and imaging biomarkers. Future studies are needed to determine its incremental value and clinical utility in diverse populations and clinical settings.

The reduced progression risk observed in the Worried Well phenotype further underscores the clinical relevance of distinguishing directional awareness patterns. Despite comparable biomarker levels to the Low Complaints phenotype, individuals with heightened but uncorroborated concern demonstrated lower progression risk. Heightened awareness may reflect non-Alzheimer-related factors such as anxiety, affective symptoms, or heightened health vigilance rather than underlying neurodegenerative pathology, consistent with prior reports that exaggerated concern is not specific to AD processes [5, 17, 18, 32].

Together, these findings suggest that unawareness may be more closely linked to AD pathology than heightened awareness in MCI.

Several limitations warrant consideration. Although the present study focused on memory self-awareness in individuals with amnestic MCI, future studies should directly compare awareness across memory, executive, language, visuospatial, and other cognitive domains to determine whether the observed associations are specific to memory self-awareness or reflect a broader phenomenon across cognitive domains. Given that validation of the ECog memory subset remains limited outside of ADNI, future work should also establish the clinical validity and generalizability of this measure in independent cohorts. The cohort was predominantly White and highly educated, potentially limiting generalizability. Additionally, only 14% of participants progressed to AD dementia during the follow-up period. Participants who progressed were observed over longer follow-up periods than non-progressors. Additionally, non-random attrition may have influenced the observed progression rates. For example, participants with non-Alzheimer neurodegenerative syndromes, such as frontotemporal dementia, may have been more likely to discontinue follow-up, potentially introducing informative censoring. As with any data-driven clustering approach, replication in independent cohorts will be important to establish the robustness, reproducibility, and generalizability of the identified phenotypes. Although the present study demonstrated consistent associations between memory self-awareness phenotypes, AD biomarkers, and longitudinal clinical progression within ADNI, future work should determine whether these phenotypes can be reproduced in independent cohorts. Regarding the use of ECog, each question asks how the participant's memory performance compares to 10 years before. The extended recall interval may introduce recall bias if either the participant or the informant inaccurately recalls functioning over the preceding decade, potentially influencing estimates of self-awareness. Additionally, literature has shown that informant scores can be influenced by informant mood, anxiety, and caregiver burden e.g. [51]. That is, if caregiver burden or distress inflates informant-reported complaints, some individuals classified as "Unaware" in our framework may instead reflect elevated caregiver-reported concern driven partly by caregiver factors rather than patient unawareness. Measures of caregiver burden and mood were not available in ADNI and therefore could not be incorporated into the present analyses. Subsequently, it would be important for future studies to incorporate informant-level psychological measures to disambiguate patient unawareness from informant-driven over-reporting. Furthermore, in the unawareness and heightened awareness subscores, individuals with deep complaints (complaints on few questions but high complaints on those questions) can be indistinguishable to individuals with broad complaints (complaints on many questions though low complaint magnitude on each). Future studies should determine whether the breadth and depth of participant-informant discrepancies contribute differentially to disease progression and whether these characteristics should be incorporated into future clustering approaches. Finally, replication in more diverse populations and validation using additional biomarkers, including PET, plasma, and blood-based measures, will be important to establish the robustness, generalizability, and potential clinical utility of these findings.

In conclusion, memory self-awareness phenotypes defined by patient-informant discrepancies were associated with Alzheimer's disease biomarker burden and differential progression risk in individuals with amnestic MCI. These findings support the biological relevance of memory self-awareness as a marker of clinically meaningful disease heterogeneity while highlighting the need for future studies to determine how awareness across different cognitive domains relates to AD pathology and clinical progression.
