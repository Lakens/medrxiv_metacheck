Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). Abstract

Effective monitoring of Severe Acute Respiratory Infections (SARI) is critical for public health but faces challenges such as reporting delays, incomplete information on negative test results, and data sparsity in specific regions and age groups. This study proposes a novel statistical framework to estimate the granular distribution of respiratory viruses among hospitalized SARI patients in Brazil. We employ Gradient Boosting Decision Trees (GBDT) to model viral proportions as conditional probabilities based on patient age, location, and time. A key methodological contribution is the development of SViDAS, a semi-parametric estimator that integrates viral probabilities over continuous lognormal age distributions, enabling estimation for arbitrary age intervals. Additionally, to address the "black box" limitations of machine learning, we introduce an interpretability strategy based on the estimator's internal embedding space, which identifies relevant training instances to explain individual predictions. Using data from the Brazilian SIVEP-Gripe system (2022-2024), results demonstrate that our models reduce the Brier Score estimation error by approximately 40% for SARS-CoV-2 and 36% for RSV compared with the empirical aggregation benchmark. Furthermore, the reporting-delay analysis indicates that estimation error decreases sharply 1.

1. Introduction

Severe Acute Respiratory Infection (SARI) is a severe form of acute respiratory illness that can be caused by several infectious agents, including SARS-CoV-2, influenza viruses, and respiratory syncytial virus (RSV), and may progress to respiratory failure requiring hospitalization and intensive medical care [1].

In Brazil, universal surveillance of SARI was implemented in 2009 following the influenza A (H1N1) pandemic. Since 2019, SARI cases have been reported through the Influenza Epidemiological Surveillance Information System (SIVEP-Gripe), the official national system used to monitor the clinical and epidemiological profile of hospitalized cases and deaths, as well as circulating respiratory viruses [2, 3, 4]. Detailed epidemiological records are made publicly available and updated weekly by the Brazilian Ministry of Health [4, 3]. Although these data provide an important basis for respiratory-virus surveillance, obtaining timely estimates at fine spatial and demographic resolutions remains challenging. Viral circulation can vary substantially across locations, age groups, and epidemiological periods, while empirical estimates often require some degree of aggregation to ensure sufficient observations. Consequently, relevant local and demographic heterogeneity may be obscured when surveillance information is summarized over broader populations.

Our objective is to complement existing surveillance systems by providing timely estimates of the distribution of respiratory viruses among viruspositive SARI cases at a finer resolution, particularly across municipalities and patient ages. Rather than relying on predefined demographic and geographical groups, the proposed framework models viral proportions as conditional probabilities based on location, population size, patient age, and epidemiological week. This formulation enables continuous monitoring of specific epidemiological contexts while leveraging information available across the surveillance dataset, supporting public health professionals in identifying changes in viral circulation that may inform prevention and control decisions.

To illustrate the level of granularity targeted by the proposed framework, Figure 1 presents estimated viral distributions across the 52 epidemiological weeks of 2024 for São José dos Campos (state of São Paulo) and Uberlândia (state of Minas Gerais), comparing patients aged 1 and 65 years. The example illustrates substantial variation across both municipalities and age profiles that would be obscured under broader aggregation. The proposed methodology is implemented in a dedicated web application (https://joaoflavio.shinyapps.io/Virus_Monitor) for continuous visualization of the estimates [5]. We also provide the Python package PySRAG [6], which supports the processing and analysis of Brazilian SARI surveillance data and facilitates the reproducibility of the framework.

A preliminary version of this work was presented in [7]. The present study substantially extends it by introducing the SViDAS estimator for arbitrary age intervals, expanding the empirical evaluation to reporting-delay and data-scarcity scenarios, and providing an embedding-space approach for interpreting individual predictions.

1.1. Related work

>Related work

Surveillance Systems

Current monitoring initiatives differ significantly in scope and methodology. The InfoGripe system [8, 9] employs Bayesian frameworks to estimate SARI incidence counts, correcting for reporting delays. However, its estimation of viral distribution-the proportion of cases attributed to specific pathogens-often relies on empirical aggregation over large geographical areas and extended time windows.

In contrast, the Instituto Todos pela Saúde (ITpS) [10] leverages data from a network of private laboratories to monitor pathogen circulation and compares these private positivity rates with SARI (SIVEP-Gripe) curves. However, two differences are particularly relevant for the specific goal of SARI surveillance. First, similar to the InfoGripe system, ITpS estimates are typically aggregated over large geographical areas (national, regional, or state levels), which obscures the heterogeneity of viral circulation at the municipality level. Second, the populations differ: SARI surveillance targets severe cases requiring hospitalization, whereas private laboratory data are based on individuals tested in partner laboratories, covering a broader clinical spectrum from mild to severe respiratory infections. Consequently, the viral distribution observed in private laboratory testing may not accurately mirror the etiological profile of severe infections, as pathogens associated with milder diseases may occur at different rates than among hospitalized SARI cases.

Our work bridges this gap by providing a granular, patient-level estimator that relies exclusively on SARI notification cases. Unlike empirical aggregation, which obscures local heterogeneity [11], our approach allows for specific viral attribution even in municipalities with sparse data.

Spatiotemporal Modeling

To estimate disease risk in areas with sparse data, the literature has traditionally leveraged spatial and spatiotemporal dependence via model-based geostatistics [12, 13]. Bayesian hierarchical models and Generalized Linear Mixed Models (GLMMs) are frequently employed to smooth incidence rates, explicitly incorporating spatial dependence through neighborhood adjacency matrices or distance-based covariance functions [14, 15].

However, these methods often require prior specification of spatial structures and, depending on the formulation, explicit interaction terms. While efficient approximations like INLA [16] exist, they can be sensitive to modeling assumptions [17] and computationally demanding for high-dimensional, real-time surveillance systems.

Consequently, recent literature has explored Machine Learning (ML) alternatives [18, 19]. Our work adopts this paradigm by utilizing Gradient Boosting Decision Trees (GBDT) [20], treating geolocation (latitude and longitude) as continuous covariates. This allows the estimator to adaptively partition the spatial domain-effectively learning data-driven neighborhoodsand to capture complex, non-linear interactions between location, time, and patient age without requiring explicit interaction terms.

Complementing Nowcasting Efforts

Finally, this work complements the extensive nowcasting literature [21, 8, 22, 23] by shifting the target from incidence counts to viral proportions. This shift allows for the characterization of the etiological landscape even when total case counts are still being consolidated.

Beyond estimation, we address the black box criticism often leveled at ML models in public health [24]. We introduce a model-specific interpretability strategy based on the estimator's internal embedding space, identifying influential training instances to explain individual predictions. Furthermore, we extend the standard point-estimation framework by proposing SViDAS, a semi-parametric estimator that integrates viral probabilities over continuous age distributions, enabling estimation for arbitrary age intervals (e.g., 0-2 years) relevant to age-specific surveillance.

2. Methodology

This work proposes a statistical framework designed to provide granular and timely estimates of respiratory viral distributions. Rather than relying on raw aggregates, we model viral proportions as conditional probabilities estimated through a machine learning approach. This section describes the main challenges associated with the surveillance data, dataset composition, and statistical procedures used to generate the proposed estimates.

2.1. Surveillance Challenges

To obtain timely and granular estimates of viral proportions, several challenges must be addressed: 1.

Information Delays: Delays in data availability arise from the time required for information to pass through several stages, from an individual's first symptoms perception to system reporting with virus identification. These stages include seeking medical care, undergoing testing, obtaining results, and entering the results into the surveillance system [8]. 2. Incomplete Data on Negative Tests: The database records a virus only when the test result is positive, except for influenza. Consequently, the data do not distinguish individuals with negative results from those who were not tested for a given virus. 3. Data Sparsity in Some Regions: Information availability varies substantially across geographical areas and demographic groups. For example, in São José dos Campos, as shown in Figure 1, obtaining sufficient observations for 1-year-old patients is highly unlikely. Such sparsity may result from factors including lower population density, limited healthcare resources, or infrequent reporting within specific demographic groups. 4. Computational Efficiency: The models must be updated weekly and deployed in a web application with limited memory resources (e.g., 1 GB RAM), making computational efficiency an important methodological constraint.

To address incomplete information on negative tests (2), we estimate the proportion of each viral agent among SARI patients with at least one positive viral test. This estimand should therefore be interpreted as the viral composition among detected positive cases, rather than as the absolute incidence or positivity rate of each virus in the underlying SARI population.

This proportional formulation may also reduce sensitivity to reporting delays (1) when delays affect viral agents approximately uniformly, such that the relative composition of positive cases is not systematically distorted. In Section 3, we empirically assess this assumption by comparing estimates obtained under different reporting lags with the consolidated data.

Data sparsity (3) is commonly addressed by aggregating observations across regions or age groups, which inevitably reduces granularity. Instead, our methodology estimates the probability of observing a specific virus conditional on relevant covariates. A probabilistic multiclass classification algorithm leverages patterns across the full dataset to generate contextualized estimates for specific combinations of location, population size, patient age, and epidemiological week, including settings with sparse observations.

Finally, computational efficiency (4) is addressed through the choice and configuration of the underlying learning algorithm, enabling efficient model training and prediction under the operational constraints of the surveillance application. Detailed model configuration and training procedures are provided in Section S1 of the Supplementary Material.

2.2. Data

Since its implementation in Brazil in 2009, SARI universal surveillance has required the collection and reporting of all hospitalized cases and/or deaths due to respiratory viruses [2, 4]. The current Brazilian Ministry of Health's definition characterizes SARI as an influenza-like illness (ILI) presenting with at least two of the following signs and symptoms: fever, chills, sore throat, headache, cough, runny nose, or olfactory or gustatory disturbances. In addition, the individual must exhibit one or more of the following: shortness of breath/respiratory discomfort, persistent chest pressure or pain, oxygen saturation below 95% in ambient air, or bluish coloration (cyanosis) of the lips or face [2].

Although the SARI criterion established by the Brazilian Ministry of Health is fundamental for national surveillance, its formulation was historically directed toward Influenza monitoring and was more recently adapted for the COVID-19 pandemic. The growing relevance of other respiratory pathogens, such as Respiratory Syncytial Virus (RSV), has led to discussions in global health organizations about broadening case definitions to improve their detection [25, 26].

Fever, a central component of the traditional criterion, is an inconsistent clinical sign in RSV infections, often being absent or blunted in vulnerable populations such as the elderly [27, 28], while also being a non-specific symptom with multiple causes in young children [29]. To enhance surveillance sensitivity for RSV and other respiratory viruses while maintaining comparability across seasonal and epidemiological periods, this study adopts a modified criterion that excludes the mandatory presence of fever. This approach aligns with the methodology employed by the InfoGripe surveillance system (Fiocruz), in accordance with updated Ministry of Health guidelines [30]. Accordingly, the datasets were filtered using the following logical rule: (cough OR sore throat) AND (dyspnea OR oxygen saturation < 95% OR difficulty breathing) AND (hospitalized OR deceased). This strategy prioritizes respiratory and severity signs that are more consistent across different etiological agents.

Data were retrieved from the Brazilian Ministry of Health's Open Data Portal [31]. To incorporate spatial and demographic dimensions, the records were enriched with geographical coordinates from the IBGE Selected Localities Register [32] and municipality population sizes based on the 2022 Demographic Census [33].

Formally, the dataset is denoted by D = {(x i , y i )} n i=1 , where x i contains information about the i-th individual, including the epidemiological week of symptom onset, patient age in years (computed from the reported age value and unit), geographical coordinates (latitude and longitude of the city, state, and region), and the population size of the notifying municipality. These covariates reflect relevant epidemiological dynamics: respiratory-virus prevalence varies substantially across age groups, while geographical and temporal information is particularly important in a country of continental dimensions such as Brazil, where viral circulation may differ markedly between regions at the same time. These choices are consistent with studies identifying age as a critical risk factor and highlighting regional disparities in SARI incidence [11, 34].

The response variable, y i , indicates the virus for which the patient's test result was positive: SARS-CoV-2, Influenza A/B Virus, Respiratory Syncytial Virus, Human Parainfluenza Virus 1/2/3/4, Human Adenovirus, Human Metapneumovirus, Human Bocavirus, Human Rhinovirus, and/or Others (other viruses or unspecified). The dataset includes only patients who tested positive for at least one virus. In cases of viral co-detection, the patient record was expanded into multiple observations, one for each detected virus. A sample representation of the resulting dataset D is provided in Supplementary Table S1.

2.3. Statistical Framework

This subsection details the statistical methodology employed to estimate the conditional distribution of viral infections. We first define the multiclass classification framework used to estimate the class-conditional probability distribution over all viruses given a patient's covariate profile. Subsequently, we extend this framework to estimate the marginal virus distribution over specific patient age intervals, employing a semiparametric integration strategy. Finally, we describe the embedding-based approach used to interpret individual estimator predictions by identifying influential training instances.

2.3.1. Virus distribution estimator

Our goal is to estimate the proportion of patients who test positive for a specific virus among positive patients, conditioned on x. We approach this task as a multiclass classification problem, where the variable Y can take values y representing different viruses being considered. The proportion is modeled by the conditional probability, denoted as P Y (y|x), which indicates the likelihood of a patient testing positive for a specific virus given the value of x. To estimate this probability, we employ a probabilistic classifier, denoted as ĝ(•), obtained based on the data in D.

For the function ĝ(•), we chose the Gradient Boosting Machine (GBM) estimator based on decision trees, widely known as Gradient Boosting Decision Trees (GBDT; see Section S1 of the Supplementary Material for detailed model configuration and training strategy). In a multi-class problem like predicting viruses, the GBDT constructs a separate additive model for each possible virus class k. Each of these models produces a raw score (or logit), which represents the evidence for that specific class. The estimated score for a given virus k, which we denote as ĥk (x), is calculated as the sum of the predictions from all the trees built for that class: where M is the total number of trees in the ensemble for each class, bmk (•) is the m-th decision tree assigned to the k-th virus class, and α m is the learning rate, adjusting each tree's contribution [35].

These raw scores, ĥk (x), are not probabilities; they are unbounded real numbers. To convert the vector of scores [ ĥ1 (x), ĥ2 (x), . . . , ĥK (x)] into a valid probability distribution, we use the softmax function. The final estimated probability for a specific virus k, denoted ĝk (x), is given by substituting the additive estimator directly into the softmax equation:

Here, the numerator is the exponentiated score for the virus class k, and the denominator is the sum of exponentiated scores across all possible virus classes j ∈ {1, . . . , K}. This transformation ensures that each probability ĝk (x) is between 0 and 1 and that their sum across all classes equals 1, forming a proper probability distribution. The complete estimator, ĝ(x), is the function that outputs this full vector of probabilities. This estimator was trained using the multi-class logarithmic loss (multi-logloss), which is suitable for multiclass classification as it works in conjunction with the softmax function to ensure the predicted values from ĝ(x) approximate these valid class-conditional probabilities [36].

The choice of a tree-based estimator is due to its effective partitioning of the covariate space. It allows detailed analysis across geographical and temporal dimensions and interaction with other relevant variables, eliminating the need for predefined groups and offering a more flexible and precise data analysis approach. For a theoretical justification on the use of geographic coordinates over indicator variables for spatial partitioning in tree-based models, refer to Section S2 of the Supplementary Material.

This method was also chosen for its practical advantages: it allows for fast deployment due to low computational cost at prediction time and has low memory requirements for storing the estimator. Furthermore, GBDT performs implicit variable selection during the boosting process. The estimator's structure also offers high interpretability; its tree-based nature supports global variable importance analysis, while its ensemble nature allows for the specific local prediction explanations (based on an internal embedding space) detailed in Section 2.3.3 and illustrated in Section 3.

2.3.2. Estimating virus distribution in age intervals

The estimator ĝ(x) provides estimates of the virus distribution for a patient with SARI given a specific age, location, and epidemiological week of symptom onset. However, epidemiological surveillance often requires estimates for age groups rather than for a single patient age, such as infants under 1 year of age or adults between 60 and 70 years old. To obtain estimates for an age interval [a, b], we proceed in two steps: first, we estimate the conditional distribution of patient age given the remaining covariates; second, we integrate the pointwise virus estimator ĝ(x) over this estimated age distribution. In our implementation, patient age is modeled conditionally using a lognormal distribution whose parameters are estimated through GBDT models.

The resulting estimator is where fX j (•|x -j ) and FX j (•|x -j ) denote the estimated conditional density and cumulative distribution functions of patient age, respectively.

This estimator represents the average predicted virus distribution over the age interval [a, b], weighted by the likelihood of observing each age within that interval conditional on the remaining covariates. In practice, the integral is evaluated numerically using a probability-weighted midpoint approximation. The complete derivation of the estimator, the GBDT-based estimation of the conditional age distribution, and the numerical integration procedure are provided in Section S4 of the Supplementary Material.

2.3.3. Explaining Individual Predictions via Embedding Space Analysis

The definition of a neighborhood is an important component of local interpretation methods, including how it accounts for the geometry of the feature domain [37]. For gradient-boosted trees, the fitted ensemble itself can provide a representation for defining local groups. For example, LoBoost uses sequences of visited leaves to construct groups for local conformal calibration [38]. Here, we define similarity through an embedding constructed from the weighted outputs of the fitted trees. We then identify neighboring training observations to provide an example-based interpretation of individual predictions, including predictions for hypothetical cases in data-scarce contexts.

The similarity between any two observations is defined by their representation within the Gradient Boosting Decision Tree (1). For a given observation x, its estimated embedding vector vx is constructed from the weighted outputs of each tree in the ensemble: where bm (x) is the prediction from the m-th tree and α m is its corresponding weight (learning rate).

The similarity between a new case and instances in the training data is then quantified by calculating the Euclidean distance between their respective embedding vectors. This technique allows us to identify the training observations that are likely most influential in the estimator's decision-making process for a specific prediction, thereby providing a clear, data-driven explanation for its output.

2.4. Summary of Proposed Estimators: The ViDAS Framework

Based on the statistical framework developed in the previous subsections, we formally define our proposed methodology, named ViDAS (Viral Distribution estimation in Acute respiratory infection Surveillance). It is implemented through three distinct variants: • NViDAS (Nonparametric ViDAS): As detailed in Section 2.3.1, this approach estimates the conditional probability of a patient testing positive for a specific virus, given the patient's individual information.

• ANViDAS (Aggregated Nonparametric ViDAS): To obtain an estimate of the virus proportion for a specific municipality in each epidemiological week, the NViDAS predictive model is applied to each observed case in the database. Then, the average of the estimated probabilities is calculated for each week. This procedure results in a weekly estimate of the virus proportion that considers the empirical age distribution observed in the data for that week and municipality.

• SViDAS (Semiparametric ViDAS): As described in Section 2.3.2, this approach is used to estimate the virus proportion for a specific age range in a given location and epidemiological week. It employs the estimator combination methodology, which estimates the average virus probability for the age range of interest by weighting the predictions of the virus probability estimator by the estimated age distribution (lognormal) for the context. The result is a weekly estimate that reflects the average proportion for an age group, based on an estimated age distribution.

In summary, both ANViDAS and SViDAS are extensions of the base NViDAS model. While NViDAS operates at the individual level, estimating the probability for a patient with a specific profile (age, location, and week), ANViDAS and SViDAS aggregate these predictions to provide proportion estimates for populations defined by a municipality, an age range, and an epidemiological week.

3. Results

This section presents the results of the evaluation of the proposed methodology for monitoring viral infections in patients with Severe Acute Respiratory Infection in Brazil. Four main objectives are addressed: 1.

To evaluate the performance of the methodology in estimating the distribution of respiratory viruses, comparing it with a standard approach and complete data (Consolidated Data). 2. To analyze the impact of reporting delays on the estimates. 3. To investigate the behavior of the methodology in data-scarcity scenarios. 4. To interpret the model, highlighting the importance of covariates and analyzing the influence of key predictor variables, particularly age and epidemiological week, on the predicted outcome.

The analyses were conducted using data released weekly between January 2022 and May 2025, referring to the years 2022, 2023, and 2024.

Based on the data released on May 5, 2025, SARS-CoV-2 was the predominant virus during the 2022-2024 period, accounting for approximately 44.7% of the total positive cases. This was followed by Respiratory Syncytial Virus (RSV), Rhinovirus, and Influenza A, with 22.1%, 13.9%, and 8.7% of detections, respectively. Together, these four viral agents accounted for almost 90% of the occurrences in the analyzed period. The complete distribution across all viral categories is provided in Supplementary Table S2.

For the comparative analysis, we evaluate the proposed ViDAS framework (NViDAS, ANViDAS, and SViDAS, as defined in Section 2.4) against a standard model, referred to here as the Naive Model. This standard model is based on the empirical proportion of each virus by epidemiological week and State. Using only these variables, its main limitation is the inability to incorporate the granularity of age or municipality, producing a single estimate for each state per week.

The following subsections detail the analyses and discussions for each of the proposed objectives.

3.1. Performance Evaluation of the Proposed Methodology

This subsection evaluates the performance of the proposed models in estimating age-stratified viral proportions. Given this emphasis, the evaluation focuses on ANViDAS and SViDAS, which generate estimates for age intervals, a functionality not supported by NViDAS. Performance was assessed through two complementary approaches: a graphical case study and a systematic quantitative evaluation of estimation errors. In both analyses, the proposed models were compared with a Naive Model using the Consolidated Data, derived from the most complete database release available (May 5, 2025), as reference.

The curves reveal distinct temporal and demographic patterns for SARS-CoV-2 and RSV. Their proportions frequently exhibit opposite temporal trends, largely reflecting the proportional structure of the outcome and the predominance of these two viruses during the analyzed period, rather than direct viral competition.

ANViDAS and SViDAS consistently track the Consolidated Data more closely than the Naive Model. SViDAS, by modeling age continuously, produces smoother trajectories, particularly in strata with fewer observations, whereas ANViDAS more directly reflects weekly fluctuations in the empirical age distribution. The visual analysis also highlights systematic agedependent errors in the Naive Model: it tends to overestimate RSV proportions in older individuals and underestimate them in young children, while the opposite pattern is observed for SARS-CoV-2. These results illustrate the importance of age stratification, with RSV predominating in the pediatric population and SARS-CoV-2 maintaining a higher proportion among older individuals during the analyzed period.

3.2. Analysis of the Effect of Delays on Estimates

We evaluated the impact of reporting delays on the performance of the estimates generated by the proposed methodology. To this end, we con- ducted an experiment simulating the operational use of NViDAS over time, mimicking a nowcasting setting in which predictions are initially generated from partial information and subsequently updated as data are consolidated.

The analysis was based on weekly data releases from January 2022 to May 2025, with a new NViDAS estimator trained for each release to reflect the information available at that specific point in time. Performance for each epidemiological week was evaluated as a function of the reporting lag, defined as the difference, in weeks, between the data release date and the epidemiological week for which the estimate was generated. A consistent pattern was observed across Brazil and its five macro-regions: estimation error decreased sharply during the first few weeks and subsequently tended to stabilize. This rapid improvement may partly reflect the increasing volume of available data, but may also be influenced by the dynamics of laboratory confirmation, whereby cases with higher clinical probability may be confirmed earlier and additional results are progressively incorporated into the surveillance system. As the data are consolidated, the estimates become increasingly precise.

For the main viruses, the estimation error tends to stabilize after a lag of approximately 3 to 5 weeks, although the stabilization point varies by virus and geographical region. From approximately the third week onward, the error is already close to the lowest levels observed after further data consolidation in most cases. These findings support the applicability of NViDAS for timely epidemiological surveillance. Detailed results on the delay-analysis design, stabilization criterion, error trajectories, and regional patterns are provided in Section S6 of the Supplementary Material.

3.3. Performance in Data-Scarcity Scenarios

This subsection evaluates the performance of the proposed methodology in a data-scarcity scenario, a common challenge in epidemiological surveillance. To simulate this condition, we used the weekly datasets released between January 2022 and May 2025, covering the 2022-2024 period. For each data release, the models were trained on a small random subsample comprising 10% of the data and evaluated on the remaining 90%. The performance of NViDAS was compared with that of the Naive Model, using the Brier Score to assess the quality of the probabilistic estimates.

The results are summarized in Figure 3. NViDAS consistently outperforms the Naive Model, particularly for the most incident viruses. The advantage is most pronounced for SARS-CoV-2 and RSV, for which NViDAS achieves lower Brier Scores and lower variability than the Naive Model. Conversely, for viruses with very low incidence, such as the Parainfluenza group (PARA1-4) and Bocavirus (BOCA), the difference between the two models is less pronounced, with both exhibiting very low error scores. Overall, this analysis supports the robustness of the proposed methodology under data-scarcity conditions. By leveraging relationships among covariates, NViDAS maintains superior predictive performance relative to the Naive Model even when trained on a limited amount of data, supporting its applicability to epidemiological surveillance in settings with sparse observations.

3.4. Interpretation and Explanation of the NViDAS Model

The global feature-importance analysis revealed a clear hierarchy among the predictor variables. Patient age emerged as the most influential predictor, followed by the epidemiological week of symptom onset. Geographical variables also exhibited a tiered importance according to their level of granularity, with city-level coordinates being more influential than state-and region-level coordinates. This pattern suggests that, in addition to demographic and temporal information, NViDAS captures relevant localized variation in viral transmission dynamics.

The analysis of the effects of age and epidemiological week further revealed distinct epidemiological patterns across the main respiratory viruses. For SARS-CoV-2, the model learned a strong positive relationship between age and predicted probability, with lower probabilities among infants and progressively higher probabilities among older individuals. In contrast, RSV exhibited a strong negative relationship with age, with the highest predicted probabilities among neonates and infants and a sharp decline during the first years of life. Rhinovirus showed a more complex, non-linear age pattern, with predicted probabilities peaking during childhood and gradually declining through adulthood. For Influenza A, epidemiological week had a particularly strong influence, characterized by pronounced seasonal peaks across age groups. Overall, these results indicate that the model learned not only the relevance of age and epidemiological week as individual predictors, but also virus-specific age and temporal patterns that reflect distinct epidemiological signatures.

We also investigated the interpretability of individual NViDAS predictions in settings where direct observations were unavailable. For this purpose, we applied the local interpretation method specifically proposed in this study and introduced in Section 2.3.3, which analyzes individual predictions through neighboring observations in the model's embedding space. This analysis showed that, in data-scarcity settings, NViDAS bases its predictions on training profiles that are similar to the target observation in demographic, temporal, and geographical characteristics. The resulting explanations indicate that the model can draw on different sets of relevant observations depending on the patient's age and epidemiological context, thereby supporting nuanced and age-appropriate predictions even when no direct observations are available for a given municipality.

Detailed results of the feature-importance analysis, the Partial Dependence Plot (PDP) and Individual Conditional Expectation (ICE) analyses, and the two case studies used to illustrate the proposed local interpretation method are provided in the Section S6 of the Supplementary Material.

4. Discussion

>Discussion

Interpretation of Main Findings

This study presented a computational framework for estimating the granular distribution of respiratory viruses among SARI patients in Brazil. Our results indicate that modeling viral proportions as conditional probabilities, using GBDT, consistently outperforms the empirical aggregation benchmark. Specifically, the proposed models (ANViDAS and SViDAS) achieved a reduction in estimation error (Brier Score) of approximately 40% for SARS-CoV-2 and 36% for RSV compared with the benchmark.

These findings directly address the research objective of efficiently monitoring viral distributions across municipalities and age groups. Patient age emerged as the most influential predictor of viral distribution, followed by the epidemiological week. The model also captured distinct non-linear agerelated patterns that are consistent with established epidemiological differences, including higher RSV proportions among infants and higher SARS-CoV-2 proportions among older SARI patients, with a sharp decline in predicted RSV probability during early childhood and a progressive increase in predicted SARS-CoV-2 probability with age.

Comparison with Related Work and State-of-the-Art

Our approach represents a methodological shift from aggregate-level surveillance to patient-level estimation. Unlike empirical aggregation over broader geographical and demographic groups, our method preserves local heterogeneity. By treating geolocation as continuous covariates (latitude and longitude), the model effectively learns data-driven neighborhoods without requiring pre-specified adjacency structures.

The ability of SViDAS to integrate viral probabilities over continuous lognormal age distributions constitutes a semi-parametric extension of the framework. This allows estimation in arbitrary age intervals (e.g., neonates vs. toddlers), offering a granularity that fixed-bin aggregation cannot match. Separately, to address the "black box" limitations often leveled at machine learning models, we introduced an interpretability strategy based on the estimator's internal embedding space. By identifying training instances associated with a prediction, this approach provides data-driven explanations for the model's outputs, including in data-scarce contexts.

Methodological Considerations and Robustness

The choice of a GBDT architecture was justified by its ability to capture complex interactions between space, time, and demographics without the need for extensive feature engineering or predefined grouping. The decision to model the proportion of positive cases rather than total incidence was a strategic response to the absence of negative test records for noninfluenza viruses in the SIVEP-Gripe database. Regarding reporting delays, this proportional formulation is expected to be less sensitive than absolute case counts when delays affect viral agents approximately uniformly, so that the relative composition of positive cases is not systematically distorted. The empirical delay analysis supports the practical applicability of this formulation: estimation error decreased sharply during the first few weeks and tended to stabilize after approximately 3 to 5 weeks for the main viruses, although the stabilization point varied by virus and geographical region.

The robustness of this approach is further supported by the data-scarcity analysis. Even when trained on only 10% of the available data, NViDAS maintained superior predictive performance relative to the Naive Model. In addition, the case study of Araporã showed that, even in the absence of local observations, the model could leverage the embedding space to identify similar training instances-geographically, temporally, and demographically related-to support predictions for the target profile. Together, these results support the applicability of the framework for timely surveillance in settings where data are still being consolidated or are locally sparse.

Age-Density Specification in SViDAS

The SViDAS estimator uses a lognormal working model for the conditional distribution of patient age, chosen to enable computationally efficient estimation of viral proportions over arbitrary age intervals. In the empirical evaluation, SViDAS achieved performance very close to that of ANViDAS, which aggregates predictions from the same virus-probability model using the empirical age distribution. This close agreement suggests that, for the settings and age intervals evaluated in this study, the lognormal specification provides an adequate practical approximation for the final estimates.

The conditional age distribution of SARI cases may nevertheless display multimodal patterns that vary across locations and epidemiological periods. A natural extension is to replace the lognormal working model with a flexible conditional density estimator, such as FlexCode [40], which estimates conditional densities through basis expansions whose coefficients are estimated by regression. This would allow the age distribution used by SViDAS to accommodate more complex shapes. Its impact on the accuracy of viral proportion estimates and on computational cost would require further evaluation.

Scope and Interpretation of the Estimates

To ensure the accurate application of this framework, it is essential to consider the context in which these viral proportions are estimated. First, the understanding of viral distribution requires caution due to its sensitivity to testing strategies and resource availability. Comparing distributions across different localities presupposes a uniformity in testing policies, assuming that individuals with similar clinical conditions are tested equivalently in all compared areas. Similarly, contrasting proportions over time within the same locality assumes that testing protocols have remained stable. This implies a stability in the testing ratio for different viruses during the analyzed period, without shifts in the prioritization or exclusion of specific viral targets. Consequently, differential testing protocols may bias the observed viral detections, particularly when specific viral targets are prioritized or omitted (e.g., if hospitals test for SARS-CoV-2 but not RSV). Therefore, the estimates should be interpreted as the relative distribution among detected positive cases under the prevailing testing practices.

Second, the proportion of different viruses among SARI cases should be interpreted as the etiological profile of severe respiratory infections, rather than as a direct measure of viral circulation in the general population. This distinction arises because respiratory viruses differ in their transmission dynamics and in their probability of progressing to severe disease. A less virulent pathogen may be more prevalent in the general population than among hospitalized SARI cases, whereas a pathogen associated with more severe outcomes may be overrepresented among SARI notifications. This severe-disease profile is precisely the intended target for hospital surveillance and resource planning, but it should not be interpreted as a direct proxy for general community circulation.

Computational Considerations

From an operational perspective, the computational efficiency was compatible with the intended deployment under limited resources. Using a standard notebook (Windows 11 Pro, AMD Ryzen 7 5700U, 16GB RAM), the models for the final epidemiological weeks of 2024-which comprised the largest dataset volume (2022-2024)-required less than two minutes to train. However, the retraining strategy requires access to the full historical dataset. As the SIVEP-Gripe database grows, the retraining time may eventually necessitate incremental learning strategies or more aggressive data subsampling.

Practical Implications and Future Directions

The practical utility of this framework is demonstrated by its implementation in the PySRAG package and a dedicated web application. For public health authorities, the ability to query viral proportions for specific ages (e.g., '1 year') supports targeted planning decisions, such as the timing of RSV prophylaxis or pediatric ICU allocation, which are often obscured in aggregate "all-ages" reports.

Future work should focus on integrating this viral proportion estimator with models for nowcasting total SARI case counts. By combining ViDAS estimates of viral proportions with nowcast estimates of SARI cases, it would be possible to obtain real-time estimates of virus-specific SARI burden. Additionally, incorporating meteorological data could further refine the spatialtemporal capture of seasonal respiratory viruses. Finally, extending the interpretability analysis using the embedding space could provide automated textual explanations for surveillance alerts, increasing trust and adoption among non-technical health officials.

Overall, the results indicate that ViDAS provides a complementary framework for timely and granular surveillance of the viral composition of SARI in Brazil. By combining spatial, temporal, and demographic information, the framework improves upon empirical aggregation, remains informative under reporting delays and local data scarcity, and provides mechanisms for interpreting individual predictions. These characteristics support its use for high-resolution surveillance of respiratory viruses among virus-positive SARI cases.
