Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Metacheck does not yet analyze automatically whether sentences which include non-significant p-values are correct, but we recommend manually checking the sentences below for possible misinterpreted non-significant p values.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. Abstract

Current motor unit assessment techniques overlook the mechanical output that underpins function and are limited: surface recordings are weighted to the superficial ~1cm and needle EMG to a ~2 mm radius. We investigated whether clinical muscle ultrasound can measure motor unit twitches directly.

Peripheral nerve stimulation targeted abductor digiti minimi (ADM), abductor pollicis brevis (APB) and tibialis anterior (TA). Stimulus intensity was set 10% above supramaximal and decreased in 0.01-0.05 mA steps until the Compound Muscle Action Potential (CMAP) was abolished. An 18 MHz transducer was placed transverse to the muscle, co-located with the active electrode, with video time-locked to each stimulus. Inter-frame motion was quantified using Farneback optical flow, and twitch/CMAP amplitudes compared to derive mechanical and electrical activation thresholds and a motor unit twitch index.

We obtained 66 recordings from 22 participants (11 female, 21-63 years); 49 met prespecified quality criteria. Threshold medians (CMAP versus ultrasound) were 6.84/5.82 mA in ADM, 5.28/4.84 mA in APB and 10.34/9.83 mA in TA. The ultrasound threshold was lower than the CMAP threshold in all three muscles (ADM +0.27 mA, p = 0.009; APB +0.08 mA, p = 0.001; TA +0.21 mA, p = 0.011). A motor unit twitch index was measurable in 32/49 recordings: 14.4 in ADM, 14.9 in APB and 18.6 in TA. Median contraction/half-relaxation times for ADM, APB & TA were 80/80, 88/95 and 79/103 ms respectively; trial-to-trial variability was 5.3/8.3 ms.

Clinical ultrasound can be used to directly measure clinically relevant twitch parameters such as twitch amplitude which recruits in parallel with CMAP and is not detectable with conventional clinical neurophysiology techniques. Mechanical activation is detected at slightly lower stimulus currents than the CMAP, and the twitch signal plateaus before the CMAP does; neither effect was confined to the larger muscle.

Introduction

Electromyography (EMG) remains the clinical gold standard for assessing motor unit (MU) properties; however, it is fundamentally limited to capturing electrical potentials rather than the resultant mechanical twitch of muscle tissue. Furthermore, EMG modalities face significant spatial constraints. Surface EMG (sEMG) is non-invasive but heavily biased toward the superficial 1 cm of tissue 1 . Conversely, needle EMG (nEMG) offers high spatial resolution but records from a highly restricted radius of approximately 2 mm 2 . Neither approach can directly assess the mechanical twitch of the muscle tissue itself, whose time course (contraction and relaxation) reflects motor unit type and metabolic properties of the muscle fibre population 3, 4.

To capture the mechanical output of motor units, traditional approaches measure limb or joint movement via force transducers or accelerometers. These methods provide an indirect assessment that is heavily confounded by the biomechanics of the joint and tendon 5, 6 . Variables such as tendon compliance and joint resistance introduce nonlinearities into the recorded response. Furthermore, these measurements can be skewed by non-neurological factors, including prior musculoskeletal injury 7 , or connective tissue disorders 8 , thereby dissociating the measured external force from the true internal muscle fascicle contraction. Mechanical measurement is not new to clinical neurophysiology. Jolly's 1895 demonstration of myasthenic fatigability was an observation of the fading mechanical contraction under repetitive stimulation 9 , and Desmedt showed that in Duchenne muscular dystrophy abnormalities in twitch properties, measured with a force transducer, preceded any recordable EMG abnormality in the same muscle 10 . Despite this, mechanical measurement did not enter routine practice. Force transducer recording requires rigid fixation and a purpose-built rig for each muscle, is limited to the few muscles acting on an accessible lever and reports only whole-muscle output; twitch measurement remains a research technique. Over the same period the electrical signal became standardised on commercial equipment and gained motor unit-level resolution through needle EMG and motor unit number estimation techniques. Diagnostic neurophysiology therefore retained Jolly's protocol but replaced his mechanical readout with an electrical one and the mechanical output of motor units is now essentially ignored in clinical practice.

Recently, muscle ultrasound has emerged as a direct, non-invasive technique for observing mechanical contractile properties in vivo. Examples include utilising high-frame-rate ultrasound in conjunction with spike-triggered averaging 11, 12 or fascicle length tracking during muscle stimulation 13 . While these ultrafast techniques provide excellent temporal resolution for isolating individual twitches, they require specialised, computationally heavy equipment that is currently restricted to research environments.

Standard diagnostic ultrasound is rapidly entering routine clinical neurophysiology practice, but these clinical systems operate at significantly lower frame rates (<150 Frames Per Second (FPS)). We have previously combined clinical ultrasound with nEMG to perform a targeted "Scanning EMG" transect of a single motor unit 14 ; however this technique assessed single motor units and did not attempt to measure the entire motor unit population. More recently, others have used ultrasound for measuring fascicular twitch properties 15 , however these have not been yet applied to measuring motor unit populations.

This study aimed to demonstrate that routine clinical ultrasound, when coupled with optical flow analysis, can determine motor unit mechanical activation thresholds and extract mechanical contractile properties.

Methods

Participants and ethics

Ethical approval was obtained from the Newcastle University Faculty of Medical Sciences Ethics Committee (Ref: 61737/2023). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to data collection. Healthy volunteers were recruited from a local participant database. Exclusion criteria were a history of diabetes, carpal tunnel syndrome, neurological disease, current pregnancy, or a cardiac pacemaker.

Instrumentation and data acquisition

Recordings were obtained from three muscles: abductor digiti minimi (ADM), abductor pollicis brevis (APB) and tibialis anterior (TA). Electrical stimulation was delivered as a 0.2 ms square-wave pulse using a Digitimer DS5 constant-current stimulator at 0.7 Hz (Digitimer, Welwyn Garden City, UK). Surface electromyography (EMG) was amplified (gain 1000) with a Digitimer D440 amplifier and sampled at 10 kHz. A CED Micro1401 acquisition unit (Cambridge Electronic Design, Cambridge, UK) served as sequencer for stimulation timing and recording.

Stimulating electrodes (Covidien ECG electrodes, Medtronic, MN) were applied over the relevant peripheral nerve after skin preparation. Surface EMG was recorded with the active electrode one-third along the muscle belly from the proximal end and the reference electrode over the distal tendon.

Ultrasound imaging used a TeleMed ArtUS device (Telemed, Vilnius, Lithuania) with an L18-7H30-A5 linear transducer (18 MHz, 30 mm field width). Imaging depth was set to 20(APB, ADM) or 30 (TA) mm to include the deepest extent of the muscle. The probe was positioned transverse to the muscle fibres, adjacent to the active surface EMG electrode, and held manually with minimal pressure, sufficient for acoustic contact without tissue compression (Figure 1A). For hand muscles, the ultrasound device was held manually by a trained operator, whilst for tibialis anterior a custom-built ball-and-socket strap attachment held the probe to the surface of the skin whilst maintaining position on the skin surface. Gain, focus and dynamic range were optimised for muscle visualisation and held constant within a given muscle.

Ultrasound capture was triggered by a transistor-transistor logic (TTL) pulse from the sequencer, after which frames were acquired at the maximum available rate. The device emitted a falling edge with each frame, recorded on a separate channel alongside sEMG and the stimulus pulse, giving frame-level temporal alignment between video and electrophysiology (Figure 1B). Ultrasound capture was triggered 200ms before electrical stimulation to derive a baseline measurement.

Stimulation protocol

Stimulus current was first increased until a supramaximal compound muscle action potential (CMAP) was obtained, then set 10% above that level. A decrementing series of stimuli was delivered in steps of 0.01-0.05 mA with a hold of 3 stimuli per level, the step size chosen to obtain at least 50 stimuli across the activation range while limiting participant discomfort.

Signal processing

Analysis used a custom pipeline in Python 3.13 (numpy 2.2.5, scipy 1.15.3, pandas 2.2.3, OpenCV 4.11.0). EMG was notch-filtered at 50 Hz and band-pass filtered 5-3000 Hz. CMAP amplitude was measured peak-to-baseline in a 2-20 ms post-stimulus window, with baseline taken 150-50 ms before the stimulus.

Video-EMG alignment was verified per recording by comparing the number of measured TTL pulses against the number of video frames; recordings with a mismatch of frames and ultrasound TTL pulses were rejected as having insufficiently certain alignment.

Motion quantification

Muscle motion between frames was quantified with the Farneback dense optical flow algorithm 16 (OpenCV 17 ). Flow was projected onto the dominant motion direction rather than taken as unsigned magnitude, preserving contraction-relaxation direction. The resulting trace was high-pass filtered (second-order Butterworth, 1.0 Hz) to remove slow drift arising from probe movement.

Ultrasound produces significant speckle noise, and optical flow measurements will detect these as constant movement. This produces a noise floor to motion quantification, which affects the reliable detection and quantification of true tissue motion. To overcome these limitations, twitch amplitude was measured as a signed spatiotemporal matched-filter projection of the tissue deformation field rather than as a peak of motion velocity.

A canonical spatial flow template was therefore constructed for each recording. Using this approach, spatially incoherent speckle projects to approximately zero mean with much reduced variance, whereas coherent muscle motion adds constructively; the resulting amplitude is signed. Per-trial baseline noise was quantified from the median absolute deviation of the pre-stimulus samples of the projection trace.

Template construction required at least 5 trials exceeding the noise threshold. Where this was not met, no template could be built, and recordings without a validated template were excluded. Chunks with a spatial coherence score below 0.1 were discarded before curve fitting, and trials whose pre-stimulus noise exceeded 5 times the cohort median were flagged as movement artifact.

Twitch timing

Motion onset for each twitch was defined as the point after stimulation at which velocity exceeded 10% of peak velocity. Detected peaks were averaged over ±2 frames.

Since the motion is signed, the first positive phase represents contraction, and the negative phase represents relaxation (Figure 2A & 2B). From this, two timing parameters were derived per trial: -Contraction time: onset of motion to the first return to zero, terminating the contraction phase.

-Half-relaxation time: from end of contraction to half-recovery toward baseline.

Per-trial values were aggregated within each recording using the median, with outliers removed at 1.5 x IQR.

Recruitment analysis

Primary: model-free thresholds

For each modality, the response was binned by stimulus current. The low-current reference level was taken as the median of the lowest 5% of stimulus bins and the plateau as the 95th percentile of the binned response. The activation threshold was defined as the current at which the response first reached 5% of this reference-to-plateau range.

Secondary: parametric (4PL) replication

As a parametric replication, a four-parameter logistic was fitted to each stimulus-response curve (CMAP and twitch) by non-linear least squares(SciPy curve_fit),: R(x) = bottom + (top -bottom) / (1 + (EC50 / x)^slope)

For each recording and modality, trials were grouped by delivered stimulus current and the median response at each current level was taken as the stimulus-response curve. The fourparameter logistic above was fitted to these medians with bottom and top initialised at the minimum and maximum observed response and constrained to lie within that range ± 20 % of its span. EC50 is the current at which the fitted response lies midway between bottom and top, i.e. the midpoint of the recruitment curve.

Motor unit twitch index

An index of the number of motor units producing muscle twitch was derived from the fitted mechanical recruitment curve. Four monotone models were fitted to every twitch curve; Hill (equivalent to the 4PL above), Gompertz, Weibull and probit. Slope parameters were bounded under a common ceiling on normalised maximum slope, so that differences between models are not artifacts of incomparable bounds. Two derivation rules were applied to each model, and all eight model × rule combinations were computed for every recording.

The primary estimate is Hill / Rule B: index = fitted top / single motor unit twitch (SMUT), where SMUT is the median increment in the sub-EC50 recruiting region exceeding twice the recording's own measured noise floor (Figure 2C). Bootstrap confidence intervals were computed by 200 stratified resamples. Recordings with fewer than five resolvable increments are reported as unmeasured. The alternative Rule A (top ÷ mean positive increment) is reported alongside.

This estimator has the form of incremental motor unit number estimation 18 : a maximal response divided by a representative single-unit increment sampled from the tail of a recruitment curve. Three properties of the ultrasound mechanical signal mean this should be read as an index rather than a count. Firstly, an increment is accepted only if it exceeds twice the recording's noise floor, so the smallest twitches do not contribute to the SMUT estimate, and accepted increments may represent more than one unit. Second, as in all incremental methods, near-threshold axons fire probabilistically, so the increment at a given current may comprise different units from trial to trial (alternation). Third, twitch displacement summates non-linearly, whereas CMAP amplitude summates approximately linearly. Values are therefore used for within-cohort comparison and ranking only and are reported with bootstrap intervals and model-to-model spread.

Quality control and exclusions

1. Curve stability: Recordings were rejected if the EC50 coefficient of variation (EC50 standard error ÷ |EC50|) reached 0.35, or if EC50 exceeded the maximum delivered current.

2. Validated amplitude template: Recordings without a spatial template (fewer than 5 trials exceeding a coherence score of 0.70) were excluded.

3. Stimulus-locked motion: recordings whose mean supramaximal motion trace showed no stimulus-locked peak (peak contrast below 6 SD of the pre-stimulus trace and post-/pre-stimulus motion ratio below 1) were excluded.

Statistical analysis

Between-modality comparisons within a muscle were paired within recording and tested with the Wilcoxon signed-rank test, since the distributions are skewed. P-values were adjusted with the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure within the family of six primary within-muscle tests (threshold and plateau in three muscles). Exact p-values are reported throughout. Analysis was performed in Python 3.13 with scipy 1.15.3.

Results

Results 66 recordings were obtained from 22 participants (n=11 female).

Cohort

The mean age of participants was 28, SD 9, range 21-63.17 recordings did not meet the prespecified qualitycontrol criteria: 13 because fewer than five trials reached the spatial coherence threshold required for template construction, 2 for curve-fit instability and 2 for absence of stimuluslocked motion. 49 recordings from 20 participants were analysed (ADM n = 17, APB n = 18, TA n = 14); 20 of 22 participants contributed at least one valid recording.

Video was acquired at 127 +/-15 frames per second (mean +/-SD), corresponding to a temporal resolution of 8.0 +/-1.3 ms. Recordings contained a median of 105 stimuli (IQR 95-128) spanning a median 3.7-11.4 mA, delivered at a median of 77 distinct current levels (IQR 68-88) when recorded currents are grouped at a 0.02 mA tolerance.

Activation thresholds

Examples of mechanical and electrical activation thresholds are shown per recording in Figure 3.

Threshold median values (CMAP/ultrasound) were 6.84/5.82 mA in ADM, 5.28/4.84 mA in APB, and 10.34/9.83 mA in TA. The within-recording difference (CMAP minus ultrasound; positive indicates a lower ultrasound threshold) was +0.27 mA in ADM (p = 0.009), +0.08 mA in APB (p = 0.001) and +0.21 mA in TA (p = 0.011). Adjusted across the six primary withinmuscle tests, all three differences remained significant (ADM p = 0.013, APB p = 0.003, TA p = 0.013).

Expressed as a proportion of the motor unit recruitment range, the difference was 7.6% in ADM (p = 0.008), 2.9% in APB (p = 0.0008) and 5.1% in TA (p = 0.017).

Because the model-free analysis involves no curve fitting, it can also be computed for the recordings excluded for curve-fit instability. On that larger set of 51 recordings the same pattern holds: ADM +0.24 mA (n = 18, p = 0.048), APB +0.08 mA (n = 18, p = 0.001), TA +0.21 mA (n = 13, p = 0.011).

Recruitment curve

The EC50 difference (CMAP minus ultrasound) was +1.07 mA in ADM (p = 0.002), +0.46 mA in APB (p = 0.003) and +0.90 mA in TA (p = 1.2 × 10⁻⁴). The per-recording EC50 difference correlated with the model-free threshold difference (Spearman rho = 0.71, p = 1.3 × 10⁻⁸, n = 49).

The plateau (the current at which the response reached 95% of its range) median values (CMAP/Ultrasound) were 9.80/7.78 mA in ADM, 7.81/7.47 mA in APB, and 15.86/13.65 mA in TA. The within-recording difference (CMAP minus ultrasound) was +0.99 mA in ADM (p = 0.020), +0.81 mA in APB (p = 0.008) and +1.21 mA in TA (p = 2.4 × 10⁻⁴). Adjusted across the same six tests, all three differences remained significant (ADM p = 0.020, APB p = 0.013, TA p = 0.0015).

Motor unit twitch index

A motor unit twitch index was measurable in 32 of the 49 accepted recordings; the remaining 17 (11 participants) resolved fewer than five amplitude increments above their own noise floor. Values are shown per recording in Figure 4A.

The index was 14.4 in ADM (n = 12, IQR 6.9-17.6), 14.9 in APB (n = 7, IQR 10.4-15.0) and 18.6 in TA (n = 13, IQR 14.7-27.7).

TA had the highest median index under every alternative estimator examined (Hill/Gompertz/Weibull/probit × Rule A/B). However, the index correlated with the twitch signal-to-noise ratio across recordings (Spearman rho = 0.54, p = 0.001), which was highest in TA, so the between-muscle difference cannot be separated from a difference in signal quality. Full comparison of different estimators is shown in Supplementary Table 1.

Precision of the index at the level of a single recording is limited. The bootstrap interval (trials resampled within stimulus level) spanned a median of 40% of the point estimate (IQR 22-51%).

Twitch timing

Contraction time and half-relaxation time are shown in Figure 4B. Median contraction time was 80 ms in ADM (IQR 73-95), 88 ms in APB (IQR 87-93) and 79 ms in TA (IQR 60-105). Median half-relaxation time was 80 ms in ADM (IQR 66-95), 95 ms in APB (IQR 88-103) and 103 ms in TA (IQR 77-129).

Timing was measured in a median of 36 trials per recording (IQR 21-48); within-recording trial-to-trial variability was 5.3 ms for contraction time and 8.3 ms for half-relaxation time.

Discussion

We have shown that the twitch parameters of muscle can be measured directly using clinical ultrasound, and that twitch magnitudes recruit in parallel with CMAP amplitude.

The mechanical recruitment threshold was lower than the electrical threshold by a small margin (about 0.2 mA, or 3-8% of the electrical recruitment range) in all three muscles. A likely contributor is the differing fields of view of the two modalities. sEMG is spatially biased toward superficial motor units, with signal amplitude attenuating rapidly with depth. In contrast, the ultrasound field of view penetrates the full muscle depth, allowing for the detection of deep motor unit contractions that may be electrically silent or attenuated on the surface. Optical flow may therefore register the first recruited units, wherever they lie in the muscle cross-section, before a measurable surface potential appears. Furthermore, muscle twitch that occurs proximal or distal to the sampled region of muscle will still produce indirect contraction of the sampled tissue. Motor units have a limited longitudinal spatial extent 19 so it is likely that some of the dissociation arises from longitudinal distance, and not just surface depth.

Conversely, the earlier plateau of the twitch signal compared to the CMAP, seen in all three muscles, likely reflects the physiological limits of mechanical temporal summation or the frame-rate constraints of the imaging system. Whilst electrical potentials can continue to summate linearly with additional recruitment of consecutive motor units, the mechanical twitch is limited by tissue compliance and the fusion of discrete motion events, which may result in an apparent saturation of the motion signal despite continued electrical recruitment.

The motor unit twitch index is the mechanical analogue of incremental MUNE and should not be read as an absolute motor unit count. Values of 14-19 are an order of magnitude below electrophysiological estimates for the same muscles (typically 100-250 by MUNIX 20 or MScanFit 21 ) and the shortfall is informative about what is being measured. The denominator is positively biased; only increments exceeding twice the noise floor are accepted, so the smallest twitches are excluded and the accepted increments are more likely to be composite of multiple units. Consistent with this, the index tracked signal-to-noise ratio across recordings, and the muscle with the cleanest signal returned the highest values. The numerator is biased downward by the nonlinear summation of twitch displacement which produces the earlier mechanical plateau.

The index is therefore best understood as a dynamic-range measure of mechanical recruitment, bounded by signal-to-noise ratio, and between-muscle differences cannot yet be attributed to differences in unit number. Approaching a true count would require a lower noise floor, correction for mechanical summation, or model-based fitting of the twitch recruitment scan.

It should be noted that whilst twitch time is derived from the point of zero-crossing of the twitch velocity, which therefore corresponds to maximal displacement as in traditional in vitro measures of twitch time, the half-relaxation time is measured from relaxation velocity, which may not be directly equivalent. With that said, the calculated twitch (79-88ms) and relaxation (80-103ms) times are similar to values produced in prior work measuring with force transducers directly on the muscle belly 22 , motor unit MRI of tibialis anterior 23 , and highframe rate ultrasound 24.

Motion was quantified with dense rather than sparse optical flow. Sparse methods track a set of salient (bright) features selected by a corner detector; in B-mode muscle images these lie preferentially on fascial planes and the bone surface, whose motion differs from that of the fascicles. These features may move in or out of plane of the ultrasound with deformation, so that tracked points drop out or jump. The number and location of surviving features then varies with gain, depth, echogenicity and muscle. Dense flow estimates displacement over the whole imaged cross-section, so the measurement domain is fixed by the field of view rather than by feature detection. It also makes our noise-suppression strategy possible: the spatial template and coherence score rely on genuine muscle motion being spatially coherent across the whole field while speckle-induced flow is not.

Several limitations must be acknowledged. The studied population was young and does not reflect muscle activity across the age spectrum. The analysis relies on 2D cross-sectional tracking, meaning out-of-plane dynamics such as longitudinal shortening, may be underestimated. Additionally, whilst the ~127 FPS capture rate reflects standard clinical capabilities, it falls short of high-speed research sonography (>2000 FPS), limiting the precise resolution of rapid contractile properties. Furthermore, despite the advantages of dense flow tracking, it is also known to perform worst at abrupt discontinuities of motion and in textureless regions 25 , both of which may occur in extremes of muscle movement or disease states.

There are specific limitations relating to the motor unit number index measurement proposed. We could only produce a measurable index in 32/66 recordings overall due to the strict criteria we imposed for signal quality in order to overcome the further limitation that this value was dependent upon the signal to noise ratio seen.

Many clinical neurophysiology labs now have ultrasound devices in their department for assessing the structural properties of nerve & muscle, and newer EMG devices have integrated, or adjacent, neuromuscular ultrasound capabilities. Whilst this study has used research neurophysiology hardware to implement a custom utility with strict temporal alignment between ultrasound frames and electrical activation, a similar programme could be easily implemented with clinical devices at known frame rates, negating the need for temporal alignment.

Future studies must validate the performance of optical flow tracking in disease states characterised by altered muscle echogenicity, such as fibrosis or fatty infiltration seen in myopathic or neurogenic disease. In myopathy, the compound twitch parameters measured here were shown half a century ago, using force transducers on intrinsic hand muscles, to change in muscle disease: in Duchenne dystrophy the contraction disorder preceded any EMG abnormality in the same muscle, with prolonged half-relaxation 10, 26 , whereas in myotonic dystrophy the contraction time was shortened 27 . Those methods required limb fixation and a transducer, and were never adopted clinically; the present method recovers the same parameters from the muscle belly with equipment already commonplace in neurophysiology departments. In neurogenic disease, unit number falls while the twitch force of surviving units changes with disease stage 28, 29 , so both the index and the single motor unit twitch from which it is derived are expected to change.

Clinical ultrasound thus provides a non-invasive measure of the mechanical output of the motor unit population across the depth of the muscle, including its recruitment, twitch kinetics and departure from the electrical response. In doing so it opens the contractile properties of individual muscles to routine, repeatable assessment in health and disease.
