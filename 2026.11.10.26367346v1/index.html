Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 225 papers containing 405 instances of non-exact p-values, the module correctly detected 269 cases (true positives) and incorrectly identified 78 (false positives). It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. Abstract

Non-ventilator-associated hospital-acquired pneumonia (nv-HAP) diagnosis is imprecise; chest x-rays (CXR) lack detail and sputum culture delays targeted prescribing. However, the evidence base to support diagnostic improvement is sparse. We assessed the feasibility of a trial of low-dose thoracic CT and rapid molecular testing of sputum.

HAP-FAST was a UK NIHR funded, open-label pilot sequential multiple assignment randomised trial with qualitative and costing substudies in four UK hospitals (ClinicalTrials.gov:NCT05483309). Using research without prior consent (RWPC) and sitestratified web-based randomisation, clinicians allocated adults with suspected nv-HAP (excluding pregnancy and palliation), before their second antibiotic dose, 1:1 to CT or CXR; participants subsequently treated for nv-HAP with sputum available underwent another 1:1 randomisation to BIOFIRE FILMARRAY Pneumonia plus Panel (FAPP) with standard microbiology or standard microbiology alone . Feasibility outcomes included recruitment, consent, intervention delivery and data completeness through 90 days. Adverse events were not systematically collected. Analyses were intention-to-treat and descriptive.

Between June 2023 and March 2024, 220 adults were randomised at stage-1 (109 CT; 111 CXR), reaching target three months early. Of 154/220 (70.0%) subsequently managed as nv-HAP, 92/154 (59.7%) could not produce sputum. 62/154 (40.3%; 28.2% of stage-1) were randomised to stage-2: 31 FAPP and 31 no-FAPP. Allocated imaging was recorded for 75/109 (68.8%) CT and 99/111 (89.2%) CXR participants; 6/31(19.4%) FAPP tests were not completed. Consent for follow-up was obtained for 142/220 (64.5%). Protocol-defined clinical cure was assessable in 1/220 participants. The day-90 resource-use questionnaire was returned by 55/110 participants (50.0%). Qualitative findings supported RWPC but identified needs for staged communication, lower-burden follow-up and improved diagnostic pathways. Excess bed days and readmissions drove costs but data capture requires refinement.

A definitive trial is plausible only with enhanced RWPC, diagnostic delivery and follow-up pathways, improved sputum procedures, stewardship-aligned outcomes and routine economic data capture.

Evidence to guide diagnostic strategies for suspected nv-HAP is limited, and the feasibility of evaluating sequential imaging and rapid sputum testing is uncertain.

HAP-FAST showed that recruitment to a sequential diagnostic trial was achievable, but identified important limitations in intervention delivery, follow-up and outcome assessment.

Integrated qualitative and costing findings explained priorities for redesign.

A definitive trial should incorporate staged consent, reliable diagnostic pathways, lowerburden follow-up, feasible clinical and stewardship outcomes, and improved resource-use measurement.

Introduction

Non-ventilator-associated hospital-acquired pneumonia (nv-HAP) is the most common healthcare-associated infection and causes substantial mortality, prolongs admission and increases costs. 1, 2 Guidelines recommend broad-spectrum empirical antibiotics. 3 Stewardship depends on correctly identifying nv-HAP as the cause of the patient's deterioration and identifying pathogens early enough to guide treatment. However, chest x-rays (CXR) are imprecise, while sputum culture is slow and insensitive. 4, 5 The 2025 National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) pneumonia guideline identified few microbial diagnostic studies and no eligible radiological diagnostic-strategy studies in nv-HAP; a systematic review found no randomised diagnostic trials. 3, 6, 7 Low-dose computed tomography (CT) could reduce overdiagnosis and identify alternative causes of deterioration, while rapid molecular sputum testing could support earlier targeted antimicrobial prescribing. 7, 8 Their evaluation requires a trial pathway compatible with urgent imaging and antibiotics. Evidence supporting these technologies comes largely from community-acquired pneumonia or critical-care populations, whose diagnostic pathways and case mix differ from ward-based nv-HAP. Establishing whether clinicians can recruit, deliver the tests and collect interpretable outcomes is therefore necessary before evaluating effectiveness.

Suspected nv-HAP arises across wards, often out of hours, in patients who may be older, frail or cognitively impaired. 1, 5, [9] [10] [11] These circumstances constrain recruitment and intervention delivery. Research without prior consent (RWPC) offers a potential solution, but its implementation and acceptability in ward-based nv-HAP require evaluation. 12 Outcome selection is also difficult: mortality, length of stay, antibiotic exposure, symptoms and quality of life are relevant, but attribution to nv-HAP is complicated by comorbidity. [13] [14] [15] HAP-FAST assessed the feasibility of a definitive diagnostic trial. 16 Embedded qualitative and costing substudies examined acceptability, barriers to trial delivery, diagnostic decisionmaking and economic data capture, to explain quantitative feasibility findings and inform future design. 17, 18

Methods

Patient and public involvement

Patient and public representatives reviewed drafts of the original grant proposal and influenced the final study design. They contributed during protocol development by reviewing the protocol and study documents and suggesting changes. They served on trial oversight committees and contributed to interpretation of findings and manuscript review. A public contributor reviewed the qualitative topic guides before data collection. In addition, patients and their relatives and friends who had direct lived experience of participation in the trial participated in qualitative interviews. Patients and public representatives reviewed and commented upon this manuscript and are named authors.

Study design and setting

HAP-FAST was an open-label pilot sequential multiple assignment randomised trial (SMART), coordinated by Liverpool Clinical Trials Centre (LCTC), in four acute hospitals in north-west England. The protocol was published previously. 16 The target of 220 participants over 12 months was based on prospective audits at two centres, assuming 30% eligibility and 40% recruitment. 16 The planned recruitment period allowed assessment across seasons and different hospital contexts.

Two sequential decisions were evaluated: stage-1 imaging (CXR versus low-dose thoracic CT) and stage-2 microbiological investigation with or without the BIOFIRE FILMARRAY Pneumonia plus Panel (FAPP). These defined four dynamic treatment regimens: CXR/no-FAPP, CXR/FAPP, CT/no-FAPP and CT/FAPP. Participants not entering stage-2 remained in follow-up and stage-1 analyses. Stage-2 randomisation followed the clinician's decision to treat HAP, preserving the sequence of diagnostic decisions rather than assigning all participants to both interventions irrespective of clinical need.

Participants and consent

Adults with suspected nv-HAP were eligible if initial trial procedures could be completed before their second antibiotic dose. In keeping with the definition of nv-HAP used by the UK NICE guidelines, the study included pneumonia developing at least 48 hours after admission or presentation after discharge from an overnight hospital stay within the preceding 10 days.

Exclusions were prior CXR for suspected HAP, ventilator-associated pneumonia, inability to undergo CT, pregnancy, no intention to deliver antibiotics, or previous participation. Stage-2 eligibility required an intention to treat HAP and sputum availability before the second antibiotic dose. Baseline sex was self-reported (male/female). RWPC permitted randomisation and interventions without delaying treatment. 12 Written informed consent for continued participation was sought as soon as practicable; personal or professional consultees were involved when participants lacked capacity.

Randomisation and interventions

An independent statistician generated site-stratified allocation schedules with variable block sizes, held centrally by LCTC and concealed until web-based allocation. Clinicians could confirm eligibility and randomise at any time. Stage-1 allocation was 1:1 to CXR or CT; stage-2 allocation was 1:1 to FAPP plus standard microbiology or standard microbiology alone. Allocations were immediately revealed and notified to clinicians because results informed care. After stage-1 imaging, managing clinicians interpreted CXRs and radiologists reported CTs urgently. For stage-2, instruments were placed in clinical areas and operated by clinicians; three sites used the FILMARRAY TORCH and one site used the FILMARRAY 2.0. FAPP results were interpreted using an antibiotic guideline approved by all sites.

Participants in the no-FAPP arm received empirical antibiotics according to local guidelines.

After consent, CAP-Sym symptom and EQ-5D-5L quality-of-life questionnaires were collected where feasible. 19, 20 Baseline clinical data included medical history and medication use. Daily reviews assessed clinical cure, with subsequent follow-up capturing symptoms, quality of life and further resource use. Participants were reviewed daily until day 10 or discharge, at day 28 in person and day 90 by telephone. Mechanistic research sampling is reported separately.

Qualitative substudy

We purposively sampled patients, personal consultees and practitioners across sites, roles and participant characteristics. Patients and consultees were contacted at least two weeks after discharge for semi-structured interviews at home or by telephone. Practitioners were recruited through site investigators for face-to-face or online focus groups, supplemented by interviews to include under-represented roles or sites. A non-clinical qualitative researcher conducted all interviews and focus groups after separate informed consent. A public contributor reviewed topic guides before data collection; guides were iteratively refined during analysis.

Verbatim transcripts were checked against recordings and pseudonymised. Collaborative reflexive thematic analysis proceeded alongside data collection. 21 The qualitative researcher led coding in NVivo whilst a clinician-trialist separately reviewed all transcripts. Patient, consultee and practitioner datasets were initially analysed separately, then compared across groups. Analytic discussions challenged and refined interpretations rather than establishing coding agreement. Findings are presented alongside quantitative process measures to explain feasibility constraints and identify design implications.

Outcomes and analysis

The primary objective was feasibility, including recruitment, consent, representativeness, retention to day 90, intervention delivery, outcome completeness, acceptability, decisionmaking and economic data capture. Several potential outcome measures for a future fully powered trial were considered (hereafter referred to as 'candidate outcomes'): clinical cure, antibiotic defined daily doses (DDD), EQ-5D-5L, length of stay and mortality at days 14, 28 and 90. The prespecified clinical cure outcome, developed from Weiss et al., required at least 35% improvement in symptoms measured by CAP-Sym, clinical stability using modified Halm's criteria, and radiological stability or improvement at days 7-10. 14, 20, 22 Additional imaging findings, FAPP pathogens and UK-AWaRe antibiotic categories were described. 23 Feasibility assessment also considered descriptive estimates and dispersion of candidate outcomes, clinician willingness to randomise, human factors affecting test delivery and factors influencing follow-up. Formal progression thresholds were not prespecified; given the novelty and complexity of HAP-FAST, feasibility was assessed holistically rather than by applying retrospective numerical thresholds. Adverse events were not systematically collected, as prespecified in the protocol.

Analyses followed the prespecified statistical analysis plan (supplementary file) after 90-day follow-up, without interim analyses. Stage-specific comparisons were by intention-to-treat regardless of deviations and were descriptive, without hypothesis testing. Missingness was reported. Real-time eligibility confirmation replaced conventional screening logs, so population denominators for eligibility, approach and recruitment were unavailable.

Representativeness was assessed against site-coded nv-HAP episodes in the routinely collected NHS Secondary Uses Service (SUS) database and the clinically validated Advancing Quality (AQ) sample, recognising possible ICD-10 miscoding. [24] [25] [26] Analyses used SAS 9.4 and R 4.4.1.

Costing analysis

This examined whether inpatient and post-discharge resource use could be captured sufficiently for a future cost-effectiveness evaluation. Costing adopted an NHS and personal social services perspective. 27 Available inpatient data from two sites were costed using Healthcare Resource Group tariffs and excess bed-day prices based on the NHS Schedule of costs and local hospital finance data. A day-90 questionnaire captured primary care, readmissions, outpatient attendance, investigations, community and domiciliary care, and new medications. Costs covered observed follow-up, truncated at death and capped at 90 days; a 90-day window was assumed where dates were unavailable. Time-dependent items were scaled to observed duration. Readmissions used excess bed-day proxies; other contacts and medications used standard national unit costs with reference to the British National Formulary and NHS drug prices with ranges retained for sensitivity analyses. Return and item completeness were summarised, with available-case post-discharge totals. 18

The fourth qualitative theme concerned pathway fit. CT delivery was constrained by transfer logistics and tolerance of lying flat, rather than rejection of the trial concept or lack of equipoise. FAPP delivery required successful sputum collection, sample handling, access to equipment, testing ownership and communication of results to clinical teams. These accounts explain how rapid tests can remain operationally fragile despite short analytical turnaround and identify modifiable steps between allocation and treatment decisions. Staff valued the prospect of pathogen-directed treatment, and patients and consultees supported appropriate antibiotic use. However, practitioners described the work involved in obtaining, analysing, transferring and interpreting the results, suggesting that influence on prescribing depended on information being early, trusted and linked to clinical review.

Candidate outcomes

Table 3 summarises candidate trial outcome measures. Protocol-defined clinical cure was assessable in only 1/220 participants; 219/220 (99.5%) lacked assessment, mainly because repeat imaging was unavailable at days 7-10. A post-hoc definition omitting radiology yielded cure in 15/109 (13.8%) CT and 12/111 (10.8%) CXR participants, and 9/31 (29.0%) FAPP and 7/31 (22.6%) no-FAPP participants. Costing feasibility

Inpatient costs were available for 101/151 participants at two sites; post-discharge NHS/personal social services data were available for 55/110 expected day-90 questionnaires (table 4). Available-case mean inpatient costs per participant were £9,187 with CT and £9,806 with CXR. In the smaller stage-2 subset, mean inpatient costs were higher without FAPP (£9,127 versus £7,261 with FAPP), largely reflecting excess bed days. Readmissions and community services accounted for 80.0% and 11.4% of aggregate recorded postdischarge costs, respectively.

Discussion

HAP-FAST demonstrated successful recruitment to a sequential diagnostic trial in suspected nv-HAP but identified why a definitive trial should not simply enlarge this protocol. When managing suspected nv-HAP imaging and microbiology address linked decisions: whether nv-HAP explains a patient's deterioration and which antibiotic should be chosen to target the responsible pathogen. The SMART design followed this sequence, while embedded qualitative and costing evidence explained implementation constraints that recruitment totals alone could obscure. 17, 18 The central finding is therefore conditional feasibility, not evidence of clinical or economic effectiveness.

The recruitment target was reached early. Radiological tests performed at stage-1 successfully excluded a diagnosis of nv-HAP in 30% of participants thus after further excluding those who could not produce sputum, 28% of participants randomized at stage-1 entered stage-2. This proportion is essential for sample-size planning but limits generalisability of microbiological comparisons to the 40% of sputum-producing patients with radiologically confirmed nv-HAP. The qualitative finding of conditional acceptance helps distinguish willingness to enter an urgent diagnostic pathway from willingness to undertake repeated research procedures. Blood sampling and in-person follow-up discouraged continued participation; future designs should minimise non-essential sampling and use remote follow-up where feasible.

RWPC and clinician-led randomisation accommodated urgent treatment, ward dispersion and cognitive impairment without requiring specialist research staff on every ward continuously.

Interviews supported RWPC but identified the need for staged explanations, later recap and consultee support. 28 Acceptability should therefore be treated as an ongoing process rather than inferred from recruitment alone. The limited qualitative representation of people who did not consent further cautions against assuming universal acceptance. A definitive trial should distinguish agreement to routine-data follow-up from agreement to additional questionnaires, visits or samples wherever its consent arrangements permit.

Imaging findings illustrate the importance of separating delivery from documentation.

Recorded CT receipt was incomplete, with missing forms contributing substantially to apparent non-delivery. Although recorded scans and reports were generally rapid, practitioners described scanner prioritisation, transfer logistics and tolerance as constraints.

Future site-readiness assessments need explicit radiology agreements covering transfer, reporting and incidental findings, together with reliable delivery recording and allowance for crossover or non-receipt in design and analysis. The longer formal CXR reporting interval should not be equated with delayed interpretation, because managing clinicians interpreted CXR directly.

FAPP implementation was more fragile. Locating instruments near patients and training clinicians did not remove dependencies on sputum production, sample transfer, test ownership, result communication and antibiotic decision making. Qualitative accounts related recruitment decisions, sample losses and test non-completion to these practical steps. Future trials should implement systems for remote results access and antibiotic decision support, linking timely results to explicit stewardship review. Improved analytical speed alone does not ensure that diagnostic information reaches decision-makers or changes treatment.

Outcome measurement was a major feasibility result. The prespecified clinical-cure composite was effectively unusable because in clinical practice repeat imaging is rarely performed within days 7-10. Removing radiology post-hoc increased the proportion assessable but did not completely resolve missingness. Symptom data collected during days 0-10 may help characterise recovery and inform future assessment timepoints and thresholds, but this would require further evaluation rather than treating the exploratory definition as a validated endpoint. CAP-derived symptom and physiological criteria may not transfer readily to this population. 14, 15, 20, 22 Cognitive impairment complicates self-report, while participants with an alternative diagnosis to pneumonia still require appropriate stage-1 outcomes. A future endpoint should therefore be diagnosis-agnostic, objective, routinely collected and suitable for an open-label trial; NEWS2 warrants evaluation.

Antibiotic stewardship outcomes may better match the mechanism of diagnostic intervention, but DDD alone does not distinguish appropriate escalation from avoidable exposure. The higher DDDs and 'Reserve' class antibiotic use seen with FAPP cannot establish benefit or harm: rapid microbiology results may prompt justified escalation as well as de-escalation.

Related studies show these context-dependent prescribing effects and uncertainty around clinical endpoints. 9, 29 A stewardship-focused outcome paired with safety or clinical response deserves evaluation. 30 Outcome selection should precede definitive sample-size calculation, rather than relying on unstable effect estimates from this pilot.

Length of stay and quality-of-life measures provided planning information, but missingness, censoring and small stage-2 groups limit interpretation. Similar demographics in our comparator cohorts are reassuring, not proof of representativeness since HAP-FAST recruited when nv-HAP was suspected, whereas SUS and AQ capture coded and confirmed nv-HAP respectively. Apparent differences in outcomes across these sources may therefore reflect case definition as well as recruitment. Without screening denominators, recruitment may still depend on recognition timing, CT tolerance, clinician availability and sputum production.

Mortality differences observed in these small, incompletely followed groups do not support effectiveness conclusions but are valuable for future trial planning.

The 50% post-discharge costing questionnaire return and available-case costing create potential selection biases, beyond item-level missingness so data inform future measurement rather than prove cost-effectiveness. Assistance with completion or routine-data extraction should be tested for acceptability and completeness, not assumed to solve missingness automatically.

The CAP-NEXT community-acquired pneumonia study also encountered incomplete delivery of low-dose CT and molecular testing, suggesting that diagnostic information must be integrated with workflow and prescribing decisions. 31 That study stopped early during

COVID-19 and did not collect reasons for non-adherence. HAP-FAST's qualitative component addresses this explanatory gap for a different pneumonia population, linking observed failures to practical delivery conditions and participant experience. HAP-FAST adds nv-HAP-specific evidence by connecting quantitative delivery and retention measures with patient, consultee and practitioner accounts. Strengths include this integration, pragmatic sequential randomisation and evaluation of RWPC in a pneumonia trial outside critical care. Limitations include incomplete interventions and outcomes, available-case costing, absent screening logs and limited qualitative representation from non-consenters. Progression is therefore conditional on redesign: enhanced RWPC, protected diagnostic pathways, enhanced sputum procedures, lower-burden follow-up, diagnosis-agnostic and stewardship-aligned outcomes, and robust economic data capture. A definitive trial should consider an internal pilot with prespecified stop-go criteria for recruitment, intervention delivery, outcome completeness and costing. Qualitative evaluation should remain integrated to explain whether refinements work, for whom and under which delivery conditions, informing implementation in clinical practice. Qualitative data comprised 23 patient-linked interviews with 24 participants (20 patients and four personal consultees), including one joint patient-consultee interview. Twenty practitioners participated in four site-based focus groups and four individual interviews. Four themes contextualised trial delivery; illustrative quotations are in table 2. The first theme was conditional acceptance of recruitment. Patients and consultees supported participation perceived as low-burden, clinically sensible and optional, but decisions during acute illness were often trust-based, altruistic or instinctive rather than demonstrating detailed understanding. The second concerned RWPC: this was broadly considered necessary, but acceptability depended on staged communication, later recap, clearer consultee support and separation of research from routine care. These findings contextualise consent rates without equating initial recruitment with informed willingness to continue. Practitioner accounts distinguished objections to later study demands from objections to RWPC itself. Patients and relatives described the need to revisit information because explanations given during acute illness might not be retained. Thus, a successful first approach did not remove the need for subsequent support and clarification.
