Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Metacheck does not yet analyze automatically whether sentences which include non-significant p-values are correct, but we recommend manually checking the sentences below for possible misinterpreted non-significant p values.

Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). Statcheck might still miss one-sided tests that are described in another sentence, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Most breast cancer (BC) risk loci identified by genome-wide association studies lie in noncoding regions, and traditional transcriptome-wide association studies (TWAS) often prioritize multiple correlated genes per locus.

We applied two gene fine-mapping methods, TGVIS and M-cTWAS, which jointly model gene expression, splicing and alternative polyadenylation (APA) with direct variant effects, to overall BC, estrogen receptor-negative (ER-negative) BC and triple-negative BC (TNBC) in women of European and African ancestry.

Using 11 BC-relevant GTEx v10 tissues, we prioritized 31, 13 and 5 candidate causal genes, respectively, in European-ancestry populations. Compared with marginal TWAS, fine-mapping reduced the number of candidate genes per locus by 72-88%, and credible sets explained 97-100% of significant marginal associations. Seven candidates were not reported by previous GTEx v8-based TWAS, including RIN3, supported only by APA, and NPAT for TNBC. Analyses across 48 tissues and brain cell types revealed brain contributions, including ARHGEF5 in oligodendrocytes. In African-ancestry populations, EN1 was prioritized for ERnegative BC and TNBC through variants common in African but rare in European ancestry. These results refine BC susceptibility genes, highlight regulatory mechanisms beyond breast tissue and gene expression, and reveal the challenge of mapping GWAS variants to causal genes with currently available data.

Introduction

Breast cancer (BC) is the most commonly diagnosed cancer among women, with an estimated 2.3 million new cases in 2022, accounting for 11.6% of all new cancer cases 1 . Among BC major subtypes, TNBC defined by the absence of estrogen receptor (ER), progesterone receptor and HER2 amplification, is one of the most aggressive subtypes 1, 2 . The clinical burden of TNBC is especially high among Black women in the United States, who are diagnosed with this subtype more frequently than White women 3, 4 . Alongside efforts to address social and healthcare determinants, this persistent disparity motivates ancestry-stratified analyses of genetic susceptibility in European-ancestry (EUR) and African-ancestry (AFR) populations. Genome-wide association studies (GWAS) have identified more than 200 genetic variants associated with BC risk 5, 6 . However, mapping the GWAS variants to causal genes remains a major challenge. Transcriptome-wide association studies (TWAS) link genetically predicted molecular phenotypes to disease and have reported hundreds of breast cancer susceptibility genes [7] [8] [9] [10] [11] . Despite these advances, traditional TWAS methods do not fully resolve correlated signals within a locus: PrediXcan 12 and S-PrediXcan 13 only provide marginal association tests for predicted gene molecular phenotypes. FOCUS 14 further fine-maps TWAS association by jointly modeling correlated predicted gene molecular phenotypes within a region, but id does not model the direct variant effects. As a result, xQTLs correlated with trait-associated variants can still be prioritized multiple genes even without gene-mediated effects 15, 16 . To overcome the limitation, gene fine-mapping frameworks such as TGVIS 15 , cTWAS 16 ,M-cTWAS 17 have been developed. These methods can jointly model multiple gene molecular phenotypes within a locus while accounting for linkage disequilibrium (LD) structure and allowing for direct causal variants. All these methods report posterior inclusion probabilities (PIPs) and credible sets (CSs), allowing correlated molecular phenotypes and direct SNPs to compete for posterior support.

Most of the previous studies of BC susceptibility genes have relied on traditional TWAS and focused predominantly on EUR populations [8] [9] [10] . A recent TWAS study in African-ancestry populations identified associated genes for BC and its subtypes 7 , but gene fine-mapping is still needed to prioritize candidate causal genes. In this study, we performed gene fine-mapping using TGVIS and M-cTWAS for BC, ER-negative BC and TNBC in EUR and AFR populations. By jointly modeling genetic variants and gene molecular phenotypes across multiple tissues, we aimed to (i) quantify how gene fine-mapping refines marginal TWAS findings, (ii) characterize the molecular phenotypes and tissues in which candidate genes act, and (iii) assess whether finemapped signals are shared across ancestries.

Results

Study design

We analyzed the GWAS summary statistics for BC, ER-negative BC and TNBC from the GWAS Catalog 18 . The EUR datasets were generated by the Breast Cancer Association Consortium (BCAC) 5 , and the AFR datasets were generated by the African-ancestry Breast Cancer Genetics Consortium (AABCG) 6 . Clumping genome-wide significant index variants, extending each locus by +/-500 kb and merging overlapping intervals defined 133, 36 and 14 EUR loci and 8, 3 and 3 AFR loci for BC, ER-negative BC and TNBC, respectively (Methods).

Fine-mapping of European BC loci

We first integrated gene molecular phenotype feature (defined as a gene signal for either gene expression, splicing, or polyadenylation (APA) in a specific tissue) models from 11 GTEx tissues selected for relevance to BC etiology (Methods). Across 133 EUR BC loci, TGVIS identified 144 CSs in 97 loci, and 26 CSs contained gene molecular phenotype features, including 149 gene-tissue pairs and 41 unique genes. In comparison, M-cTWAS identified 200 CSs in 128 loci, and 41 CSs contained gene features, representing 154 gene-tissue pairs and 89 unique genes. Most identified CSs (79.5-81.9%) contained only SNPs, which suggested most of the BC loci cannot be mapped to potential causal genes by either TGVIS or M-cTWAS (Figure 1a).

We observed that TGVIS detected CSs in fewer loci than M-cTWAS. To examine whether the genetic infinitesimal effects contribute to this difference, SNP fine-mapping was applied to the 32 loci with CSs detected only by M-cTWAS. SuSiE 19 identified variant-level CSs at 27 loci, whereas SuSiE-inf 20 that incorporates infinitesimal effects, which are also incorporated in TGVIS, retained only five of these, consistent with TGVIS yielding fewer CSs than M-cTWAS.

Together, TGVIS and M-cTWAS selected 99 unique genes, of which 31 were supported by both methods and were designated as the primary candidate causal genes (Table 1 and Supplementary Table 1). 44 of the 99 genes were present in the breast carcinoma database DisGeNET 21 , representing the significant enrichment (FDR-adjusted P = 0.002), and the enrichment was further improved for the primary candidate causal genes with 20 of the 31 genes present in DisGeNET (FDR-adjusted P =1.5×10⁻⁴).

Characterizing candidate genes across multiple molecular phenotypes and multiple tissues can capture complementary regulatory mechanisms and tissue contexts overlooked by analyses limited to a gene expression or breast mammary tissue. The 31 primary candidate genes consist of 190 unique causal gene-tissue molecular phenotypes that contribute to 107 unique gene-tissue pairs, indicating substantial pleiotropic effects (Supplementary Table 1). At the molecular phenotype level, 13 genes were supported by more than one molecular phenotype, whereas 12 were supported only by gene expression, 5 only by splicing and one gene (RIN3) only by APA (Figure 2a). CASP8, ELL, TLR1 and CYP51A1 were primary candidate causal genes supported by expression, splicing and APA. At the tissue level, we calculated proportion of each tissue in all gene-tissue pairs involving the 31 primary candidate genes. Cultured fibroblasts (16.8%) ranked the highest, followed by whole blood (15%), Spleen (13.1%), EBV-transformed lymphocytes (13.1%), visceral omentum (11.2%), and breast mammary tissue (11.2%)The result indicates that restricting analysis to breast tissue only likely misses a substantial proportion of relevant regulatory signals.

To assess how gene fine-mapping refined marginal TWAS findings, we compared our prioritized genes with the GTEx v8 S-PrediXcan results of previous studies 8, 9 . Across the same 11 BC relevant tissues, these studies reported 421 genes significantly associated with BC 8, 9 . Of these, 278 were mapped to the loci and molecular phenotypes evaluated in this study. M-cTWAS and TGVIS prioritized 65 of the 278 genes, including 28 were supported by both methods and designated as primary candidate causal genes (Supplementary Table 2). The remaining three primary candidates, RIN3, AP4B1-AS1 and RUSC1-AS1, were not significant in the GTEx v8 analyses. We next compared fine-mapping with our own marginal S-PrediXcan analysis to assess locus-level resolution. Across the 72 loci with at least one gene passing Bonferroni correction in our marginal S-PrediXcan analysis, the mean number of candidate genes per locus decreased from 3.64 in the marginal analysis to 0.43 after fine-mapping (88.2% reduction). This reduction suggests that most marginal TWAS signals could be explained by correlated gene molecular phenotype signals or direct SNP effects within the same locus. This interpretation was illustrated by COX11-liver, one of the strongest S-PrediXcan associations at the locus. Neither method retained the association of COX11-liver after jointly modeling STXBP4-cultured fibroblast and direct SNP effects (Figure 3a). PAX9-breast mammary tissue provided a second example. Although it was the strongest TWAS signal at its risk locus, its association was no longer significant after conditioning on fine-mapped direct SNP effects (Figure 3b).

We next tested whether significant marginal features outside the CSs remained associated after conditioning on representative direct SNPs from the fine-mapped CSs (Methods). Among 1,963 genes with at least one eligible non-CS molecular phenotype feature, 233 showed a Bonferronisignificant marginal association. Only 7 remained significant after CS-informed conditioning analysis in the 129 loci with a CS from either gene fine-mapping method (Figure 4a; Supplementary Table 3). By comparison, conditioning on the conventional GWAS lead SNP only attenuated 182 of the 233 signals (Supplementary Table 3). Together, these results indicate that fine-mapped CSs accounted for most (97%) significant marginal signals outside them.

We further assessed concordance with Ping et al 22 , who applied S-PrediXcan to breast-tissue gene expression, splicing and APA in a multi-ancestry analysis. After restricting our analysis to EUR overall BC loci with breast tissue supporting, 12 primary candidate causal genes were observed. Apart from RUSC1-AS1 and PPM1K, the remaining 10 genes were included in the gene set reported by Ping et al 22 . (Supplementary Table 4), showing the concordance between the analyses. Beyond this concordance, Ping et al. identified an average of 2.96 genes per GWAS locus in comparing with 0.27 primary candidate genes per locus using our methods, suggesting substantial narrow the candidate genes (Supplementary Table 5). For example, at the comparable locus of 19p13.11, Ping et al. reported significant associations for SSBP4, HAPLN4, LRRC25, but SSBP4 was the only primary candidate with breast-tissue support in our analysis. Conditioning on the credible set containing SSBP4 made the 2 genes are non-significant after Bonferroni correction.

Subtype-specific fine-mapping in EUR populations

At the 36 EUR ER-negative BC loci, TGVIS identified 34 CSs across 28 loci, including 8 genecontaining CSs involving 33 gene-tissue pairs and 15 unique genes. M-cTWAS identified 43 CSs across 34 loci, including 10 gene-containing CSs involving 26 gene-tissue pairs and 18 unique genes (Figure 1b). Twenty genes were selected by at least one method, and 13 were primary candidate causal genes supported by both methods (Figure 2b, Table 1, and Supplementary Table 6). Fine-mapping also narrowed the candidate genes within loci of ER-negative BC: across 18 loci with significant marginal associations, the mean number of candidate genes per locus decreased from 2.61 to 0.72 (72.3% reduction). Of the 66 genes reported in a previous multi-tissue S-PrediXcan study 11 , 50 were comparable after filtering; 14 were selected by at least one method and 11 were primary candidate causal genes (Supplementary Table 7). The remaining two primary candidates, RP5-855D21.3 and ENSG00000286613, were not reported in that study. RP5-855D21.3 has prior eQTL target-mapping and colocalization support 23 , whereas ENSG00000286613 was a newly prioritized gene.

Consistent with the overall and ER-negative BC analyses, across seven loci with significant marginal associations, the mean number of candidate genes per locus decreased from 3.57 to 0.71 (80.0% reduction). Of the 32 genes reported in a previous multi-tissue S-PrediXcan study 10 , 23 were comparable after filtering, only 5 were selected by at least one method and 3 met the primary-candidate criterion (Supplementary Table 9). The remaining two primary candidates, NPAT and the lncRNA ENSG00000289091, were newly prioritized for TNBC.

CS-informed conditional analysis reinforced the subtype results. For ER-negative BC, none of the 30 significant marginal genes located outside our identified CSs remained after conditioning, compared with 3 remaining after lead-SNP conditioning (Supplementary Table 10). For TNBC, none of the 20 significant genes located outside of our identified CSs remained after CSinformed conditioning, compared with 6 remaining after lead-SNP conditioning (Supplementary Table 11). These analyses do not prove that CS genes are causal, but show that most of the Bonferroni-corrected significant marginal associations of gene-tissue features can be explained by the fine-mapped CS signals.

Expanded tissue analyses highlight brain-associated regulatory signals

To assess the sensitivity of gene prioritization to tissue coverage, we repeated the fine-mapping analysis across the 48 non-male-specific GTEx tissues. Tissue expansion increased the number of primary candidate causal genes from 31 to 56 for overall BC and from 13 to 20 for ERnegative BC, but reduced from 5 to 4 for TNBC. All the 31 overall BC candidates and 12 of the 13 ER-negative BC candidates remained as the primary-candidate genes, but only 2 of the 5 TNBC primary candidates remained (Supplementary Tables 12 13 14). It shows that gene prioritization was stable for overall BC and ER-negative BC, but TNBC results were sensitive to tissue inclusion, possibly owing to its smaller GWAS sample size.

Within the expanded 48-tissue analysis, we quantified the contribution of each tissue separately for TGVIS and M-cTWAS (Methods). For overall BC, contributions were distributed across many tissues. For ER-negative BC and TNBC, several brain tissues were among the largest contributors under both methods, particularly the substantia nigra and caudate (Supplementary Figure 1). Notably, TERT and ENSG00000285800 became primary candidates for all three BC phenotypes only when brain-tissue models were included, and both were supported exclusively by brain-derived expression models. Overall, 28 of 56 (Overall BC), 13 of 20 (ER-negative BC) and 3 of 4 (TNBC) primary candidates had at least one brain-associated molecular phenotype feature (Supplementary Tables 12 13 14). Thus, brain-derived models contributed candidate genes beyond those identified with the 11 BC-relevant tissues, which we examined further below.

To assess independent breast-tissue support for the brain-supported primary candidates, we re-evaluated the brain-supported primary candidates in an independent breast dataset restricted to Europeanancestry participants from the Susan G. Komen Tissue Bank 7, 22 . Across the three BC phenotypes, 22 unique genes were supported by at least one brain tissue but not by breast tissue in the original 48 tissue analysis. Only RCCD1 and RPL23AP53 were replicated in the independent dataset (2 of 22, 9.1%; Supplementary Table 15). Although both genes had brain-tissue support, their strongest original signals arose from EBV-transformed lymphocytes and cultured fibroblasts, respectively, rather than brain tissue. By comparison, 10 of 23 genes with breasttissue support in the 48 tissue analysis were replicated (43.5%). As a complementary analysis, we compared the largest absolute S-PrediXcan Z scores across tissue domains for the 22 genes (Supplementary Figure 2). Among genes with an available breast result, the largest breast |Z| score was consistently lower than the largest brain |Z| score, suggesting no evidence of a breastdominant association pattern. Together, these findings show limited breast support for these brain-supported candidates in the analyses examined here.

To refine bulk brain signals to specific cell types, we performed fine-mapping using eight celltype-specific prediction models generated by MV-SuSiE 24 from single-nucleus RNA-seq data from 424 EUR samples 25 . However, some candidates from the 48-tissue analysis, including TERT, could not be evaluated because the corresponding cell-type-specific prediction models were unavailable. For BC, ARHGEF5 showed the strongest gene-cell-type signal under both TGVIS (PIP = 0.993) and M-cTWAS (PIP = 0.902) and was assigned to oligodendrocytes (Figures 5a; Figure 6a). ARHGEF5 was not prioritized in the 11-tissue analysis, but all its selected molecular phenotype features in the 48-tissue analysis were fine-mapped from brain tissues. Both methods supported its expression signals in the hippocampus and substantia nigra (Figure 6b). The agreement across bulk brain and oligodendrocyte models supports ARHGEF5 the prioritization of ARHGEF5. CASP8, a primary candidate in both the 11-and 48-tissue analyses, was prioritized in excitatory neurons, with additional TGVIS support in inhibitory neurons and microglia. This gene has also been linked to brain-tissue regulation in a cross-tissue TWAS of BC brain metastasis 26.

For ER-negative BC, LGR6, KCNN4, ZNF45 and ZNF404 were primary candidate causal genes in the 48-tissue analysis. Each had at least one bulk brain gene phenotype supported by both TGVIS and M-cTWAS and received support from brain cell-type models.

LGR6 and KCNN4 were assigned by both method to astrocytes and microglia, respectively, and ZNF45 and ZNF404 showed broader cell-type support (Figure 5b). EBF1 showed strong support from both methods in astrocytes (TGVIS PIP = 0.953; M-cTWAS PIP = 0.736), despite not being prioritized in either bulk-tissue analysis. Experimental evidence has shown that EBF1 is highly expressed in TNBC, an ER-negative subtype, and promotes tumor progression through regulation of the HIF1α pathway 27 . This contrast suggests that cell-type-specific models may reveal signals not observed in bulk-tissue fine-mapping.

For TNBC, RPS18 was the only primary candidate in the brain cell-type analysis and retained the primary-candidate status in both the 11-and 48-tissue analyses (Figure 5c). TGVIS distributed its support across all eight cell types, whereas M-cTWAS concentrated its support in endothelial cells (PIP = 0.992). These findings show the gene-level robustness of RPS18 but provide more limited evidence for a unique cell-type assignment.

Fine-Mapping in AFR Ancestry

AFR fine-mapping was conducted using breast molecular phenotype prediction models trained in the 150 African-ancestry individuals (Methods). Neither method selected a gene-tissue feature for overall BC. For ER-negative BC, EN1-breast met the primary-candidate criterion, with PIP = 0.97 in TGVIS and 1.00 in M-cTWAS (Figure 7a). The same primary candidate causal genetissue signal was identified for TNBC (TGVIS PIP = 0.983; M-cTWAS PIP = 1.00; Figure 7b). A previous AFR TWAS study also reported ENSG00000258910 for both ER-negative BC and TNBC in addition to EN1 7 , but this gene was not prioritized by either method in our analysis. The five EN1 eQTLs (rs11892280, rs6719337, rs73948203, rs55761715, and rs76664032) had frequencies above 0.15 in AFR but below 0.01 in EUR populations in ALFA 28 , suggesting an ancestry-enriched regulatory signal. To explore additional candidates in the smaller AFR GWAS, we relaxed the locus-selection threshold to 𝑃 < 5 × 10 !" but did not detect any extra candidate genes.

Cross ancestry comparison

Since EUR GWAS has more power than AFR GWAS because of the large sample size in EUR population, we focus on the BC loci reported in AFR. All eight AFR-defined overall BC loci physically overlapped previously reported EUR GWAS loci. By contrast, only two of the three AFR-defined loci overlapped for each subtype. Because the same non-overlapping chr2 locus had rs76664032 as the leading SNP in both subtypes, which was nearly absent in EUR populations (minor-allele frequency(MAF) <0.01) 28.

We compared the fine-mapping results between AFR and EUR and observed no concordant primary candidate causal genes, but five of the eight loci contained at least one shared CS, suggesting common candidate causal variants of these loci. The three loci without a common CS signal were on chr4, chr 10 and chr 18 (Supplementary Table 16).

On chr4, distinct CSs were identified in AFR and EUR. The AFR CS was represented by rs61751053 with MAF 1.246% in AFR and 0.002% in EUR 28 . The EUR CS was represented by SNP rs62331151 with MAF 23% in EUR and 6.9% in AFR. The linkages disequilibrium r 2 is 0.001 but D′ is 1 in AFR in the 1000 Genomes Project, suggesting that the low r² is driven by their large MAF difference rather than by historical recombination. Because the two lead variants lie on the same, unrecombined haplotype, the distinct ancestry-specific signals may reflect a single shared underlying association whose lead marker differs between populations owing to MAF differences.

On chr 10, where the AFR CS contains rs17542768 and EUR CSs were represented by rs2981579, rs9420318, and rs45631563. The SNP rs17542768 was associated in both AFR and EUR (PEUR = 1.4 × 10 -24 , PAFR =2.03 × 10 -8 ). However, under joint modeling of multiple signals in the locus, the association evidence of rs17542768 disappeared in EUR (PIP=0.00068), which is consistent with the two CSs reflecting the same underlying signal.

On chr18, AFR CS was represented by rs57478295 and EUR CS was represented by rs72933507. These two SNPs are in high LD (r 2 EUR = 0.664; r 2 AFR= 0.52). Again, these two CSs may therefore arise from the same underlying association through different variants in LD.

We further performed MESuSiE 29 as a complementary analysis across all eight loci (Methods). MESuSiE supported shared components at seven of the eight loci, including those on chr10 and chr18 (Supplementary Table 16). At the remaining locus on chr4, MESuSiE assigned slightly higher support to an EUR-specific than a shared configuration (PIPEUR = 0.117 versus PIPEUR-AFR = 0.074) but both were low, providing no clear resolution. Additional independent AFR data or functional studies at this locus are needed to confirm a single shared causal variant at this locus.

For both ER-negative BC and TNBC, the AFR and EUR fine-mapping results identified common CSs and supported signal sharing at the two overlapping loci, which was also supported by MESuSiE (Supplementary Table 16).

Overall, our results suggest that overlapped GWAS loci between AFR and EUR likely share the same underlying causal variants with the attributable to different MAF and LD patterns.

Discussion

In this study, integrative variant-to-gene fine-mapping for BC, ER-negative BC and TNBC was performed by jointly modeling multiple molecular phenotypes of multiple tissues in EUR and AFR populations. TGVIS and M-cTWAS allowed gene-mediated and direct SNP effects to compete within each locus. They narrowed the broad gene sets identified by marginal TWAS and prioritized candidate causal genes supported by specific molecular phenotypes. These analyses further revealed regulatory signals beyond breast tissue and cell-type-specific associations captured by brain-derived models. However, our study also reveals a significant challenge to identify the underlying causal genes in the GWAS loci using the molecular phenotypes we studied, with less than 43% GWAS loci being able to map to potential causal genes. Incorporating other types of molecular phenotypes such as proteomics may help to map GWAS loci to potential causal genes.

A major strength of this study is its ability to distinguish candidate gene-mediated signals from correlated marginal associations across BC phenotypes. Across the same 11 tissues, we prioritized 28 of 278, 11 of 50 and 3 of 23 comparable previous S-PrediXcan genes for BC 8, 9 , ER-negative BC 11 and TNBC 10 , respectively. Across loci with significant marginal associations, fine-mapping reduced the number of candidate genes by 72-88% for the three BC phenotypes. Many marginal associations did not retain independent support in the joint model. The COX11liver association provides a clear example: despite its strong marginal association with BC, COX11 lost support when evaluated jointly with STXBP4-cultured fibroblasts and direct SNP signals at the locus (Figure 3a). The significant COX11-liver association may reflect strong LD between its eQTL and the candidate direct causal variant rs1812715 and the eQTL of STXBP4-Cultured fibroblasts. Credible-set-informed conditioning was consistent with this pattern: 97.0%, 100% and 100% of significant non-CS marginal gene-tissue associations for BC, ER-negative BC and TNBC, were no longer significant. These findings show that marginal TWAS signals should not be interpreted in isolation.

We defined the primary-candidate genes as observed by both TGVIS and M-cTWAS because the two methods allocate posterior support differently. M-cTWAS uses genome-wide group-level priors 17 , whereas TGVIS relies more directly on local GWAS, xQTL and LD information while accounting for infinitesimal background effects 15 . Agreement between the methods therefore provides convergent support for the prioritized genes.

Fine-mapping also prioritized seven candidates not identified in previous GTEx v8 TWAS [8] [9] [10] [11] . Among them, RIN3 was prioritized for overall BC, with the association driven mainly by APA signals. Variant fine-mapping coupled with functional target-gene mapping has independently implicated RIN3 30 , and our analysis further points to an APA-mediated mechanism. This pattern suggests that the RIN3 association may involve post-transcriptional regulation. APA can influence 3′UTR usage, transcript stability and miRNA-mediated regulation 31 , effects that are not fully captured by expression-based TWAS. Biologically, RIN3 belongs to the RIN family of Ras effectors and functions as a Rab5 guanine nucleotide exchange factor involved in early endocytic trafficking 32 . Its knockdown in MCF-7 cells reduced proliferation, migration and invasion 33 . In our analysis, support across immune and stromal tissues further suggests that RIN3-associated risk may involve receptor trafficking or tumor-microenvironment processes rather than acting only through breast epithelial cells.

Apart from RIN3, the other newly prioritized transcripts also extend the biological scope of the fine-mapped signals for BC: AP4B1-AS1 and RUSC1-AS1 are lncRNAs supported through molecular models that were limited in earlier GTEx v8 analyses. External TWAS evidence has implicated APA-associated signals for both transcripts 34 . AP4B1-AS1 remains relatively less functionally characterized in BC, making its support in cultured fibroblast model. RUSC1-AS1 has more direct functional evidence: it is upregulated in BC, and its experimental depletion suppresses proliferation, migration, invasion, angiogenesis and tumor growth, potentially through the miR-326-XRCC5 axis 35 . These observations provide the support for the lncRNA signals identified by gene fine-mapping.

Compared with previous TWAS analyses based on GTEx v8 models 10, 11 , subtype-specific finemapping added two primary candidate causal genes for ER-negative BC and two for TNBC. For ER-negative BC, RP5-855D21.3 had independent support by previous eQTL-based target mapping and GWAS-eQTL colocalization 23 . By contrast, the novel candidate ENSG00000286613 remains largely uncharacterized and needs functional follow-up. For TNBC, NPAT had previously been implicated by integrating ER-negative BC GWAS with multi-tissue eQTL data 36 . Our TNBC-specific fine-mapping extends this evidence to TNBC. NPAT has been linked to cyclin E2-dependent cell-cycle regulation 37 and and its suppression in CD8⁺ T cells has been linked to anti-PD-1 resistance in TNBC 38 , providing plausible connections to TNBC biology. The second novel candidate, ENSG00000289091, is an lncRNA newly available in GTEx v10 with little functional information. Its prioritization defines a genetic hypothesis for functional follow-up.

A particularly unexpected result was the contribution of brain tissues in the expanded 48-tissue analysis, especially for ER-negative BC and TNBC (Supplementary Figure 1). Single-cell finemapping further assigned several brain-associated signals to specific cell types (Figure 5). Modest contributions from the pituitary and adrenal gland raise the possibility that some of these signals reflect neuroendocrine regulation of hormonal pathways relevant to BC risk. Emerging evidence also indicates that neural cells and signaling pathways contribute to tumor development and progression [39] [40] [41] , although this evidence concerns tumor progression rather than germline susceptibility. Alternatively, brain eQTLs may tag trait-associated haplotypes through linked variants, in which case the prioritized genes themselves may still be correct but with wrong tissue of action 42 . Further investigation with improved molecular phenotype data is warranted.

Several prioritized genes provide possible links between the brain-derived signals and BC biology. For overall BC, ARHGEF5 provided the clearest example: it was supported exclusively by brain-derived models in the 48-tissue analysis and was further localized to oligodendrocytes. Biologically, ARHGEF5 encodes a Rho guanine nucleotide exchange factor and Rho GTPase signaling regulates neuronal development and cytoskeletal organization 43 . Its role in cytoskeletal regulation, together with reports of oncogenic transcripts in aggressive breast cancer cell lines and primary tumors 44 , suggests a possible link between glial regulatory programs and BC biology. In addition, CASP8 was prioritized as a primary candidate gene using models derived from both bulk and single-cell brain data. A previous cross-tissue TWAS also linked brain-tissue regulation of CASP8 to BC brain metastasis 26 . In ER-negative BC, EBF1 was supported in astrocytes but not retained in the bulk-tissue analyses. Its reported role in the progression of TNBC , the ER-negative subtype, by restraining HIF1α activity 27 supports its relevance to ERnegative BC and illustrates how single-cell fine-mapping may recover signals diluted in bulktissue data. In TNBC, RPS18, a primary candidate in the 11-tissue analysis, was the only primary candidate in the brain cell-type analysis, consistent with evidence linking ribosome-related pathways to BC aggressiveness and prognosis 45 . Together, these findings suggest that brainderived gene molecular phenotype prediction models relevant to BC biology, but they do not establish the underlying effects originate in the brain. The signals may reflect regulatory architecture shared across tissues or systemic neural influences 46 . Functional studies are needed to distinguish these possibilities.

AFR fine-mapping highlighted signals that EUR-focused analyses may miss. EN1-breast, previously reported in AFR TWAS 7 , was prioritized for both ER-negative BC and TNBC through eQTLs common in AFR but rare in EUR populations, illustrating the value of AFR molecular resources. Alongside these AFR-enriched signals, our analyses also supported signal sharing between AFR and EUR at overlapping GWAS loci. Sharing was supported by common variants in the separately derived AFR and EUR CSs. When these CSs did not overlap, further analysis of LD among the variants in AFR and EUR CSs suggested that allele-frequency and LD differences may explain the apparent discordances. However, shared variant-level signals did not generate shared primary candidate causal genes in our study. This may partly reflect the limited AFR gene-expression reference data and smaller AFR BC GWAS sample size.

Limitations

Several limitations of this study should be noted. First, although agreement between TGVIS and M-cTWAS increases confidence in gene prioritization, it does not constitute independent replication because both methods use the same input data. Second, molecular-model coverage was also highly unequal across ancestries: EUR analyses benefited from multi-tissue GTEx resources, whereas AFR analyses were limited to breast-expression models trained in 150 individuals. Together with the smaller AFR GWAS sample size, this imbalance reduced finemapping power and limited cross-ancestry comparisons. Third, the analysis considered only available cis-regulatory molecular phenotypes; trans effects, unmeasured cell types and ancestryspecific xQTLs were not captured, potentially leaving mediated signals unresolved or assigned to direct variants. Fourth, GTEx v10 prediction models were approximated by the top-PIP variant of each xQTL credible set. Most models contained one or a few variants, which limit the separation of gene-mediated from direct SNP effects and may shift posterior support between a gene and its correlated xQTL variants (Supplementary Note 1). Fifth, the brain single-nucleus eQTL data were derived from an aging cohort enriched for Alzheimer's disease pathology 25 so cell-type-specific regulatory effects may therefore differ in younger or neurologically healthy populations. Sixth, brain-associated signals need further independent replication. A recent evaluation suggested that eQTLs in tissues unrelated to a trait can tag trait-associated haplotypes through distinct, tightly linked variants 42 . Consequently, TWAS and colocalization may identify the correct gene while misattributing its tissue of action and direction of effect. Our brain evidence relied exclusively on GTEx brain tissues and brain cell-type prediction models, which requires independent data to confirm the contribution of brain tissues in BC.

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that integrating multiple molecular traits, tissues, ancestries, and fine-mapping methods substantially improves the precision of causal gene identification for BC, and supports the common BC biology across populations in overlapped GWAS loci. The refined candidate gene sets, the diverse regulatory modalities captured through gene expression, APA, and splicing modeling, and the unexpected but brain tissue signals together may guide the future functional and translational studies. 436and GRCh38 (BP38) for AFR. Method

Breast Cancer GWAS Summary Statistics

For European ancestry (EUR), GWAS summary statistics for BC (GCST010098), ER-negative BC (GCST005076), TNBC (GCST90454344) were downloaded from the GWAS Catalog and were originally generated by the Breast Cancer Association Consortium (BCAC) 5, 47 . EUR GWAS summary statistics were imputed using the common SNPs and the imputation function implemented in SBayesRC with the European-ancestry UK Biobank LD reference panel 48 . This step recovers association statistics at variants not directly reported in the original GWAS through LD.

For African ancestry (AFR), GWAS summary statistics for BC (GCST90296719), ER-negative BC (GCST90296721), and TNBC (GCST90296722) were obtained from the GWAS Catalog and were originally generated by individual of African-ancestry Breast Cancer Genetics Consortium (AABCG) 6.

GWAS locus definition

GWAS summary statistics were clumped with PLINK v1.9 (--clump-p1 5×10^{-8}, --clump-p2 5×10^{-8}, --clump-r2 0.01, --clump-kb 500) to identify approximately independent genomewide significant index SNPs using ancestry-matched UK Biobank LD reference panels. Each risk locus was defined as the ±500-kb region around an index SNP, with overlapping loci subsequently merged.

For the exploratory and sensitivity analyses in AFR populations, loci were additionally defined using a suggestive threshold of P < 5 × 10⁻⁶, with all other clumping parameters unchanged.

Weights of Gene Molecular phenotype prediction model

To conduct gene fine-mapping for BC and its subtypes, the weights of gene molecular phenotype prediction models are required as inputs. In this study, we constructed several sparse prediction models for three molecular phenotypes: gene expression, APA, and splicing (intron excision ratios/exon-junction levels), using four primary data sources: (1) GTEx v10 49 , (2) breast tissue data from 150 African-ancestry individuals from the Susan G. Komen Tissue Bank (KTB) 7 , (3) breast tissue data from 182 European-ancestry individuals from KTB 22 (4) eight cell-typespecific gene expression models derived from brain single-cell eQTL 25 . These models estimate each molecular phenotype as a sparse linear combination of nearby cis-variants, with the cisvariant was defined followed the original data source framework (±1 Mb from the transcription start site for GTEx and single-cell brain data, and ±500 kb for KTB).

These prediction models consist of sparse linear weights that estimate each molecular phenotype as an additive function of cis-variant genotypes, where only a subset of variants receives nonzero effects. For a given gene or molecular trait, the genetically predicted level can be written as: where 𝑌 . is the predicted molecular trait level, 𝐺 ' is the genotype dosage of the 𝑗-th cis-variant, 𝑤 ' is the estimated effect weight for the variant, and 𝑝 is the number of cis-variants included in the model.

Under sparse modeling approaches such as SuSiE, most 𝑤 ' = 0, such that only a limited number of informative variants contribute to the prediction model.

Weights of GTEx v10 molecular phenotype prediction models

For the GTEx-based models, cis-xQTL summary statistics (eQTL, sQTL, and apaQTL) were downloaded for the 48 non-male-specific tissues in GTEx v10. GTEx v10 released SNP-level fine-mapping results from SuSiE-based joint models for cis-xQTL signals, which we used to construct the weights of molecular phenotype prediction models. However, GTEx v10 only released the CS but not the effect size for a gene-tissue pair. So we selected the highest posterior inclusion probability (PIP) in the CS as the representative variant. For the gene molecular phenotype with a single CS, the marginal effect size of the top-PIP variant was used as a proxy for its joint effect. For a gene molecular phenotype with multiple CSs, the marginal effects of the top-PIP variants from each CS were collected and subsequently adjusted to approximate joint effects using LD estimated from unrelated European-ancestry individuals in the UK Biobank.

Weights of African-ancestry breast molecular phenotype prediction models

Individual-level molecular phenotypes (including transcripts per million (TPM), Percentage of Distal polyA site Usage Index (PDUI), and Percent Spliced Isoform (PSI)) and genotype data were obtained from KTB 7 to model gene expression, APA events, and exon junction usage.

Following the quality control pipeline of the previous work 7 , for gene expression, genes expressed in fewer than 5% of samples were excluded. TPM values were log2-transformed and quantile normalized across samples. For APA events and exon junctions, features with more than 5% missing values were removed, and PDUI and PSI values were quantile normalized.

To correct for batch effects and other experimental confounders, 30 PEER factors were estimated separately for each molecular phenotype using the corresponding normalized data and included in downstream prediction models 50, 51 . Model training is adjusted for age, PEER factors, and the top three genetic principal components. SuSiE models were fitted with L = 10, and weights were retained only for variants included in a SuSiE CS.

Weights of European-ancestry breast molecular phenotype prediction models from the independent KTB cohort

Breast molecular phenotype prediction models were also trained using 182 European-ancestry individuals from the KTB. These models followed the same preprocessing, covariate adjustment, SuSiE fitting and CS-based weight selection pipeline described above for AFR.

Weights of Single-cell gene expression prediction models for brain tissues

Cell-type-specific gene expression prediction models for brain tissues were constructed by jointly analyzing eQTL association statistics across eight brain single-cell types from the 424 European-ancestry individuals 25 . For each gene, SNPs observed across cell types were pooled and their LD structure was estimated from a UK Biobank European-ancestry reference panel. Following allele harmonization, SNP-by-trait Z-score matrices were assembled and modeled using SuSiE-RSS for genes measured in a single cell type, or MV-SuSiE-RSS 24 for genes measured across multiple cell types.

A sparse multiple-effect model was applied with an initial maximum of L = 3 effects per gene. For multivariate models reaching this limit, L was increased to 5 to capture additional signals. Trait-specific effects were determined using the posterior local false sign rate (LFSR), with effects considered supported in each cell type if LFSR ≤ 0.05. This framework identified cell type-specific genetic regulatory effects across eight brain cell types: astrocytes, endothelial cells, excitatory neurons, inhibitory neurons, microglia, oligodendrocytes, oligodendrocyte precursor cells (OPCs), and pericytes. The resulting SNP effect-size estimates were used as molecular prediction weights in downstream fine-mapping analyses.

LD reference panels and allele harmonization

Genotype data from the UK Biobank were used as the LD reference panel. For ancestry-specific fine-mapping, all unrelated individuals of European ancestry (EUR; n = 390,521) were included for EUR analyses, and all unrelated individuals of African ancestry (AFR; n = 3,934) were included for AFR analyses. Related individuals were excluded based on a kinship coefficient threshold of ≥ 0.0884.

Reference and alternative alleles for each variant were defined consistently across the GWAS summary statistics, molecular phenotype prediction models, and the LD reference panel. When the reference and alternative alleles were flipped between the GWAS or gene-phenotype prediction model and the reference panel, the variant's GWAS Z-score or prediction model weight was inverted to make sure its sign was aligned with the alternative allele in the reference panel.

Marginal S-PrediXcan analysis

We performed marginal S-PrediXcan analysis for all gene tissue molecular phenotype features included in the 11-tissue EUR analysis. Significance was assessed separately for each phenotype using Bonferroni correction for the total number of tested features. The resulting thresholds were P < 3.51×10 -6 for BC, P < 1.65×10 -5 for ER-negative BC and P < 2.75×10 -5 for TNBC. For locus-level summaries, a gene was considered significant if at least one feature passed the corresponding threshold, and each gene was counted once per locus.

Gene fine-mapping Methods

TGVIS

TGVIS is a locus-based gene fine-mapping framework that jointly models gene-mediated effects and direct SNP effects within each genomic region while accounting for infinitesimal effects 15 . TGVIS takes genetically predicted gene molecular phenotypes and SNPs as candidate predictors. This method performs Bayesian variable selection to prioritize causal gene tissue pairs and direct causal variants, reporting PIPs and CSs for fine-mapped features. In this study, the maximum number of effects (L) was selected from 1 to 5, with the optimal value chosen by minimizing the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC). CSs were defined at a 95% cumulative PIP threshold, and gene molecular phenotype features included in a CS were considered as candidate causal regulatory signals.

M-cTWAS

M-cTWAS is a Bayesian gene fine-mapping framework that integrates genetically predicted molecular traits across multiple groups to improve causal gene prioritization 17 . The method jointly models SNP effects and gene-mediated effects while allowing group-specific prior variances, enabling information sharing across tissues or molecular contexts while accommodating heterogeneity in genetic regulation. The number of causal signals L was set to 5 by default. CSs were defined at a 95% cumulative PIP threshold, and gene molecular phenotype features included in CSs were considered candidate causal regulatory signals.

Gene fine-mapping

TGVIS and M-cTWAS were applied separately to BC, ER-negative BC and TNBC loci. Within a locus, both methods jointly evaluated genetically predicted molecular phenotypes and direct SNP effects, allowing fine-mapping to distinguish likely gene-mediated associations from signals better explained by direct SNP effects or by other correlated gene-tissue molecular phenotype predictors in the same LD block.

For fine-mapping analyses in EUR populations, three sets of gene-phenotype prediction models were used. First, GTEx v10 gene-phenotype prediction models for gene expression, splicing, and APA were applied across 11 tissues relevant to breast cancer etiology, including female reproductive tissues (breast, ovary, uterus, and vagina), tissues resembling connective and adipose components of the breast (subcutaneous adipose, visceral adipose, and cultured fibroblasts), immune-related tissues (spleen, EBV-transformed lymphocytes, and whole blood), and liver-tissues potentially relevant to breast cancer development or carcinogen metabolism 9 . Second, GTEx v10 gene-phenotype prediction models were applied across all 48 non-malespecific tissues, excluding prostate and testis. Third, cell-type-specific gene expression prediction models for eight brain cell types were used to refine brain-associated signals, constructed using MV-SuSiE as described above.

For fine-mapping analyses in African ancestry (AFR) populations, SuSiE-based gene molecular phenotype prediction models generated from breast tissue of 150 African-ancestry females were applied.

Credible-set-informed conditional S-PrediXcan analysis

To compare fine-mapped causal gene prioritization against marginal TWAS associations, we performed a credible-set-informed conditional S-PrediXcan analysis. This analysis tested whether marginal gene molecular phenotype associations outside the CSs could be explained by genetic signals represented by the fine-mapped CSs at the same locus.

For each eligible locus, CSs from only one gene fine-mapping method were used. We preferentially used M-cTWAS CSs because the infinitesimal background effects modeled by TGVIS cannot be directly represented by a small set of conditioning variants. TGVIS CSs were used only when M-cTWAS identified no CS at that locus. Loci without a CS from either method were excluded from conditional analysis. Conditioning variants were selected from CSs containing direct SNP components, including both SNP-only CSs and mixed SNP/gene CSs. Each CS was represented by one direct SNP: the SNP with the highest SNP-level posterior inclusion probability within that CS. So the number of conditioning SNPs varied across loci according to the number of direct-SNP-containing CSs identified by the selected fine-mapping method. This CS-informed strategy differs from conventional lead-SNP conditioning because it can account for multiple fine-mapped signals within the same locus.

Definition of candidate causal genes

A primary candidate causal gene was defined by concordance between TGVIS and M-cTWAS at the gene level. For each method, a gene was selected when at least one of its molecular phenotypes was included in a 95% credible set. A gene was designated a primary candidate causal gene only when it was selected by both methods. The same tissue or molecular phenotype feature was not required to be selected by both methods.

We used a gene-level definition because the two methods can produce different credible-set structures at the same locus. In our results, TGVIS CSs often contained more gene molecular phenotype features and assigned lower PIPs to individual selected features than M-cTWAS. This likely reflects how posterior support is allocated among correlated features. M-cTWAS estimates genome-wide group-level priors for tissue and molecular-phenotype groups and uses these priors during locus-level fine-mapping, which can concentrate support on a smaller number of features from globally supported groups. In contrast, TGVIS relies more directly on local GWAS, xQTL, and LD evidence and does not use tissue-or phenotype-specific priors to favor one group over another. Therefore, when several features share similar or highly correlated xQTL evidence, TGVIS will include many of them in the same credible set. For this reason, we compared the two methods at the gene level rather than requiring the same tissue or phenotype feature to be selected by both methods.

Tissue and molecular-phenotype contribution analyses

We summarized tissue support using counts and a normalized contribution. For each phenotype and fine-mapping method, the contribution for a tissue was calculated as the sum of PIPs assigned to gene-tissue pairs in that tissue divided by the total number of gene-containing CSs in the corresponding analysis.

For the molecular-phenotype summary, each primary candidate causal gene was annotated according to support from expression, splicing, APA or combinations of these phenotype classes.

Cross-ancestry fine-mapping comparison

Cross-ancestry evaluation of the AFR-defined loci proceeded in two stages. First, eight overall BC, three ER-negative BC and three TNBC loci identified by the AFR GWAS were compared with previously reported EUR GWAS loci to determine physical overlap. For overlapping loci, we used the AFR-defined genomic boundaries to extract the corresponding regions from the EUR GWAS and then re-fine-mapped these regions within the same boundaries. A locus was classified as shared between AFR and EUR if the corresponding CSs overlapped by at least one variant.

Because exact CS overlap can be sensitive to ancestry differences in LD and statistical power, we additionally applied the SNP fine-mapping method MESuSiE 29 to assess signal sharing. It jointly modeled AFR and EUR GWAS summary statistics at the variant level to estimate shared and ancestry-specific components. MESuSiE CSs labeled EUR_AFR were classified as shared signal, whereas those labeled EUR or AFR were classified as ancestry-specific.
