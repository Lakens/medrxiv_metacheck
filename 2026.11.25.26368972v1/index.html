To fix Exact P-Values

What we found

Show details

P values are never exactly zero. A p value of .000 is a rounding artifact — the actual value is simply smaller than the reported precision. Very small p values should be reported as p < .001 rather than p = .000. A p = 0 can also be a model parameter, such as an ARIMA order; check that it is a p value.

To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 1 non-significant p value that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Metacheck does not yet analyze automatically whether sentences which include non-significant p-values are correct, but we recommend manually checking the sentences below for possible misinterpreted non-significant p values.

Text Sentence
p = 0.30 For these simulations, the baseline MAF and covariate-effect multiplier were fixed at p = 0.30 and κ = 1, respectively.

For metascientific articles demonstrating the rate of misinterpretations of non-significant results is high, see:

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. Abstract

Sex differences in minor allele frequency (sdMAF) are widely used as a quality control (QC) metric for variants in the non-pseudoautosomal region (NPR) of the X chromosome. However, conventional sdMAF tests assess marginal allele-frequency differences and generally do not account for imbalances in measured covariates, such as genetic ancestry. We develop a general regression-based framework for covariate-adjusted subgroup allele-frequency inference. Using the Frisch-Waugh-Lovell theorem, we derive adjusted subgroup mean estimators and their joint sampling covariance under a groupwise residual-variance structure, enabling both targeted one-degree-of-freedom tests and joint tests of multiple subgroup contrasts. For X-NPR sdMAF analysis, we consider a parsimonious groupwise variance estimator motivated by the distinct genotype distributions of hemizygous males and diploid females, alongside the conventional homoskedastic and heteroskedasticity-robust HC1 estimators. Simulation studies evaluate Type I error control, robustness to Hardy-Weinberg disequilibrium, and statistical power. The homoskedastic variance estimator exhibited Type I error distortion under imbalanced sex ratios, whereas HC1 and the groupwise estimator demonstrated broadly comparable Type I error control and power across the simulated scenarios. In an application to 1000 Genomes Project data, adjusted and unadjusted sdMAF estimates were highly correlated, although covariate adjustment altered effect estimates and statistical significance for individual variants. The framework also supports a theoretical extension to sex-prevalence-adjusted allelic contrasts, with groupwise variance estimation providing a parsimonious alternative to HC1 for X-NPR sdMAF analysis.

1 Introduction

Sex differences in genetic variation have important implications for population genetics, genetic association studies, and genetic data quality control (QC). In particular, sex differences in minor allele frequency (sdMAF) have been used as a QC metric for variants in the non-pseudoautosomal region (NPR) of the X chromosome, where males are hemizygous and females are diploid. Significant sdMAF has been reported in the 1000 Genomes Project, gnomAD, and UK Biobank, motivating closer examination of both technical and biological sources of these differences [1, 2]. The sdMAF test evaluates the null hypothesis of equal allele frequencies between males and females. Significant sdMAF may indicate technical artifacts, including sequencing or genotyping errors, differential missingness, and sex-specific QC procedures. However, observed sdMAF does not necessarily imply poor data quality, as allele-frequency differences may also arise from evolutionary processes or selection associated with disease status and study participation. These considerations highlight the importance of accounting for relevant covariates when interpreting and testing sdMAF.

Conventional sdMAF tests compare allele frequencies between males and females using allelecount-based tests, with variance estimation accounting for their distinct genotype distributions and potential departures from Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium in diploid genotypes [1, 3]. However, these tests assess marginal allele-frequency differences, which may be influenced by imbalances in covariate distributions between sexes. For example, when allele frequencies vary across ancestral populations, differences in ancestry composition between male and female samples can produce marginal sdMAF even when allele frequencies are identical within each ancestral population. This motivates inference on covariate-adjusted allele-frequency differences, a target distinct from the marginal contrast. Regression provides a natural approach to such adjustment. Adjusted subgroup means share nuisance coefficients estimated from the full sample, introducing uncertainty and potentially nonzero covariance among the adjusted estimates. Inference on their contrasts must therefore account for this joint covariance. Moreover, hemizygous male and diploid female genotypes in the X-NPR exhibit different variance-mean relationships even when allele counts are placed on a common scale, so a common residual-variance assumption need not hold.

Regression-based allelic methods provide a foundation for covariate adjustment. Zhang and Sun developed a robust allele-based score test for autosomal association analysis that accommodates covariates and departures from HWE [4]. For sdMAF, Wang et al. proposed a population-aware retrospective regression with sex-by-population interactions and group-specific residual variances. Although their model permits additional covariates, these were omitted from the explicit test derivations [5]. More recently, Zhang et al. developed an X-chromosomal HWE framework that distinguishes female HWD from sdMAF, with the derived X-NPR tests formulated without additional nuisance covariates [6]. These developments motivate an explicit treatment of covariate-adjusted X-NPR allele-frequency contrasts, their joint sampling covariance, and the residual-variance assumptions required for inference.

We develop a covariate-adjusted inferential framework for assessing subgroup differences in allele frequency, with a particular focus on sdMAF in the X-NPR. We define adjusted subgroup mean estimators, express them using the Frisch-Waugh-Lovell theorem, and derive their joint sampling covariance under a groupwise residual-variance structure. These results provide estimates, standard errors, and tests for prespecified subgroup allele-frequency contrasts, with covariate-adjusted sdMAF as a two-group application. Through simulation studies, we evaluate the groupwise variance estimator against the conventional homoskedastic estimator, the heteroskedasticity-robust Huber-White HC1 estimator, and the unadjusted one-degree-of-freedom sdMAF test in terms of Type I error control and statistical power. Finally, we apply the proposed framework and comparator methods to data from the 1000 Genomes Project to investigate the impact of covariate adjustment on sdMAF inference in the X-NPR. Although the framework accommodates more general subgroup allele-frequency contrasts, our empirical investigations focus on sdMAF.

2.4.2 Type I error and HWD sensitivity

Type I error was first evaluated under the null hypothesis of no conditional sex difference in allele frequency by setting ∆ p = 0.

For the primary simulations, female genotypes were generated under Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium by setting ρ F = 0. We considered baseline MAFs p ∈ {0.01, 0.10, 0.35}, covariate-effect multipliers κ ∈ {0, 1, 2}, representing no, moderate, and strong covariate effects, respectively, and target male proportions η M ∈ {0.20, 0.35, 0.50, 0.65, 0.80}.

The full factorial design therefore consisted of 3 × 3 ×5 = 45 primary simulation scenarios.

We additionally conducted a sensitivity analysis to evaluate the impact of female HWD on test calibration. For these simulations, the baseline MAF and covariate-effect multiplier were fixed at p = 0.30 and κ = 1, respectively. The magnitude of female HWD was varied according to ρ F ∈ {-0.20, -0.10, 0.15, 0.30, 0.50}, thereby including both heterozygote excess (ρ F < 0) and heterozygote deficiency (ρ F > 0). To allow HWD to affect only a subset of females, an HWD indicator was randomly assigned among female individuals, with the proportion of females subject to HWD set to ω HWD ∈ {0.25, 0.50, 1.00}.

For females not assigned to the HWD subgroup, ρ F was set to zero. Target male proportions of η M ∈ {0.20, 0.50, 0.80} were considered. This resulted in an additional 5 × 3 ×3 = 45 HWD sensitivity scenarios. Genotype probabilities were required to remain within the valid probability space for all simulated individuals.

For each scenario, 10,000 independent Monte Carlo replicates of n = 5,000 individuals were generated. Four methods were evaluated: the conventional unadjusted sdMAF test, covariateadjusted regression using the conventional homoskedastic variance estimator (Naive), covariateadjusted regression using the HC1 heteroskedasticity-consistent variance estimator (HC1), and covariate-adjusted regression using the sex-groupwise residual variance estimator (Groupwise). Each method was evaluated using a two-sided test at the nominal significance level α = 0.05. For the adjusted methods, empirical type I error was calculated as the proportion of replicates rejecting the adjusted null. The unadjusted procedure tests a marginal null, which need not hold when κ > 0 even if ∆ p = 0; its rejection rate in those settings was not interpreted as its own Type I error. With 10,000 replicates, the approximate 95% Monte Carlo interval around a true type I error rate of 0.05 was 0.0457-0.0543. We additionally assessed variance estimation by comparing the mean estimated variance with the empirical variance of the corresponding effect estimator across Monte Carlo replicates.

2.4.3 Power

Power was evaluated under alternatives defined by ∆ p = λp, where λ denotes the effect multiplier. Thus, the true adjusted sex coefficient was β S = 2λp, rather than 2λ. We considered p ∈ {0.01, 0.05, 0.10, 0.20, 0.30, 0.40}, λ ∈ {0.05, 0.10, 0.15, 0.20, 0.30}, and target male-proportion parameters η M ∈ {0.20, 0.50, 0.80}, giving 6 × 5 ×3 = 90 scenarios. The covariate-effect multiplier was fixed at κ = 1. Female genotypes were generated under conditional Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium by setting ρ F = 0; male genotypes retained the 0/2 coding. The remaining data-generating mechanisms were unchanged from the simulation setup above.

For each scenario, we generated 10,000 Monte Carlo replicates of n = 5,000 individuals. Naive, HC1, and Groupwise used the same adjusted OLS sex-coefficient estimate and differed only in variance estimation. Adjusted tests rejected when | ˆ︁ β S |/ √︂ ˆ︃ Var( ˆ︁ β S ) > z 0.975 . The unadjusted comparator used its one-degree-of-freedom Wald test of the marginal male-female allele-frequency difference. Empirical rejection rates were calculated at α = 0.05. Because the unadjusted and adjusted procedures target marginal and conditional contrasts, respectively, their rejection rates need not represent power against the same effect parameter. Power comparisons among adjusted methods were interpreted together with their null calibration.

We also recorded the mean estimated sex coefficient, the empirical variance across replicates, the mean estimated variance, and the ratio of mean estimated to empirical variance. For the unadjusted method, the allele-frequency difference was multiplied by two and its variance by four to express these summaries on the genotype-coefficient scale. The Monte Carlo standard error of a rejection-rate estimate ˆ︁ r was √︁ ˆ︁ r(1 -ˆ︁ r)/10,000, at most 0.005. Random-number seeds were set to 123 + j for scenario j = 1, . . . , 90 in the factorial grid.

2.4.4 Application to the 1000 Genomes Project

We applied the proposed covariate-adjusted allelic testing framework to chromosome X data from the high-coverage 1000 Genomes Project. Phased genotype data were obtained from the 20220422 release of the 1000G high-coverage SNV/INDEL/SV dataset, aligned to the GRCh38 reference genome (see Data availability). Variants were left-aligned and normalized against the GRCh38 reference genome, atomized, and split at multiallelic sites using bcftools. The dataset was subsequently restricted to 2,504 individuals from the 1000 Genomes reference panel [11].

To account for population structure, the top 20 genetic ancestry principal components (PCs) for these 2,504 individuals were obtained from the PCs_1000G dataset in the PCAmatchR R package and included as covariates [12]. For comparisons between adjusted and unadjusted analyses, we restricted the data to biallelic single-nucleotide variants (SNVs). Variants were matched by chromosome, genomic position, and allele pair, with ambiguous duplicate matches excluded. Allele orientations were harmonized to ensure that adjusted and unadjusted allelic differences were expressed relative to the same allele. Only variants successfully matched between the two analyses were retained for comparison.

3 Results

3.1 Type I error under varying sample composition

Across the simulation scenarios, we observed several distinct patterns in empirical Type I error rates (Figure 1). First, the unadjusted test exhibited increasingly inflated Type I error rates with increasing MAF under moderate and strong covariate effects, highlighting the consequences of failing to account for covariate effects. Second, the naive variance estimator showed a systematic dependence on the sex composition of the sample, with inflated rejection rates when males were underrepresented (20% or 35%) and conservative rejection rates when males were overrepresented (65% or 80%). This suggests that the direction of Type I error distortion under the naive variance estimator is sensitive to sex imbalance. In contrast, both the HC1 and groupwise variance estimators maintained empirical Type I error rates within the 95% Monte Carlo reference bounds across nearly all scenarios. The only exceptions occurred at MAF = 0.01 with 20% male participants under no or moderate covariate effects. These isolated departures may reflect finite-sample limitations of the asymptotic Wald approximation when sex-specific minor allele counts are particularly small.

We further evaluated the sensitivity of empirical Type I error rates to HWD among females (Figure 2). The unadjusted test consistently exhibited inflated Type I error rates across all scenarios, although the magnitude of inflation varied with the simulation settings. Similar to the findings under Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium, the naive variance estimator exhibited a systematic dependence on sex composition, with inflation when males were underrepresented and conservativeness when males were overrepresented. Interestingly, as the magnitude of female HWD increased, the rejection rates of the naive estimator tended to approach the nominal significance level from either direction. The rate of this convergence depended on the proportion of females exhibiting HWD, with a greater proportion associated with a more pronounced attenuation of Type I error distortion. In contrast, HC1 and the groupwise variance estimator demonstrated similar operating characteristics across the HWD scenarios, with no clear systematic divergence between the two methods.

3.2 Statistical power

Empirical statistical power generally increased with increasing minor allele frequency (MAF) and effect size (Figure 3). Consistent with the Type I error simulation results, the unadjusted test exhibited higher empirical rejection rates than the covariate-adjusted methods across most scenarios. However, this apparent power advantage should be interpreted cautiously, as the unadjusted test also exhibited inflated Type I error rates, suggesting that its higher rejection rates may partly reflect inadequate calibration rather than greater statistical efficiency.

The naive variance estimator showed a similar dependence on sex composition to that observed in the Type I error simulations. When males accounted for 20% of the sample, the naive estimator tended to be anti-conservative, whereas it became conservative when the male proportion increased to 80%. These differences in calibration were reflected in the corresponding empirical power patterns. In contrast, the HC1 and groupwise variance estimators exhibited nearly identical empirical power across all simulation scenarios, consistent with their comparable Type I error control.

3.3 Effects of covariate adjustment on sdMAF inference

Covariate adjustment affected sdMAF estimates differently across MAF strata (Figure 4). For variants with MAF below 0.01 and between 0.01 and 0.05, the density plots showed a predominant upward shift relative to the identity line. This corresponds to larger positive sdMAF estimates and negative estimates closer to zero after adjustment. In contrast, variants with MAF between 0.05 and 0.20 and those with MAF at least 0.20 showed a more balanced distribution above and below the identity line, with no similarly pronounced directional shift. These patterns suggest that the effects of genetic principal component adjustment on sdMAF estimates varied across MAF strata in this dataset.

We next compared SNP-level significance from the unadjusted test with that from covariateadjusted regression using Groupwise variance estimates (Figure 5). For variants with MAF below 0.01, the distribution appeared more balanced around the identity line. In the remaining MAF strata, regions of higher variant density were predominantly above the identity line, indicating larger -log 10 (p) values and therefore smaller p-values after adjustment. A similar pattern was observed using HC1 variance estimates (Figure S1). This pattern is consistent with Wang et al.'s observation that the unadjusted sdMAF test can be conservative in samples comprising multiple populations, owing to the use of a population-pooled HWD estimate in variance estimation [1].

At the genome-wide significance threshold of p < 5 × 10 -8 , 22 variants that were nonsignificant in the unadjusted analysis became significant with both HC1 and Groupwise variance estimates. Four additional variants gained significance with HC1 only, and one with Groupwise only, yielding 26 and 23 gained variants, respectively. Six variants significant in the unadjusted analysis were no longer significant with either adjusted variance estimator. Individual-variant estimates and significance results are provided in Table S1.

3.4 Sensitivity to variance estimation

The QQ plots showed that, in the upper tail, the unadjusted test generally produced smaller -log 10 (p) values than the three covariate-adjusted tests (Figure S2). This pattern is consistent with the previously reported conservativeness of unadjusted sdMAF testing in multi-population samples [1], although real-data QQ plots alone do not establish null calibration. To examine the contribution of variance estimation to differences among the adjusted tests, we compared their estimated variances directly (Figure 6).

Overall, HC1 and Groupwise produced larger variance estimates than the Naive estimator. The HC1/Naive and Groupwise/Naive ratios showed greater dispersion among variants with MAF below 0.01, whereas their distributions became more concentrated as MAF increased. In contrast, Groupwise/HC1 ratios were generally close to one. Their medians were slightly below one in the lower-MAF strata, indicating somewhat smaller Groupwise variance estimates relative to HC1, while agreement was closer in the higher-MAF strata.

The corresponding p-value comparisons showed a similar MAF-dependent pattern (Figure S3). For variants with MAF below 0.01, the binned densities were more dispersed around the identity line. As MAF increased, the densities became more closely concentrated along this line, indicating stronger concordance between HC1 and Groupwise inference. Together, these comparisons suggest broadly comparable performance of the two variance estimators in this dataset, with greater differences among lower-frequency variants.

4 Discussion

4.1 Main findings

We developed a regression-based framework for covariate-adjusted sdMAF inference in the X chromosome non-pseudoautosomal region (NPR). The framework provides adjusted subgroup estimates and their joint covariance, allowing inference on prespecified allele-frequency contrasts. In the scenarios examined, the Groupwise variance estimator performed similarly to HC1, whereas the Naive estimator was sensitive to sex sample imbalance. The unadjusted test frequently rejected the simulation null defined by the adjusted sex effect, illustrating the importance of distinguishing marginal and covariate-adjusted targets. The 1000 Genomes application further showed that adjustment can change SNP-level estimates and significance despite broad agreement between adjusted and unadjusted results.

4.2 Interpretation and relation to existing methods

Covariate-adjusted and marginal sdMAF address different questions. Marginal sdMAF describes the allele-frequency difference between the sampled female and male groups, including differences associated with their covariate distributions. The adjusted sex coefficient instead represents the contrast under the specified regression model. Neither target is inherently preferable in every setting; the choice depends on the scientific question and the role of the covariates.

Wang et al.'s omnibus test evaluates the joint null of no sdMAF in any population, whereas our primary application evaluates a single sex contrast adjusted for measured covariates. These hypotheses need not agree: population-specific sex differences may be present even when a specified overall contrast is zero. Their regression already accommodates sex-and population-specific variances. Our work makes the estimation and joint covariance of adjusted subgroup means explicit when nuisance coefficients are estimated from the full sample [5].

The distinction from the RA framework concerns both the inferential target and the treatment of nuisance covariates. Zhang and Sun's autosomal method derives a score test for allelic association, whereas the subsequent X-chromosomal framework primarily addresses HWE and its relationship with sdMAF [4, 6]. Our analysis instead estimates adjusted subgroup allele-frequency contrasts and uses their covariance for Wald inference. The contribution is not the use of a Wald statistic itself, but the explicit covariance treatment and evaluation of residual-variance specifications for covariate-adjusted X-NPR sdMAF.

4.3 Variance estimation and its assumptions

Under male 0/2 coding, hemizygous male and diploid female genotypes have different variance-mean relationships. For example, at the same allele frequency under female HWE, male genotype variance is twice female genotype variance. A common residual variance can therefore be inappropriate.

The simple two-group comparison provides intuition for the Naive estimator's sensitivity to sample imbalance. The pooled variance is weighted toward the larger group. When the smaller group has the larger variance, this can underestimate the variance of the group difference and inflate rejection rates. The reverse arrangement can produce conservative inference. Equal group sizes remove this discrepancy in the simple comparison, but do not generally guarantee validity after covariate adjustment, where the regression weights also matter. These considerations help explain the sex-ratio-dependent patterns observed in our simulations.

Groupwise estimation retains separate residual variance estimates for the prespecified groups. Its close agreement with HC1 in our analyses supports its use in the settings examined, but does not establish general equivalence. Groupwise assumes constant conditional residual variance within each group, whereas HC1 allows individual-level heteroskedasticity [9, 10]. Our simulations already permit within-sex variance variation through covariate-dependent allele frequencies, but do not exhaust the structures that could challenge Groupwise estimation.

4.4 Implications of the 1000 Genomes application

Adjustment for genetic principal components changed both sdMAF estimates and significance for individual variants. The tendency toward smaller adjusted p-values is consistent with Wang et al.'s observation that unadjusted sdMAF testing can be conservative in multi-population samples because population-pooled HWD estimates affect variance estimation [1]. However, our adjusted and unadjusted comparisons differ in both point estimates and estimated uncertainty, so the observed changes cannot be attributed solely to this mechanism.

The variance comparisons provided a more direct assessment of the adjusted estimators because Naive, HC1, and Groupwise use identical coefficient estimates. HC1 and Groupwise generally produced larger variances than Naive and agreed more closely with each other, particularly at higher MAF. Differences were greater among lower-frequency variants. Changes in genome-wide significance demonstrate the practical consequences of adjustment and variance selection, but do not establish which individual findings represent technical artifacts or biological differences.

4.5 Limitations

The adjusted contrast depends on the conditional mean model. HC1 can accommodate heteroskedasticity, but cannot recover the intended conditional contrast when relevant mean-model features are omitted. Groupwise estimation imposes additional variance assumptions whose adequacy should be assessed for the study population.

Both HC1 and Groupwise showed departures from nominal rejection rates in particular low-MAF scenarios, indicating finite-sample limitations. Our simulations used a nominal significance level of 0.05 with 10,000 replications per scenario. They therefore do not establish calibration at the much smaller thresholds used in genome-wide analyses. Further evaluation should examine extremetail behavior, a broader range of within-group variance structures, and sensitivity to mean-model misspecification. The derivations also assume independent observations; related samples require an appropriate covariance specification.

4.6 Extensions and conclusion

The joint covariance formulation allows adjusted subgroup estimates to be combined through prespecified linear contrasts. One application is the covariate-adjusted SPADE extension, in which sex-specific prevalence weights combine case and control estimates [13]. This extension is currently theoretical and requires empirical evaluation, including careful definition of the covariate reference distribution and consideration of uncertainty in prevalence estimates.

X-chromosomal association analysis requires careful treatment of genotype coding and dosage compensation [14]. For the allele-frequency estimand considered here, coding male genotypes as 0/2 places both sexes on the common scale E(G) = 2p, without imposing a dosage-compensation model for a disease phenotype. This rescaling does not equalize genotype variances. Groupwise or HC1 variance estimation addresses the remaining variance differences under its respective assumptions.

Allelic comparisons between sexes also have applications within disease cohorts. Mendes et al. used a case-only sdMAF analysis in an X-chromosome-wide study of ASD, illustrating the relevance of these comparisons beyond QC [15]. Extending covariate-adjusted inference to such applications requires specifying the target contrast, which should not be interpreted automatically as a logistic-scale gene-sex interaction.

The present empirical evidence concerns covariate-adjusted sdMAF in the X-NPR. Within this scope, the framework provides explicit estimation and inference, with Groupwise and HC1 yielding broadly comparable results across the settings examined.
