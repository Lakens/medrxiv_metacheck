What we found

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download files from additional data repositories reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

In Germany, statutory health insurance covers the rental or purchase of breast pumps when a medical indication is present for the mother or infant, and a physician has issued a prescription. However, parents must pay out of pocket for commercially available breast pumps for returning to work or personal circumstances. High indirect costs for continuation of breastfeeding and direct costs for breast pumps and related equipment may pose substantial barriers to breastfeeding families, an issue relevant for health equity. To our knowledge, no studies have assessed the direct financial costs associated with the purchase of commercially available breast pumps. In this cross-sectional study, we examined digital marketplaces in Germany for commercial electric breast pumps between March 2025 and May 2026, across major online platforms. Our objective was to explore pump features that predict price. We identified 282 commercial electric pump models from 108 brands. Basic information was unavailable for half of surveyed breast pump brands. The median price for surveyed commercial electric pumps was €72.70 (IQR: €82.70), with costs ranging widely from €19.99 to €659.57. Prices varied by marketplace, manufacturer location, accessories, pump system type, power source, battery capacity, flange sizes, availability of instruction manuals, and "single user" claims; predicting significantly higher costs. Half of pumps surveyed offered only one flange size, signaling additional expenses for mothers needing alternative sizes. These findings highlight important considerations for maternal health and consumer safety. Health technology assessments and a review of current health policy on reimbursement for commercial electric breast pumps is needed.

1. Introduction

Healthcare systems indisputably save money on healthcare costs when families breastfeed, through both the prevention and lessening of the burden of diseases and associated healthcare expenditures (1) (2) (3) (4) (5). Yet scientific literature has paid far less attention to estimating the direct costs of provision of expressed breast milk to families (6). In recent years, studies have demonstrated that an increasing number of lactating women report expression of breast milk using breast pumps (7) (8) (9) (10). In Germany, prescriptions for breast pumps have steadily increased over the past decade (11). This epidemiological data aligns with commercial market data estimating the German breast pump market at approximately $107.6 million, with projected growth to $170.5 million by 2030 (12). Despite their apparent widespread use, limited empirical research has explored direct costs associated with human milk expression for families. To estimate direct costs, economic studies on breast pumps and related infant feeding equipment are needed.

Broadly speaking, there are two main categories of breast pumps. Multi user, medical grade electric pumps are generally larger, heavier, and equipped with more powerful motors (13). These are typically stationary medical devices that must be plugged into a wall outlet, lack a battery, and are designed for use by multiple individuals, like in healthcare settings (13, 14). In contrast, commercially available electric breast pumps are normally intended for only one user, also known as 'personal-use' pumps, are smaller, lighter, and are usually portable with rechargeable batteries (15). Commercially available breast pumps are also less expensive compared to multi-user pumps. These pumps are typically available for purchase in drug stores, pharmacies or online marketplaces (16).

The cost of commercial breast pumps are an important consideration since statutory health insurance, which covers around 90% of the German population (17), only reimburses breast pump expenses if there is a medical indication in the mother or infant (18). This means that pumps purchased for reasons such as returning to work or school, or lifestyle preferences are not reimbursed (18). As a result, commercial breast pumps electively acquired must be paid by parents out of pocket (18).

To our knowledge, no empirical studies have examined the direct costs of commercially available breast pumps in European contexts. However, this information is essential to understand the potential impact of breast pump costs on parental decisions for the continuation of breastfeeding. It is also important for healthcare professionals supporting parents in informed decision making on infant feeding. The objective of this study is to explore commercially available electric breast pumps sold in Germany, describing their characteristics and costs. Secondary objectives are to analyze the pump characteristics that predict cost and explore the flange sizes sold together with commercial electric breast pumps.

2. Methods

A cross-sectional study was conducted to explore and describe the range of commercially available breast pumps sold on digital marketplaces in Germany. A cost analysis was conducted to examine the relationship between cost and breast pump characteristics. This study is reported based on a modified version of the 'Consolidated Health Economic Evaluation Reporting Standards' (CHEERS II) statement (19). Items 4, 13, 14, 16, 17, 19, 21, 25 were not included since they were not applicable to our study.

2.1 Protocol and registration

We publicly registered the protocol for this study with the Open Science Framework (OSF) before conducting data analysis (20).

2.2 Study population, setting and location

Although no participants were enrolled in this study, its findings are directly relevant to women of reproductive age living in Germany, from varying socioeconomic status (SES), who plan to use a breast pump. The setting of this study is German digital marketplaces. We expected a wider variety of commercially available electric breast pumps to be sold online, compared to brick and mortar stores.

2.3 Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Commercially available breast pumps with an electric motor were included. Medical-grade pumps and manual pumps were excluded.

2.4 Outcome variable

The primary outcome was price. A cross-sectional approach was used due to the volatility of price data, effects of discounts, and rapid product turnover on digital marketplaces. We recorded prices from March to September 2025 in Euros (EUR). We recorded the Recommended Retail Price (RRP) in EUR and discount rate (%) where applicable. Discount rates were applied to final analysis to reflect real-time price of pumps.

2.5 Predictor variables

We extracted predetermined variables on breast pumps, including the brand name, brand headquarter country, country of manufacturer, and availability of the Conformité Européenne (CE) safety certificate. Technical characteristics of pumps were recorded including: the pumping system configuration (open/closed), wearable versus non-wearable design, single-or double-sided, pump size (in m 3 ), battery type (rechargeable or not), battery capacity (mAh), maximum noise level (dB), weight (grams), minimum and maximum vacuum pressure (mmHg), number of milk expression modes, number of suction intensity levels, volume of milk collection containers (in mL), availability of flanges, and flange number and sizes.

We recorded whether the breast pump was intended for a single user or multiple users, and whether resale was prohibited. Marketing claims, such as 'hands-free' and 'BPA-free' were extracted.

2.6 Search strategy

Data sources

We searched the following websites: amazon.de, otto.de, kaufland.de, mediamarkt.de/saturn.de, conrad.de, baby-walz.de, babyone.de, babyshoppen.de, babymarkt.de, rossmann.de, mueller.de, dm.de, medpex.de, shop-apotheke.com, docmorris.de, marktkauf.de, netto-online.de, lidl.de, aldi-nord.de/aldi-sued.de for pumps available online in Germany. We identified brand websites. Where available, we then also searched each brand website.

We used the search term 'electric breast pump' (e.g., German language: 'elektrische Milchpumpe'). Search results frequently included products other than electric breast pumps (e.g., accessories, manual breast pumps, and replacement parts), which were excluded from the study.

Data collection and extraction

A master list was developed in Microsoft Excel comprising all eligible breast pump listings identified across all websites. Breast pump data were taken directly from the brand's website or marketplace. Where information was inconsistent between the brand website and the marketplace, we recorded both but used information from the brand website for the analysis. Where information was missing, we searched the instruction manual. Data was extracted by one reviewer (JGA) and was checked by two others (MT, EO). Extracted data was in German language but translated into English for the purpose of writing this manuscript.

2.7 Data analysis

Data analysis was performed using R-programming version 4.3.3. Distribution of data was checked using density plots, QQ plots and boxplots. Price exhibited right-skewness and heteroscedasticity. Descriptive statistics (median and interquartile range) were used for continuous variables, while frequency and percentage distribution were used for categorical variables. Overall differences in price distributions were evaluated using Mann-Whitney U (Wilcoxon rank-sum test) and the Kruskal-Wallis tests. Pairwise associations were assessed using Pearson's chi-squared test. Holm-adjusted p-values were used in cases for multiple comparisons (21). A Spearman rank correlation was conducted to assess the relationship between continuous variables and price. Multivariate linear regressions were fit to assess predictors of price. Regression analyses modeled a natural logarithmic of price (log-price: %Δ=(e β -1) ×100) as the dependent variable. A backward selection procedure based on Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) was applied for model selection. Regression coefficients were subsequently interpreted as percentage changes in price using exponentiated estimates (22).

2.8 Sensitivity analysis

Given the paucity of studies on this topic, we conducted a sensitivity analysis for predictor variables for the pumps with missing values. Missing data in regression analysis was handled through multiple imputations using predictive mean matching. Imputation was restricted to independent variables with less than 30% missingness (23). Predictor variables with more than 30% missingness were excluded from the main analysis. Missingness is reported in Supplementary Figure 1: Overview of Missing and Observed Data for Commercial Pumps on German Digital Marketplaces.

2.9 Photographs

We chose a very small, purposive selection of commercially available electric breast pumps to photograph. We based the selection on innovations in the breast pump design (multipurpose milk collection vessels, tubing, handsfree design, remote control, touchscreen/buttons, control through a mobile device, etc.).

3 Results

3.1 Included commercial electric breast pumps

In all, 282 commercially available electric breast pumps were identified from 108 brands. A total of 73 websites were searched, including 54 brand websites and 19 online marketplaces. Digital marketplaces include electronic stores, pharmacies, supermarkets, infant or maternity online stores, and general multi-vendor marketplaces. A flowchart of the selection process is outlined in Supplementary Figure 2: Flowchart of selection process for commercial electric breast pumps on digital marketplaces.

3.2 Brands and manufacturers

Of the 108 identified breast pump brands, company headquarter addresses were provided by 66 brands (61%), but only half (n=54, 50%) had an official company website. Of the 66 brands with a physical address, just over half (n=38, 58%) were based in Europe, commonly in Poland (n=9, 14%), with only two brands (3%) headquartered in Germany.

Manufacturing countries were identified for just over half of the surveyed brands (n=61, 56%), with China being the most prevalent country of manufacturing (n=51 brands). A comprehensive list of surveyed breast pumps brands, with headquarters and manufacturer information are listed in Supplementary Table 1 Breast pump brand headquarters and manufacturing country information.

3.3 Characteristics and technological features of commercial electric breast pumps

The proportion of single-sided vs double-sided pumps was similar, with approximately half (n=144, 51.1%) being single-sided. There were 123 (43.6%) wearable pumps. Breast pumps were classified as 'wearable' if they were designed to be worn entirely inside the bra, with an integrated compartment to collect expressed breast milk and without external tubing. 'Handsfree' claims were common (n=148, 52.5%). Pumps had milk collection containers holding up to a median volume of 180 ml (IQR: 30) of expressed breast milk. The median weight of breast pumps was 275g (IQR: 129.9). See Table 1: Descriptive characteristics of breast pump brands with at least four breast pumps Photographs of a purposive sample of commercial electric breast pumps are depicted in Figure 1. Some models offered 'low noise' operation modes, touchscreens, memory functions and smart remote control via apps. Additionally, certain pumps included adapters for feeding bottles or breastmilk storage bags. Other pumps were advertised as waterproof or with lightweight configuration.

Noise and battery life

Noise volume was available for approximately half of pumps (n=150, 53.2%). The median noise volume was 48 dB (IQR: 11dB). Most pumps (n=241, 85.5%) used integrated lithium rechargeable batteries to function. Battery capacity was reported for 217 of these pumps, with a median capacity of 1300 milliamp-hours (mAh) (IQR: 700 mAh). The median operating time for rechargeable pumps was 120 minutes (IQR: 50). This value was reported for 165 of the 241 rechargeable pumps.

Vacuum and cycling

Breast pumps reported a median minimum applied vacuum of 45mmHg (IQR: 25mmHg; n=104, 36.9%), and a median maximum of 285mmHg (IQR: 38); (n=170, 60.3%). Most breast pumps (n=278; 98.6%) mentioned holding multiple 'Modes of milk expression' with a median of 3 modes (IQR: 1), and a median of 9 (IQR: 1) adjustable levels of intensity. Some of the modes of milk expression included: massage, stimulation, pumping, silent pumping, power pumping, deep pumping, expression, suction, deeper suction, deep extraction, sucking, milk drawing, recovery, prolactin mode, dual frequency, automatic, bionic.

Related pumping equipment

Most of the pumps (n=252 89.4%) had additional equipment shipped with the breast pump. Apart from bottle accessories (n=120, 43%) such as teats, and bottle caps, the most common extra items included with the pump were travel bags for carrying the breast pump (n=79, 28%), replacement valves (n=78, 28%), bra adjustment buckles (n=74, 26%), and breast milk storage bags (n=52, 18%). Other less common accessories were flange size rulers (9%), nursing pads (7%), adapters to express milk directly into breast milk bags (4%), brushes to clean pump parts (2%), and remote controls (2%).

Safety and hygiene

'Single-user' use was specified in just over half of the pumps' instructions (n=149, 52.8%). Although marketed for personal use, the instructions for 5 pumps (1.8%) mentioned that except for the pumping kits, the pump itself could be used by multiple users. In 45 pumps (16%) resale was expressly prohibited, whereas no information on this could be found for the remaining pumps.

Over half of the breast pumps surveyed (n=169, 59.9%) provided information on the type of pumping system (open versus closed). The most common terminology used to refer to a pump having a barrier between the pumped breast milk and the internal pump parts was 'closed system' (n=55, 19.5%) and 'anti-backflow/anti-reflux system' (n=78, 27.7%). A further 32 pumps (11.3%) claimed both 'closed' and 'anti-backflow/anti-reflux' systems. No pumps claimed to have an 'open system'. Only 172 pumps (61%) displayed the European Union (EU) Conformité Européenne, known as the 'CE mark. Claims that breast pumps were free from bisphenol A-free (BPA) were reported for most pumps (n=211, 75%).

3.4 Flange characteristics

A total of 271 (96.1%) breast pumps had data on the total number of flanges, also known as 'breast shields,' included with the pump. Half of all pumps (n= 155, 55.0%) offered a single flange, followed by two flanges (n=51, 18.1%). The median number of flanges provided per pump was 1 (IQR 1-3; range 1-7). Information on flange material was available for 228 (80.9%) pumps. Silicone was the most common material (n=205, 72.7%), followed by polypropylene (n=15, 5.3%), alone or combined with other materials (e.g., thermoplastic elastomer), and silicone combined with other materials (n=7, 2.5%). Fifty-four pumps (19.1%) did not specify the flange material.

Data on flange sizes were available for most pumps (n= 207, 73.4%). The reported flange sizes ranged from 15mm to 32mm. Size 24mm was the most frequently available flange size; included with 147 pumps (52.1%). Nineteen pumps (6.7%) marketed their flanges as "universal", and two pumps provided generic sizes of "Small" and "Medium." Only eight pumps (3.9%) provided five or more size options. As shown in Figure 2a and Figure 2b pump companies differed considerably in the range and combination of flange sizes offered. An evaluation of the co-occurrence network for flange sizes demonstrated that sizes 21 and 24 occurred most commonly together (weight = 60), followed by sizes 24, 19, and 21.

3.5 Retail cost of electric breast pumps

All 282 pumps had available price data with the price varying according to product configuration and discount rate. Prices varied widely (range: €19.99 -€679.57). Of the 282 pumps, 48 (17.0%) were discounted. The median list price was €76.35 (IQR €85.68), with a median discounted price of €72.73 (IQR €80.66, V =1172, p<0.001). The median absolute discount was €23.00 (IQR €37.98), corresponding to a median percentage discount of 17.2% (IQR 28.7%; range 2.0%-49.0%).

Prices significantly varied by certain pump characteristics (see Figure 3: Boxplots of the distribution of prices by pump characteristics). In addition to characteristics shown in the boxplots, battery capacity (ρ = 0.384, p < 0.001) and the number of flange sizes was positively correlated with the outcome (ρ = 0.254, p < 0.001). Pump volume demonstrated a weak negative correlation (ρ = -0.221, p < 0.001). No significant correlation was found for the number of intensity levels (ρ = 0.040, p = 0.516).

Significant differences in price were observed between pumps with both single-user claims and an available instruction manual (χ² = 158.69, adjusted p < 0.001), instruction manual availability and continent (χ² = 85.08, adjusted p < 0.001), single-user claims and continent (χ² = 81.43, adjusted p < 0.001), and marketplace and instruction manual availability (χ² = 59.63, adjusted p < 0.001). Pumps with both a single-user claim and an instruction manual had the highest median price (€99.99, IQR €92.04), whereas those lacking both features had the lowest (€47.28, IQR €35.21). Pumps that were non-wearable, intended for single-person use, and did not advertise a hands-free function had a median price of €66.05 (IQR: €43.50-€109.60), whereas wearable double pumps that advertised hands-free operation had a higher median price of €99.99 (IQR: €71.49-€153.99). Overall, prices differed significantly across pump configurations (Kruskal-Wallis χ² = 39.5, df = 4, p < 0.001).

3.5 Predictors of price

Several predictors were associated with meaningful changes in price. Prices sourced exclusively through a digital marketplace were, on average, approximately 31% lower than those sourced through the brand website platform, and those available through both marketplaces and brand websites were about 26% lower. Double-sided pumps were 65% more expensive than single-sided models, while pumps with a "hands-free" claim were 31% more expensive than those without. Products with resale restrictions were 49% higher in price, whereas pumps with more pumping modes were 27% lower in price than those with less. Products from Asia were roughly 28% cheaper than those from Europe. Devices with four intensity levels were 43% less expensive than those with one.

The overall model contained a complete-case analysis of 282 products (n=200), was statistically significant and explained a substantial proportion of the variation in log price (adjusted R² = 0.65, (F (15, 184) = 25.26, p < 0.001). After variable selection, pumps that were resale prohibited, double-sided, and pumps from companies located in the European Union had significantly higher prices. These remained largely unchanged after multiple imputation (see Table 3, & supplementary Table 1).

4. Discussion

Breastfeeding and breast milk expression are not without costs-women who breastfeed bear both direct and indirect financial costs (6). This study found substantial costs associated with commercially available electric breast pumps. Direct costs associated with breast milk expression are a health equity issue, since these costs could be prohibitive to continuation of breastfeeding in mothers with socioeconomic constraints. Socioeconomic markers like low parental education have been shown to be associated with lower breastfeeding rates in cohorts in Germany (24, 25). Therefore, high direct costs for breast milk expression could potentially widen existing breastfeeding disparities and associated health outcomes (6, 18).

Indirect costs associated with breastfeeding and breastmilk expression

Although paid parental leave is ensured by law in Germany, mothers with lower SES are disproportionately pressured to return to work earlier to lower-wage jobs (26). This phenomenon has been shown in other countries and is associated with premature breastfeeding cessation (27, 28). Acceleration in the separation of mothers and infants in the postpartum period heightens the uncompensated 'opportunity costs' of breastfeeding. Opportunity costs for breastfeeding mothers include the four to six hours per day dedicated to expressing breastmilk, time which might be otherwise spent on earning wages (6). In addition, self-employed women in Germany are not given paid time off. Whereas high-income professions tend to offer greater flexibility, such as greater autonomy and accommodations that support continued breastfeeding (6). For this reason, breastfeeding mothers in high-income professions might more easily balance breastfeeding with professional responsibilities (6). Comparatively, women working in low-wage positions might have limited workplace flexibility, unpredictable shifts, excessive workload, inadequate childcare facilities at work, and inadequate facilities for milk expression, making continued breastfeeding very challenging or impossible (27, 28). Consequently, structural socioeconomic inequalities are strengthened by unequal distribution of hidden labor costs which remain largely unrecognized and uncompensated by healthcare systems globally. Finally, the additional time required for feeding infants expressed breast milk (including storage, bottle-feeding, washing, and sterilization) compared to direct breastfeeding, should not be overlooked when calculating the full indirect cost burden associated with pumping.

Coverage of direct costs for breast milk expression: benefits and remaining gaps

Reimbursement policies for direct costs associated with breast pumps in Germany differs from countries like the United States, where public health insurance covers the expenses of commercial electric breast pumps (29). Implementation of the Patient Protection and Affordable Care Act in the US requires new private insurance plans to provide breast pumps with no cost sharing since 2012 (30). This policy change showed trickle effects, demonstrating an uptick in the technological innovations in breast pumps (29), and an increase in the monthly rate of breast pump claims, which also translated into higher levels of breastfeeding for women with both private and public insurance (30). Breast pump use in the US context has in general been associated with a longer breastfeeding duration, even in marginalized women who are disproportionately affected by breastfeeding inequities (31). Despite healthcare coverage for commercial breast pump expenses, costs for breast milk expression for US families are not completely covered; parents report out-of-pocket expenditures for breastmilk storage bags, accessories and replacement parts (32).

Direct costs related to breast pump characteristics and innovation

We found basic technical and location information for commercially available breast pumps was missing on digital marketplaces. Pumps that had more complete information were associated with higher prices. We suspect this is because regulatory compliance often requires investments in administrative processes, legal support, and customer service infrastructure. Products that are primarily produced in the EU showed a significant relationship with higher prices compared to those outside the EU. This supports the notion that availability of contact information and EU regulation may affect pump prices.

The heterogeneity in breast pump pricing is reflected in the very broad price range in our data set (range: €19.99 to €659.57). The observed variation in prices appears to reflect differences in underlying pump design, functionality, and intended use. Direct costs for the commercially available electric breast pumps surveyed varied significantly by source of information, manufacturer location, availability of accessories, double-sided pumping, power source, battery capacity, flange sizes, availability of instruction manuals, and "single user" claims, which all predicted significantly higher costs. Higher prices associated with some pump characteristics may be due to technological innovations or greater engineering complexity. Features such as double-pump capability, rechargeable batteries, hands-free operation, and milk collection systems require additional components and design considerations, which are likely to increase research and development and manufacturing costs and respective retail prices. Whether these characteristics translate into superior clinical or breastfeeding outcomes, however, is uncertain. One study of physicians using wearable pumps showed a longer breastfeeding duration, when compared to traditional pumps (33). There are comparatively few published pump efficacy studies encompassing a large sample of pumps, nor could we identify any health technology assessments in this area.

Price and health equity considerations

These findings are relevant for mothers from lower SES who in the context of gaps in health insurance reimbursement in Germany might not be able to afford premium-priced pumps. Mothers with low SES are more likely to choose less expensive pumps because they are more affordable. However, lower-priced commercial breast pumps in our data set were statistically associated with less characteristics related to consumer accountability. For example, lower priced pumps were less likely to have a manufacturer website, manufacturer contact information, CE certificates, less likely to be manufactured in the EU, and in general were lesser-known brands with fewer pumps available on the market. These results have health equity implications, since mothers who purchase cheaper pumps in digital markets will receive less information about these pumps from manufacturers.

Price likely also indicates overall lower technological quality breast pumps; however, we are unable to draw strong conclusions related to the quality of commercially available breast pumps surveyed. This is because we did not directly measure technical performance indicators such as milk removal performance, accuracy of stated vacuum levels, battery capacity, or noise level. Therefore, we are unsure about the accuracy of reported information on digital marketplaces or how prices relate to the technical quality of commercially available pumps. Future studies should therefore explore whether cheaper pumps are of lower technical quality and whether they are associated with less maternal satisfaction or undesirable experiences with milk expression.

Our results demonstrated that double-sided commercially electric breast pumps were 65% more expensive than single-sided pumps. Although the direct costs associated with doublesided designs were higher, this additional upfront expenditure could translate to reduced indirect costs for mothers who pump their milk. Double-sided pumping has been shown to remove more milk and result in more milk ejections than pumping from each breast sequentially, thereby reducing the total pumping time (34). These indirect savings may include less time away from paid work or other responsibilities. Direct cost differences between single and double sided pumping raise important health equity considerations within the German healthcare system, which currently reimburses only one flange even with double sided electric breast pumps. This reimbursement structure effectively shifts indirect cost burden to families, which may disproportionately affect families with fewer financial resources.

There appears to be an increase in technological innovation in breast pumps, including wearable pump designs that allow for hands-free use, along with wireless and rechargeable functionality. We found that pumps with a "hands-free" claim were 31% more expensive than those without this feature or claim. While "hands-free" designs have higher direct cost for families, these designs may reduce certain indirect costs by allowing mothers to multitask while pumping, thereby lowering the opportunity cost of the time spent expressing milk, and allowing them to reach their breast milk feeding goals (33).

Maternal experiences with commercial breast pumps

More studies on maternal experiences with pumping are needed, like comfort and degree of ease of use. Pumping does not only involve removing milk and feeding it to the baby, but managing all tasks around it, such as washing and sterilizing equipment, and managing milk storage (35). Surveys from higher income mothers have reported maternal needs related to pump portability, mobility, ease of use, ease of cleaning, fast and efficient milk extraction, comfort, discretion and low noise (32, 36).

Food safety considerations

Personal use pumps hold the potential to cross-contaminate expressed breastmilk if mothers share or resell breast pumps intended for a single-user. However, second-hand online marketplaces list pre-owned personal-use breast pumps and related pump parts for resale. Breast pumps labeled as "not suitable for resale" therefore may reduce the risk of cross contamination. To our knowledge, the practice of resale of pre-used personal-use breast pumps and associated hygiene risks have not yet been empirically explored. In general, there is a paucity of studies on breast milk expression and pump hygiene in realworld settings (37). National guidance in Germany has been issued on the importance of proper hygiene when handling expressed milk and breast pumps at home and in clinic settings (38). However, hygiene aspects related to resale of personal use pumps and sharing pumps were not addressed. Therefore, our findings underscore gaps both in research and in policy guidance for consumer safety.

Device safety

Many of the breast pumps surveyed did not have instructions for use available online. This is an important aspect for consumer safety, considering potential adverse events resulting from malfunction or improper use of breast pumps (39, 40). These findings correspond to other studies showing that oftentimes women are left to figure out pumping on their own, which shows women do not feel well-informed about how to use breast pumps to express milk (32, 41). Importantly, internet information on this topic may be inconsistent and not evidenceinformed (42).

Improperly fitting flanges may cause or exacerbate nipple or breast pain, nipple trauma, and inadequate emptying of the breasts, which can, in turn, lead to infections or discontinuation of breastfeeding (15). Our findings demonstrate that commercially available breast pumps do not offer a range of flange sizes, with half of all pumps offering only a single flange size option. These findings highlight a critical gap in the availability of individualized flange sizes. Future research should evaluate how manufacturers, retailers, and health systems can improve access to appropriately fitting flanges to determine whether such efforts help reduce nipple trauma, pain, inadequate breast emptying, and early breastfeeding cessation.

Limitations

Due to the cross-sectional study design, we were unable to capture volatility of price data over time. In addition, our study surveyed digital marketplaces but not brick and mortar stores. Therefore, we cannot make comparisons of costs of pumps online versus in stores.

Conclusion

This study provides first insights into commercially available electric breast pumps in Germany and their associated prices. Commercial electric breast pumps sold on digital marketplaces have strikingly incomplete information that should therefore be more closely monitored by regulatory authorities. Accurate and complete information is of great importance for healthcare professionals and families and supports safe and appropriate use of medical devices (43, 44). Manufacturers should consider offering a broader range of breast-flange sizes to facilitate appropriate flange fit and provide information to consumers on appropriate flange fitting. We recommend health technology assessments and further studies on direct expenditures for infant feeding equipment in order to gain accurate estimations of the direct costs of infant feeding for families. Future health policy in this area should consider the substantial costs of breast pumps for families and the potential impact of current reimbursement policies on breastfeeding inequities in Germany.
