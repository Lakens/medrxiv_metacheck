Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). Statcheck might still miss one-sided tests that are described in another sentence, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

If you want to extend the package to be able to download files from additional data repositories reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Plasma brain-derived p-tau217 and its ratio over brain-derived tau improve the specificity of the leading blood-based Alzheimer's disease biomarker p-tau217.

.

INTRODUCTION

Alzheimer's disease (AD) remains the most common cause of dementia worldwide, affecting 55 million individuals globally and costing over 1.3 trillion US dollars (1). AD pathology -with the hallmark features of amyloid-beta (Aβ) plaques and tau neurofibrillary tangles (NFTs) -starts to manifest years before clinical symptoms appear, and many more years before dementia onset (2) (3) (4). This clinical-biological disconnect necessitates the development and application of high-performance biomarkers to enable early and accurate detection, prediction and monitoring, as well as to support therapeutic and observational trials (5) (6) (7) (8). While molecular imaging and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) biomarkers fulfill these criteria, their limited scalability, high costs and invasiveness are notable limitations against widespread implementation in specialist clinical settings as well as in primary care and population-based screenings (9) (10) (11) (12). In recent years, well-validated blood biomarkers have become available as viable alternatives with added advantages of scalability, accessibility and cost effectiveness (11) (12) (13). Among these, plasma tau phosphorylated at threonine 217 (p-tau217) has shown the highest diagnostic accuracies, and the strongest and most consistent correspondence with brain Aβ and tau NFT pathology (14) (15) (16) (17). Consequently, clinical practice guidelines recommend p-tau217 testing as an integral part of the clinical diagnostic and prognostic workup for AD (18, 19). Furthermore, commercial plasma p-tau217 tests with regulatory approval or Laboratory Developed Test status are now available, facilitating clinical uptake (20) (21) (22) (23) (24).

Despite the high performance, plasma p-tau217 testing has its own challenges (25). For example, plasma p-tau217 diagnostic accuracies are substantially decreased in individuals with common comorbidities of aging such as hypertension, myocardial infarction, diabetes, chronic kidney disease (CKD), hypercholesterolemia, and obesity (26) (27) (28). Moreover, although it is highly specific for AD in the context of neurodegenerative diseases, plasma p-tau217 is also elevated in non-AD conditions such as amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS), HIV-associated neurocognitive disorders, Niemann Pick disease type C, systemic amyloidosis and CKD (29) (30) (31) (32) (33). These findings can be partly explained by the presence of p-tau217 signal not only in central nervous system (CNS) tissue (34, 35) but also in peripheral tissues such as muscle biopsies of ALS patients and unaffected controls (29) and in blood samples from individuals diagnosed with polyneuropathy due to systemic amyloidosis (33). The reduced biological specificity and diagnostic precision caused by these mixed-origin measurements raises concern that conventional plasma p-tau217 may not exclusively capture CNS-derived neuropathology.

A central challenge, therefore, is to distinguish CNS-derived pathological tau species from peripheral background signal. Tau, encoded by the MAPT gene, is expressed as multiple isoforms that can be broadly divided into low-molecular-weight (LMW) and high-molecular-weight (HMW, or big tau) forms based on inclusion of exon 4a (36, 37). LMW tau isoforms constitute the predominant tau species in the CNS (38), whereas big tau is primarily expressed in the peripheral nervous system (PNS) and peripheral organs such as the heart and liver (39) (40) (41) (42). We therefore hypothesized that selectively quantifying circulating LMW tau phosphorylated at threonine 217, a brain-enriched species, would enhance biological specificity and clinical performance.

To test this hypothesis, we employed the Nucleic acid Linked Immuno-Sandwich Assay (NULISA™) proximity ligation proteomic platform (43) to quantify CNS-relevant plasma biomarkers for optimal AD diagnosis (see Data S1 for biomarker list). Using this platform, we evaluated the clinical performance of plasma brain-derived-p-tau217 (BD-p-tau217) and BD-p-tau217% vs. other biomarkers. While BD-p-tau217 is a single immunoassay, BD-p-tau217% takes a ratio of BD-p-tau217 to the total amount of phosphorylation-independent CNS-derived tau protein (i.e., BD-tau) in plasma expressed as a percentage, leveraging our innovation of plasma BD-tau as a biomarker of CNS-selective tau protein in circulating blood (44, 45) (Figure S1). We demonstrate that BD-p-tau217% and BD-p-tau217 outperform conventional plasma p-tau217, p-tau217/Aβ42 (an Food and Drugs Administration [FDA] approved plasma AD biomarker), and p-tau217% (non-CNS-selective immunoassay alternative to BD-p-tau217%, calculated as a ratio of p-tau217/total tau) originally described by Barthélemy et al (46), across multiple diagnostic contexts, including in individuals with common age-related comorbidities, and provides superior prediction of future clinical progression.

RESULTS

Cohort characteristics

We selected three cohorts (clinical [n=1386], neuropathology [n=323], and neuroimaging [n=242]) from the University of Pittsburgh Alzheimer's Disease Research Center (PittADRC) academic memory clinic. The cohorts included participants recruited between 1993 and 2023 and monitored clinically for up to two decades. Figure S2 summarizes the participant selection criteria, relationship between cohorts, and the derivation of subcohorts employed in longitudinal analyses. The clinical cohort comprised 53.0% females, 91.5% non-Hispanic Whites, and 46.7% APOE4 carriers; these characteristics were similar in the neuropathology and neuroimaging cohorts. The median (interquartile range [IQR]) age at blood draw was 71.4 (12.8), 68.5 (12.8) and 74.5 (14.6) years for the clinical, neuroimaging and neuropathology cohorts, respectively.

The neuropathology cohort included individuals with matched antemortem clinical and biomarker assessments in addition to postmortem autopsy diagnosis. Median (IQR) blood-to-death interval was 5.9 (5.4) years, and there were 17.3%, 14.6% and 68.1% participants with low, intermediate and severe Alzheimer's disease neuropathological change (ADNC), respectively. The neuroimaging cohort included 242 participants who underwent positron emission tomography (PET) imaging for Aβ plaques (Aβ-PET; 53.1% positive) and/or NFTs (tau-PET; 54.1% positive). The clinical cohort, focusing on clinical diagnoses alone, included 29.7% clinically unimpaired (CU), 20.7% mild cognitive impairment (MCI), 34.6% AD dementia (probable/possible AD), and 14.9% non-AD dementia. Table 1 summarizes participant characteristics for cohorts while Tables S1-3 show cohort-specific characteristics.

Additionally, we included 1428 participants (529 CU, 676 MCI, 223 dementia) from the Alzheimer's Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI; phase 1 to 4) as an external validation cohort. The median age was 72.8 (10.3) years, consisting of 47.3% female, 95.3% non-Hispanic White, and 45.4% APOE4 carriers. Aβ-PET data were available for 999 participants (49.5% positive), and tau-PET data for 360 participants (20.6% positive). Discriminative accuracy for ADNC in the Pitt-ADRC neuropathology cohort

Plasma BD-p-tau217% was the topmost elevated biomarker which best distinguished the low, intermediate and severe ADNC cases, followed by BD-p-tau217 (Fig. 1). For the primary analyses discriminating low ADNC (i.e., minimal or no ADrelated neuropathologic change) from severe ADNC (definite AD diagnosis), BD-p-tau217% had the largest age-and sex-adjusted Cohen's d effect size of 2.53, relative to 2.12, 1.87, 1.77 and 1.86 for BD-p-tau217, p-tau217%, p-tau217 and p-tau217/Aβ42, respectively (Fig. 1A). BD-p-tau217% and BD-p-tau217 achieved equivalent AUCs of 0.93 each, both being significantly higher than the AUC of 0.90 (95%CI=0.83-0.93) for p-tau217 (p=0.003) (Fig. 1A and Data S2-3). Although p-tau217% showed a numerically higher AUC than p-tau217, the difference was not significant. BD-p-tau217% correctly classified 93% of individuals, compared with 85%-90% for the other biomarkers.

In secondary analyses differentiating low from intermediate ADNC, BD-p-tau217% was again the bestperforming biomarker with the highest effect size (1.05, vs. 0.60-0.85 for all others; Fig. 1B). BD-p-tau217% achieved an AUC of 0.85 (95%CI=0.75-0.91; 82% correctly classified), yielding a higher AUC than p-tau217% (0.81[0.70-0.88]; 77% correctly classified), and significantly outperforming each of BD-p-tau217 (0.76[95%CI=0.65-0.85; 75% correctly classified]; p=0.002), p-tau217/Aβ42 (0.75[95%CI=0.62-0.82; 72% correctly classified]; p=0.021) and p-tau217 (0.74[95%CI=0.63-0.83; 71% correctly classified]; p=0.005) (Fig. 1B and Data S2-3).

Plasma BDptau217% and BDptau217 achieved equivalent AUCs of 0.86 (95%CI=0.79-0.90) and 0.85 (95%CI=0.79-90) respectively, both significantly outperforming ptau217%, ptau217, and ptau217/Aβ42, whose AUCs ranged from 0.71 to 0.76 (p<0.01). These results were corroborated by boxplots showing the least group-level overlaps between ADNC categories for BD-p-tau217% and BD-p-tau217 vs. the other analytes (Fig. 1D).

Consistent results were recorded when the neuropathology cohort was assessed according to brain NFT Braak stage (Fig. 2) and neuritic plaque density (Fig. S3). Plasma BD-p-tau217% was the topmost hit and had the largest effect sizes and highest AUCs across all pathology comparisons, except for contrasting between moderate and frequent neuritic plaque density where all biomarkers performed equivalently (Fig. S3), and between primary agerelated tauopathy (PART) and midBraak stages where BD-p-tau217% outperformed BD-p-tau217 and p-tau217 but not the ratio-based biomarkers i.e., p-tau217/Aβ42 and p-tau217% (Fig. 2). BDptau217% demonstrated significantly higher AUCs than ptau217 for distinguishing low from frequent neuritic plaque density, as well as for all comparisons across NFT Braak stages. BDptau217% also outperformed BDptau217 in distinguishing PART from midBraak.

Relation with in vivo Aβ and tau PET status in the Pitt-ADRC neuroimaging cohort

Plasma BD-p-tau217% levels best discriminated abnormal vs. normal Aβ-PET (A+ vs. A-) groups, with the greatest effect size (2.18 vs. 1.65 for p-tau217, 1.53 for p-tau217% and 1.34 for p-tau217/Aβ42) and an AUC of 0.94(95%CI=0.90-0.96; 90% correctly classified). This performance was significantly stronger (p<0.001) than the AUCs of 0.87(95%CI=0.81-0.91; 80% correctly classified), 0.87(95%CI=0.82-0.92; 83% correctly classified), and 0.82(95%CI=0.76-0.87; 76% correctly classified) for p-tau217, p-tau217%, and p-tau217/Aβ42, respectively, but not BD-p-tau217 (0.93; 95%CI=0.89-0.95; Fig. 3A-B and Data S2-3).

Regarding separating tau PET-positive (T+) from tau PET-negative (T-) individuals, BD-p-tau217% had the greatest effect size (adjusted Cohen's d =1.76 vs. 1.28-1.61 for all others; Fig. 3C). However, there was no significant difference in classification accuracy despite numerically higher AUCs for BD-p-tau217%. The AUC for BD-p-tau217% for distinguishing T+ from T-individuals was 0.90(95%CI=0.81-0.95) compared to 0.88(95%CI=0.78-94) for BD-p-tau217, 0.84(95%CI=0.72-0.92) for p-tau217, 0.84(95%CI=0.74-0.91) for p-tau217%, and 0.81(95%CI=0.70-0.89) for p-tau217/Aβ42 (Fig. 3C-D and Data S2-3).

Relation to clinical diagnosis of AD vs. non-AD in Pitt-ADRC clinical cohort

For differentiating AD dementia from CU, MCI and other neurodegenerative diseases (i.e., non-AD dementia), plasma BD-p-tau217% was the most promising across all biomarkers, had the greatest effect sizes, and the highest AUCs (Fig. 4 and Data S2-3). For instance, the AUC of plasma BD-p-tau217% for distinguishing AD dementia from CU was 0.87(95%CI=0.84-0.89; 82% correctly classified) which significantly outperformed BD-p-tau217 (0.85[95%CI=0.83-0.88]; p=0.014), p-tau217 (0.81[95%CI=0.78-0.84]; p<0.001), p-tau217% (0.84[95%CI=0.81-0.87]; p=0.003) and p-tau217/Aβ42 (0.79[95%CI=0.75-0.81]; p<0.001).

In discriminating AD dementia vs. non-AD dementias, plasma BD-p-tau217% had an AUC of 0.79(95%CI=0.75-0.83; 74% correctly classified) which was significantly superior to those of BD-p-tau217, p-tau217/Aβ42, and p-tau217 with classification accuracies of 70%, 71%, and 66% respectively, and numerically higher than that of p-tau217% (0.77[95%CI=0.73-0.80]; Fig. 4B and Data S2-3). In separating MCI and AD dementia, BDptau217% and p-tau217% achieved a similar effect size of 0.75 and AUC of 0.71, also significantly higher than those of ptau217, p-tau217/Aβ42, and BD-p-tau217 (Fig. 4C and Data S2-3). Figure 4D shows boxplots of plasma p-tau levels according to clinical diagnosis.

Improved discriminative accuracies in individuals with comorbidities in Pitt-ADC clinical cohort

In the clinical cohort, we evaluated diagnostic accuracies of the plasma biomarkers in discriminating clinically classified AD dementia from CUs with six common comorbidities, including diabetes, obesity (body mass index >=30), any cardiovascular disease (CVD), hypercholesterolemia and active depression, based on information collected from health information interviews (see Materials and Methods).

Among participants whose health information indicated an absence of the respective comorbidities (i.e., without comorbidity), plasma BDptau217% consistently maintained a higher diagnostic accuracy, with significantly greater performance than ptau217 and ptau217/Aβ42 across all subanalyses, mirroring the superiority observed in the full cohort (Fig. 5). The AUCs for BD-p-tau217% ranged between 0.87 and 0.90, compared with smaller AUCs between 0.80 and 0.83 for both p-tau217 and p-tau217/Aβ42, and 0.84 to 0.88 for p-tau217%.

In all sub-analysis focusing on participants with the indicated comorbidities, BD-p-tau217% maintained higher AUCs in all conditions of interest (except for "any CVD" where all AUCs were statistically indifferent). BD-p-tau217% maintained relatively stable AUCs in the presence of comorbidities, with AUCs ranging from 0.84 to 0.88. In participants with comorbid obesity, hypertension, and hypercholesterolemia, BD-p-tau217% and BD-p-tau217 remained significantly more accurate than p-tau217 and p-tau217/Aβ42 in identifying AD dementia vs. CUs (Fig. 5). The AUCs for BDptau217% were 0.84(95%CI=0.77-0.90) for obesity, 0.88(95%CI=0.83-0.91) for hypertension, and 0.88(95%CI=0.84-0.91) for hypercholesterolemia. In contrast, the corresponding AUCs for ptau217 were 0.75(95%CI=0.67-0.83), 0.83(95%CI=0.78-0.88) and 0.84(95%CI=0.79-0.88). For p-tau217/Aβ42, they were 0.76(95%CI=0.67-0.83), 0.80(95%CI=0.74-0.84) and 0.82(95%CI=0.77-0.86).

Focusing on participants with comorbid diabetes and active depression, BD-p-tau217% showed significantly better performance than p-tau217%. Notably, p-tau217% showed relatively large (~10%) reductions in AUC values among participants with vs. without diabetes and active depression, resulting in significantly inferior accuracies compared with BDptau217%. For p-tau217%, the AUC values decreased sharply from 0.86(95%CI=0.77-0.92) to 0.78(95%CI=0.68-0.85) for those without vs. with diabetes, respectively, and from 0.88(95%CI=0.85-0.91) to 0.78(95%CI=0.70-0.84) when considering participants without vs. with active depression (Fig. 5).

Prediction of future clinical progression

We next evaluated performance in predicting future diagnostic progression among individuals who were CU or had MCI at baseline (participant characteristics in Table S3). We defined optimal thresholds for each biomarker by contrasting A+ and A-participants in the neuroimaging cohort (Data S2) and compared clinical progression trajectories in individuals with high vs. low levels (Fig. 6). Dichotomization by plasma BD-p-tau217% resulted in the clearest between-group separation. In CU, the median survival times (MSTs) were 8.0 vs. 19.0 years (ΔMST=11), and in those with MCI, 3.1 vs. 12.0 years (ΔMST=8.9) for individuals with elevated vs. normal BD-p-tau217% values, respectively. BDptau217 showed similarly robust discrimination in MCI but less so in CU (corresponding ΔMSTs of 8.7 in CU and 8.9 in MCI). In contrast, non-CNS-selective ptau217based biomarkers exhibited weaker separation. The ΔMSTs for ptau217 were 6.7 in CU and 7.9 in MCI. For ptau217%, the ΔMSTs were 5.9 and 4.9, and for ptau217/Aβ42, 5.1 and 7.3 (Fig. 6A-B).

Consistently, dichotomizing participants into high versus low plasma BDptau217% most effectively separated individuals by their incidence rate (IR) and Cox hazard ratio (HR) for clinical progression (Fig. 6C). High BDptau217% was associated with the greatest risk elevation in both CU and MCI groups, with HRs of 3.21 (95%CI=2.20-4.66) and 3.65 (95%CI=2.47-5.39), respectively, compared with the corresponding low BDptau217% groups. Dichotomization using BDptau217% also produced the largest separation in IRs, with 8.68 versus 2.45 per 100 personyears (ΔIR=6.23) in CU, and 24.38 versus 6.06 per 100 personyears (ΔIR=18.32) in MCI.

When categorized according to plasma biomarker quartiles, the MST for plasma BD-p-tau217% showed a more consistent stepwise reduction across quartiles than the other biomarkers in CU, with much clearer doubling patterns when comparing quartile-1 with quartile-2 and so on (Fig. S4A). Stratification by BDptau217% quartiles also produced the largest overall separation in IR and HR values in CU individuals (Fig. S5A). For example, the HR for BDptau217% quartile 4 relative to quartile 1 was 8.17 (95%CI=4.16-16.06), higher than the corresponding HRs of 5.17 (95%CI=2.69-9.93) for BD-p-tau217, 3.87 (95%CI=2.02-7.40) for p-tau217%, 2.99 (95%CI=1.54-5.82) for p-tau217, and 2.24 (95%CI=1.21-4.14) for ptau217/Aβ42. Among individuals with MCI, BDptau217% also provided clearer risk stratification across quartiles than BDptau217, ptau217, and ptau217/Aβ42. Ptau217% showed slightly greater quartilebased separation in IR and HR than BDptau217% (Fig. S4B and S5B).

When analyzing clinical progression by progression status (progressor vs. stable; participant characteristics in Table S8), plasma BD-p-tau217% emerged as the strongest predictor of 5-year progression in both CU (progressing to MCI or AD dementia) and individuals with MCI (progressing to AD dementia; Fig. S6). BDp-tau217% had adjusted Cohen's d effect sizes of 1.39 and 0.92 for MCI and CU respectively. In contrast, p-tau217 managed only 0.69 and 0.59. In agreement, BD-p-tau217% recorded the highest AUC of 0.83 (95%CI=0.80-0.85) to distinguish progressor MCI from stable MCI, which was significantly superior to 0.78 (95%CI=0.75-0.80; p=0.013) for BD-p-tau217, 0.69 (95%CI=0.66-0.72; p<0.001) for p-tau217 and 0.65 (95%CI=0.62-0.68; p<0.001) for p-tau217/Aβ42 (Fig. S6A and Data S2-3). In terms of separating progressor controls from stable controls, we found no statistical difference in the AUC values despite that of BD-p-tau217% being numerically higher than the rest (Fig. S6B). Yet, progressor controls had significantly higher BD-p-tau217% and other p-tau217 biomarker levels than stable controls, just as progressor MCIs had higher levels than stable MCIs (Fig. S6C).

Sensitivity analyses

Statistical details of all biomarker performance comparisons beyond AUCs, including accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, positive (PPV) and negative predictive values (NPV) are shown in Data S2. DeLong tests for the significance of AUC comparisons are provided in Data S3. Risk tables of all survival analysis were available in Data S4. In addition to the AUCs and effect size findings described above, plasma BD-p-tau217% maintained superiority over p-tau217 and p-tau217/Aβ42 (and in several key comparisons p-tau217%) in nearly all primary and secondary analyses when comparing other important metrics such as sensitivity, specificity, accuracy, NPV and PPV, further reinforcing the strong diagnostic value of BD-p-tau217% (Data S2).

All AUC values described above were derived from biomarkeronly models. AUCs from models additionally adjusted for age and sex are provided in Data S5 and show trends consistent with those observed in the biomarkeronly analyses. All effect sizes reported above were adjusted for age and sex, demographic factors known to influence AD blood biomarker levels. As a sensitivity analysis, we also evaluated effect sizes for the same comparisons using biomarkeronly models, as well as models adjusted for a broader set of covariates (age, sex, race, education, and APOE4 carrier status; Data S6). Under both modeling strategies, BDptau217% consistently demonstrated the largest effect sizes across nearly all comparisons, ranging from 0.75 to 2.55 in biomarkeronly models and from 0.66 to 2.72 in the fully adjusted models. In contrast, the corresponding effect size ranges were 0.40-2.04 and 0.39-2.10 for ptau217, 0.40-2.07 and 0.35-2.10 for ptau217/Aβ42, 0.66-2.23 and 0.54-2.38 for BDptau217, and 0.57-1.89 and 0.48-2.01 for p-tau217%.

External validation in the ADNI cohort

Results in the ADNI cohort mirrored those in the Pitt-ADRC cohorts. BD-p-tau217% outperformed all other NULISA biomarkers in detecting abnormal Aβ-PET and tau-PET and in discriminating CU from AD (Fig. 7). For discriminating A+ from A-participants, BD-p-tau217% achieved the largest effect size of 2.20, followed by 1.94 for BD-p-tau217, 1.87 for p-tau217/Aβ42, 1.77 for p-tau217%, and 1.76 for p-tau217 (Fig. 7A). BD-p-tau217% also achieved the highest AUC (0.93; 95% CI =0.91-0.95), significantly exceeding those of BD-p-tau217 (0.92; 95% CI=0.90-0.94; p = 0.013), p-tau217/Aβ42 (0.91; 95% CI=0.89-0.93; p = 0.007), p-tau217 (0.90; 95% CI=0.88-0.92; p < 0.001), and p-tau217% (0.89; 95% CI=0.87-0.91; p < 0.001). For detecting tau-PET positivity, BD-p-tau217% achieved an effect size of 2.27, closely followed by 2.22 for BD-p-tau217 (Fig. 7B). Both biomarkers achieved similar AUCs of 0.92, significantly higher than that of p-tau217 (0.89 [95%CI=0.84-0.92]; p = 0.029 and p = 0.012 for BD-p-tau217% and BD-p-tau217, respectively). In addition, the AUC of BD-p-tau217% was significantly higher than that of p-tau217/Aβ42 (0.88 [95%CI=0.81-0.92]; p = 0.046).

In discriminating CU from AD, BD-p-tau217% and BD-p-tau217 achieved the largest effect sizes (both 1.58) and highest AUCs (both 0.92), compared with effect sizes of 1.24-1.35 and AUCs of 0.88-0.90 for p-tau217-based measures (Fig. 7C). The AUCs of both BD-p-tau217% and BD-p-tau217 were significantly higher than that of p-tau217% (p<0.001). Similar superiority of BD-p-tau217-based measures over p-tau217based analytes were observed for discriminating MCI from CU and AD from MCI (Fig. S7).

When dichotomized into high-and low-biomarker groups, all five biomarkers showed similar performances for predicting clinical progression among baseline CU participants, with HRs ranging from 2.38 to 2.60 (Fig. 7Di). In contrast, among baseline MCI participants, BD-p-tau217% and BD-p-tau217 provided greater separation of progression risk, with ΔIRs of 13.49 and 14.70 per 100 person-years and HRs of 6.04 and 5.43, respectively, compared with ΔIRs <12 and HRs of 4.49-4.60 for the other biomarkers (Fig. 7Dii).

Evaluation of alternative ratio-based biomarkers

We next evaluated whether alternative ratio-based biomarkers provided improvements similar to those observed for BD-p-tau217% relative to BD-p-tau217. Figure S8 summarizes the effect sizes across neuropathological, PET, and clinical outcomes for combinations of BD-p-tau217 or p-tau217 normalized to BD-tau, total tau, Aβ42, and Aβ40. Among all ratios examined, only BD-p-tau217/BD-tau (BD-p-tau217%) consistently improved performance over the corresponding single biomarker across both the Pitt-ADRC and ADNI cohorts. In contrast, normalization of BD-p-tau217 to total tau, Aβ42, or Aβ40 generally reduced performance, with the largest decline observed for Aβ40, followed by total tau and Aβ42. Normalization of p-tau217 to Aβ42 modestly improved the discrimination of low versus severe ADNC but did not consistently enhance the detection of Aβ-PET or tau-PET positivity or the discrimination of clinical diagnostic groups across cohorts (Figure S8).

DISCUSSION

We have shown that selective quantification of brain-derived p-tau217 substantially improves the diagnostic and prognostic performance of plasma p-tau217 using data from independent cohorts. Plasma BD-p-tau217% and BD-p-tau217 were significantly more accurate than standard p-tau217-based biomarkers, including p-tau217 alone, p-tau217% and the p-tau217/Aβ42 ratio as biomarkers for AD. These superior accuracies were observed across neuropathologically-verified ADNC, amyloid-PET, tau-PET, clinical diagnostic, and longitudinal progression outcomes. Furthermore, the plasma BD-p-tau217 versions remained the best-performing biomarker with the largest accuracies to detect AD in older adults with common comorbidities. These findings demonstrate that BD-p-tau217% and BD-p-tau217 capture AD pathological signatures with greater specificity and sensitivity and are much less prone to unwanted impacts from comorbid conditions.

This work introduces a conceptual advance: molecular source specificity as a principle for blood-based biomarker development. Instead of measuring total circulating analyte concentration alone (as is being done for current p-tau217-based methods), enriching for predominantly CNS-derived pools can substantially enhance disease relevance and robustness. This is the most comprehensive study yet on BD-p-tau, across multiple prognostic, diagnosis and longitudinal angles, extending recent studies limited to Aβ-PETcharacterized cohorts (47, 48). Moreover, it is the first to introduce the BD-p-tau217% concept, building on its early adoption for standard p-tau217 (46).

While both BD-p-tau217% and BD-p-tau217 outperformed conventional p-tau217 and p-tau217-based biomarkers in most analyses, the former had a slight advantage for the following reasons: (1) the ratio appeared less prone to comorbidity effects; (2) BD-p-tau217% had numerically higher AUCs, accuracies, sensitivity, specificity, NPV and PPV despite these not always being significant; (3) BD-p-tau217% had larger effect sizes, hazard ratios and incident rates; and (4) BD-tau -and for that matter -BD-p-tau217% is less prone to effects of variables such as age and sex (45). Interestingly, among all ratio-based biomarkers evaluated (BD-p-tau217 and p-tau217 normalized to BD-tau, total tau, Aβ40, or Aβ42), only BD-p-tau217% consistently improved performance across both the Pitt-ADRC and ADNI cohorts. Biologically, BD-p-tau217 reflects the abundance of CNS-enriched tau phosphorylated at threonine 217, whereas BD-p-tau217% may more closely approximate phosphorylation occupancy by accounting for its corresponding tau substrate. The improved performance of BD-p-tau217% suggests that phosphorylation occupancy may provide additional pathological information beyond absolute p-tau217 levels, particularly during the early stages of AD.

It is worth noting that while p-tau217% showed modest improvements over p-tau217 in the Pitt-ADRC cohorts for distinguishing low or intermediate from severe ADNC and for predicting clinical diagnosis and progression, these improvements were not replicated in the ADNI cohort. P-tau217% was originally developed in the PrecivityAD2 mass spectrometry assay, where p-tau217 is normalized to its nonphosphorylated counterpart (np-tau217) and this normalization was shown to improve performance relative to p-tau217 alone (46, 49, 50). Unlike p-tau217% in the PrecivityAD2 assay, which normalizes p-tau217 to non-phosphorylated (np)-tau217, our implementation used total tau as the denominator since np-tau217 is not measured in the NULISA panel. Whether differences in normalization strategy contributed to the discrepant findings remains unclear and warrants further investigation. It will also be of interest to determine whether normalizing mass spectrometry-based p-tau217 to BD-tau, quantified using peptides spanning the exon 4/5 junction, provides greater diagnostic utility than normalization to np-tau217.

The advantages of BD-p-tau217% and BD-p-tau217 over traditional p-tau217 based measures were mainly observed in: (1) detecting Aβ-PET positivity but not tau PET abnormalities; (2) distinguishing low from intermediate as well as intermediate vs. high ADNC stages, but not low and high ADNC groups; (3) tracking longitudinal cognitive decline; and (4) in individuals with comorbidities. This indicates that BD-p-tau-based biomarkers are superior for detecting early, emerging AD with incipient Aβ pathology when tau NFTs are not yet highly accumulated. What is the clinical relevance of these findings? The closer a blood biomarker mirrors the intended features of a standard of truth measure, the better its performance in linking clinical symptoms to the underlying disease biology (51). Plasma p-tau217 holds a crucial role in accessible AD diagnostics and care (17, 52, 53). Our results show BD-p-tau217% and BD-p-tau217 as substantive next generation biomarkers with enhanced performance to transform the detection, treatment and monitoring of AD.

For more direct head-to-head comparisons, we focused on biomarkers measured using the same NULISA proteomic panel, sharing identical p-tau217 antibodies, buffer systems, and platform sensitivity, therefore minimizing variability related to sample processing, batch effects, and cross-platform analytical differences. Previous head-to-head comparison studies have reported that NULISA performs comparably to other leading immunoassay-based platforms (54) (55) (56). Although it was not the top-performing platform in a large roundrobin study (56), it offers high analytical sensitivity and the ability to simultaneously quantify many lowabundance proteins from a small plasma volume (43). Importantly, BD-p-tau217% achieved AUCs for detecting Aβ positivity comparable to those reported for mass spectrometry-based plasma p-tau217/np-tau217 assays. This level of performance is notable given that mass spectrometry is generally considered the benchmark platform yet requires substantially greater analytical complexity and sample volume.

The enhanced performance of BD-p-tau217% and BD-p-tau217 has several potential clinical implications. Current AD diagnostic guidelines recommend plasma p-tau217 testing as part of clinical decision making in specialist memory clinics (8, 18). The superior diagnostic accuracy of BD-p-tau217% and BD-p-tau217, particularly in detecting Aβ pathology, suggest that these biomarkers may be better suited for triaging and confirmatory diagnosis. This may be especially relevant in the context of anti-Aβ therapeutics, which requires demonstration of Aβ pathology among cognitively impaired individuals (57, 58). Other relevant situations include enrolment and monitoring in therapeutic trials as well as in primary care-and populationbased screening programs.

The improved prognostic performance further enhances the clinical value of these BD-p-tau biomarkers. BDp-tau217% was the most accurate for identifying MCI individuals who will progress to AD dementia within 5 years in the Pitt-ADRC cohort and provided the greatest separation of incident AD risk in both the Pitt-ADRC and ADNI cohorts. Among MCI individuals eligible for anti-Aβ therapy, this increased predictive capacity may be crucial in classifying who should be prioritized for treatment based on their projected disease course. In addition, the stronger accuracies of BD-p-tau217 forms in individuals with comorbidities is an attractive attribute that addresses a well-known challenge for p-tau217 (26), with similar performances in people without those comorbidities providing proof-of-concept for the versatility of BD-p-tau217%. By removing the negative impacts of comorbidities on p-tau217, plasma BD-p-tau217% may enable more widespread applications of blood-based molecular testing irrespective of comorbidity burden.

Despite the strong overall performance of all biomarkers evaluated, particularly for detecting Aβ pathology, we observed a subset of individuals with low biomarker levels despite high pathology. The causes of this apparent discordance remain unclear, as we found no consistent associations with the interval between blood collection and pathological assessment, clinical progression, or demographic characteristics. Notably, the discordance appeared more pronounced for ratio-based biomarkers, likely reflecting the additional variability introduced by both the numerator and denominator measurements. The complex biological origins of tau may also contribute to the observed discordance. Although the LMW tau species measured by the BD-tau and BD-p-tau assays are enriched in the CNS, their expression has also been reported in the PNS, where it may account for up to 50% of total tau (38). Likewise big tau is primarily expressed in PNS, but its expression has also been found in selected brain regions such as the cerebellum and cranial nerve motor nuclei, as well as non-neuronal cells (42, 59). These findings highlight the complexity of tau biology and warrant the need for additional studies to better understand the sources of biomarker-pathology discordance.

This study has several strengths, including inclusion of cohorts spanning neuropathological diagnosis, molecular imaging of Aβ and tau, clinical diagnosis, and real-world electronic health record-based comorbidity assessments -enabling in-depth clinical-biological evaluation. We further validated our findings from a large academic memory clinic cohort enriched for individuals with cognitive concerns in the independent ADNI cohort, a highly characterized research cohort, demonstrating the reproducibility and generalizability of the observed associations.

A few limitations should be noted. First, although we included individuals from independent studies, they mostly included non-Hispanic White participants, and it remains unclear whether these findings generalize to other racial and ethnic populations. Second, while the NULISA proteomic panel enables simultaneous measurement of a large number of low-abundance proteins from a small plasma volume, the current assay provides relative rather than absolute quantification, which may limit direct clinical implementation. However, single-analyte biomarker test kits for BD-p-tau217 and BD-tau potentially suitable for in vitro diagnostic tests or laboratory developed tests have been developed (47, 60, 61). Future work will aim toward clinical translation of these tests to support clinical decision making.

Together, plasma BD-p-tau217% and BD-p-tau217 capture AD pathology and clinical progression prediction with significantly superior performances -including AUCs, accuracies, effect sizes, sensitivity and specificity -over conventional, non-CNS-selective p-tau217 biomarkers. These findings were verified in cohorts with paired antemortem blood and postmortem neuropathology assessments, molecular imaging of brain Aβ and tau, and in memory clinic participants with up to two decades of clinical monitoring. These results show that BD-p-tau217% has the potential to address key limitations of existing p-tau217 assays while further transforming their clinical performances. Future studies should evaluate longitudinal biomarker dynamics, and implementation in population studies and in primary care. Yet, the breadth and consistency of evidence presented here support BD-p-tau217% as an important biomarker for the next generation of precision AD diagnosis and prognosis that will minimize false positives and maximize diagnostic and prognostic utility.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study design & population

The PittADRC cohort comprises adults self-or provider-referred for cognitive evaluation, volunteering for research, and/or recruited via educational outreach events largely from the greater southwestern Pennsylvania area, enrolled in a registry to study Alzheimer disease, aging and related disorders. Inclusion criteria consist of individuals aged 60 years or older, as well as younger adults who selfreport symptoms suggestive of Alzheimer disease and related dementias, and availability of reliable study partner/informant. Exclusion criteria include significant psychiatric conditions or other medical disorders that could interfere with the accurate assessment of cognitive impairment, and history of cancer (other than skin and in situ prostate) within the past 5 years. Detailed sociodemographic data, such as age, sex, selfreported race, and educational background, were obtained at each participant's baseline visit. Participants also completed an extensive set of baseline evaluations, including questionnaires assessing overall cognitive, behavioral, emotional, and medical status, a comprehensive battery of clinical assessments, and provided blood for biomarker measurement and genotyping. Following baseline, individuals returned annually for clinical followup evaluations. The PittADRC study was approved by the University of Pittsburgh Institutional Review Board (STUDY19110245), and all participants provided written informed consent.

This study included a subset of participants who were enrolled in the PittADRC between 1993 and 2023. Participants were selected based on the availability of plasma samples and were prioritized if they had available AD pathology from postmortem neuropathology (death within 10 years from blood draw), antemortem neuroimaging (within two years from blood draw), or longitudinal clinical followup. Because all participants were drawn from the PittADRC, some individuals appear in more than one of the analytic cohorts (Fig. S2).

The ADNI study was launched in 2004 as a public-private partnership led by Principal Investigator Michael W. Weiner, MD, with the goal of characterizing longitudinal changes in cognition, function, neuroimaging measures, and fluid biomarkers in cognitively unimpaired individuals, participants with MCI, and those with AD. ADNI data used in the preparation of this article were obtained from the ADNI website (https://adni.loni.usc.edu), downloaded in May 2026. Detailed study design has been previously described (62, 63).

Clinical assessments for the Pitt-ADRC

A detailed description of the clinical assessments, including neurologic examination, neuropsychological assessment, psychiatric evaluation and diagnostic tests such as brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), has been published previously (64). Clinical diagnoses were assigned at baseline and at each annual followup during a clinical consensus conference, in which the study team (comprising neurologists, neuropsychologists, psychiatrists, and related dementia care specialists) reviewed all available clinical data, laboratory results, and MRI findings based on published criteria. In this study, participants were classified as clinically unimpaired (CU; include both cognitively normal and subjective complaint with normal test score), MCI, AD dementia (both probable and possible AD), or nonAD dementia. For MCI, we excluded individuals with nonamnestic presentations.

We classified participants who were CU or had MCI at baseline as either stable or progressors based on their clinical trajectory over a 5year period. Baseline CU who progressed to MCI or AD dementia within five years were classified as progressor CU, whereas those who remained CU at their first visit after the 5year interval were classified as stable CU. Similarly, baseline MCI participants who progressed to AD dementia were classified as progressor MCI, while those who remained MCI or reverted to CU at the first post5year visit were classified as stable MCI.

Medical history was extracted from participants' health history questionnaires. For this study, we focused on six common comorbidities: diabetes, obesity, any cardiovascular disease (defined as having at least one of the following conditions or histories-angina, myocardial infarction, atrial fibrillation, congestive heart failure, prior heart attack, angioplasty, cardiac bypass, pacemaker implantation, heart valve replacement, or other cardiovascular diseases), hypertension, hypercholesterolemia, and active depression. For all comorbidities, we categorized individuals as either having no history of the condition or having a recent/active condition, defined as occurring within the past year (except for depression which was classified based on occurrences within the past two years) or still requiring ongoing clinical management. Remote or resolved conditions, those that occurred in the past and no longer required treatment, were considered missing and excluded from statistical analyses.

Pitt-ADRC neuropathological assessment

The neuropathological data was collected following the National Alzheimer's Coordinating Center (NACC) data collection procedures (versions 1, 7, 9, 10, and 11) (65). Due to missing Thal phase for a subset of cases, we implemented a modified NIA-Reagan approach for classifying ADNC (66). Neuritic plaque burden was assessed using the CERAD score for neocortical neuritic plaque density(67), where scores of 0-1 indicate low, 2 indicates moderate, and 3 indicates frequent plaque density. NFT pathology was classified into four groups using the Braak staging system(68): Braak stages 0-II were considered lowBraak; stages III-IV with no or sparse neuritic plaques were classified as primary agerelated tauopathy (PART); stages III-IV with moderate or greater neuritic plaque density were considered midBraak; and stages V-VI were categorized as highBraak. ADNC was defined as followed: intermediate ADNC was Braak III-IV with moderate/frequent CERAD neuritic plaque score, and high ADNC was Braak V-VI with moderate/frequent CERAD neuritic plaque score. Everyone else was considered Low/Not ADNC. In the neuropathology cohort, all individuals with intermediate ADNC belonged to the midBraak group and vice versa, and all individuals with severe ADNC had highBraak and vice versa. Therefore, comparisons between midBraak and highBraak groups were not included in the analysis.

Amyloid and tau PET imaging in Pitt-ADRC

All PET imaging scans for Pitt-ADRC were acquired similar as described (69-71). [¹¹C]Pittsburgh CompoundB ([¹¹C]PiB) was used as the tracer for AβPET, and [¹F]AV1451 was used for tauPET. Participants received each tracer via slow bolus injection through the antecubital vein, followed by a brief resting period before being positioned in the scanner for image acquisition. [¹¹C]PiB scans were obtained 50-70 minutes postinjection, and [¹F]AV1451 PET scans were obtained 80-100 minutes postinjection, each acquired as a series of 5minute frames. For AβPET, scans were processed using CapAIBL, a tool developed by the Australian eHealth Research Centre to simplify and standardize the quantification of amyloid burden in PET imaging (72). Participants were classified as A+ or A-using the CapAIBL Centiloid cutoff of 20, with values ≥20 indicating A+ status. For tauPET, composite standardized uptake ratios (SUVRs) were calculated by normalizing the composite Braak regional values (Braak I-VI) to cerebellar gray matter activity as defined by FreeSurfer. Participants with SUVR > 1.18 were classified as T+, and those with SUVR ≤ 1.18 were classified as T-.

PET imaging for the ADNI cohort was performed as previously described (73, 74). Aβ-PET positivity was defined using UC Berkeley-harmonized Centiloid values, with Centiloid >20 indicating A+. Only [¹F]AV-1451 tau-PET scans were included, with positivity defined using a previously established meta-temporal SUVR cutoff of 1.37 (74).

Pitt-ADRC biospecimen collection and biomarker measurement

Blood was collected into standard ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) tubes at baseline visits and processed into plasma and buffy coat by centrifugation at 2,000 g for 10 minutes. APOE genotyping was performed as previously described (75). For the NULISAseq CNS panel proteomic assay, plasma samples were further centrifuged at 4,000 g for 10 minutes to remove particulates, after which 30 μL of cleared plasma was transferred to the sample plate. Assays were conducted on the Argo HT following the manufacturer's protocol. Immunoreaction involved incubating plasma samples with a cocktail containing 132 pairs of oligoDNA-conjugated capture and detection antibodies targeting 131 biomarkers (Data S1), along with an mCherry internal standard. Reporter DNA corresponding to each biomarker/mCherry complex was quantified using nextgeneration sequencing (NGS) on a NextSeq 2000 system. Reporter counts were converted to NULISA Protein Quantification (NPQ) values using Alamar Analysis software by normalizing against mCherry and inter-plate controls, followed by log2 transformation. To monitor assay performance, we included three pooled inhouse QC plasma samples, run in singleton on each plate, along with vendor built-in sample control (SC), which were run in triplicate on each plate.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was performed using MATLAB (version R2021b). For demographic variables, the Wilcoxon ranksum test was used for twogroup comparisons of continuous variables, and the Kruskal-Wallis test was used for comparisons involving more than two groups. Fisher's exact test was applied to categorical variables with two levels, and the chisquare test was used for categorical variables with more than two levels.

Biomarker ratios were calculated using the NPQ difference. For the ptau217/Aβ42 ratio, the value was computed as NPQ(ptau217) -NPQ(Aβ42). For BDptau217%, it was NPQ(BDptau217) -NPQ(BDMAPT). For ptau217%, the ratio was calculated as NPQ(ptau217) -NPQ(MAPT). To enable direct comparison of effect sizes, all biomarker levels were converted to zscores to standardize their scales.

Linear regression models were used to evaluate the association between biomarker levels and sample groupings reflecting different AD pathology or clinical diagnostic statuses. Zscore-normalized biomarker levels were used as the outcome, with sample group as the predictor, adjusting for age and sex. The adjusted Cohen's d effect sizes were calculated by taking the regression coefficient for the twocategory group variable (which corresponds to the adjusted group difference) and normalizing it by the residual standard deviation. For sensitivity analyses, effect sizes were also estimated using linear regression models that included either no covariates or an expanded covariate set additionally comprising race, education, and APOE4 carriership. Multiple comparisons were controlled using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure (1995) (76), and an FDRadjusted pvalue < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves and area under the curve (AUC) values were estimated using generalized linear regression models, with sample grouping as the outcome and biomarker levels as the predictor for the primary analysis. For sensitivity analyses, age and sex were added as covariates. Confidence intervals were obtained using bootstrap resampling (1,000 replicates). Classification performance was evaluated using the Youden index, which identifies the cutoff that maximizes sensitivity and specificity. Pairwise comparisons of AUCs derived from the same sample set were conducted using DeLong tests (77).

For longitudinal analysis, an event was defined as clinical progression from CU to MCI or AD dementia, or from MCI to AD dementia. Timetoincident progression was calculated as the interval between the baseline blood draw and the first visit at which an event occurred, with participants who did not experience an event censored at their last completed visit. Incidence rates (IRs) were computed as the number of events divided by the total personyears of followup, and 95% confidence intervals were estimated using the exact Poisson method. Comparisons against the reference group (quartile-1 for quartilebased analyses or the lowbiomarker group using PETderived thresholds for dichotomized analyses) were performed using Poisson generalized linear models with logtransformed followup time as the offset. Cox proportional hazards models were used to evaluate the association between baseline biomarker levels and timetoincident clinical progression among participants who were cognitively normal or had MCI at baseline, adjusting for age and sex. Kaplan-Meier models were generated to visualize survival curves and estimate median time to progression.
