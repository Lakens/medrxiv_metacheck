How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Acute intermittent hypoxia (AIH) therapy is a promising neurorehabilitative intervention with welldocumented improvement in motor function in people with spinal cord injury. Its effect on lower-limb motor control in multiple sclerosis (MS), where walking impairment is a leading source of disability, remain uncharacterized, and the supraspinal effects of AIH therapy are unknown. In a sham-controlled, double-blind crossover trial (NCT05164705), 23 adults with MS completed 5 sessions of AIH therapy and of sham therapy; magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) was performed before and after each therapeutic intervention. At each timepoint, blood oxygenation level-dependent (BOLD) functional MRI was collected during a repeated plantar-and dorsi-flexion task. Task-evoked activation was modeled at the group level and compared across timepoint and therapeutic arm. Activation changes differed for the leftand right-foot task. AIH augmented activation in the basal ganglia, thalamus, and cortical motor regions during left-foot movement with similar but sub-threshold patterns for the right foot. Right-foot movement additionally showed enhanced deactivation in prefrontal, parietal, and temporal cortices, although these effects were primarily in white matter voxels, limiting their interpretability. These findings suggest that changes in subcortical activation may contribute to motor function improvements following AIH therapy, implicating the basal ganglia-thalamo-cortical motor loop as a target of AIH-induced neuroplasticity. These results also indicate that AIH therapy modulates supraspinal motor circuitry, supporting further investigation of the cortical and subcortical effects of this emerging therapeutic treatment.

1 Introduction

Acute intermittent hypoxia (AIH) is an emerging rehabilitative therapy that drives neuroplastic changes and supports motor recovery in the damaged central nervous system 1 . It consists of brief, repeated exposures to low oxygen (~9% O 2 ) alternating with periods of normoxia. The hypoxic stimulus activates chemoreflex-driven serotonergic neurons in the raphe nuclei, triggering synthesis of brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) and enhancing motoneuron excitability 2, 3 . AIH therapy has been shown to improve walking speed and endurance, joint strength, and dexterity in persons with spinal cord injury, and there is growing interest in expanding the clinical applications of this promising therapeutic intervention 2, [4] [5] [6] [7] [8] [9] [10] . Investigations are needed to determine whether similar mechanisms may promote functional recovery in further populations with impaired motor pathways, including persons with multiple sclerosis (PwMS).

Multiple sclerosis significantly impairs motor function as demyelination and neuroaxonal inflammation disrupt communication between the central nervous system and the musculature 11 . Persons with multiple sclerosis experience muscle weakness, pain, spasticity, abnormal reflexes, and poor fine motor control, contributing to difficulty maintaining balance and gait and executing everyday tasks 11, 12 . Approximately 75% of PwMS have difficulty walking due to sensorimotor impairments 11, 12 . Current treatment strategies for multiple sclerosis focus primarily on suppressing inflammation to mitigate disability progression and strategies to manage symptoms. Rehabilitation approaches, including physical and occupational therapies and adaptive devices, have been used for decades to help maintain mobility and quality of life, yet these treatment strategies do not target the neuroplastic mechanisms underlying lasting motor function recovery [13] [14] [15] . Given that AIH appears to modulate neural transmission in corticospinal circuits involved in motor control, this therapy presents a promising and distinct method of promoting motor function recovery in PwMS, complementary to existing treatments 16, 17.

Our group has recently presented preliminary evidence that a single session of AIH therapy improves motor function in PwMS, showing increased ankle strength, walking ability, and cognitive processing 18 . However, relatively little is known about how AIH alters the underlying neural control of movement in these individuals 18, 19 . While lesions and neurodegeneration may impact the integrity of the corticospinal tract in multiple sclerosis, the disease typically involves more diffuse pathology across the brain and spinal cord 20 . Thus, while the mechanisms underlying AIH efficacy may overlap between SCI and MS cohorts, this therapy may act through distinct supraspinal pathways in PwMS. Functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) provides a noninvasive method of studying neural activity, and when combined with a task, can be used to probe changes in sensorimotor activation [21] [22] [23] . Previous fMRI studies have shown that patterns of brain activation during voluntary movement are altered in PwMS, with increases in the magnitude and extent of activation compared to healthy subjects, suggesting compensatory activation and functional reorganization of the brain [24] [25] [26] . Understanding how AIH therapy alters neural activation associated with motor tasks in PwMS could provide insight into the mechanisms through which this promising treatment improves motor function, facilitating its translation into clinical rehabilitative practice.

We therefore investigated whether repeated sessions of AIH changed neural activation associated with a lower-limb motor task in PwMS. We conducted a randomized sham-controlled crossover trial, performing motor-task fMRI at the beginning and end of five sessions of daily AIH (or sham AIH) therapy. We hypothesized that compensatory neural activation associated with the lower limb motor task would be reduced following AIH therapy.

2 Methods

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board at Northwestern University (STU00219535) and written, informed consent was provided by all subjects before any study procedures took place. Study activities were performed at the Shirley Ryan AbilityLab and Northwestern University. Twenty-seven individuals with relapsing-remitting or secondary-progressive multiple sclerosis were recruited for this study. To be included in the study, participants must have an expanded disability status scale (EDSS) score between 3 and 6.5 and a motor functional system scale (FSS) score between 2 and 4, be between the ages of 18 and 75, free of relapses for at least the past year, safe to be scanned with MRI, and if prescribed dalfampridine, taking the same daily dose for at least two months prior to enrollment. Individuals with active MS lesions, chronic uncontrolled hypertension or sleep apnea, a history of epilepsy or chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, or who were pregnant were ineligible.

2.1 Study Design

A randomized, double-blinded, sham-controlled crossover study design was employed. Participants completed two five-day arms, separated by a washout period of at least four weeks. The order of interventions was randomized. Functional magnetic resonance imaging and motor function assessments were performed on Day 1 and Day 5 of each intervention week, and AIH or sham AIH was delivered on Days 1-5 (Figure 1A). Scanning occurred at a different site to the intervention sessions and motor function assessments, and participants were transported between the two sites in a wheelchair with the support of a clinical aide.

2.2 Intermittent Hypoxia Protocol

Participants underwent 5 sessions each of AIH and sham AIH, with interventions delivered in the morning on consecutive days within each arm. Participants were seated and fit with a non-rebreathing face mask through which gas mixtures were delivered. The intermittent hypoxia protocol (Figure 1B) lasted approximately 30 minutes and consisted of 15 bouts of hypoxia (9-10% FiO 2 ) or sham hypoxia (21% FiO 2 ) alternated with bouts of normoxic room air (21% FiO 2 ). Hypoxic bout durations were determined individually in each session to achieve a target arterial oxygen saturation (SpO 2 ) of 84-88%. Normoxic intervals lasted at least 60 seconds to ensure that participants' SpO 2 recovered to at least 92%. Hypoxic and sham hypoxic air were generated by a hypoxia generator (Hyp-123, Hypoxico Inc), and an oxygen monitor continuously monitored the fraction of inspired oxygen of generated gas mixtures. Oxyhemoglobin saturation and heart rate were continuously monitored (Masimo Rad-57, Danaher; Nonin 4 in Model 7500 pulse oximeter, Nonin Medical Inc.) and blood pressure was taken before and after each session (Welch-Allyn Connex, Hillrom).

2.3 Imaging Protocol

fMRI was performed on Day 1 and Day 5 of each intervention arm. Scanning was performed on a Siemens 3T Prisma scanner using a 64-channel head coil. A structural multi-echo T1-weighted MPRAGE was acquired with scan parameters 27 : TR = 2170.0 ms, TEs = 1.69/3.55/5.41 ms, FA = 7°, FoV = 256 x 256 mm 2 , voxel size = 1.0x1.0x1.0 mm 3 . fMRI data were acquired with a 2D T2*-weighted multi-band, multi-echo gradient echo echo-planar imaging sequence: TR = 1500.0 ms, TEs = 10.60/27.83/45.06/62.29/79.52 ms, FA = 70°, multi-band factor = 4, GRAPPA acceleration factor = 2, FoV = 210x210 mm 2 , voxel size = 2.5x2.5x2.5mm 3 , phase encoding direction A>>P. A reverse-phase encode sequence (P>>A) was acquired prior to functional scans to aid in distortion correction. Physiological monitoring was performed throughout scanning at a sampling rate of 100 Hz (PowerLab 8/35 and LabChart, ADInstruments): end-tidal carbon dioxide and oxygen were monitored via nasal cannula and gas analyzer (ADInstruments), breathing via respiration belt (BIOPAC), and pulse via a fingertip pulse transducer (ADInstruments). Participants performed repeated ankle flexion during the fMRI acquisition, with a wedge pillow positioned under their knees for comfort. Visual instructions prompted participants to perform fourteen 16-second blocks of unilateral dorsi-and plantar-flexion, paced at 0.5 Hz, with 10-18 seconds of rest between blocks, and alternating sides for each consecutive block. Participants were given the chance to practice this task outside of the scanner before each session to ensure comfort and understanding.

2.4 Data and Statistical Analysis

2.4.1 STRUCTURAL MRI PRE-PROCESSING

Subject T1-weighted images were processed with (fsl_anat, FSL) 28 , which performs bias-field correction and brain extraction, and registered to the MNI152 standard-space T1-weighted 2mm template image using FSL flirt and fnirt 29 , which perform linear and non-linear registration, respectively.

2.4.2 FUNCTIONAL MRI PRE-PROCESSING

Data were pre-processed using tools in the FSL and AFNI software suites. The first 10 volumes of fMRI data were removed for each echo to allow for magnetization stabilization. Data were distortion-corrected (topup, FSL) 30, 31 and realignment was performed to correct for head motion, registering data from the first echo to a single-band reference image taken at the start of the functional acquisition (3dvolreg, AFNI) and applying realignment parameters to subsequent echoes (3dAllineate, AFNI). Images were brain-extracted (bet, FSL) 32.

Tedana (version 24.0.2) 33, 34 was used to calculate the optimal combination of the multi-echo data and to perform subsequent multi-echo independent component analysis (ME-ICA). The resulting components were automatically classified and accepted or rejected as noise based on the tedana demo_taskexternalregressors_model decision tree, as described by Reddy et al. 35 The optimally combined dataset was smoothed with a 3mm full-width at half maximum Gaussian kernel (3dmerge, AFNI) and converted to signal percent change.

2.4.3 GENERATION OF REGRESSORS

Left-and right-foot task regressors were generated by convolving task block timings with the canonical hemodynamic response function. End-tidal peaks in CO 2 timeseries were automatically detected in MATLAB and manually inspected. Peaks were interpolated to create the end-tidal CO 2 trace, which was convolved with the canonical hemodynamic response function, rescaled, and demeaned. The demeaned trace was down-sampled to match the temporal resolution of the fMRI timeseries, forming the end-tidal CO 2 regressor.

2.4.4 SUBJECT-LEVEL MODELING

Functional data for each subject and session were modeled from the signal percent change maps using a general linear model of the left-and right-foot task regressors, the end-tidal CO 2 regressor, the six motion parameters and their derivatives generated by head-motion realignment, Legendre polynomials up to the fourth order, and rejected ME-ICA components orthogonalized using the conservative method described by Moia et al. and validated for motor task fMRI by Reddy et al. 35, 36

2.4.5 MOTOR TASK GROUP ANALYSIS

Activation maps from each subject and session were transformed to standard space, applying concatenated transforms from functional to anatomical and anatomical to standard spaces (flirt and fnirt, FSL). Group-level activation analysis was performed with only data from pre-intervention scan sessions (n=46 from 23 subjects): AFNI's 3dMEMA was used to run a one-sample test for the data from each foot separately, with outliers modeled and no covariates included 37 . Resulting group maps were thresholded at p<0.001 and cluster-level α<0.05 (bi-sided, NN=1) after estimating spatial autocorrelation and determining minimum cluster size (3dFWHMx, 3dClustSim, 3dClusterize, AFNI).

2.4.6 GROUP ANALYSIS OF THE INTERVENTION EFFECT

AFNI's 3dANOVA3 was used to determine the group-level impact of the sham and AIH intervention on task activation and significant differences between the two interventions for each foot. A three-way mixed-effects ANOVA was run (3dANOVA3), modeling timepoint (pre and post) and intervention (AIH and sham) as fixed effects and subject as a random effect. Minimum cluster size was determined from estimated spatial autocorrelation and generated ANOVA results were thresholded and clustered at α<0.05 (bi-sided, NN=1); p<0.001 was used for activation maps and p<0.05 was used for changes in activation (3dFWHMx, 3dClustSim, 3dClusterize, AFNI). Details and locations of significant clusters were summarized with AFNI's 3dClusterize, whereami, and 3dBrickStat, using the CA_N27_ML atlas to localize clusters and the Harvard-Oxford subcortical structural atlas' white matter mask thresholded at 50%.

4 Discussion

This study examined the effects of AIH therapy on neural activation associated with lower limb motor control in PwMS. Contrary to our initial hypothesis, significant decreases in gray matter activation in motor regions were not observed following AIH therapy under this experimental design, suggesting that this AIH therapy did not measurably reverse the compensatory cortical activation patterns that have been identified in PwMS during motor task performance. Instead, the primary effect of AIH therapy in this trial was an increase in activation in the thalamus and basal ganglia regions (putamen, caudate, and pallidum). These changes may reflect a distinct mechanism by which AIH therapy influences lower limb motor function in MS.

4.1 Task Activation in Multiple Sclerosis

Motor task fMRI in PwMS probes how the brain maintains motor function despite demyelination and axonal loss. In this study, we examined activation associated with lower-limb movement in PwMS. Lower-limb tasks are less common than upper-limb and hand tasks due to enhanced task-correlated motion artifacts and difficulty isolating movement to the joint of interest, and few previous studies have examined the neural activation patterns associated with lower-limb motor tasks in PwMS [44] [45] [46] [47] . Evidence from upper-limb and hand studies suggest that compensatory activation enables patients to maintain relatively normal motor function, and the few studies that have been performed indicate that compensatory activation patterns are also present for lower-limb movement 24, [38] [39] [40] [44] [45] [46] [47].

While no control group was included in the present study, comparisons of the BOLD activation evoked by the lower-limb task in this population with previous research and with expected activation patterns suggests that compensatory activation may be present. Activation was seen in the typical regions activated by this task in healthy controls, i.e. the medial primary and supplemental motor cortices and the primary somatosensory cortex [38] [39] [40] [41] . Additional activation was identified bilaterally in the insular, frontal opercular, and parietal opercular (secondary somatosensory) cortices, regions that are commonly implicated in compensatory activation patterns in PwMS [38] [39] [40] [41] . The recruitment of additional cortical regions to aid in adequate task performance is unsurprising, given the level of disability in this study cohort and participants' reports of difficulty performing the task.

4.2 Changes in Neural Activation with AIH Therapy

Neural activation showed both significant increases and significant decreases in the AIH intervention arm versus the sham AIH arm (Figure 5). Increases were largely confined to the basal ganglia, insular and opercular cortices, and the primary and supplementary motor and somatosensory regions. BOLD increases were seen bilaterally, with slightly more activation on the side contralateral to the task. Although increases were seen for both feet, significant clusters of increased activation were only found for the left-foot task. Given that the majority of the study cohort showed greater motor and sensory deficits on the left side (Table 1, 12 participants with left-sided weakness, 9 with right-sided weakness), there may be greater potential for improvement on this side. Increases in activation after AIH therapy compared to sham were detected primarily in the deep grey matter structures of the thalamus, putamen, caudate, and pallidum (Figure 5B). These regions comprise key structures of the basal ganglia motor circuit, which coordinates motor control and is particularly involved in the learning, execution, and automation of complex motor functions such as this rhythmic flexion task [48] [49] [50] . AIH therapy induces neuroplastic changes by activating serotonergic raphe nuclei, triggering serotonin release and activating serotonin receptors in the neurons to which they project 1, 51, 52 . Many of these serotonergic projections target the basal ganglia, particularly the caudate and putamen 53 . Increased activation in these regions by the raphe nuclei may explain the observed increases in BOLD signal following AIH therapy, and these enhancements in activation may reflect additional supraspinal mechanisms through which AIH therapy ultimately achieves improvements in motor function.

Decreases were seen in the posterior parietal lobe, the posterior temporal lobe, and the frontal lobe. Frontal lobe hyperactivity has been previously associated with task performance in PwMS, and decreases in activation have been found with previous therapeutic techniques, suggesting increased task network efficiency 54 . However, the posterior parietal and occipital lobe activation changes were primarily confined to white matter, with all clusters exceeding 50% white matter overlap. While white-matter fMRI is increasingly of interest, BOLD signals in white matter are less understood than those in gray matter 55 . Lower blood flow and weaker hemodynamic responses decrease sensitivity, and the relationship between neural activity and BOLD changes in white matter is not well characterized 55, 56 . Furthermore, BOLD signals in white matter will be influenced by the presence of MS lesions, which alter local energy consumption and uncouple metabolic demand from vascular supply 55, 57 . Negative BOLD signals in white matter have also been associated with changes to blood and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) compartment volume, rather than neurovascular coupling mechanisms, particularly in voxels with deep medullary veins or located proximal to the boundary between tissue and CSF, where MS lesions are also frequently located 58 . Thus, while they should not be discounted as purely noise, the observed white matter fMRI changes should therefore be interpreted with caution, particularly at the lower field strength and spatial resolution used in this study. Future studies specifically investigating white-matter fMRI changes in MS could clarify interpretation. Cerebrovascular changes may also contribute to changes in activation seen with AIH therapy. MS has been associated with cerebrovascular abnormalities, including hypoperfusion and reduced cerebrovascular reactivity, and MS lesions typically form around a central vein, demonstrating that there is a significant vascular component to the disease [59] [60] [61] . While changes to whole-brain cerebrovascular function have not previously been seen with AIH therapy in healthy adults, supporting the safety profile of this intervention, we have shown that 12 sessions of AIH enhance focal perfusion to bilateral sensorimotor cortices 62 . The specific cerebrovascular impact of AIH in MS, as well as an understanding of how these changes may influence fMRI activation mapping, will be important targets in future research.

4.3 Association of Activation Changes with Fatigue

While fatigue was not measured under the present experimental design, it is likely that our experimental protocol modulated fatigue. The post-intervention scans were performed at the end of a long study visit, after both the AIH therapy and extended functional motor outcome assessments. Anecdotally, participants reported a) feeling tired at the end of the day and b) feeling less tired following what they believed was the true AIH intervention arm (note, the sham AIH is widely accepted to be indistinguishable from the true AIH intervention in practice). Thus, the unintentional fatiguing effects of our experimental design may drive some of the brain changes observed in the sham intervention arm (e.g., reduced deactivation of the precuneus for both left and right foot motor tasks). Furthermore, a major effect of the true AIH intervention may be to simply mitigate those effects, as the precuneus was not significantly different between pre-and post-intervention scans in the AIH arm. The precuneus is part of the DMN, which has previously been implicated in fatigue in PwMS, with individuals experiencing greater fatigue displaying lower functional connectivity of the DMN and basal ganglia [63] [64] [65] . However, the observed changes in taskevoked negative signal changes in this study cannot be directly compared to such literature reports of resting state functional connectivity.

Neural activation in the basal ganglia has also been inversely related to the severity of fatigue in neurological disorders 66, 67 . Reductions in basal ganglia connectivity and lower activation of the basal ganglia during cognitive tasks have been associated with greater subjective and objective measures of fatigue in multiple sclerosis, suggesting that dysfunction in these regions may be foundational to central fatigue in MS 68, 69 . We observed that AIH increased activation of the thalamus, putamen, pallidum, and caudate significantly more than the sham intervention, again suggesting that this intervention may have a beneficial effect on mitigating fatigue. However, measures of fatigue were not obtained in this study, Future work should obtain measures of cognitive and physical fatigue to explore whether this intervention is a viable therapy to modulate central fatigue in MS and other neurological disorders.

4.4 Limitations

Limitations of this experimental design present further opportunities for future studies of the effect of therapeutic AIH on motor control in PwMS. This preliminary crossover trial did not include measurements of task performance during the MRI; it is possible that participants' performance of the ankle flexion task improved with AIH therapy and with practice, and changes in task activation may reflect this improvement. Anecdotal reports from participants suggested that they felt they could perform the task better, and future studies should include quantitative measures of this task performance for incorporation in fMRI analysis to account for this. This study did not include a concurrent training component. Therapeutic AIH has been found to have a synergistic effect with motor training, priming the motor system to enhance the outcomes that can be obtained from solely AIH therapy or motor training 17 . Although we did observe increased ankle flexion strength in this study following AIH therapy alone (functional outcomes will be reported separately), future trials combining AIH with training will be necessary to evaluate whether the neural changes we have observed relate to meaningful functional improvement (e.g., walking and balance).

The fMRI acquisition was limited in spatial coverage and in spatial resolution. The acquisition did not include the cerebellum in most participants, and it was therefore excluded from analyses. The cerebellum is highly involved in motor control, with particular importance in coordination, balance, and motor learning 70 . Cerebellar pathology is common in MS, causing gait and coordination problems, tremor, and ataxia as well as cognitive problems due to lesions, atrophy, and disruptions to cerebellar connectivity 71- 73 . The cerebellum is directly linked to the raphe nuclei, which act as the central command center modulating neuroplasticity, but no study to date has assessed cerebellar changes with AIH therapy 2, 74 . Future work should investigate the effects of AIH therapy in the cerebellum. The voxel spatial resolution implemented in this study also did not allow for detailed investigation of the small subcortical regions that showed the greatest changes with the intervention. Future studies of the supraspinal effects of AIH therapy with MRI should implement a smaller voxel size to allow for more accurate localization and coregistration of these salient structures, increasing confidence in analyses of subcortical activation changes.

Finally, MS lesion load, lesion location, and the subsequent motor impairments are highly variable across individuals. Precision mapping in fMRI is becoming increasingly practical with technological and analytical advancements, and single-subject activation in subcortical structures can be robustly detected with approximately 30 minutes of multi-echo fMRI data 75 . Precision mapping approaches would eliminate need to average across the inter-subject variability introduced by MS pathology and could further help to clarify known variability in the efficacy of AIH treatment, as previous studies have reported substantial inter-individual variability in treatment response magnitude 2, 76 . Future work should consider applying precision mapping fMRI techniques to studies of supraspinal changes in neural control of movement with AIH therapy.

5 Conclusion

Further study of the neuroplastic changes associated with AIH therapy in MS is needed to clarify how the alterations observed here relate to behavioral and functional outcomes. This preliminary crossover trial suggests that neuroimaging may play a valuable role both in identifying candidates likely to respond to AIH therapy and in optimizing AIH protocol parameters to enhance efficacy, positioning basal ganglia activation as a potential candidate biomarker for future trials combining AIH with structured motor rehabilitation.
