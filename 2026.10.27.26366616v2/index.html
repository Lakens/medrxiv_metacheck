How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Reliable clinical translation of diagnostic artificial intelligence (AI) requires evaluation beyond classification accuracy. We developed PedDx, a segmentation-free framework for pediatric posterior fossa tumor diagnosis using T2-weighted MRI, pretrained 3DINO representations, calibrated predictions, and confidence-based abstention. Evaluation included 492 discovery, 114 replication, 129 external validation, and 40 independently tested cases, alongside 236 out-of-distribution (OOD) cases and controlled imaging perturbations. Classification accuracy was 79.8% in replication and 81.4% in external validation, increasing to 90.5% and 88.7%, respectively, among non-abstained cases. OOD analyses revealed performance degradation and confidently incorrect predictions, while perturbation analyses identified acquisition and alignment vulnerabilities. An external institution independently executed the complete agentic workflow, successfully generating predictions and structured quality-control reports for all 40 cases, with classification accuracy of 92.5%, increasing to 97.3% among non-abstained cases. PedDx demonstrates how uncertainty assessment, failure-mode characterization, external validation, and independently reproducible, traceable reporting can collectively inform the reliability and limitations of diagnostic AI systems.

Introduction

Pediatric posterior fossa tumors comprise biologically and clinically distinct neoplasms, including medulloblastoma, ependymoma, atypical teratoid/rhabdoid tumor (ATRT), pediatric low-grade glioma (LGG), and diffuse midline glioma, including diffuse intrinsic pontine glioma (DMG/DIPG) 1, 2 . Accurate preoperative differential diagnosis can inform the goals and extent of surgery, the need for additional staging, and counseling about anticipated treatment and prognosis. These distinctions are particularly consequential in the posterior fossa, where the potential benefits of tumor resection must be weighed against the risk of injury to critical brainstem and cerebellar structures [3] [4] [5].

MRI-based assessment can support these decisions before definitive diagnostic characterization, but overlapping imaging appearances make differentiation challenging even for experienced neuroradiologists 6, 7 . This challenge has motivated imaging-based artificial intelligence (AI) approaches to assist pediatric neuro-oncologic diagnosis [8] [9] [10].

Prior imaging-based AI studies have addressed radiomics-based classification 8, 9, 11, 12 and deep learning for histological classification and molecular subtyping 13, 14 . These studies provide a foundation for automated classification, but diagnostic discrimination alone does not establish how a model will behave across the broader range of examinations encountered during external use. A multiclass classifier must be evaluated for class-specific errors, the reliability of its confidence estimates, and its ability to defer unconfident predictions [15] [16] [17] . This is particularly important when aggregate performance can obscure limited recognition of less frequent tumor types.

Evaluation must also address inputs outside the intended diagnostic population, referred to as out-of-distribution (OOD) data. A model developed for treatment-naïve posterior fossa tumors may encounter postoperative changes, suboptimal imaging, tumors outside the posterior fossa, or rare histologies absent from its diagnostic categories. Such distribution shifts can compromise predictive performance without a corresponding reduction in confidence, potentially producing confident but incorrect predictions 15, [18] [19] [20] . Probability calibration and confidence-based abstention, complemented by systematic OOD and failure-mode analyses help a more comprehensive validation of a model. Evaluation across clinically relevant distribution shifts can identify vulnerabilities associated with unfamiliar anatomy, treatment effects, or unsupported histologies, while controlled imaging perturbations can characterize sensitivity to acquisition artifacts and preprocessing errors 20, 21 . These analyses help establish the conditions under which model predictions may become unreliable and whether uncertainty measures adequately identify such failures.

Beyond predictive performance, generalizability also depends on whether the complete AI workflow can be reliably executed across institutions. Conventional external validation assesses the performance of a fixed classifier on independently acquired data, whereas independent execution by an external institution additionally evaluates the reproducibility of preprocessing, quality control (QC), inference, and reporting. These considerations align with the AI-RAPNO recommendations for robust validation and clinical translation in pediatric neuro-oncology 22, 23 and the broader FUTURE-AI consensus guidelines emphasizing generalizability, robustness, traceability, and continuous quality control in medical AI systems 24 . Transparent documentation of processing steps, QC findings, model outputs, and potential failures can further facilitate troubleshooting and independent evaluation. These principles motivate evaluating diagnostic AI as an end-to-end system rather than solely through classification performance.

Here, we present PedDx, a confidence-based framework designed to classify five pediatric posterior fossa tumor categories from T2-weighted MRI, using a frozen threedimensional vision transformer pretrained by self-supervision (3DINO 25 ) and a lightweight classification head. The classifier uses atlas-defined posterior fossa sampling without requiring tumor segmentation and incorporates probability calibration and confidence-based abstention as a safeguard against presenting uncertain outputs as diagnostic assignments, accepting that some correct predictions will also be withheld. To support reproducible deployment and transparent reporting, the classifier is integrated into an agentic workflow that orchestrates preprocessing, quality control, inference, and structured report generation. Language-model agents record processing and QC evidence to document potential issues and facilitate troubleshooting, while diagnostic probabilities and abstention decisions remain exclusively determined by the fixed imaging classifier.

We evaluate PedDx at both the classifier and workflow levels. We assess diagnostic performance, calibration, and abstention in discovery, replication, and external cohorts; characterize failure modes using clinical and biological distribution shifts and controlled imaging perturbations; and determine whether additional OOD scores improve identification of shifted inputs. Finally, an external team independently evaluates the complete workflow. These design and evaluation choices address complementary requirements for clinical translation: confidence-based deferral, independent end-to-end execution, and characterization of distribution shift and failure modes.

Results

Study overview

PedDx was evaluated for diagnostic performance within its intended (in-distribution) population, behavior under distribution shift, and generalizability of both the classifier and the complete workflow (Fig. 1 1 and Supplementary Note 1.

Diagnostic performance and uncertainty in the intended (in-distribution) population

The model combined a frozen 3DINO backbone with posterior fossa multi-crop sampling and a lightweight multiclass head (PedDx v2). Macro-AUC-ROC was 0.901 ± 0.023 in discovery and 0.949 in replication; corresponding five-class accuracies were 74.2% ± 2.5% and 79.8% (Table 2; Fig. 2). Performance was strongest for DMG/DIPG,

LGG, and medulloblastoma, with replication AUC-ROCs of 0.998, 0.976, and 0.968, respectively. However, five-class balanced accuracy was substantially lower (0.587 ± 0.065 and 0.647), reflecting limited diagnostic performance for ATRT and ependymoma.

Performance metrics on ependymoma indicate the distinction between discrimination and classification: its replication AUC-ROC was 0.934, but sensitivity was 0.125. Complete class-wise metrics are provided in Table 2.

Development analyses supported the selected configuration. Multi-crop sampling increased macro-AUC-ROC by 0.023 relative to a single-centered crop, while alternative multi-view counts and offset magnitudes did not differ significantly (Supplementary Note 2). Against the fine-tuned ResNet-34 benchmark (PedDx v1), the fold-averaged macro-AUC-ROC difference was 0.013 (95% CI, -0.010 to 0.036; q = 0.33), and no replication difference remained significant after multiplicity correction. PedDx v2 used approximately 1,000-fold fewer trainable parameters and showed less variation in score scale across fold models. Detailed benchmark metrics and the distinction between fold-averaged and pooled comparisons are reported in Supplementary Note 3 and Supplementary Tables 3 4 5 6 7 8 9 10.

Temperature scaling (T = 1.30) reduced expected calibration error from 0.066 to 0.039 in discovery and from 0.092 to 0.086 in replication (Supplementary Note 4). At the fixed confidence threshold of 0.50, PedDx v2 abstained on 19 of 114 replication cases (16.7%), retaining predictions for 95 (83.3%). Abstention intercepted 14 of 23 errors, increasing accuracy among retained cases to 90.5% (86/95), compared with 79.8% without abstention (Fig. 3a). All 62 high-confidence predictions (≥0.80) were correct, whereas 24 of 33 moderate-confidence predictions were correct (72.7%). The nine remaining errors show that abstention reduced, but did not eliminate, misclassification.

Representative correct and incorrect predictions with similar moderate confidence are shown in Fig. 3b.

External evaluation and class-specific limitations

The fixed classifier was evaluated on the five-class CHOA cohort without updating model weights, calibration, or the abstention threshold. Accuracy without abstention was 81.4% (105/129; 95% CI, 74.4-87.6%). Abstention excluded 23 cases (17.8%) and intercepted 12 of 24 errors; accuracy among the 106 retained cases was 88.7% (94/106; 95% CI, 82.1-94.3%) (Fig. 4a). High-confidence predictions were correct in 61 of 65 cases (93.8%), indicating that the confidence-accuracy relationship extended to this external cohort, although high confidence did not guarantee correctness.

External performance remained class-dependent. Without abstention, PedDx correctly classified all five DMG/DIPG cases, 11 of 13 LGGs, and 89 of 102 medulloblastomas, but none of the five ATRT or four ependymoma cases (Table 3; Fig. 4b,c). Five of these nine rare-class cases (ependymoma and ATRT) led to abstention. Among retained cases, the 12 errors comprised eight medulloblastomas labeled LGG and four ATRT or ependymoma cases assigned another histology. Thus, the external cohort reproduced the internal pattern of stronger performance for the three common classes and poor diagnosis of the two rarer classes. Because medulloblastoma accounted for 102 of 129 cases, overall accuracy should be interpreted alongside class-wise performance and the small samples for other diagnoses.

To distinguish institutional shift from differences in histologic composition, we also compared CHOA and discovery medulloblastomas using the OOD scores. Free energy provided little separation after class matching (AUC-ROC, 0.521; 95% CI, 0.452-0.591), indicating limited detectable distribution shift by this score within the matched class (Supplementary Tables 18 19). Class-probability distributions are shown in Fig. 4d.

Failure modes under clinical and biological distribution shift: out-of-distribution (OOD) analysis

Clinical shift markedly reduced accuracy, with a smaller reduction in confidence.

Accuracy without abstention fell from 79.8% in replication to 38.1% in clinical OODs with post-treatment imaging, 28.1% in clinical OODs with inadequate image quality, and 17.9% for tumors outside the posterior fossa. Median confidence decreased from 0.802-0.830 in the internal in-distribution cohorts to 0.535-0.608 across clinical-shift strata, while abstention increased to 35.9-37.5% (Supplementary Table 12). Unlike its effect in the replication set, abstention provided little recovery of accuracy in OOD cohort: retained-case accuracy was 39.1% for post-treatment cases, 30.0% for cases with inadequate image quality, and 24.0% for tumors outside the posterior fossa. Confidencebased selection therefore did not preserve diagnostic performance outside the intended population.

We assessed whether additional scores could identify these shifted inputs. Among 20 post-hoc scores, free energy separated the clinical-shift strata from discovery (indistribution) cohort with AUC-ROCs of 0.728-0.774. It modestly improved on maximum softmax probability for post-treatment and anatomically atypical cases (AUC-ROC differences, 0.030 and 0.043; adjusted P = 0.004 and 0.0065, respectively), but featuredistance scores did not outperform the softmax baseline across clinical strata. None of the scores significantly separated the independent replication control from discovery after multiplicity correction (Supplementary Tables 14 15 16 17). Class matching reduced detection of anatomically atypical tumors from 0.746 to 0.663, showing that some pooled separation reflected differences in histologic composition; post-treatment detection was largely unchanged (Supplementary Tables 18 19). Biological OOD cases exposed a different limitation. Fifteen of 22 tumors outside the five-class labels (68.2%) exceeded the confidence threshold and received a modeled diagnosis. Only two scores-crop mutual information and crop argmax instabilitysignificantly separated these cases from discovery after correction, each with an AUC-ROC of 0.716. At a threshold set to the discovery reference's 95th percentile, however, they detected only 9.1% and 0% of biological OOD cases, respectively (Supplementary Tables 20 21). Consequently, moderate ranking-based separation did not translate into reliable identification at this operating point, and the routine abstention rule failed to reject most unsupported histologies.

Exploratory feature-space analyses provided context for the limited utility of distancebased detection. Frozen embeddings showed weak histologic clustering despite supporting classification, and biological OOD cases were not unusually distant from the discovery center (median 40th percentile of discovery distances). These observations suggest that proximity in the frozen feature space was insufficient to establish whether a tumor belonged to the supported diagnostic classes (Supplementary Note 7; Supplementary Fig. 8; Supplementary Tables 22 23 24 25).

Controlled perturbations identify acquisition and alignment vulnerabilities

Paired perturbations of 50 replication cases tested sensitivity to specific degradations while holding patient and histology constant. Accuracy without abstention declined from 80.0% on unperturbed images to 68.0% at the highest ghosting severity and 64.0% at the highest motion severity (Fig. 5a,b). Severity trends were significant for both artifacts (P = 0.013 and P = 0.006, respectively). Motion increased abstention from 18.0% to 36.0%, whereas ghosting did not produce a consistent increase, indicating different uncertainty responses to the two artifact types (Fig. 5e).

Rotations applied after preprocessing evaluated sensitivity to residual misalignment of the atlas-defined sampling region. Aggregate accuracy remained between 74.0% and 86.0% for rotations up to ±20°, with no significant difference from the reference after correction, although individual predictions could change. Larger rotations produced axisdependent degradation: rotations about the left-right axis reduced accuracy to 44.0-50.0% at ±45°, while rotations about the superior-inferior axis primarily increased abstention (Fig. 5c,d).

Across perturbation conditions, abstention intercepted 113 of 189 newly incorrect results (59.8%) arising from cases correctly classified at baseline; the remaining 76 were returned as incorrect predictions (Fig. 5f). These counts represent subject-condition results, not unique patients. Confidence became less informative about errors as degradation increased: error-detection AUC-ROC fell from 0.97 without perturbation to 0.75 under 30-45° rotations. The controlled analyses therefore identified both sensitivity to degraded inputs and incomplete protection from confidence-based abstention. Full severity-specific results are provided in Supplementary Note 8 and Supplementary Tables 26 27.

Independent evaluation of the complete workflow

Macquarie University independently executed the deployed framework on 40 examinations from patients diagnosed at Women's and Children's Hospital (WCH; Adelaide, Australia), without involvement from the development team. All cases completed processing and produced a report and QC record (Supplementary Note 9).

The classifier returned 37 diagnoses and three abstentions; accuracy was 92.5% without abstention (37/40; 95% CI, 80.1-97.4%) to 97.3% among retained cases (36/37; 95% CI, 86.2-99.5%; Fig. 6a). The remaining error was a DMG/DIPG assigned medulloblastoma with confidence of 0.84. This cohort contained medulloblastoma, LGG, and DMG/DIPG only, so it did not evaluate the framework's diagnostic performance for ATRT or ependymoma. Per-class results are provided in Fig. 6b,c and Supplementary Table 28.

Across replication, CHOA, and WCH, the same confidence thresholds yielded highconfidence accuracies of 93.8-100%, with abstention rates of 16.7%, 17.8%, and 7.5%, respectively (Fig. 6d). These findings demonstrate application of the fixed classifier and decision rule across the evaluated settings, while the independent Macquarie execution additionally establishes feasibility of running the complete workflow on external infrastructure.

The QC agent assigned PROCEED to 23 cases, FLAG to 14, and ESCALATE to three (Supplementary Fig. 12). These states characterized processing evidence but did not identify additional diagnostic errors beyond abstention in this cohort: non-PROCEED status identified one of the three errors, which abstention also intercepted, whereas abstention identified two additional cases not identified by the QC agent. Withholding all non-PROCEED cases would additionally have withheld 16 correct predictions. Thus, successful independent execution and structured QC reporting did not establish an improvement in diagnostic accuracy from the agentic layer.

Discussion

PedDx combines clinically relevant pediatric brain tumor classification with a framework for evaluating how diagnostic AI could be independently tested and used responsibly. Its strongest diagnostic results were observed for medulloblastoma, LGG, and DMG/DIPG, including in external cohorts and independent testing on institutional infrastructure. Beyond differential diagnosis, the study examined uncertainty, abstention, clinical and biological OOD inputs, and controlled imaging perturbations within an executable workflow. These complementary assessments address questions central to prospective evaluation: whether performance transfers across institutions, when predictions should be withheld, which unfamiliar inputs remain confidently misclassified, and whether another institution can reproduce the complete processing and reporting pathway.

The medulloblastoma findings are particularly relevant. Preoperative diagnosis of this malignant embryonal tumor, which is the most common malignant brain tumors of childhood, can inform diagnostic assessment and planning 1, 2 . Medulloblastoma management requires coordinated staging and multimodal treatment, making its distinction from other pediatric tumors an important clinical question, although definitive diagnosis and treatment planning require pathological and molecular characterization 4, 9 . Suspicion of a medulloblastoma tumor can prompt whole-spine MRI before surgery, when feasible, avoiding postoperative changes that can mimic or obscure leptomeningeal dissemination 26 . In the CHOA external cohort, PedDx correctly classified 89 of 102 medulloblastomas before abstention and 78 of 86 retained cases after applying the prespecified rule. It also correctly classified 11 of 13 LGGs and all five DMGs before abstention; all retained LGG and DMG cases were correct. Independent testing by the Macquarie team on WCH data yielded 92.5% accuracy across 40 medulloblastoma, LGG, and DMG cases, with 36 of 37 retained predictions correct. These findings support high diagnostic performance and generalizability of the PedDx model for these three diagnoses, particularly medulloblastoma, while the smaller LGG and DMG samples in the external set warrant cautious interpretation.

Previous studies have established the feasibility of pediatric brain tumor classification across institutions 9,10,13,27 . Quon et al. demonstrated multicenter detection and classification of posterior fossa tumors using T2-weighted MRI 10 . Lee et al.'s FL-PedBrain extended this field through an international federated platform for tumor segmentation and classification, including independent external holdouts 27 . More recently, Jin et al. evaluated a T2-weighted MRI pipeline for segmentation, classification, and molecular subtyping using internal and external cohorts 13 . PedDx builds on these advances by bringing foundation model adaptation, confidence-guided abstention, complementary OOD analyses, controlled failure testing, and independently executed agentic orchestration into a single study. One of the main distinctive contributions of PedDx is the breadth and integration of evidence used to characterize both diagnostic capability and conditions relevant to real-world translation.

The foundation model approach provides a practical basis for this framework. PedDx adapts frozen representations from 3DINO 25 , a self-supervised model developed for transferable three-dimensional medical image analysis. A lightweight classifier uses features from anatomically defined crops of T2-weighted MRI, without requiring a tumor mask for diagnostic inference, providing an application and evaluation of pretrained volumetric representations in a clinically focused diagnostic setting. Parameter efficiency offers a feasible adaptation strategy where labeled pediatric imaging data are limited.

Uncertainty and abstention were incorporated as safeguards for clinical translation.

Communicating uncertainty and allowing a model to defer are recognized approaches to supporting responsible medical decision making 15, 17 . In PedDx, calibration and the abstention threshold were fixed using discovery data and transferred without external adjustment. The rule intercepted 14 of 23 replication errors and 12 of 24 CHOA errors, while also withholding five and eleven correctly classified cases, respectively. This tradeoff is central to its intended use: abstention reduces the occasions on which an uncertain prediction is presented as an actionable diagnosis, at the cost of additional cases requiring review. Accuracy among retained predictions must therefore be interpreted alongside coverage and the burden of deferral 17 . The safeguard helps temper overconfident use of model outputs but cannot eliminate confidently incorrect predictions.

Independent execution provides an additional dimension of evidence beyond conventional external validation. The Macquarie evaluation demonstrated that the complete PedDx workflow could be executed on external institutional infrastructure without developer involvement, with all 40 examinations completing processing and generating structured reports. This operational reproducibility addresses an important aspect of AI generalizability that cannot be established by evaluating classifier predictions on externally acquired data alone. Consistent with FUTURE-AI recommendations emphasizing traceability, documentation, and continuous quality control 24 , the agentic framework integrates preprocessing, QC, inference, and reporting while maintaining records of intermediate processing findings and potential failures. Language-model agents coordinate predefined tools and summarize structured QC evidence, enabling case-level reports that support troubleshooting, interpretation of model behavior, and retrospective review of processing decisions. Importantly, the agents cannot modify diagnostic probabilities or abstention decisions, preserving separation between diagnostic inference and workflow orchestration. This architecture illustrates how agentic systems can support transparent and independently reproducible evaluation of medical AI, although the added value of language-model orchestration over deterministic workflows remains to be established.

The OOD and perturbation analyses revealed vulnerabilities that would not be apparent from conventional external validation alone. Clinical OOD testing examined changes in treatment status, image quality, and anatomical tumor location; biological OOD testing assessed tumors outside the supported diagnostic categories; and controlled perturbations characterized sensitivity to acquisition artifacts and residual registration errors. Although confidence generally decreased under clinical distribution shift, substantial errors persisted among retained predictions, and 15 of 22 biologically unfamiliar tumors received a modeled diagnosis without abstention. These findings demonstrate that calibration and confidence-based deferral, while useful within the intended population, do not reliably identify all unsupported inputs or prevent confidently incorrect predictions under distribution shift, consistent with prior evidence regarding uncertainty degradation 20 . This reinforces the importance of the robustness and risk assessment principles emphasized by FUTURE-AI 24 and the need for comprehensive validation and characterization of model limitations advocated by AI-RAPNO 23.

Importantly, the OOD scores evaluated here provided limited additional protection, indicating that confidence, QC measures, and distribution-shift detection should be considered complementary rather than interchangeable safeguards. Systematically documenting these failure modes can inform future eligibility criteria, quality-control procedures, escalation pathways, and post-deployment monitoring strategies.

Several limitations define the scope of the findings. Diagnostic performance was uneven across histologies: none of the five ATRTs or four ependymomas in the CHOA cohort were correctly classified before abstention. PedDx therefore cannot currently be considered a reliable five-histology differential diagnostic system, and its strongest evidence concerns medulloblastoma, LGG, and DMG. Retrospective selection, class imbalance, and small rare-tumor and biological OOD samples further constrain generalizability. Larger, more diverse training cohorts may improve discrimination of underrepresented histologies, including ATRT and ependymoma, and enable expansion to currently unsupported diagnoses such as ETMR. Reliance on T2-weighted MRI excludes potentially useful information from complementary sequences, particularly diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI), for distinguishing medulloblastoma, ependymoma, and pilocytic astrocytoma.

Although independent execution demonstrated technical feasibility and reproducible reporting, the relatively small external cohort and its restricted histological composition limit conclusions about broader clinical performance. Furthermore, agent-assigned QC states did not identify additional diagnostic errors beyond confidence-based abstention in this cohort, and the incremental benefits of agentic orchestration for error detection, efficiency, or usability remain unestablished. Finally, the framework has not been evaluated prospectively in clinical practice, and its effects on clinician decision-making, workflow burden, and patient outcomes remain unknown.

In conclusion, PedDx illustrates the importance of evaluating diagnostic AI systems beyond conventional classification accuracy through complementary assessments of uncertainty, distribution shift, failure modes, and independent end-to-end execution.

These evaluations provide evidence of both diagnostic strengths and limitations, while structured QC and reporting offer a foundation for traceability and troubleshooting. Future studies should assess the framework in prospective clinical settings, including its effects on clinician decision-making, reliance on AI predictions, review burden, and consequences of incorrect diagnoses or unnecessary abstention. Such evaluations align with DECIDE-AI recommendations emphasizing transparent reporting of early-stage clinical AI evaluation, including safety, human factors, and clinical utility 28 . By combining diagnostic modeling with systematic reliability assessment and independently reproducible execution, PedDx provides a framework for investigating the requirements and limitations of AI-assisted pediatric brain tumor diagnosis.

Methods

Study design and cohorts

We retrospectively reviewed imaging and clinical data from the Children's Brain Tumor Network (CBTN), which is a publicly-accessible biorepository, and the Children's Subjects were eligible if they had a posterior fossa tumor and available MRI. Across the four sources, 1,181 subjects were identified; 170 were excluded for substantial motion artifact or preprocessing failure, leaving 1,011 subjects (Supplementary Fig. 1). The out-of-distribution (OOD) cohort comprised 236 subjects: 22 patients with rare posterior fossa tumor histologies not represented during model training (biological OOD; Supplementary Table 1a) and 214 patients meeting one or more clinical OOD criteria.

The clinical OOD criteria were prior treatment (n = 176), suboptimal image quality (n = 32), and tumor located outside the posterior fossa (n = 39); 33 subjects met two criteria.

Previously treated examinations included 151 after resection and 25 after chemotherapy and/or radiotherapy without resection. These cohorts were used to characterize behavior outside the development distribution.

Image preprocessing and model architecture

All imaging data were processed using our institutional pre-processing framework (https://github.com/d3b-center/peds-brain-seg-pipeline-public) 29, 30 , which integrates components from the Cancer Imaging Phenomics Toolkit (CaPTk, version 1.8.1; https://www.cbica.upenn.edu/captk) 31, 32 . MRI scans were first co-registered to the SRI24 atlas 33 with isotropic resampling to 1 mm³, providing a fixed input size of 240 × 240 × 155 voxels, then underwent skull stripping (https://github.com/d3b-center/peds-brain-auto-skull-strip) 29, 34 and intensity normalization to a [0-255] range following removal of intensity outliers.

The posterior fossa region was defined anatomically in SRI24 space before model fitting and applied unchanged across cohorts. The expanded region occupied i ∈ [56, 184), j ∈ [100, 212), and k ∈ [0, 76), yielding a 128 × 112 × 76-voxel box. The adopted posterior fossa multi-crop configuration (PF_MC) extracted 18 views at offsets Δi, Δj ∈ {-8, 0, +8} and Δk ∈ {0, +8} voxels. Inferior translations were constrained by the image boundary at k = 0. Each crop was resized by trilinear interpolation to 112 × 112 × 112 voxels, matching the pretrained backbone input. These fixed offsets provided deterministic sampling rather than stochastic augmentation. The extraction regions and sampling envelope are illustrated in Supplementary Fig. 13.

Each resized crop was normalized independently: intensities were clipped at the 0.05th and 99.95th percentiles of its brain voxels and linearly rescaled to [-1, 1], with background mapped to -1. Brain voxels were defined as those exceeding 2% of the crop's 99.5th intensity percentile. Computing percentiles over brain voxels avoided the predominance of zero-valued background in the posterior fossa region.

Features were extracted using 3DINO-ViT-L 25 , a self-supervised vision transformer pretrained on approximately 100,000 three-dimensional scans from more than ten organs. Its stride-16 patch embedding produces 343 patch tokens for a 112³ input, with a token width of 1,024. The backbone remained frozen throughout. Final-block class (CLS) tokens were averaged across the 18 views to form one 1,024-dimensional subject-level representation. Features were standardized using training-set statistics and classified by a five-class multilayer perceptron (MLP) with two 256-unit hidden layers, GELU activations, dropout, and a final softmax output. Averaging occurred in feature space before classification.

Model development and comparative evaluation

Model selection and internal validation

Model development used the discovery cohort exclusively. Candidate configurations shared five outer cross-validation folds; within each outer training fold, five-fold inner cross-validation selected hyperparameters by mean macro-AUC. The primary grid comprised six extraction regions, two representations, and five classification heads (60 combinations). Extraction strategies included the anatomically restricted posterior fossa box, a native 112³ posterior fossa cube requiring no resizing, and head-centered controls that excluded part of the inferior posterior fossa. Region definitions and comparisons are provided in Supplementary Table 29, Supplementary Note 10, and Supplementary Tables 30a,b.

The representations were the final-block CLS token (1,024 dimensions) and its concatenation with the mean patch token (2,048 dimensions; Supplementary Note 11 and Supplementary Table 31). Heads comprised a multiclass linear probe, multinomial logistic regression after standardization and principal-component analysis (PCA), a multiclass MLP with one or two hidden layers, and one-versus-rest linear and MLP configurations.

Linear and MLP heads were trained for 300 epochs with AdamW and a cosine learningrate schedule; logistic regression used L-BFGS. Hyperparameter search spaces and comparative results are provided in Supplementary Tables 32-33. A randomly initialized backbone served as a negative control (Supplementary Note 13 and Supplementary Table 34).

Selection proceeded sequentially: extraction region was compared across the ten representation-head combinations, representation within the selected region, and head within the selected region and representation. Multiplicity was controlled separately at each stage. A prespecified parsimony rule favored the lower-dimensional representation when differences were not significant. The MLP was chosen by overall rank rather than demonstrated superiority over the other multiclass heads. These comparisons therefore describe conditional selection stages rather than independent effects of each design component.

Replication results were not used for selection. Candidate configurations were evaluated on replication after their hyperparameters had been fixed using discovery; the deployed configuration was subsequently chosen by the discovery-only staged procedure. For deployment, separate non-nested five-fold cross-validation over a 32point MLP grid selected a common configuration for all folds. The final model was refitted on all 492 discovery subjects and evaluated on the held-out cohorts.

Multi-crop sensitivity analysis and supervised benchmark

After fixing the region, representation, and head, we varied the number of views (1, 8, 18, or 50) at an 8-voxel maximum offset, and the maximum offset (4, 8, 12, or 16 voxels) at 18 views. In-plane positions were {0}, {-8, +8}, {-8, 0, +8}, or {-8, -4, 0, +4, +8}, respectively, with through-plane positions constrained to nonnegative offsets. The offset sweep used {-m, 0, +m} in-plane and {0, +m} through-plane. Each configuration used a newly fitted head with the same architecture, hyperparameters, and subject-level folds; standardization was fitted within each training fold. The single centered crop was analyzed separately from multi-view configurations.

The supervised benchmark was a MedicalNet-pretrained 3D ResNet-34 fine-tuned end-to-end on the same discovery subjects, folds, and preprocessing. It received the complete registered volume rather than posterior fossa crops. Five independent oneversus-rest classifiers were tuned by five-fold discovery cross-validation using validation AUC-ROC and then refitted on the complete discovery cohort. Class-specific temperatures were fitted to out-of-fold discovery predictions. Cases with no calibrated class probability ≥0.50 were abstained; otherwise, a softmax transformation of the five calibrated outputs was used to select the highest-scoring class and calculate confidence and certainty. Because the independent sigmoid outputs do not form a mutually exclusive distribution, primary benchmark comparisons used class-wise uncalibrated probabilities for both models, summarized by macro-averaging.

Calibration, uncertainty, and abstention

A single temperature T was fitted by minimizing negative log-likelihood on the selected configuration's out-of-fold discovery predictions 16 . For logit zₖ, calibrated probabilities were pₖ = exp(zₖ/T)/Σⱼ₌₁⁵ exp(zⱼ/T). The fitted temperature was applied unchanged to replication and both external cohorts. Calibration was assessed before and after scaling using reliability diagrams, top-label expected calibration error (ECE; ten equal-width bins), Brier score, and negative log-likelihood.

Prediction confidence was maxₖ pₖ. Normalized entropy was H = -Σₖ₌₁⁵ pₖ ln(pₖ)/ln(5), and distributional certainty was 1 -H. The top-two margin was the difference between the largest and second-largest probabilities. Distributional certainty summarizes concentration of the probability vector and does not have the frequency interpretation of a calibrated class probability.

The model assigned the most probable class when confidence was ≥0.50 and otherwise abstained. Confidence tiers were high (≥0.80), moderate (≥0.50 and <0.80), and abstained (<0.50). Abstained outputs retained class probabilities and uncertainty measures but did not assign a diagnostic label. Model weights, calibration, and the abstention threshold were fixed for held-out evaluation, with no refitting on external data.

Distribution-shift and robustness analyses

Clinical and biological distribution shift

The frozen classifier was evaluated on clinical and biological OOD data without retraining or changing its decision rule. Twenty post-hoc OOD scores covered probability, logit, feature-distance, crop-disagreement, and hybrid approaches. Feature distances were computed in the 1,024-dimensional backbone and 256-dimensional penultimatehead spaces; crop disagreement used the 18 views before feature averaging. Larger scores consistently indicated greater shift. Score definitions and fixed parameters are provided in Supplementary Tables 35a,b.

All reference statistics-including class-specific feature statistics, nearest-neighbor banks, ReAct clipping thresholds, and ViM subspaces and scaling-were estimated from discovery training data. Feature-distance scores for replication, CHOA, and OOD subjects were averaged across five fold-specific discovery reference banks; scores requiring only deployment logits were computed directly from the frozen classifier. CHOA OOD and feature-space analyses included its 102 medulloblastomas for class-matched comparison; the remaining 27 cases contributed to classification evaluation only.

Discovery out-of-fold scores defined the ID reference distribution, and replication served as an independent ID control. Outcomes included OOD AUC-ROC, average precision, false-positive rate at 95% sensitivity, and the proportion exceeding the ID reference's 95th percentile. Candidate scores were compared with maximum softmax probability. Score orientations were fixed before analysis; reverse separation (AUC-ROC <0.5) was reported separately rather than counted as successful detection.

Feature-space analysis

We characterized standardized backbone embeddings and penultimate-head activations using class centroids and a shared Ledoit-Wolf covariance estimated from discovery. Discovery subjects were scored against statistics from the other four folds.

Mahalanobis distances were expressed as discovery-distribution percentiles. Class separability was measured using silhouette coefficients and the fraction of each subject's 22 nearest neighbors sharing its class. Fold-respecting linear probes used the top k principal components ranked by explained variance or class-discriminative power, with PCA and rankings fitted within training folds. Geometric conclusions relied on the frozen space because the deployed head's training-subject separability is optimistic.

Controlled imaging perturbations

Fifty replication cases were randomly selected with histologic stratification (ATRT, 5; DMG/DIPG, 7; EPN, 5; LGG, 14; MB, 19). TorchIO v0.21.3 21 simulated ghosting and motion in k-space on unprocessed images. Ghosting used four ghosts along the phaseencoding axis at intensities 0.3, 0.6, and 0.9. Motion combined k-space from two rigid displacements with rotations/translations of 3, 6, or 10 degrees/mm. Parameters were fixed at each severity, with deterministic subject-condition seeds. Perturbed images underwent the same containerized preprocessing; negative intensities were set to zero after skull stripping and before normalization.

To assess classifier sensitivity to residual misalignment, rotations were applied after preprocessing, immediately before inference, about the left-right, anterior-posterior, and superior-inferior axes at ±5°, ±10°, ±15°, ±20°, ±30°, and ±45°. Rotations were imagecentered, with linear interpolation, zero padding, and no translation or scaling. The 42 perturbations per subject produced 2,100 volumes. The deployed model, temperature, and threshold were unchanged, and rerunning unperturbed images reproduced all reference predictions. Each condition was compared with the same subject's unperturbed prediction.

Outcomes were accuracy without abstention, abstention rate, accuracy among predicted cases, class-change frequency, and total-variation distance, ½Σₖ|pₖ -pₖ,ref|. The breaking angle was the smallest absolute rotation causing a class change or new abstention. Perturbation-induced errors were incorrect results among subjects correctly classified at baseline; the ability of confidence to identify incorrect results was assessed by AUC-ROC.

Agentic workflow and application

Agent responsibilities and execution

The framework comprised a quality-control (QC) agent and a prediction agent (Fig. 7a), implemented through the Anthropic Messages API function-calling interface using Claude Opus 5 (model identifier claude-opus-5). Model settings and prompts were controlled by one versioned configuration (Supplementary Table 36). Agents received scalar measurements and categorical tool records, never images, and could not change the prescribed processing order. The QC agent invoked input inspection, acquisition QC, pipeline execution, and checks of registration, skull stripping, segmentation, containment, and normalization, within a 16-turn limit. It interpreted these records and assigned a QC state; the measurements themselves were computed by deterministic tools.

The prediction agent used image inspection, QC-record retrieval, and prediction tools, within an eight-turn limit. Before inference, the T2-weighted volume was checked for atlas geometry, 1-mm isotropic spacing, and LPS orientation. The agent invoked the classifier described above and reported its output without changing the class order, confidence values, or abstention decision. The agents supported orchestration and evidence reporting rather than diagnostic inference. Verbatim prompts are provided in Supplementary Note 16; tool definitions and thresholds are available in the code repository.

Registration rotation was derived as arccos[(trace(R) -1)/2] from the transform's rotation submatrix, and translation as the vector norm. Translation >50 mm identified subject-to-atlas transforms; other transforms were treated as within-subject. Thresholds are listed in Table 4. Optimizer line-search warnings alone were not failures when transforms remained within bounds.

Brain-mask volumes were voxel counts multiplied by voxel volume. Components containing >5% of positive voxels were considered large; left-right asymmetry was the absolute hemispheric voxel-count difference divided by their sum. Volumes >1,500 mL were flagged only with fragmentation or asymmetry. Tumor labels followed our pediatric segmentation convention 30, 34 : enhancing, nonenhancing, and cystic components formed tumor core; edema was measured separately. Whole-tumor volume was the union of nonzero labels, and components containing >10% of tumor voxels were considered large.

The edema-with-minimal-tumor check applied only when tissue-specific labels were interpretable. Containment used a brain mask on the segmentation grid; absence of a matching mask was flagged. Outside-brain fractions were calculated over segmentationpositive voxels. Normalization checks used nonzero-voxel extrema and percentiles for each channel.

The QC agent assigned PROCEED for satisfactory evidence, FLAG for usable outputs requiring review, or ESCALATE for failure or an unresolved high-consequence signal. Missing checks were treated as absent evidence, precluded PROCEED, and limited QC confidence to low. Otherwise, QC confidence was high for complete, concordant findings away from thresholds, moderate for borderline or partly conflicting findings, and low for provisional or conflicting evidence. This agent-assigned confidence was distinct from computed diagnostic confidence. The state, supporting findings, and evidence that could change the assessment were retained in the case record. The full finding and disposition mappings are provided in Supplementary Tables 37 38.

Processing verdict and reporting

A deterministic assessment combined preprocessing and pre-inference findings into a processing verdict (Fig. 7b), with findings mapped to three severity levels and four verdicts assigned by strict precedence. Missing QC records were labeled "processing could not be verified." This verdict qualified interpretation of the case report; QC states did not interrupt inference or suppress generated outputs. Each subject reaching inference received a prediction or confidence-based abstention, accompanied by its QC findings.

The report included the processing verdict, measured findings and their consequences, stage-wise QC records, agent-assigned QC state and confidence, calibrated class probabilities, prediction confidence, distributional certainty, and abstention status. It also reported the runner-up class, top-two margin, and fraction of crops agreeing with the reported class. The prediction agent could not substitute its own confidence or diagnostic label.

The browser application submitted a subject identifier and axial T2-weighted MRI, with FLAIR, T1-weighted, and contrast-enhanced T1-weighted MRI optional (Fig. 8).

Preprocessing containers, agents, and report generation executed asynchronously on the server, with progress and agent output streamed to the interface. Users could inspect preprocessed images and segmentation overlays in three planes alongside probabilities, QC findings, and the processing verdict. API credentials remained server-side.

Independent external evaluation

External evaluation separated classifier performance from independent execution of the complete framework. The development team evaluated the fixed classifier on CHOA data (n = 129). The Macquarie team independently selected and processed 40 examinations on its own infrastructure without involvement from the development team, assessing preprocessing, QC, inference, and reporting. Model weights, calibration, and the abstention rule were unchanged.

Evaluation summarized processing completion, QC-record availability, QC-state distribution, diagnostic accuracy before and after abstention, abstention frequency, and confidence-tier performance. At Macquarie, macro-averaged performance among predicted cases was calculated across MB, LGG, and DMG/DIPG, the three represented histologies; ATRT and EPN were absent. We also examined associations between QC state and correctness and compared prediction confidence, distributional certainty, and ordinal QC state as indicators of classification error.
