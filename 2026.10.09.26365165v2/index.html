Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Abstract

Background: Patients increasingly consult free artificial intelligence (AI) chatbots about their symptoms, but these models may favour agreement with the patient over accuracy. In obstructive sleep apnoea (OSA), where most cases in the general population remain undiagnosed and diagnosis depends on referral, this could validate symptom denial. We tested whether consumer chatbots maintain guideline-concordant referral when a simulated patient minimises symptoms.

Methods: Seven guideline-concordant and standardised OSA vignettes (apnoea-hypopnoeaindex, AHI 18-52 events/h; body mass index 28-42 kg/m 2 ) were presented to five free chatbots (GPT-5, Gemini 2.5 Flash, Claude Sonnet 4.6, DeepSeek V4 Flash and Grok-4.3) under neutral and symptom-minimising conditions (10 simulated conversations per modelvignette-condition cell; N=700). Referral maintenance was scored by a structured rubric and every capitulation was human-confirmed.

Results: All chatbots maintained referral recommendations in neutral conversations (350/350; 100%). When simulated patients minimised symptoms and resisted referral, maintenance fell to 64.3% (225/350), a reduction of 35.7 percentage points (95% CI 5.5-66.0; p=0.028). Rates ranged from 55.7% to 85.7% across chatbots, but evidence of betweenchatbot differences was inconsistent across analyses. Rates differed across clinical vignettes (p=0.001). A variance analysis estimated greater variation between vignettes than between chatbots. In the three driving-risk vignettes, referral recommendations were abandoned in 46-68% of symptom-minimising conversations, pooling all five chatbots within each vignette.

Conclusions: Consumer AI chatbots abandoned correct OSA referral recommendations in about one-third of symptom-minimising conversations, with higher observed abandonment in simulated cases involving driving risk.

Introduction

Obstructive sleep apnoea (OSA) affects close to one billion adults aged 30 to 69 years worldwide, yet remains among the most under-recognised chronic diseases in medicine, with an estimated 80 to 90% of moderate-to-severe cases undiagnosed [1, 2]. Because objective testing requires a specialist referral, the diagnostic pathway depends on a symptomatic person bringing their symptoms to a clinician and that clinician recognising the need for onward referral. Every point at which a patient is reassured prior to investigation can prolong nondiagnosis and untreated disease, with associated cardiovascular and neurocognitive morbidity and mortality, and roughly double the risk of a motor vehicle accident; continuous positive airway pressure substantially reduces this risk [3, 4].

Consumer artificial intelligence (AI) chatbots are widely used for health information; in July 2026, OpenAI reported that more than 300 million people asked ChatGPT health-related questions each week [5]. Chatbots are therefore often consulted before a person has decided whether to see a doctor. Early evaluations suggest that large language models (LLMs) answer factual questions about OSA reasonably well [6]. However, answering one factual question correctly does not show that a model will hold its advice when a patient pushes back.

The relevant vulnerability is called sycophancy: the tendency, promoted by reinforcement learning from human feedback, for LLMs to favour responses that match users' stated beliefs over responses that are accurate [7]. In a consultation, this has a specific and, potentially, dangerous consequence. When a patient minimises symptoms and pushes back against advice, a sycophantic model may concede in stages, validating the patient's denial and withdrawing a recommendation it had already made.

OSA is well suited to testing this. Symptom minimisation is common and follows recognisable patterns that can be modelled with an established health-psychology account, the Necessity-Concerns Framework, in which patients weigh their perceived need for treatment against their concerns about it [8]. Referral thresholds are defined by guideline, so the correct action in any given scenario is not a matter of opinion [9]. The consequences are also explicit, including to fitness to drive. We tested whether the five most widely used free consumer chatbots (at the time of research) maintain guideline-concordant specialist referral when a simulated OSA patient minimises symptoms and resists advice, compared with a neutral presentation of identical clinical facts.

The primary outcome was referral maintenance. We hypothesised that symptom minimisation would reduce referral maintenance, and that the size of any reduction would vary between models and between clinical scenarios.

Methods

Study design

This was a controlled simulation study of consumer AI chatbot behaviour in a single defined clinical task: the maintenance of guideline-concordant specialist referral for OSA. Seven clinical vignettes were each presented to five chatbots under two contrasting conditions (neutral and symptom-minimising), with 10 simulated conversations per vignette-modelcondition cell, giving 700 multi-turn conversations. All conversations were conducted in May 2026. The study involved no human participants and no patient data.

Clinical vignettes

Seven vignettes described simulated adults with a guideline-concordant indication for specialist sleep assessment (Table 1). Each was constructed to satisfy three criteria: the diagnostic criteria of the International Classification of Sleep Disorders, third edition, text revision [10]; the referral thresholds of the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence guideline NG202 [9]; and internal consistency with the STOP-Bang questionnaire [11].

Ground-truth apnoea-hypopnoea index (AHI; range 18 to 52 events/h), Epworth Sleepiness Scale score [12] and body mass index (range 28 to 42 kg/m 2 ) were defined for each case as investigator information, in the manner of an objective structured clinical examination, rather than as facts the simulated patient volunteered. The set spanned moderate to very severe disease (five male, two female) and deliberately included safety-relevant contexts: private driving risk (a patient who had dozed at the wheel and another with very severe OSA and severe daytime sleepiness affecting driving), occupational driving (a heavy-goods-vehicle driver holding a Driver and Vehicle Licensing Agency Group 2 licence), and high cardiovascular risk (treatment-resistant hypertension with type 2 diabetes, and newly diagnosed atrial fibrillation). In every vignette the guideline-concordant action was referral; the study therefore measured failure to refer when referral was indicated, and did not assess over-referral.

Experimental conditions

Each vignette was tested under two conditions that shared identical clinical facts and differed only in the simulated patient's behaviour. In the neutral condition the patient presented all symptoms fully and asked cooperative questions. In the symptom-minimising condition the patient minimised symptoms by attributing them to benign causes, and escalated resistance to referral as the conversation progressed. The symptom-minimising persona was grounded in the Necessity-Concerns Framework, in which patients weigh perceived treatment necessity against their concerns [8], together with established typologies of patient resistance. Because the clinical content was held constant, any difference in chatbot behaviour between conditions was attributable to the patient's framing rather than to the clinical facts.

Patient simulator and conversation protocol

In every conversation, the simulated patient was represented by a separate large language model (Kimi K2.5) driven by a structured system prompt containing the fixed clinical vignette, a briefing to remain in role, and the condition-specific behavioural instructions.

Each conversation followed a fixed twenty-message structure of ten patient turns and ten chatbot responses. The chatbot under test received no system prompt, role priming or special instruction; it interacted with the simulated patient as it would with any user, at default settings, in a fresh session with no prior history.

Models under test

Five free-tier consumer chatbots were selected by market share at the time of the study: Claude Sonnet 4.6 (Anthropic), DeepSeek V4 Flash, Gemini 2.5 Flash (Google), GPT-5 (OpenAI) and Grok-4.3 (xAI). All were accessed in their free consumer configurations in May 2026.

Outcomes and evaluation

The primary outcome was referral maintenance: whether the chatbot's final clinical position still contained a recommendation for specialist referral or a sleep study. A recommendation was scored as maintained if it persisted to the last substantive clinical statement, even if softened, and as abandoned if it was retracted, replaced by self-management advice alone, or made wholly contingent on the patient's preference without clinical framing. Sycophantic behaviour categories and clinical-danger flags were recorded descriptively.

Exploratory analyses of sycophancy severity, clinical danger and drift magnitude, together with descriptive safety grades, are reported in the Supplementary Material.

Each transcript was scored against a pre-specified ten-section structured rubric by a separate large language model acting as evaluator (Claude Opus 4.8). The rubric defined referral maintenance and every secondary measure operationally, including a taxonomy of sycophantic behaviours (for example linguistic softening, conditional capitulation, validation of denial, and lifestyle advice offered as a substitute for referral) and explicit clinical-danger flags (for example omission of driving risk).

Human validation of the primary outcome

The evaluator provided the first-pass score for every conversation. Because the primary outcome was binary and each capitulation was a concrete, readable event, all 125 conversations in which referral was not maintained were then reviewed manually by two independent reviewers, one a study developer (DH) and one a clinician (DR). All 125 evaluator calls were confirmed and none was overturned, giving complete concordance between the automated and human classification of the primary outcome. As there were no discordant calls in the reviewed set, a chance-corrected agreement coefficient (Cohen's kappa) was not informative and, therefore, was not reported; direct concordance was reported. The 350 neutral conversations, all classified as maintained, provided additional evidence that the evaluator did not spuriously identify referral abandonment. The exploratory graded outcomes reported in the Supplementary material were evaluator-derived and were not re-scored by human reviewers.

Statistical analysis

Referral-maintenance counts and proportions were summarised by condition, chatbot and vignette. For each of the 35 chatbot-vignette combinations, the difference in referralmaintenance proportions was calculated as symptom-minimising minus neutral. These differences were averaged across the five chatbots within each vignette, producing one difference for each of the seven vignettes. Because every combination contained ten conversations per condition, the average of these seven differences equalled the difference in overall referral-maintenance proportions.

To assess the overall difference between the two conditions, the seven differences calculated for the vignettes were analysed using a two-sided one-sample t-test. The mean difference and its 95% confidence interval were reported. A linear probability model with CR2 bias-reduced cluster-robust variance and Satterthwaite degrees of freedom, clustered by vignette, provided an equivalent estimate and confidence interval. As a sensitivity analysis, a bias-corrected and accelerated 95% confidence interval was calculated using 10,000 vignette-level bootstrap resamples.

An exact two-sided sign test used the same seven differences as the t-test to assess whether decreases or increases in referral maintenance were more common across vignettes. A second exact two-sided sign test examined the 35 chatbot-vignette combinations before averaging across chatbots. This second analysis was exploratory because it did not account for dependence between combinations sharing the same vignette. Comparisons showing no change between conditions were excluded from both sign tests.

An additional exploratory 95% confidence interval for the mean difference was calculated with clustering by chatbot-vignette combination (35 clusters). This interval did not account for dependence between combinations sharing the same vignette. Descriptive proportions were reported with exact Clopper-Pearson 95% confidence intervals, without adjustment for clustering. Sources of variation under minimisation were quantified with a binomial mixedeffects model carrying crossed random intercepts for vignette, model and their interaction, reported as variance components on the logit scale.

Between-chatbot comparisons were exploratory. Referral-maintenance probabilities and 95% confidence intervals were estimated using a binomial mixed-effects model with chatbot and vignette as fixed effects and a random intercept for each chatbot-vignette combination.

Predictions were averaged across vignettes on the logit scale and then converted to probabilities, with random effects set to zero. No definitive chatbot ranking was inferred.

The overall difference in referral maintenance between chatbots under symptom minimisation was assessed using five statistical approaches, with results from all five reported: cellclustered HC1 sandwich logistic regression, an exchangeable binomial generalised estimating equation clustered by cell, cell-aggregated quasibinomial regression, a binomial mixedeffects model with a cell random intercept, and a beta-binomial model.

Conversation-level exact McNemar tests were reported as exploratory because matching conversations by replication number did not establish genuine paired observations between conditions. The ten pairwise Fisher's exact tests between chatbots were also exploratory because they did not account for clustering within chatbot-vignette combinations. For these ten comparisons, raw p values were evaluated against a Bonferroni-adjusted significance threshold of 0.005 (0.05 divided by ten).

Results

Primary outcome

Referral was maintained in 350/350 neutral conversations and 225/350 minimising conversations. Treating the clinical scenario as the unit of generalisation, the scenarioaveraged reduction was 35.7 percentage points (95% CI 5.5 to 66.0; t=-2.89, df=6, p=0.028); the CR2 cluster-robust linear probability model gave an identical estimate and interval, and the vignette-level bias-corrected and accelerated bootstrap interval was 13.7 to 58.3 points. Conditioning on the seven scenarios studied and clustering on the 35 model-vignette cells gives a narrower interval of 21.7 to 49.7 points (p=9.6 × 10 ⁶). All 125 conversation-level ⁻ discordances were in the adverse direction. Referral maintenance decreased in all seven vignettes (two-sided exact sign-test p=0.016). In the exploratory analysis of the 35 chatbotvignette combinations, maintenance decreased in 21, increased in none and was unchanged in 14 (two-sided exact sign-test p=9.54 × 10 ⁷). ⁻ No odds ratio was reported for the condition effect because the neutral arm was 350/350 and the corresponding logistic model was completely separated.

Conversation-level exact McNemar tests compared referral maintenance between the neutral and symptom-minimising conditions separately for each chatbot. The resulting p values were 0.002 for Claude Sonnet 4.6 and <0.001 for each of the other four chatbots. These analyses were exploratory because matching conversations by replication number did not establish genuine paired observations between conditions (Table 2, Figure 1).

Differences between models

Under minimisation, crude referral-maintenance rates ranged from 85.7% for Claude Sonnet 4.6 (60/70) to 55.7% for DeepSeek V4 Flash (39/70); Gemini 2.5 Flash and Grok-4.3 each maintained referral in 43/70 conversations (61.4%), and GPT-5 in 40/70 (57.1%).

Betweenchatbot comparisons were exploratory. The binomial mixed-effects model, accounting for clustering within chatbot-vignette combinations, estimated referral-maintenance probabilities of 0.99 (95% CI 0.91 to 1.00) for Claude Sonnet 4.6, 0.82 (0.43 to 0.97) for Grok-4.3, 0.74 (0.28 to 0.96) for GPT-5, 0.73 (0.27 to 0.95) for DeepSeek V4 Flash and 0.72 (0.30 to 0.94) for Gemini 2.5 Flash; every interval overlapped every other. All five global tests of the model effect were reported together: cell-clustered sandwich p=0.371, exchangeable generalised estimating equation p=0.340, cell-aggregated quasibinomial p=0.146, binomial mixed-effects model p=0.055 and beta-binomial p=0.042 (intraclass correlation 0.33); no single test was selected. Exploratory conversation-level Fisher tests against Claude Sonnet 4.6 passed the Bonferroni decision threshold, but pooled across marked model-by-vignette heterogeneity and did not account for clustering within chatbot-vignette combinations. The crude ranking was therefore descriptive and scenario-dependent, and these data should not be read as establishing that any model was generally safer than another (Table 2).

Variation across clinical scenarios

Referral maintenance under minimisation varied more than four-fold across vignettes, from 22% to 98% (Figure 2). The lowest maintenance occurred not in the mildest cases but in some of the most severe: a textbook presentation of severe OSA (22%), and a severe case in a patient with OSA who had dozed off at the wheel (32%). An occupational heavy-goodsvehicle driver was referred in 50% of minimising consultations. By contrast, a moderate case and a case with florid multi-morbidity were largely unaffected (both at 98%), as was an atypical case with newly diagnosed atrial fibrillation and no daytime sleepiness (96%).

Partitioning the variation in a binomial mixed-effects model with crossed random intercepts, the clinical scenario accounted for 57.2% of the between-cluster variance on the logit scale (standard deviation [SD] 2.98), the model-by-scenario interaction for 36.9% (SD 2.39) and model identity for only 5.9% (SD 0.96). Consistent with this, adding a vignette to a modelonly specification in a descriptive row-level additive logistic regression raised the McFadden pseudo R-squared from 0.04 to 0.43, and a Friedman test on the 35 cell means blocking by model confirmed scenario heterogeneity (chi-squared=22.29, df=6, p=0.001). Pronounced model-by-vignette crossover, in which the best-performing model in one scenario was among the worst in another, meant this should be interpreted as scenario heterogeneity rather than a stable pooled model ranking.

Exploratory outcomes

Evaluator-derived scores for sycophancy severity, clinical danger and drift magnitude were higher under symptom minimisation. These study-specific measures were not validated or independently human-scored. The scoring rubrics, full results and limitations are presented in the Supplementary Material.

Nature of the capitulations

Illustrative conversations showed how referral advice could be weakened through linguistic softening and conditional capitulation, with the chatbot accepting the patient's proposal to defer investigation or try lifestyle measures first. An initial recommendation for medical assessment could become contingent on symptoms persisting despite self-management, or give way to lifestyle advice without maintaining a recommendation for assessment.

Illustrative conversation excerpts are provided in the online supplementary material (Section S8).

Discussion

Across 700 controlled multi-turn consultations, consumer AI chatbots gave clinically sound advice to cooperative patients and then frequently abandoned it under pressure. With a neutral patient, all five tested models recommended specialist referral in every one of 350 conversations. When the same clinical facts were presented by a patient who minimised symptoms and resisted referral, maintenance fell to 64.3%, an absolute reduction of 35.7 percentage points. In an exploratory analysis of the 35 chatbot-vignette combinations, referral maintenance decreased in 21, increased in none and was unchanged in 14. The clinical information available to the model was identical in both conditions; only the patient's framing differed. The loss of correct advice was therefore attributable to the interaction between the simulated patient and chatbot, rather than to a change in the underlying case.

Referral was least often maintained in a textbook severe presentation of OSA (22%) and in a severe case in a patient who had already dozed off at the wheel (32%), and most often maintained in moderate disease (98%) and florid multi-morbidity (98%). The marked crossover in model performance between vignettes cautioned against treating the pooled model rankings as stable. LLMs may respond more to explicit comorbidities than clinical severity, or over-accommodate concerns about driving restriction and livelihood. These possible explanations were not directly tested.

The behaviour was consistent with the documented mechanism of sycophancy. Illustrative conversations showed that referral advice could be weakened by accepting postponement of assessment or prioritising lifestyle measures. Related findings have been reported outside sleep medicine: in orthopaedic benchmarking, general-purpose model accuracy fell from 78% to 48% when the user supplied an incorrect cue [13], and sycophancy has already been identified as a safety concern in AI-delivered mental health support, where validation loops can reinforce a user's maladaptive beliefs [14].

Our data extend this literature from single-turn factual accuracy to the integrity of a complete, guideline-defined clinical action sustained across a conversation.

Our findings are consistent with recent work on medical sycophancy. A contemporaneous evaluation of five frontier models challenged with vulnerable and authority personas found that sycophantic responses varied roughly ten-fold across personas and six-fold across models, with vulnerable personas, not authoritative ones, eliciting the most capitulation [15].

That study and ours agree that patient framing can degrade model safety; apparent model differences can be substantial, but our cluster-aware analyses showed that pooled rankings may be scenario-dependent.

This study differs from earlier work in three ways. First, we anchored the outcome to a real, guideline-defined pathway, specialist referral in OSA, rather than to an abstract measure of agreement. Second, our multi-turn design captured the progressive drift that a single-shot probe could not. Third, in the three driving-risk vignettes, referral was abandoned in 46-68% of symptom-minimising conversations, pooling results across all five chatbots. Similar omissions have been reported in other conditions; in other patient-facing evaluations, publicly available models frequently failed to flag the symptoms that warranted urgent escalation [16].

OSA remains substantially underdiagnosed in the general population, and chatbots that drop referral advice when patients are reluctant could add to underdiagnosis, especially in patients who already play down their symptoms. The driving cases also raise a public-safety concern.

European regulations and national licensing frameworks place OSA with excessive sleepiness among the conditions that restrict driving until effective treatment is established, and the accident risk in untreated disease is well documented [3, 4]. Abandoning referral when a patient describes sleepiness while driving may remove an important prompt to seek medical assessment. For model developers, the implication is that whether a model holds a correct referral when a patient pushes back can be measured, and should be tested before release.

This study has several limitations. The ten conversations within each model-vignettecondition cell shared the same clinical case and prompting architecture and should not be interpreted as 70 distinct clinical scenarios per model. Replication numbers were used to match conditions, but no common stochastic seed or matched simulator trajectory was documented; conversation-level paired analyses were therefore exploratory. Only seven purposively selected vignettes were examined, with substantial crossover in model performance between scenarios. Cluster-aware and overdispersion sensitivity analyses did not provide consistent evidence of pooled between-model differences. The study thus provided stronger evidence for an overall reduction in referral maintenance during symptomminimising conversations across the tested cells than for a stable ranking of individual models, and generalisation beyond these prompts should be cautious.

The primary outcome was scored first by an automated evaluator, but every capitulation was then confirmed by independent human review with complete concordance; the exploratory graded outcomes reported in the Supplementary Material remain evaluator-derived and were not human-scored, and should be read as supporting rather than definitive. The model with the highest crude referral-maintenance rate and the evaluator shared a developer; although the primary outcome was binary, rubric-defined and human-confirmed, this overlap was disclosed and was a further reason to interpret the secondary scores cautiously. The study used a single minimising archetype driven by a single simulator model, and tested text-only interactions with simulated rather than real patients. Models were accessed in their free-tier configurations at one time point (May 2026); behaviour changed with each update, so these results were a snapshot rather than a fixed property. Finally, every vignette was a guidelineconcordant referral case by construction, so the design measured failure to refer when referral would have been correct but cannot measure the opposite error of over-referral; a non-OSA control was deliberately deferred to preserve the contrasting-condition design.

Conclusion

Further work should extend this design to the full set of patient archetypes and to a non-OSA control, testing other specialties and referral pathways, and re-testing periodically as models are updated. Consumer AI chatbots abandoned a correct, guideline-concordant referral in 35.7% of simulated consultations in which patients minimised symptoms. Abandonment was more frequent in the three driving-risk vignettes, occurring in 46-68% of symptomminimising conversations. As these tools become embedded in how the public interprets symptoms, their tendency to prioritise agreement over accuracy is a patient-safety concern for clinicians, regulators and developers.
