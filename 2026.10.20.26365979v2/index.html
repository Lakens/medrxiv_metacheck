Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Metacheck does not yet analyze automatically whether sentences which include non-significant p-values are correct, but we recommend manually checking the sentences below for possible misinterpreted non-significant p values.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). Statcheck might still miss one-sided tests that are described in another sentence, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. Abstract

Background: We examined the relationships between acute pain intensity, patient-controlled dosing behaviour, and drug exposure during postoperative oxycodone patient-controlled analgesia (PCA) with or without adjunctive S-ketamine.

Methods: This observational secondary analysis included 100 patients undergoing lumbar fusion surgery in a randomized, double-blind trial comparing oxycodone PCA alone with three concentrations of adjunctive S-ketamine.Plasma concentration data were available for 99 patients. Generalized estimating equations were used to examine dosing behaviour, drug exposure, and adequate analgesia (pain intensity at rest ≤4/10) during the first 24 h.

Results: Plasma concentrations of oxycodone and S-ketamine increased rapidly after PCA initiation, with substantial between-patient variability. Metabolite concentrations accumulated over time. PCA activity decreased over time, and older age was associated with fewer delivered doses (rate ratio [RR] 0.905 per 10 years, 95% CI 0.829-0.987). Each 1-point higher current pain score was associated with more subsequent PCA attempts (RR 1.09, 95% CI 1.04-1.14) and delivered doses (RR 1.07, 95% CI 1.03-1.11). The association between oxycodone concentration and adequate analgesia weakened over time (interaction P < 0.001), with odds ratios per 10 ng/mL of 1.16 (95% CI 1.07-1.25), 1.13 (1.06-1.21), and 1.00 (0.979-1.03) at 1, 4, and 24 h, respectively. In S-ketamine-treated patients, these associations persisted after adjustment for measured S-ketamine and metabolite concentrations.

Conclusions: During PCA, drug exposure develops from patient-generated dosing histories while pain and recovery change. Pain intensity, patient-controlled dosing behaviour, and drug exposure are interconnected but non-equivalent features of postoperative analgesia.

What is already known on this topic

Opioid consumption during patient-controlled analgesia is commonly used as an outcome in postoperative analgesia trials, but its relationship with pain intensity and systemic drug exposure over time is incompletely understood.

What this study adds

Higher current pain was associated with greater subsequent patient-controlled dosing activity.

The association between plasma oxycodone concentration and adequate analgesia weakened during the first 24 postoperative hours. Achieved S-ketamine exposure reflected both randomized treatment allocation and patient-controlled dosing behaviour.

How this study might affect research, practice or policy

Pain intensity, opioid consumption, and systemic drug exposure should be considered distinct but interrelated outcomes when interpreting postoperative patient-controlled analgesia studies.

INTRODUCTION

Opioid patient-controlled analgesia (PCA) allows patients to self-administer intravenous opioids within predefined safety limits 1 . Compared with conventional systemic opioid administration, PCA provides slightly better pain control and greater patient satisfaction, although opioid consumption may be higher 2 . Intravenous oxycodone PCA may provide effective analgesia at lower doses than morphine in some surgical settings 3, 4.

PCA differs fundamentally from a predetermined dosing regimen because the patient participates in creating the dose history 1, 2 . PCA requirements vary with pain state and patientrelated factors 5, 6 , while successful requests generate delivered opioid doses from which cumulative administration and systemic exposure develop over time 3, 4, 7 . We therefore conceptualized PCA as a patient-responsive titration process in which pain state, dosing behaviour, administered drug, and exposure evolve together over time. This framework specifies temporal relationships rather than an automated or causal feedback system.

The pharmacokinetics of oxycodone and its relationship with analgesic effects have been characterized in several clinical settings 7, 8 . Plasma oxycodone concentration is a pharmacologically relevant measure of exposure, but its interpretation during PCA is not straightforward. A concentration measured at a given postoperative time reflects the preceding dosing history, while the clinical state in which that concentration is observed is also evolving.

Consequently, the association between plasma oxycodone concentration and pain during PCA needs to be considered in the temporal context in which both exposure and postoperative pain are evolving. How this relationship changes during the early course of postoperative PCA has not been well characterized.

Systematic reviews have shown opioid-sparing effects of adjunctive ketamine, including in patients undergoing spinal surgery 9, 10, 11 . In our randomized trial after lumbar fusion surgery, adjunctive S-ketamine reduced oxycodone consumption dose-dependently, with modest improvement in pain at rest at the highest dose 12 . During PCA, however, achieved S-ketamine exposure depends on both randomized treatment allocation and patient-controlled dosing.

Age has been associated with postoperative PCA requirements 5, 6 as well as oxycodone clearance and systemic exposure 13, 14, 15 . In our lumbar fusion cohort, preoperative emotional stress was associated with severe postoperative pain but not with oxycodone consumption 16.

Together, these observations suggest that pain intensity, opioid requirements, and systemic drug exposure need not vary in parallel.

The primary objectives of this analysis were to examine the association between current pain and subsequent PCA activity and whether the association between plasma oxycodone concentration and adequate analgesia varied over time. We also characterized drug exposure and age-related PCA behaviour during the first 24 h and assessed the robustness of the primary associations to adjustment for S-ketamine and metabolite concentrations.

METHODS

Study design

We conducted an observational secondary analysis of the DoseRespKeta study, a randomized, double-blind trial comparing postoperative PCA with oxycodone alone or combined with Sketamine at three dose ratios after lumbar fusion surgery. The trial protocol, eligibility criteria, and primary results have been published previously. ¹² Blood sampling and plasma drug concentration measurements were included in the approved study protocol, but the present analyses were not prespecified. The study was approved by the institutional review board of the

Randomization and postoperative analgesia and PCA protocol

Participants were randomized 1:1:1:1 to PCA with oxycodone 1 mg/mL alone (G1) or combined with S-ketamine 0.25 mg/mL (G2), 0.5 mg/mL (G3), or 0.75 mg/mL (G4). Randomization, masking, anaesthesia, and perioperative care have been described in detail previously 12.

Treatment group was retained as an adjustment variable in the present analyses; parent oxycodone was the principal exposure analyte.

The PCA pump was activated at the end of surgery. In the post-anaesthesia care unit, the study solution was titrated in 2-mL doses at 5-min intervals until pain at rest was ≤4 on the numerical rating scale (NRSR). Thereafter, each successful PCA request delivered 1 mL with a 5-min lockout. The randomized study solution was continued for 24 h 12.

Pain assessment

Pain intensity at rest was assessed using an 11-point numerical rating scale (NRSR;0 = no pain and10 = worst pain imaginable). Pain assessments were performed according to a predefined schedule as part of postoperative care, with additional assessments obtained at the time of blood sampling. Adequate analgesia was defined as NRSR ≤4.

Blood sampling and bioanalysis

Arterial blood (4 mL) was collected through the intraoperative arterial cannula at baseline and at 5, 15, 30-60 min, 4 h, and 18-24 h after PCA initiation into EDTA tubes. Plasma was separated within 30 min, aliquoted, and stored at -70 °C until analysis.

Plasma concentrations of oxycodone, noroxycodone, oxymorphone, and noroxymorphone and of ketamine, norketamine, and 6-hydroxynorketamine were determined using validated HPLC-MS/MS methods. The ketamine assay was not enantioselective 18, 19 . We administered only Sketamine, and ketamine enantiomers do not undergo interconversion 19 . Thus, the measured ketamine represented S-ketamine. The lower limits of quantification were 0.05 ng/mL for oxycodone and its metabolites, 0.025 ng/mL for ketamine, 0.25 ng/mL for norketamine, and 0.5 ng/mL for 6-hydroxynorketamine. Full analytical methods are provided in the Supplementary Methods.

Statistical analysis

The sample size was determined by the parent trial 12 without additional sample size calculation.

Analyses used available observations without imputation of missing data. Participants with missing plasma samples contributed to analyses not requiring concentration data.

Continuous variables are presented as mean (standard deviation) or median (interquartile range), and categorical variables as counts and percentages. Analyses were restricted to the first 24 h after PCA initiation. Drug concentrations were summarized descriptively at protocol sampling times.

Longitudinal associations of time and age with PCA attempts, delivered doses, cumulative oxycodone dose, and plasma oxycodone concentration were analysed using generalized estimating equations (GEEs), adjusting for randomized treatment group. Models included time, age, and their interaction. Count outcomes used Poisson GEEs with log interval duration as an offset and continuous outcomes identity-link GEEs. Age was centred at the sample median; tertiles were used only for visualization. Exploratory models replaced age with body weight.

The relationship between current pain and subsequent PCA activity was examined over intervals between consecutive observed NRSR assessments. PCA attempts and delivered doses during each subsequent interval were analysed using Poisson GEEs including current NRSR, time, age, and treatment group, with log interval duration as an offset.

The association between plasma oxycodone concentration and adequate analgesia (NRSR ≤4) was analysed using logistic GEE including concentration, NRSR assessment time, their interaction, age, and treatment group. Concentrations were paired with simultaneous NRSR assessments or, if unavailable, the nearest assessment within ±15 min; each concentration was used once. Time-specific odds ratios were estimated per 10 ng/mL higher oxycodone concentration.

Sensitivity analyses in the active S-ketamine groups (G2-G4) examined whether these associations were robust to measured S-ketamine and metabolite exposure. Analyses used the 15-, 60-, and 240-min epochs for pain-PCA models and additionally 1440 min for oxycodoneanalgesia models. Log2-transformed S-ketamine, norketamine, and 6-hydroxynorketamine concentrations were centred within treatment group and sampling epoch. The original model was refitted on the common subset (M0), followed by addition of S-ketamine (M1) and all three analytes (M2). Additional sensitivity analyses excluded the 15-min sampling epoch or observations with any S-ketamine or metabolite concentration below the limit of quantification.

As measured concentrations are post-randomization exposures influenced by preceding PCA use, their coefficients were interpreted as associations rather than causal effects.

All GEEs accounted for within-patient clustering using robust sandwich standard errors. Independence working correlation structures were used for longitudinal age and weight analyses, and exchangeable structures for pain-PCA, oxycodone-analgesia, and S-ketamineadjusted analyses.

Associations are reported as effect estimates with 95% confidence intervals. Statistical tests were two-sided, with a significance level of 0.05 and no adjustment for multiple comparisons.

Analyses used R version 4.5.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria), with tidyverse and ggplot2 for data management and visualization and geepack for GEE modelling.

Patient-controlled dosing behaviour and exposure

The longitudinal PCA activity analyses included 4529 intervals from 96 participants. PCA attempt and delivered-dose rates decreased with postoperative time (RR 0.965 and 0.966 per hour, respectively; both P<0.001; Supplementary Table S2 and Figure 2). Older age was associated with a lower delivered-dose rate (RR 0.905 per 10 years, 95% CI 0.829-0.987; P=0.02), whereas the association with attempt rate was less precise (RR 0.904, 95% CI 0.805- 1.02; P=0.09).

There was no evidence of age-by-time interactions for either outcome. Cumulative oxycodone dose increased by an estimated 3.03 mg/h (95% CI 2.69-3.37; P<0.001) at median age. Plasma oxycodone concentration increased by an estimated 1.57 ng/mL/h (95% CI 0.346-2.79; P=0.01) at median age, with no evidence of an age-by-time interaction (Supplementary Table S2).

Plasma oxycodone and adequate analgesia

Adequate analgesia was observed in 37/96 (38.5%), 77/97 (79.4%), and 66/95 (69.5%) patients at the 1-, 4-, and 24-h sampling epochs, respectively. The association between plasma oxycodone concentration and adequate analgesia varied with postoperative time (concentrationby-time interaction, P<0.001; Table 2 and Figure 3C). Per 10 ng/mL higher plasma oxycodone concentration, the odds ratio for adequate analgesia was 1.16 (95% CI 1.07-1.25) at 1 h and 1.13 (95% CI 1.06-1.21) at 4 h, compared with 1.00 (95% CI 0.979-1.03) at 24 h.

Among the ketamine-treated groups, this temporal pattern persisted after inclusion of measured S-ketamine, norketamine, and 6-hydroxynorketamine concentrations (Supplementary Table S3); in the fully adjusted model, the corresponding odds ratios were 1.30 (95% CI 1.10-1.53), 1.26 (1.09-1.45), and 1.02 (0.971-1.07) at 1, 4, and 24 h, respectively.

Within this postrandomization exposure analysis, higher measured S-ketamine concentration was associated with lower odds of adequate analgesia, whereas no clear associations were observed for norketamine or 6-hydroxynorketamine (Supplementary Table S4). This association was not reproduced in the analysis restricted to the 60-, 240-, and 1440-min epochs (Supplementary Table S4).

Exploratory analysis of body weight

Body weight showed no clear associations with PCA attempts, delivered doses, cumulative oxycodone dose, or plasma oxycodone concentration, and no weight-by-time interactions were observed (Supplementary Table S5).

DISCUSSION

During postoperative patient-controlled analgesia, opioid exposure reflects the patient's dosing history while pain and recovery change over time. Our results show that higher current pain was associated with greater subsequent PCA activity, whereas the association between plasma oxycodone concentration and adequate analgesia weakened with postoperative time. Achieved S-ketamine exposure reflected both randomized treatment allocation and patient-controlled dosing.

PCA allows patients to request doses in response to perceived need. Previous studies have described substantial variability in PCA requirements and identified patient-related factors associated with opioid use 1,2,5,6 . Our findings extend previous observations by showing a temporal association between pain and subsequent PCA use. Higher pain was associated with more subsequent PCA attempts and delivered doses. These associations remained essentially unchanged after adjustment for measured S-ketamine and metabolite concentrations. The temporal relationship is consistent with a patient-responsive titration process but does not establish a causal feedback relationship.

Age may influence postoperative oxycodone use through several mechanisms 5, 6 . Previous studies of morphine PCA have reported lower opioid requirements with increasing age [20] [21] [22].

Oxycodone clearance decreases with advancing age, and age-related differences in systemic exposure have been reported in postoperative patients 13, 14, 15 . In the present longitudinal analyses, the association between age and PCA attempts was less certain. Furthermore, there was no clear evidence of an association between age and plasma oxycodone concentration over time. The clearest age association was observed for delivered PCA dosing, although our data do not distinguish between behavioural, pharmacological, and other possible explanations.

Pain intensity, patient-controlled opioid use, and plasma exposure should not be regarded as interchangeable measures of the postoperative state. Previous studies of morphine PCA have demonstrated that postoperative pain and opioid use are related, but their associations with patient characteristics do not necessarily coincide 22, 23 . In our previous analysis of this cohort, preoperative emotional stress was associated with severe postoperative pain but not with oxycodone consumption 16 . In the present study, current pain was associated with subsequent PCA activity, showing that pain and patient-controlled dosing behaviour are related but distinct observations. The age analyses further indicated that associations with PCA attempts, delivered doses, and plasma oxycodone concentration were not uniform. These findings indicate that pain, patient-controlled dosing behaviour, and opioid exposure are related, but not interchangeable and need not vary in parallel.

S-ketamine concentrations increased early after PCA initiation, whereas norketamine and 6hydroxynorketamine exposure built up more gradually. Given the pharmacological activity of these metabolites, their slower formation may have clinical relevance during the early postoperative period, when analgesic requirements are high [24] [25] [26] . Randomization determined the S-ketamine concentration in the PCA solution, but achieved exposure also depended on patientcontrolled dosing. Indeed, cumulative S-ketamine dose explained more variability in plasma Sketamine concentration than randomized treatment group during the early and intermediate sampling periods. Higher S-ketamine concentration was associated with lower odds of adequate analgesia, but this post-randomization association should not be interpreted as evidence of impaired analgesia. Greater analgesic need may lead to more PCA use, a higher S-ketamine dose, and consequently higher exposure. This interpretation is consistent with the parent trial, in which adjunctive S-ketamine reduced oxycodone consumption dose-dependently and the highest dose also modestly improved pain at rest 12.

Previous clinical PK/PD studies have described a relationship between oxycodone exposure and analgesic effect 7, 8, 27 . Consistent with these findings, higher plasma oxycodone concentrations were associated with a greater probability of adequate analgesia early after PCA initiation.

However, this association weakened with postoperative time and was no longer apparent at 24 h. The same temporal pattern persisted after inclusion of measured S-ketamine, norketamine, and 6-hydroxynorketamine concentrations. This attenuation does not imply that concentration is unimportant. Rather, our findings suggest that the relationship between plasma oxycodone concentration and analgesia changes over the course of postoperative PCA.

During PCA, postoperative time is closely linked to oxycodone exposure because the observed concentration reflects the dosing history accumulated up to that point. At the same time, pain and recovery change as the postoperative period progresses. The close relationship between time and exposure therefore partly arises from the clinical setting itself, although it also complicates their statistical separation.

Several features of the study support the present analyses. The standardized lumbar fusion cohort and randomized trial design provided a well-defined clinical setting. Detailed PCA event histories and pain assessments allowed subsequent PCA activity to be related to the pain state observed at the beginning of each interval without interpolation of pain scores. Plasma sampling at predefined postoperative time points allowed these observations to be related to oxycodone, S-ketamine, and metabolite exposure. These data permitted separate analyses of exposure buildup, longitudinal dosing activity, and the relationships between pain, PCA activity, and drug exposure.

Several limitations should be considered. Although the parent trial was randomized, the associations examined here are observational. Plasma concentrations were available at a limited number of postoperative sampling points. Finally, the analyses examined selected relationships within the PCA process rather than modelling pain, dosing behaviour, and drug exposure simultaneously.

Chemicals and supplies.

Preparation of calibration standards and quality control samples. Stock solutions and working solutions were prepared in methanol. Working solutions contained either all four analytes or all three internal standards. Calibration standards and quality control (QC) samples were prepared by spiking pooled, drug-free human K2EDTA plasma with appropriate volumes of working solutions. The calibration standards contained oxycodone, noroxycodone, oxymorphone, and noroxymorphone at 0.05, 0.1, 0.3, 2.0, 10.0, 20.0, 45.0, and 50.0 ng/mL. Sample preparation.

Study samples, calibration standards, and QC samples were prepared using solid-phase extraction (SPE). Briefly, 200 µL of plasma was mixed with 25 µL of internal standard working solution (100 ng/mL) and 775 µL of 0.1 % formic acid in water (v/v). After vortex-mixing, samples were transferred to conditioned Sep-Pak® tC18 96-well plates (Waters, Milford, MA, USA).

Analytes were eluted with 1 mL methanol, evaporated under nitrogen, and reconstituted in 100 µL of 20 % methanol in water (v/v). Chromatograms were acquired and processed using Analyst® software (version 1.6.3, AB Sciex). Calibration curves were constructed using weighted (1/x²) linear regression.

>Determination of ketamine, norketamine and 6-hydroxynorketamine concentrations in human plasma

Chemicals and supplies.

The analytes ketamine and norketamine were purchased from Merck (Darmstadt, Germany), and 6-hydroxynorketamine from Bio-Techne (Abingdon, UK), and the deuterated internal standards (S)-ketamine-d6 and norketamine-d4 from LGC (Guildford, UK).

Sample preparation.

The spiked plasma calibrators and quality control samples, and the patient plasma samples, were pretreated using Oasis MCX µelution 96-well plates (Waters, Milford, MA, USA). The solid phase extraction (SPE) was carried out with vacuum manifold and according to the manufacturer's instructions. Briefly, the SPE plate was conditioned with 200 µL of methanol followed by 200 µL of water. Next, 270 µL of the mixture was loaded into the SPE plate, which was first washed with 200 µL of 2% formic acid, and then with 200 µL of methanol. After the washes, the plate was dried under vacuum for 1 minute. The analytes were eluted from the SPE plate into polypropylene 96-well plate with two 50 µL aliquots of 3% ammonium hydroxide in 60% acetonitrile: 40% methanol. The sample extracts were evaporated to dryness under vacuum at 58 ⁰C, using a Genevac® miVac centrifugal concentrator (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), and reconstituted in 50 µL of water.

The gradient programme for the mobile phase B was a linear gradient from 5% to 30% over 4.5 min, followed by rapid increase to 90% in 0.1 min, after which the mobile phase B was kept constant at 90% for 2 min, followed by balancing at 5% before the next injection. Between-day precision (CV) was below 15%, and accuracy was within ±15%, except at the LLOQ, where precision and accuracy met the corresponding 20% acceptance limits.
