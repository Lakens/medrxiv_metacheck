Reporting p values imprecisely (e.g., p < .05) reduces transparency, reproducibility, and re-use (e.g., in p value meta-analyses). Best practice is to report exact p-values with three decimal places (e.g., p = .032) unless p values are smaller than 0.001, in which case you can use p < .001.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (e.g., “decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

P-Value Text
p<0.01 Pairwise comparisons using DeLong’s test confirmed the superiority of serum BD-tau over each comparator biomarker (all p<0.01).

The APA manual states: Report exact p values (e.g., p = .031) to two or three decimal places. However, report p values less than .001 as p < .001. However, 2 decimals is too imprecise for many use-cases (e.g., a p value meta-analysis), so report p values with three digits.

To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 3 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Metacheck does not yet analyze automatically whether sentences which include non-significant p-values are correct, but we recommend manually checking the sentences below for possible misinterpreted non-significant p values.

Text Sentence
p=0.40 Similar age differences were observed in the CSF cohort (p=0.001) with SCI participants being older than those with TBI, while sex did not differ (p=0.40) (Extended Data Table 2).
p=0.30 In the matched serum-CSF cohort, SCI participants remained older than TBI participants (p=0.004), but sex was comparable (p=0.30) (Extended Data Table 3).
p=0.51 Stratified analyses indicated that this association was primarily driven by the TBI group (ρ=0.20, p=0.002) and was not significant in the SCI group (ρ=0.12, p=0.51).

Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Full text

Abstract

Traumatic brain injury (TBI) and spinal cord injury (SCI) are two common forms of neurotrauma that can present with overlapping clinical features, complicating early diagnosis and management particularly in acute and mixed-trauma settings. [1] [2] [3] Accurate and rapid biological differentiation remains an unmet need. 4 We hypothesized that brain-derived tau (BD-tau), an ultrasensitive blood-based biomarker preferentially selective for tau protein originating from the brain, would distinguish TBI from SCI. In a multi-cohort assessment of 342 patients admitted to the neuro-intensive care unit (285 with TBI, 57 with SCI), and 22 controls, baseline serum BD-tau concentrations were markedly elevated in TBI compared with SCI, discriminating between the two populations with high accuracy (area under the curve [AUC]=0.91, 95%CI 0.87-0.96). In paired serum-cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) samples, serum BD-tau significantly outperformed CSF BD-tau for injury-type discrimination (AUC=0.95, 95%CI 0.89-0.99 vs. 0.80, 95%CI 0.71-0.89). Baseline serum BD-tau also showed superior discrimination of TBI from SCI compared with established blood biomarkers of neurotrauma, including neurofilament light chain, glial fibrillary acidic protein, and ubiquitin C-terminal hydrolase L1 (AUC=0.82, 95%CI 0.76-0.88; vs. 0.53, 95%CI 0.43-0.63; 0.67, 95%CI 0.58-0.76; 0.71, 95%CI 0.64-0.79; all DeLong p<0.001). Longitudinally, serum BD-tau remained persistently elevated in TBI, but not SCI, for up to 144 hours post-injury, with distinct temporal trajectories between injury types. Together, these findings indicate that serum BD-tau enables rapid and minimally invasive differentiation of TBI from SCI and may support early clinical decision-making, triage, and enrollment into injury-specific therapeutic trials, highlighting the potential of circulating biomarkers to improve precision diagnostics in acute neurotrauma care.

Main

Traumatic brain injury (TBI) and spinal cord injury (SCI) are the first and second leading causes of disability and morbidity in the United States. [5] [6] [7] The gold standard methods to assess injury severity and classification are the Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) score and Computed Tomography (CT) neuroimaging for TBI and the American Spinal Injury Association (ASIA) Impairment scale for SCI [8] [9] [10] . However, these baseline examinations have varied accuracies in the acute care setting or can be unobtainable due to the unstable condition of the patient. 11, 12 This is further complicated by substantial differences in clinical management, prognostication, and treatment indications differ substantially between TBI and SCI. [13] [14] [15] [16] Therefore, there is a crucial need for rapid, reliable, and accurate tools to support the confirmation and differential diagnosis of TBI and SCI.

Blood-based biomarkers show promise for improving diagnosis, longitudinal monitoring and prognosis of patients with TBI and SCI. 17, 18 Several blood biomarkers that reflect the complex and dynamic acute pathophysiology and temporal trends of TBI and SCI is already available. 19, 20 For example, plasma/serum glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP) and ubiquitin C-terminal Hydrolase L1 (UCH-L1) increase immediately post-injury in response to astrocytic and neuronal cell injury/death and are cleared by the US Food and Drugs Administration to aid in the diagnosis of mild TBI and for determining need of a CT scan among adults. 21, 22 Additionally, plasma/serum neurofilament light chain (NfL) and total tau (t-tau) show promise as blood biomarkers of axonal injury. 23 However, these blood biomarkers are not ideal. The most notable limitation is their inability to differentiate between common acute neurotrauma disorders such as TBI and SCI, as they are similarly elevated in both conditions. 24- 26 Moreover, plasma/serum GFAP, UCHL-1 and t-tau levels increase acutely, however contributions from systemic sources reduce confidence in diagnostic utility and specificity for central nervous system (CNS) disorders. 27, 28 Plasma NfL levels peak in the sub-acute period, limiting its use as an acute marker for rapid triage. 29 Additionally, the levels of these proteins are affected by co-morbid conditions and normal aging. [30] [31] [32] We recently described brain-derived tau (BD-tau), a novel blood-based biomarker that selectively recognizes tau protein isoforms released into blood circulation predominantly from the brain. 33, 34 This biomarker assay was designed to recognize the tau protein isoforms abundantly expressed in the adult human brain 34 while excluding the longer "big tau" isoforms predominantly produced outside of the brain, including peripheral nerves and neuronal components that project outside of the brain. [35] [36] [37] In fact, big tau makes up less than 1% of the total brain tau signal but accounts for the vast majority of non-CNS tau. 38 Additionally, big tau isoforms account for a larger proportion of the total amount of tau protein in the adult human spinal cord than the brain, providing a potential opportunity for differential diagnosis of disorders of the brain vs. spinal cord. 38 We hypothesized that baseline and longitudinal serum BD-tau levels will be distinctively sensitive to the diverging localization, clinical classification and outcome of acute TBI and SCI. Here, we show that serum BD-tau concentrations accurately enable the differential diagnosis of TBI from SCI among patients admitted in a neuro-intensive care unit (ICU), with temporal dynamics over a six-day period robustly predictive of clinical severity and long-term functional outcomes specifically in TBI but not in SCI. Importantly, serum BD-tau outperformed cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) BD-tau as well as serum NfL, GFAP and UCHL1 as a differential biomarker for acute neurotrauma. Together, serum BD-tau uniquely captures biochemical signatures specific to CNS injury reflected in TBI but not in SCI. Thus, serum BD-tau is a potential precision diagnostic tool to improve accessible neurotrauma care through accurate and rapid stratification of injury types.

Results

Cohort characteristics

This study included 364 total participants, with 342 neurotrauma patients admitted to the emergency department (ED) and/or intensive care unit (ICU) of the University of Pittsburgh Medical Center (UPMC) Trauma Center, Pittsburgh, Pennsylvania, USA (n=285 TBI, n=57 SCI), and 22 healthy controls. The TBI group included 12 mild, 6 moderate and 267 severe cases classified using the Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) criteria (mild: 13-15, moderate: 9-12, severe: 3-8), while the SCI group included 22 AISA-A, 7 AISA-B, 16 AISA-C and 4 AISA-D cases respectively based on the ASIA Impairment Scale (AIS) criteria, with AIS grade missing for 8 SCI cases (see Methods). 39, 40 The SCI subtypes C and D were grouped together for sample size purposes. Baseline serum samples were available for all 364 participants, CSF samples for 261 participants, and paired serum-CSF samples for 258 participants, with a mean serum-CSF collection time differences of 3 hours and a maximum of 12 hours. These are hereafter referred to as the "serum cohort," the "CSF cohort" and the "matched serum-CSF cohort," respectively, with their demographic characteristics shown in Extended Data Tables 1 2 3. Longitudinal sampling was performed up to 144h post-injury in the TBI and SCI groups, as sample availability permitted.

Age and sex distributions differed across injury groups in the serum cohort, with individuals with SCI being older than both controls and TBI patients (p=0.003), and a higher proportion of females in the controls (p=0.014) (Extended Data Table 1). Similar age differences were observed in the CSF cohort (p=0.001) with SCI participants being older than those with TBI, while sex did not differ (p=0.40) (Extended Data Table 2). In the matched serum-CSF cohort, SCI participants remained older than TBI participants (p=0.004), but sex was comparable (p=0.30) (Extended Data Table 3). Accordingly, age and sex were included as covariates in subsequent analyses.

Serum BD-tau robustly differentiates TBI from SCI and controls

Baseline serum BD-tau concentrations were significantly elevated in patients with TBI compared with those with SCI or controls (all pairwise comparisons p<0.001; Fig. 1a). Within the TBI group, the concentrations showed a stepwise increase with injury severity, with significantly higher levels observed in moderate and severe cases compared with mild cases (Fig. 1b). In contrast, serum BD-tau levels in SCI remained low and did not vary across SCI severity (ASIA) subgroups (Fig. 1b).

Given the marked separation in baseline serum BD-tau concentrations across injury groups and TBI severity, we evaluated the discriminative performance of serum BD-tau using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analyses. Age-and sex-adjusted ROC analyses demonstrated high accuracy of serum BD-tau in differentiating all TBI from all SCI cases (area under the curve [AUC]=0.91, 95% CI 0.87-0.96) and controls (AUC=0.99, 95% CI 0.99-1.00). These accuracies remained unchanged when limiting the TBI group to the severe TBI cases only (Fig. 1c).

Furthermore, serum BD-tau accurately differentiated between the severe and mild TBI groups (AUC=0.99, 95% CI 0.97-1.00). However, as a confirmation of serum BD-tau not being a biomarker for SCI, it had suboptimal AUCs to differentiate SCI subtypes (Fig. 1c). Supplementary Table 1 summarizes sensitivity, specificity, and related performance metrics; it also reports the corresponding estimates from unadjusted ROC models and 5-fold cross-validation, which were consistent with the primary findings.

To confirm that these findings were not assay-dependent, we repeated the ROC analyses in a sub-cohort (n=246; TBI n=177, SCI n=47 and control n=22) with a different BD-tau assay -an in-house method utilizing a separate set of antibodies. 41 We observed the same pattern of strong discriminative performance in distinguishing TBI from SCI, and differentiating TBI cases according to injury severity but not SCI subtypes (Fig. 1d). Both serum and CSF BD-tau concentrations were higher in TBI than in SCI (all p<0.001; Fig. 2b-e). However, the magnitude of separation was substantially greater in serum than in CSF, with larger fold differences between TBI and SCI observed in serum measurements. Consistent with this, ROC analyses showed significantly higher discriminative accuracy for serum BD-tau than for CSF BDtau in distinguishing TBI from SCI (AUCs = 0.95, 95%CI 0.89-0.99 vs. 0.80, 95%CI 0.71-0.90; Fig. 2f).

When stratified by severity, serum BD-tau concentrations were significantly higher in severe TBI than in all SCI subgroups (p<0.001) and did not differ across SCI severity categories (Fig. 2b). By contrast, CSF BD-tau concentrations increased with injury severity in both TBI and SCI, with comparable levels observed in severe TBI and the most severe SCI subgroup (ASIA A) (Fig. 2e).

Serum BD-tau outperforms established serum biomarkers including NfL, GFAP and UCHL1

To contextualize this discriminative performance of serum BD-tau, we compared its performance with those of established serum biomarkers of neurotrauma, including neurofilament light (NfL), glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP), and ubiquitin C-terminal hydrolase L1 (UCH-L1), in a subcohort of 228 participants (TBI: n=162, SCI: n=52, and control: n=14) (see Supplementary Table 2 for characteristics). In this sub-cohort with paired measures of all four biomarkers, serum BD-tau had a significantly higher discrimination accuracy for TBI versus SCI (AUC=0.82, 95%CI 0.76-0.88) than GFAP (AUC=0.67, 95%CI 0.58-0.76), NfL (AUC=0.53, 95%CI 0.43-0.63, or UCH-L1 (AUC=0.71, 95%CI 0.64-0.79 (Fig. 3a-b). Pairwise comparisons using DeLong's test confirmed the superiority of serum BD-tau over each comparator biomarker (all p<0.01).

Temporal serum BD-tau dynamics distinguish TBI from SCI Consistent with the separation observed at baseline (Fig. 1), longitudinal profiling of serum BDtau up to 144 hours (6 days) post-injury revealed sustained discrimination between injury groups (Fig. 4a). Patients with TBI maintained elevated serum BD-tau concentrations across all sampled time points, whereas concentrations in SCI patients remained low and closely approximated those observed in trauma controls. Linear mixed-effects models demonstrated significant group-by-time interactions, indicating distinct temporal trajectories between TBI and SCI (Fig. 4a).

When stratified by severity, moderate and severe TBI showed persistently higher serum BD-tau concentrations throughout the sampling window, with severe TBI exhibiting the highest levels at nearly all time points (Fig. 4b). In contrast, serum BD-tau trajectories in SCI were flat across severity subgroups, with no evidence of temporal divergence. Group-specific sample sizes and corresponding statistical results are provided in Supplementary Table 3.

Serum BD-tau trajectories are associated with clinical outcomes after TBI Given the sustained elevation and heterogeneity of serum BD-tau trajectories among patients with TBI, longitudinal analyses showed distinct serum BD-tau trajectories by 3-month GOS outcome, with linear mixed-effects models identifying a significant outcome-by-time interaction for favorable versus unfavorable recovery (p = 0.022). Patients with unfavorable outcomes generally exhibited higher serum BD-tau concentrations and slower declines over time, although individual timepoint comparisons were not significant after adjustment (Supplementary Table 4).

Discussion

In this study, we have measured serum BD-tau by an immunoassay designed to selectively measure tau protein predominantly originating from the CNS in patients with acute neurotrauma. This is the first reported investigation of BD-tau in SCI and the most comprehensive evaluation in TBI by injury severity, sample time-point and patient number, to date. We have demonstrated that serum BD-tau can reliably differentiate the two common forms of neurotrauma diagnoses in the acute phase, with higher classification accuracy than CSF BD-tau. Notably, serum BD-tau performed significantly superior to serum GFAP, UCH-L1and NfL, the most promising blood biomarkers for neurotrauma currently, in this context of use.

The superior performance of baseline serum BD-tau over GFAP, UCH-L1 and NfL in discriminating TBI from SCI is a novel finding. CSF and blood levels of GFAP, UCH-L1, and NfL postinjury appear to capture shared pathophysiological and mechanistic processes between TBI and SCI. 13, [24] [25] [26] 42 GFAP and UCH-L1 are released into the bloodstream immediately following neurotrauma in response to astrocytic and neuronal damage. For this reason, they have excellent utility for clinical diagnosis and predicting outcomes across TBI, SCI, and many other acute injury etiologies. Furthermore, NfL, a marker of axonal injury, is released in the acute to sub-acute injury phase, reducing its utility in the immediate hours following TBI and SCI. 23, 43 The ubiquitousness of the temporal signatures of these proteins may limit their specificity for neurotrauma sub-types, and may explain why serum GFAP, NfL and UCHL-1 do not differentiate TBI from SCI as reliably. The distinct serum BD-tau magnitude and temporal trajectory of TBI compared to SCI mirrors the diverging injury pathophysiology, including the mechanisms of injury, the tissue types affected, and thus the proteins released into the bloodstream.

Serum BD-tau more accurately discriminates between severe TBI and SCI compared to CSF BD-tau. We hypothesize that this may be due to the release of this protein biomarker into systemic circulation following blood brain barrier damage after acute injury that circumvents ventricular drainage and proteins of CSF origin. 44 This is corroborated by the statistically significant correlation between serum and CSF BD-tau in TBI patients, which is consistent with previous reports of BD-tau correlations in paired serum and CSF in chronic neurodegeneration. 45, 46 These findings are similar to our previous results showing that plasma/serum GFAP is a better marker of microglia activation than CSF GFAP. 47 CSF tau appears relatively understudied, particularly in the severe TBI setting. Of the handful of studies examining the potential of CSF tau as a prognostic indicator, a small cohort showed an association with 12-month GOS-E 48 , while another showed phosphorylated and total tau in CSF slightly outperformed corresponding serum levels for outcome predictions. 27 Conversely, CSF tau is considered a leading SCI biomarker and is more commonly investigated. 13 Studies have consistently reported that serum and CSF tau are elevated in TBI compared to controls. In SCI, CSF total-tau distinguished between AIS grades acutely and predicting long-term AIS conversion. 13 Similarly, our data show CSF BD-tau outperforms serum BD-tau at differentiating ASIA scores. Altogether, BD-tau appears to reflect fundamental differences in injury biology and biomarker kinetics between TBI and SCI.

Measurements of serum BD-tau at 12-hour increments demonstrated differing temporal profiles between moderate/severe TBI (based on initial GCS score) and severe SCI patients. Serum BD-tau levels decreased over time among the TBI patients with the rate of decline dependent on injury severity. Moreover, no TBI group recovered to control levels within the six-day observation period, highlighting the high sensitivity of serum BD-tau to TBI even in less severe presentations. Importantly, while baseline serum BD-tau levels were overlapping for moderate and severe TBI, the longitudinal profiling resolved that individuals with moderate TBI recorded faster serum BD-tau decreases at which point the levels remained high in the severe TBI cases. This approach can be employed clinically to ascertain disease profiles and personalize treatment for TBI patients. Conversely, and remarkably, serum BD-tau levels in SCI remained similar to baseline levels up to 144 hours post-injury and trajectories were similar across ASIA scores or injury location across the spine, indicating that this biomarker is indeed unchanged in SCI.

Serum BD-tau demonstrated distinct prognostic trajectories according to dichotomized longterm outcome classification, defined as unfavorable for GOS 1-3 and favorable for GOS 4-5. While baseline serum BD-tau showed no differences in functional outcome, there was a significant difference in longitudinal trajectory up to 144 hours post-TBI when comparing patients with a favorable versus unfavorable outcome. In a study of 1728 patients across 65 ICUs, fluid biomarker trajectories also had substantial discriminating power above clinical, physiological and imaging variables. 49 These results support clinical use of serial measurements of serum BD-tau to predict short-and long-term clinical outcomes among TBI patients.

Previous work has primarily focused on establishing prognostic and diagnostic utility of bloodbased biomarkers in TBI 50, 51 or SCI alone 52 . By measuring and analyzing TBI and SCI concurrently, our results identify serum BD-tau as an accessible tool to support precision (differential) diagnosis of neurotrauma and to predict outcome among TBI patients. The neurotrauma field aims to modernize characterization of acute TBI with the adaptation of the CBI-M framework. 53 In addition to the traditional application of clinical (C) and neuroimaging (I) classifications, the CBI-M approach aims to incorporate blood biomarkers (B) and external modifiers (M) to improve clinical management and treatment of TBI. 53 Our findings provide an opportunity to integrate BD-tau and other blood-based biomarkers into this multi-step workflow for acute neurotrauma care, in conjunction with standard clinical and imaging procedures. For instance, following the generation and external validation of reference values, use of serum BD-tau could establish injury etiology, alongside established markers GFAP and UCH-L1.

Together, these findings advance biomarker efficacy in neurotrauma by directly focusing on the unmet clinical need for tools capable of differentially diagnosing these potential co-occurring injury types and improving outcomes following trauma. This study has several strengths, including a pioneering direct evaluation of TBI and SCI patients in acute intensive care, the inclusion of a relatively large cohort, paired CSF and serum samples, cross-sectional and longitudinal assessments and comparison of serum BD-tau with hallmark blood-based biomarkers of neurotrauma. A few limitations should be noted. There are slight differences in the collection and processing of CSF and blood across the cohorts, primarily that CSF was collected via extra ventricular drain in TBI patients and from lumbar drain in SCI patients, in line with standard of care procedures. 54 While cell and protein count differ between ventricular and lumbar fluid 55 , tau levels appear to be comparable and highly correlated when directly compared. 56 Although this study represents the largest and most detailed evaluation of serum BD-tau in acute neurotrauma to date, larger studies are needed to increase statistical power for subgroup analyses and enhance generalizability of findings. Future multicenter studies involving larger and more diverse populations, extended follow-up, and prospective validation will be required to establish the clinical utility of serum BD-tau.

In conclusion, serum BD-tau differentiating TBI from SCI is a novel finding and a significant step toward achieving precision diagnostics in acute neurotrauma patients. Additionally, it can accelerate patient selection for therapeutic clinical trials and observational studies. Finally, these findings demonstrate the potential of circulating biomarkers to improve precision diagnostics in acute neurotrauma care.

Biospecimen Collection and Biomarker Measurement

TBI Cohort

All TBI cohort samples were collected and processed by the Neurotrauma Clinical Trials Center (NCTC) at the University of Pittsburgh. Serum specimens (n=3,807) were collected in a 6mL CAT serum clot activator sterile tube (Greiner; cat # 456089) via arterial line or venipuncture. The specimen sat upright for 30-60 minutes and was then centrifuged at 1500 x g for 15 minutes at room temperature.

The supernatant was aliquoted into 1.5mL polypropylene tubes in 500uL aliquots and immediately stored at -80°C. CSF specimens (n=6,325) were collected via external ventricular drain and placed in a polypropylene conical tube. The CSF was then aliquoted in 1.5ml polypropylene tubes in aliquots 500uL and immediately stored at -80°C within 30 minutes of collection.

SCI Cohort

All SCI cohort samples were collected and processed by the Neurotrauma Clinical Trials Center (NCTC) at the University of Pittsburgh. Serum specimens collected from Spine-Track (n=202) were collected in a 6mL CAT serum clot activator sterile tube via arterial line or venipuncture. The specimen sat upright for 30-60 minutes and was then centrifuged at 1500 x g for 15 minutes at room temperature.

The supernatant was aliquoted into 1.5ml polypropylene tubes in 500uL aliquots and immediately stored at -80°C. CSF specimens collected from Spine-Track (n=80) were collected via lumbar ventricular drain. 6mL of CSF was collected in a conical polypropylene tube. Within 30 minutes from collection, the specimen was centrifuged at 1500 x g for 15 minutes at room temperature. The supernatant was then aliquoted into 1.5mL polypropylene tubes in 500uL aliquots and immediately stored at -80°C. Serum specimens collected from Casper (n=202) were collected via arterial line or venipuncture and sat at room temperature for a minimum of 30 minutes. The sample was then centrifuged at 2,000 x g for 20 minutes at room temperature. The supernatant was then aliquoted into 250uL polypropylene tubes and stored at -80°C. CSF specimens collected from Casper (n=168) were collected by a lumbar intrathecal catheter insertion. 6mL of CSF was collected in a conical polypropylene tube. The CSF was then divided into 1.5mL Eppendorf tubes and then centrifuged at 2,000 x g for 10 minutes. The supernatant was then aliquoted in 200uL increments into 250uL polypropylene tubes and immediately frozen in an ethanol-dry ice bath and stored at -80°C.

Biomarker Assessments

Serum and CSF samples were brought to room temperature and centrifuged at 10,000× g for 5 min to remove particulates prior to analysis. Serum and CSF BD-tau concentrations were measured on the Quanterix Simoa HD-X Analyzer using the commercially available Quanterix BD-tau Advantage PLUS singleplex assay (lot numbers. 504574 and 504575; Quanterix, Billerica, MA). The same calibrator batch was used across runs. The assay was performed using the recommended 4× dilution for serum and 100× dilution for CSF as previously reported. 57 For BD-tau singleplex runs, an extended calibration curve beyond the manufacturer-recommended 8-point curve was used, and sample concentrations were calculated from this extended curve.

A subset of patients (TBI: n=162, SCI: n=52, and control: n=14) was additionally analyzed using the Quanterix Neurology 4-Plex D Advantage Plus assay (N4PD+; cat #104822, lot no. 504300) on the Simoa HD-x Analyzer (Quanterix, Billerica, MA). This assay was used to measure serum NfL, GFAP, and UCHL-1 concentrations. Although BD-tau is included in the N4PD+ panel, BD-tau values generated from the multiplex assay were excluded from downstream analyses. For N4PD+ runs, samples were analyzed using the manufacturer-recommended 4× instrument dilution, as previously described. 58 An extended calibration curve was run, and all N4PD+ samples were refitted to the extended calibration curve.

Each assay plate included calibrators, internal kit controls, and plasma quality-control samples. Samples were loaded onto 96-well plates and assayed in singlicate, whereas calibrators and qualitycontrol samples were assayed in duplicate. Quality-control performance was monitored across runs using measured QC concentrations, intra-plate coefficients of variation, and inter-plate coefficients of variation. Recovery was calculated for quality-control samples but not for calibrators.

For the in-house Pitt-BD-tau assay, after development and validation 59 , BD-tau monoclonal antibody was coupled to paramagnetic beads as the capture agent, and biotin-conjugated Tau12 antibody (Biolegend; cat #SIG-39416) was used as the detector antibody. In vitro phosphorylated recombinant full-length tau441 (SignalChem; cat #T08-50FN-50) was used as the assay calibrator.

Serum samples were diluted using Quanterix Homebrew Sample diluent B (lot no. 506221), whereas Quanterix p-tau181 sample diluent (lot no. 104101) was used for both calibrator and detector dilutions.

Outcome Assessment Functional outcomes of TBI patients at 3 and 6 months after injury were assessed using the Glasgow Outcome Scale (GOS) score 60 and dichotomized into two mutually exclusive categories: Unfavorable outcome (GOS=1-3, representing vegetative state or severe disability), and favorable outcome (GOS=4-5, representing moderate disability or good recovery). Missing outcome scores were due to loss to follow-up and/or if information was unavailable in the medical records.

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted in R (version 4.4.0; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Analyses were performed using participant-level data. Unless otherwise specified, each biological replicate represents one independent participant. For longitudinal analyses, each participant could contribute one measurement per sampled time point. Continuous variables were summarized as median with interquartile range (IQR: 25 th -75 th percentile). Categorical variables were reported as counts and percentages. All statistical tests were two-sided. Exact p-values are reported for both significant and non-significant comparisons where relevant; adjusted p-values are reported when multiple-comparison correction was applicated.

Statistical significance was defined as p<0.05 after adjustment where applicable. Group comparisons for continuous variables were performed using the Kruskal-Wallis rank sum test, with post-hoc comparisons performed using Dunn's test and Benjamin-Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) correction when applicable. Categorical variables were compared using Fisher's exact test or Pearson's chi-square test, as appropriate. Spearman rank correlation coefficients were calculated on original concentration scale.

Diagnostic performance

Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were constructed to assess the diagnostic performance of BD-tau. Primary comparisons included traumatic brain injury (TBI) versus controls, TBI versus spinal cord injury (SCI), and SCI versus controls. Secondary analyses examined discrimination across injury severity groups, including TBI severity (mild, moderate, severe) and SCI severity (ASIA A, B, C/D), with pairwise comparisons and adjusted for sex and age.

Area under the ROC curve (AUCs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) was estimated using the DeLong method. Optimal classification thresholds were determined by maximizing the Youden index (sensitivity + specificity -1). Sensitivity, specificity, and predicted values were estimated at the optimal cutoffs. Pairwise AUC comparisons were performed using the DeLong test for correlated ROC curves.

To assess robustness and mitigate overfitting, ROC analyses were additionally evaluated using resampling approaches. Five-fold cross-validation was performed by partitioning the data into five folds, fitting the model in four folds and evaluating performance in the held-out fold, with performance metrics averaged across folds. In addition, nonparametric bootstrap resampling with 1,000 iterations was used to estimate the variability and confidence intervals of AUC estimates.

Longitudinal Trajectory Analysis

Longitudinal BD-tau levels were visualized on the original concentration scale using spaghetti plots with group-level means and standard error (SE) bars at each timepoint. For statistical modeling, BD-tau concentrations were analyzed on the log 2 -transformed scale to reduce right-skewness.

Linear mixed-effects models were fitted with fixed effects for time after injury, group, and the group-by-time interaction, with adjustment for age and sex. Random intercepts and slopes for time were included at participant level to account for within-participant correlation and heterogeneity in individual trajectories. The group-by-time interaction was used to evaluate whether longitudinal BD-tau trajectories differed between groups.

To complement mixed-effects modeling, nonparametric timepoint-specific comparisons were performed to evaluate group differences at individual post-injury time points.
