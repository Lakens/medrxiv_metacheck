Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

To characterize 12-month ocular safety of bio-interventional uveoscleral outflow enhancement in open-angle glaucoma (OAG).

Participants: Adults with OAG treated by bio-interventional uveoscleral outflow enhancement with acellular scleral allograft spacers (AlloFlo™, Iantrek, Inc.), standalone or with phacoemulsification, reaching 12 months of follow-up.

Methods: Adverse events were captured prospectively at each protocol-specified visit against pre-defined definitions; data were analyzed at the eye level.

Main Outcome Measures: Pre-specified ocular safety endpoints: adverse events, clinically significant hypotony, sustained intraocular pressure (IOP) elevation ≥ 10 mmHg beyond 30 days, allograft migration, corneal endothelial decompensation, secondary incisional glaucoma surgery, and endothelial cell density.

Results: Of 395 eyes (mean age 72.8 years; 52% female), 267 were combined with phacoemulsification (Cohort 1) and 128 standalone (Cohort 2). Medicated IOP decreased from 19.1 to 14.5 mmHg (p < 0.001) and medications from 1.9 to 1.2, with 42% medicationfree. Sustained IOP elevation occurred in 34 eyes (8.6%), BCVA loss ≥ 2 lines in 15 (3.8%), hyphema > 2 mm in 6 (1.5%) and hypotony in 1 (0.3%). Two allograft spacers entered the anterior chamber in one eye and were successfully repositioned intraoperatively. Serious events occurred in 12 eyes (3.0%). In the N=91 eyes with paired specular microscopy data, endothelial cell density changed -6.5% (-8.7%, -4.4%). There were no reports of endothelial decompensation, endophthalmitis, or sympathetic ophthalmia.

Conclusions: Through 12 months no endothelial decompensation, endophthalmitis or sympathetic ophthalmia occurred, and most events were self-limiting or medically managed. Endothelial cell loss tracked concurrent cataract surgery rather than the allograft, though that subgroup is small. Follow-up remains shorter than the interval over which an earlier supraciliary microstent diverged from controls.

Introduction

Open-angle glaucoma (OAG) is a leading cause of irreversible blindness worldwide, affecting more than 80 million people globally [1]. While IOP reduction remains the cornerstone of glaucoma management, a substantial proportion of patients require surgical intervention when medical therapy fails to achieve adequate IOP control. The surgical management of OAG has evolved considerably with the introduction of minimally invasive glaucoma surgery (MIGS), yet a fundamental limitation constrains the efficacy of trabecularbased procedures: the IOP-lowering effect of canal-based MIGS is limited by resistance in distal outflow pathways and the episcleral venous pressure, creating a physiologic IOP floor of approximately 16 mmHg [2].

Targeting the uveoscleral outflow pathway offers a physiologically distinct route for aqueous drainage and may overcome this limitation. The uveoscleral pathway accounts for 35%-50% of total aqueous outflow in healthy eyes and is largely independent of episcleral venous pressure [3]. Surgical access through cyclodialysis has been attempted since Heine's original description in 1905, but the technique fell out of favor due to unpredictable cleft closure, rebound hypertension, and hypotony [4]. More recently, synthetic supraciliary microstents have demonstrated the hydraulic efficacy of the pathway with significant and sustained IOP reduction, though long-term safety has been challenged by reports of corneal endothelial cell loss associated with anterior-chamber hardware protrusion [5, 6].

Bio-reinforced cyclodialysis with AlloFlo™ (Iantrek, Inc.) uses a biocompatible acellular allogeneic scleral allograft scaffold to reinforce the cyclodialysis cleft from within, without supraciliary or intracameral hardware protrusion. The scaffold is biologically homologous to the surrounding sclera and is intended to support cellular repopulation, rather than the chronic foreign-body response associated with synthetic supraciliary devices.

Despite the expansion of MIGS evidence, the quality of adverse event reporting across MIGS studies has been inconsistent. A systematic review of 21 MIGS randomized controlled trials found that, on average, only 10 of 16 CONSORT for Harms checklist items were fulfilled, and only 4 of 21 trials provided pre-specified definitions of adverse events [7]. Pre-specified, protocol-defined adverse event criteria are therefore methodologically important for characterizing the safety of novel procedures.

Early clinical experience with bio-interventional uveoscleral outflow enhancement has shown encouraging efficacy and safety profile [8] [9] [10]. Given the relative novelty of the procedure, systematic characterization of its ocular safety in a large, prospective, multicenter cohort is warranted. This study reports the surgical safety of bio-interventional uveoscleral outflow enhancement across 395 eyes enrolled in the CREST Study with at least 12 months of followup. To date, this is the largest prospective safety assessment performed for this procedure.

Methods

Study Design

The CREST Study (NCT05506423) is a multicenter, observational, real-world evidence study, in which patients with OAG who underwent bio-interventional uveoscleral enhancement surgery with AlloFlo Uveo are prospectively followed through 24 months postoperatively. CREST is conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The study has received approval from the relevant Institutional Review Boards (IRBs) for all participating investigators. All participants provided written informed consent prior to enrollment.

The present analysis includes all eyes that completed at least 12 months of postoperative follow-up at the time of the data cutoff. Two cohorts were defined per-protocol, based on whether the procedure was performed in conjunction with cataract surgery (Cohort 1) or as a standalone procedure (Cohort 2). No additional inclusion or exclusion criteria beyond the study's four eligibility requirements (confirmed diagnosis of OAG, procedure attempted or completed, provided signed written consent, and ability and willingness to follow study instructions) were applied. The CREST Protocol contains no exclusion criteria.

Surgical Procedure

One, two, or three units of processed, acellular scleral allograft (AlloFlo™, Iantrek, Inc.) were deployed into the supraciliary space to maintain cyclodialysis cleft patency and serve as a porous conduit for aqueous humor drainage into the uveoscleral pathway. The bioreinforced cyclodialysis procedure was performed using an ab-interno approach under gonioscopic visualization. Elongated 5 mm × 500 µm acellular scleral allograft bioscaffolding constructs are prepared and introduced into the AC (Figure 1) under goniovisualization (Figure 2). The reinforcing spacers are deployed at the endoscleral surface and adjacent to the edges of the cyclodialysis. The treating surgeon has discretion to use the number of allograft spacers needed to achieve the desired sustained cleft opening. Components do not protrude the drainage angle or anterior to Schwalbe's line, and placement is confirmed under direct visualization with the bio-tissue spacers deployed flush with the iris root. Any residual viscoelastic is evacuated with irrigation or irrigation/aspiration. Patients were prescribed a surgeon's choice of postoperative regimen of topical antibiotics and corticosteroids with taper.

Safety Endpoints

The primary focus of this analysis is ocular adverse events through 12 months of follow-up. Intraoperative ocular adverse events captured on the operative-day case report form included allograft malposition or dislodgement at the time of surgery, hyphema requiring intraoperative management, and unplanned conversion or termination of the procedure. Anterior chamber cells and flare were graded on a 0 to 3+ scale at each protocol visit and, as for corneal edema, are reported at visits occurring ≥ 30 days after surgery, excluding the transient inflammation expected in the immediate postoperative period.

Each event was graded for severity (mild, moderate, severe) and for seriousness. An event was recorded as serious if it resulted in death, was life-threatening, required inpatient hospitalization or prolongation of existing hospitalization, resulted in persistent or significant disability or incapacity, or required intervention to prevent permanent impairment or damage. Relatedness was recorded on four separate fields, according to the investigator's assessment: relationship to any of the allograft, delivery system, intraocular lens, or other materials. Both severity and relatedness were assigned by the treating site investigator at the time of the event. Investigators were not masked, and no independent or blinded adjudication committee reviewed causality. Relatedness is therefore reported throughout as an investigator-assessed, rather than adjudicated determinations.

Statistical Analysis

Safety endpoints are reported as proportions with 95% confidence intervals (95% CIs) computed using the Wilson score method. Longitudinal IOP change was analyzed using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test for paired baseline and 12-month data, as IOP differences did not follow a normal distribution (Shapiro-Wilk p < 0.001). Medication burden change was similarly analyzed using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. Change in corneal endothelial cell density was analyzed using a two-sided paired t-test of the within-eye percent change from baseline, and paired binary outcomes such as the proportion of eyes on no IOP-lowering medication were compared using McNemar's test. The two cohorts were compared on endothelial cell change using a two-sample t-test; no adjustment was made for multiple comparisons. Efficacy results are presented as means with 95% CI or ± standard deviation (SD) where appropriate. No statistical comparison was made between this cohort and any external dataset. Published rates referenced in the Discussion sections are provided for context only. Analyses were performed using R version 4.5.3 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing).

Results

Demographics and Baseline Characteristics

A total of 395 eyes of 298 patients completed at least 12 months of follow-up and were included for analysis. Mean age was 72.8 (8.6) years; 207 eyes (52%) were from female patients, and 272 eyes (69%) were from white patients (Table 1). The cohort reflected a realworld OAG population with moderate-to-advanced disease: mean baseline medicated IOP was 19.1 (18.5, 19.7) mmHg, patients were prescribed a mean of 1.9 (1.5) IOP-lowering medications at baseline (33% on ≥ 3), and 24% of eyes were designated by the treating physician as candidates for bleb-forming surgery prior to the study procedure.

Most eyes (267 [68%]) underwent combined cataract surgery and bio-reinforced cyclodialysis (Cohort 1), and 128 (32%) underwent standalone bio-reinforced cyclodialysis (Cohort 2). Most eyes (211 [54%]) received two allograft spacers; 162 (41%) received a single spacer, and 21 (5%) received three or more. Allograft count was not recorded in one eye.

Ocular Adverse Events

Pre-specified adverse events are summarized in Table 2. The most common were sustained IOP elevation ≥ 10 mmHg above baseline occurring > 30 days postoperatively (34 eyes, 8.6%) and BCVA loss of ≥ 2 lines at ≥ 90 days postoperatively (15 eyes, 3.8%). IOP elevations were managed with topical or oral therapy, office laser, or secondary incisional surgery.

Across both cohorts, 37 ocular adverse events in 31 eyes (7.8%) were assessed by the investigator as possibly, probably, or definitely related to either the allograft or the delivery system. Thirteen events in 12 eyes (3.0%) met the protocol definition of serious.

In one Cohort 2 eye, two of the scleral allograft spacers were found in the anterior chamber during the early postoperative period, having moved forward out of the cyclodialysis cleft. The eye was returned to the operating room, where the spacers were retrieved and repositioned within the cleft. The investigator graded the event moderate in severity and coded it as possibly related to the allograft. The eye recovered without sequelae and required no further intervention. This was the only eye in which allograft tissue moved into the anterior chamber; no other case of allograft migration, dislocation, or extrusion was recorded. No case of corneal endothelial decompensation, endophthalmitis, or sympathetic ophthalmia was recorded across the 395-eye cohort.

IOP elevations (≥ 10 mmHg above baseline, occurring > 30 days postoperatively) were recorded in 34 eyes (8.6%) overall and were distributed unevenly between cohorts: 27 eyes (21.1%) in Cohort 2 compared with 7 (2.6%) in Cohort 1. This disparity reflects the more refractory disease profile of the standalone cohort, which had a higher burden of prior glaucoma procedures and a greater preoperative medication burden. The rate is higher than the 5.0% observed when the same cohort was examined at 6 months, as expected when the observation window is doubled.

Confirmed worsening in visual field mean deviation of ≥ 2.5 dB was recorded in 13 eyes (3.3%), 6 in Cohort 1 (2.2%) and 7 in Cohort 2 (5.5%). BCVA loss of ≥ 2 lines measured at ≥ 90 days postoperatively was recorded in 15 eyes (3.8%), 9 in Cohort 1 (3.4%) and 6 in Cohort 2 (4.7%). Fourteen of these were recorded under the protocol BCVA definition, and one, in a Cohort 1 eye, was recorded as an other postoperative adverse event with the narrative term BCDVA loss; it is counted here because it is a loss of the same magnitude. Investigators coded 14 of the 15 events as unrelated to both the allograft and the delivery system, and one as probably related to both (Table 2). One further event, in a Cohort 2 eye, was recorded during the study and subsequently withdrawn after site query confirmed that the acuity had not been measured with best correction; it is excluded from all counts reported here.

The etiologies recorded in the case narratives for the unrelated BCVA-loss events were: preexisting ocular comorbidities, disease progression (including epiretinal membrane), nonarteritic ischemic optic neuropathy, and advanced glaucomatous progression, consistent with the advanced-disease characteristics of this cohort. These are investigator-assigned attributions recorded at the time of the event rather than independent adjudications, and in a single-arm study the contribution of the procedure to vision loss in eyes with advanced disease and multiple comorbidities cannot be isolated. In Cohort 1, five of the BCVA loss events ultimately resolved per investigator assessment, while the remainder either persisted or had unresolved or unknown status at the time of analysis. One of the resolved events was attributable to posterior capsular opacification and recovered completely following YAG capsulotomy; the other resolved events recovered with conservative management. Macular edema was observed in 7 eyes (1.8%), all in Cohort 1 (2.6%), consistent with the known risk of cystoid macular edema following phacoemulsification. Corneal edema persisting beyond 30 days was recorded in two Cohort 1 eyes (0.5%), one of which was coded possibly related to the delivery system; both resolved. One Cohort 2 eye had an event recorded as corneal endothelial touch, graded mild and coded probably related to the allograft, which also resolved. No eye met the protocol definition of corneal endothelial decompensation.

Hyphema > 2 mm that remained after day 1 postoperatively occurred in 6 eyes (1.5%), similarly distributed across cohorts (Cohort 1: 4 eyes, 1.5%; Cohort 2: 2 eyes, 1.6%); all resolved with conservative management, and 6 were investigator-assessed as related, as expected for an intraoperative bleeding event. One case of clinically significant hypotony was observed in a Cohort 2 eye (0.8%; 0.3% of the cohort) that had presented with severe, refractory open-angle glaucoma at baseline (preoperative IOP 32 mmHg on more than five IOP-lowering medications). Postoperative IOP decreased to 6 mmHg by Month 1 and persisted at 3-4 mmHg through Month 12 despite treatment with topical corticosteroids, difluprednate and atropine; the eye remained under active observation at the time of data extraction. The investigator classified this event as not related to the bio-interventional procedure.

Anterior chamber inflammation was uncommon beyond the first postoperative month. At visits ≥ 30 days after surgery, to which every eye contributed at least one graded assessment, cells were recorded in 32 of 395 eyes (8.1%) and reached grade ≥ 2+ in 6 (1.5%). Flare was recorded in 4 eyes (1.0%). Rates were similar in the two cohorts (cells in 7.5% of Cohort 1 and 9.4% of Cohort 2). Prevalence declined over follow-up, from 9.4% of eyes at Month 1 (21 of 224) to 4.1% at Month 6 (14 of 340) and 2.3% at Month 12 (9 of 394), and 18 of the 21 eyes (86%) with cells at Month 1 had none at Month 12. One eye (0.3%) had an adverse event recorded as chronic anterior uveitis, graded mild and coded as unrelated to the allograft and the delivery system.

One case of shallow anterior chamber with iridocorneal apposition (without lens or corneal touch) was reported. This event followed a subsequent tube shunt procedure performed for uncontrolled IOP, was investigator-assessed as not related to the study procedure, and resolved with same-day anterior-chamber washout.

Twenty-seven eyes (6.8%) progressed to secondary incisional glaucoma surgery (trabeculectomy, tube shunt, or XEN gel stent) within the 12-month follow-up window. The overwhelming majority occurred in Cohort 2 (25 eyes, 19.5%), with only two Cohort 1 eyes (0.7%) requiring secondary incisional surgery. Sixteen of the 27 (4.1% of the cohort) had occurred by 6 months, so roughly 40% of the secondary surgeries in this cohort took place in the second half of the first year. This imbalance between cohorts is consistent with the substantially greater preoperative disease burden and higher rate of prior failed glaucoma procedures in the standalone cohort, for whom bio-reinforced cyclodialysis represented a later-line intervention.

Serious Ocular Adverse Events

Among the 395 eyes included for analysis, 13 serious ocular adverse events were recorded in 12 eyes (3.0%): 4 eyes in Cohort 1 (1.5%) and 8 in Cohort 2 (6.2%). Most serious AEs (10/13) were assessed as unrelated to the allograft or delivery system. Three were assessed as related: one (0.3%) severe loss of BCVA and two (0.5%) sustained IOP elevations.

Endothelial Cell Safety

Specular microscopy is optional in the CREST Study because it is a real-world protocol and such measurements are not considered part of routine care for glaucomatous eyes. However, endothelial cell counts are recorded when specular microscopy is performed for any reason. Results are summarized in Table 3. A baseline measurement was recorded in 149 of 395 eyes (38%), and at the time of analysis 91 eyes (23%) had paired baseline and month-12 measurements. Among these eyes, mean endothelial cell density decreased from 2,371 to 2,210 cells/mm², a mean change of -6.5% (-8.7%, -4.4%; p < 0.001). The decrease was larger in Cohort 1: density changed -7.6% (-10.0%, -5.2%; p < 0.001; N = 74), whereas in Cohort 2 the change was -1.9% (-6.5% to +2.6%; p = 0.386; N = 17). The between-cohort difference was 5.7 percentage points (95% CI 0.6, 10.7; p = 0.029), though the cohorts differ in lens status and baseline density (2,446 vs 2,044 cells/mm²) and were not randomly selected for specular testing.

IOP and Medication Outcomes

At the 12-month assessment, mean medicated IOP decreased from 19.1 mmHg (18.5, 19.7) to 14.5 mmHg (14.1, 14.9), a mean absolute reduction of 4.6 mmHg (3.9, 5.3) and relative reduction of 18.4% (15.6%, 21.2%; p < 0.001). Mean IOP at Month 1 was 15.3 mmHg. At Month 12, 85% of eyes (337/395) achieved IOP ≤ 18 mmHg, and 66% (260/395) achieved IOP ≤ 15 mmHg. Fifty-one percent of eyes (200/395) achieved ≥ 20% IOP reduction from baseline. By cohort, IOP decreased from 18.1 to 14.0 mmHg in Cohort 1 (-4.1% [-4.7%, -3.5%) and from 21.3 to 15.6 mmHg in Cohort 2 (-5.6% [-7.3%, -4.0%).

On the more stringent composite outcome of IOP between 6-18 mmHg without medication increase from baseline, 77.2% of eyes with paired medication and IOP data (305/395) met this target (Cohort 1, 83.5%; Cohort 2, 64.1%).

Among 395 eyes with paired baseline and 12-month medication data, mean medication burden decreased from 1.9 ± 1.5 to 1.2 ± 1.4, a within-eye reduction of 0.71 ± 1.36 medications (p < 0.001). The proportion of eyes on no IOP-lowering medication increased from 18% at baseline to 42% at 12 months (p < 0.001).

Discussion

This multicenter real-world study represents the largest dataset to date evaluating the safety of bio-interventional uveoscleral outflow enhancement. Through 12 months, no eye developed corneal endothelial decompensation, endophthalmitis, or sympathetic ophthalmia. Allograft tissue moved into the anterior chamber in just one eye and was successfully repositioned without sequelae. Most events were self-limiting or managed conservatively. The cohort includes both combined cataract and standalone procedures across multiple centers, providing a breadth of evidence that single-center series and small pivotal trials cannot offer.

Three features of the design bound what can be concluded from these data. First, CREST is a single-arm study with no concurrent comparator and no randomization, so contextualization with published literature must be interpreted with caution. Second, seriousness and relatedness were assigned by unmasked treating investigators without independent adjudication, and attribution is therefore subject to bias of unknown direction. Third, the allograft manufacturer funded the database.

Corneal Endothelial Safety

Endothelial safety is a central question for any supraciliary procedure, because it is the question on which the CyPass supraciliary micro-stent was withdrawn in 2018 [5, 6]. In the present procedure, deployment of the soft, bio-conforming scaffold within the cyclodialysis cleft, without anterior-chamber protrusion, is a design feature intended to limit corneal endothelial exposure.

In understanding the safety implications of ECD changes after bio-reinforced cyclodialysis, both cohorts are clinically relevant and generally go into the procedure with very different baseline disease characteristics and prior interventions. The combined setting (Cohort 1) is more reflective of the state in which most patients would encounter the procedure, while the standalone setting (Cohort 2) is the one in which the allograft acts without the confounding endothelial trauma of phacoemulsification. As expected, the observed loss was larger in the combined cohort, and the between-cohort difference was 5.7 percentage points (95% CI 0.6, 10.7; p = 0.029). The observed 7.6% decline at one year in Cohort 1 lands within the range reported for routine cataract surgery and for the phacoemulsification-only control arms of the MIGS pivotal trials, in which significant endothelial cell loss at five years occurred in 6% to 11% of control eyes [11]. The magnitude seen in Cohort 1 is therefore what cataract surgery alone would be expected to produce, and it is difficult to attribute an additional effect to the allograft on these data.

The change in Cohort 2 did not reach significance, but the confidence interval runs from -6.5% to +2.6% and is therefore compatible with a loss as large as that seen in Cohort 1. The two cohorts also differ in more than the presence of cataract surgery, including lens status, prior surgeries, and baseline endothelial density. Because this is a real-world study, eyes were not randomized or stratified to account for these imbalances. The between-cohort difference is therefore descriptive rather than a controlled estimate of any contribution from the allograft. Additionally, the eyes measured are not a random sample of either cohort, and the direction of the resulting selection bias cannot be determined: sites may have imaged eyes of particular concern, or conversely only those with the equipment and the most routine postoperative courses.

The more important limit is temporal. In the pooled pivotal-trial analysis, endothelial cell loss with the CyPass micro-stent accelerated relative to controls only after the second postoperative year, whereas the Hydrus microstent showed its loss early and then tracked controls [11]. Twelve-month data cannot distinguish between those two trajectories. What the present analysis supports is narrower than an endothelial safety claim: no early signal is apparent in either cohort, and the loss that did occur is of the magnitude and distribution that concurrent cataract surgery would predict. Whether that holds beyond two years is the question the 24-month CREST analysis is designed to answer, and protocol-mandated specular microscopy in a pre-specified subset would strengthen it considerably.

The clinical findings are consistent with the quantitative data: no eye met the protocol definition of corneal endothelial decompensation, persistent corneal edema was recorded in two eyes, and endothelial touch in one. Other supraciliary approaches that use a flexible or conforming form factor have reported more reassuring endothelial findings than the rigid microstent withdrawn in 2018 [12, 13]. However, those are separate studies in separate populations, and we draw no comparison with them here.

IOP Elevations

The 8.6% rate of protocol-specified IOP elevations (> 30 days postoperatively) is clinically relevant to characterize, and is higher than the 5.0% seen in the same cohort at 6 months simply because the observation window is longer. For context, the 2025 AAO special commentary on MIGS clinical endpoints noted that IOP spikes are among the most commonly reported adverse events across canal-based MIGS, with reported rates ranging from 1% to 33.3% in randomized clinical trials depending on the procedure and the definition used [14]. The present rate falls within that range, which spans more than an order of magnitude and reflects definitional heterogeneity as much as procedural difference; it should not be read as evidence of an advantage. The concentration of these events in the standalone cohort (21.1% versus 2.6%) is the more informative observation, and is consistent with the markedly greater disease burden of eyes referred for a later-line procedure. Most resolved without additional incisional surgery.

On case-level review, 49% of IOP elevation events occurred within the first 90 days postoperatively and 5% within the first 30 days, at a median of 106 days. Roughly half therefore arose in the second half of the first year, a distribution that a 6-month observation window would have captured only partially. Documented mechanisms by site investigators included corticosteroid response (managed by tapering topical corticosteroids), late cleft scarring or membrane formation (managed by laser suture lysis or revision), and IOP elevation in eyes with concurrent risk factors such as intravitreal injection or prior failed surgery. Most events were managed medically; a subset ultimately required incisional secondary surgery, predominantly in eyes with advanced baseline disease.

Hyphema Rates

The 1.5% rate of hyphema (> 2 mm, occurring > 1 day postoperatively) can be placed alongside published figures from other MIGS categories, with the caveat that these come from different populations and use different hyphema definitions and ascertainment windows. A 2024 systematic review and meta-analysis of 95 studies and 9,733 eyes undergoing combined phacoemulsification and MIGS reported procedure-specific hyphema rates of 27.7% for gonioscopy-assisted transluminal trabeculotomy (GATT), 15.5% for non-GATT goniotomy approaches, and 3.5% for trabecular meshwork bypass procedures [15]. The rate observed here sits at the low end of that published range, which is notable given that the procedure involves instrumentation of the ciliary body, a more vascularized territory than the trabecular meshwork, and may reflect the controlled ab-interno technique that avoids scleral incisions and limits direct ciliary body trauma. In the absence of a concurrent comparator this remains a descriptive observation. All 6 hyphema events resolved with conservative management; the one eye on anticoagulant therapy resolved over approximately 77 days without medication change or surgical intervention.

Inflammation Rates

Inflammation is a reasonable concern on anatomical grounds, since cyclodialysis separates the ciliary body from the scleral spur and places allograft tissue against uveal tissue. The observed rates were low: beyond the first postoperative month, cells were recorded in 8.1% of eyes and reached grade ≥ 2+ in 1.5%, flare in 1.0%, and prevalence fell to 2.3% by Month 12 with most affected eyes quiet at one year. Unlike the endothelial data, these gradings were captured almost completely, so the estimate does not depend on a selected subset. Two caveats remain: all eyes received a postoperative corticosteroid regimen at the surgeon's discretion, so these are rates under treatment rather than natural history, and slit lamp grading is a subjective assessment made by unmasked investigators without a concurrent comparator.

BCVA Loss and Disease Severity

The 3.8% rate of BCVA loss ≥ 2 lines measured at ≥ 90 days requires careful contextual interpretation. Canal-based MIGS trials have generally reported BCVA loss rates of 1-2% in lower-risk elective populations, but these studies typically exclude eyes with significant preexisting ocular comorbidities and are frequently performed combined with phacoemulsification, which itself improves vision. The CREST population differs in important ways: 24% of eyes were designated as candidates for bleb-forming surgery, reflecting severe or refractory disease; the cohort included pseudophakic eyes in which phacoemulsification-related vision improvement cannot offset procedure-related loss; and enrollment imposes no ceiling on disease severity or comorbidity. Investigators coded 14 of the 15 events as unrelated to the allograft and the delivery system, recording pre-existing epiretinal membrane, non-arteritic ischemic optic neuropathy, advanced glaucomatous field loss and systemic conditions as the etiologies. We report these as the attributions recorded in the study rather than as an independent determination of cause. Because the assignments were made by unmasked treating investigators and no comparator group exists, these data cannot exclude that some portion of the vision loss in this cohort is procedure-related, and the one event coded as related is reported above.

A comprehensive review of MIGS device safety profiled trabecular bypass implants and Schlemm's canal procedures as generally the safest class of MIGS, with predominantly selflimited, non-vision-threatening complications, while subconjunctival gel stent insertion carries bleb-related and stent-specific adverse events broadly analogous to trabeculectomy [16]. Where bio-reinforced cyclodialysis will ultimately sit in that hierarchy cannot be determined from a single-arm cohort followed for twelve months, and we make no such claim. What these data establish is narrower: across 395 eyes followed for a year, the procedure was not associated with corneal endothelial decompensation, endophthalmitis or sympathetic ophthalmia; allograft tissue entered the anterior chamber in one eye and was repositioned; endothelial cell loss was larger in eyes undergoing concurrent cataract surgery and of the magnitude that cataract surgery alone would predict; and transient IOP elevation, hyphema and secondary surgery occurred at the rates described above. Whether the supraciliary route can be accessed without the late endothelial attrition that emerged with a rigid supraciliary microstent beyond two years remains the central open question for this procedure class, and twelve-month data narrow it without closing it.

Limitations

Several limitations bound the interpretation of these findings. The first is follow-up duration. Twelve months is longer than the 6-month window in which this cohort was first examined, and long enough to capture the second half of the first postoperative year, during which roughly half of the IOP elevations and 40% of the secondary surgeries in this cohort occurred. It is still shorter than the interval over which the endothelial signal with an earlier supraciliary micro-stent became apparent. Complications with a characteristically delayed onset, including progressive fibrosis, late cleft closure, chronic low-grade inflammation, delayed migration and progressive endothelial cell loss, may still be incompletely captured. CREST follows all enrolled eyes to 24 months, and that analysis remains the relevant test of durability.

Second, the study is single-arm. There is no concurrent comparator and no randomization, so event rates cannot be attributed to the procedure rather than to the underlying disease, to concurrent phacoemulsification, or to the natural history of an advanced-glaucoma population. Published rates from other procedures are cited in the Discussion for orientation only; differences in population, adverse event definition, ascertainment and follow-up make formal comparison inappropriate, and none was performed.

Third, causality assessment was neither masked nor independent. Severity, seriousness and relatedness were assigned by treating site investigators, some of whom have financial relationships with the sponsor, and the sponsor funded the study and maintains the database. No blinded adjudication committee reviewed the events. Attribution of adverse events to preexisting comorbidity rather than to the procedure is therefore an investigator judgement subject to bias whose direction cannot be established from these data. Readers should weight the event counts, which are objective, more heavily than the relatedness coding, which is not. Fourth, endothelial cell data remain incomplete and non-randomly obtained. Specular microscopy is optional under the protocol and was performed at investigator discretion, yielding a baseline measurement in 38% of eyes and paired measurements in 23%. The standalone subgroup, on which the interpretation above rests, comprises only 17 eyes. No unbiased estimate of endothelial change can be derived from a sample selected in this way, and the analysis should be read as hypothesis-consistent rather than confirmatory. Protocolmandated specular microscopy in a pre-specified subset is planned for the longer-term analyses.

Finally, eyes were analyzed at the eye level without adjustment for inter-eye correlation in the 97 patients who contributed both eyes, which may produce modestly narrow confidence intervals; and the January 2026 data export means that eyes enrolled closer to the export date contribute shorter follow-up than those enrolled earlier, so late adverse event capture is less complete in the more recently enrolled eyes.

Conclusions

In this prospective, multicenter study of 395 eyes followed for at least 12 months, biointerventional uveoscleral outflow enhancement was not associated with corneal endothelial decompensation, endophthalmitis or sympathetic ophthalmia. Allograft tissue entered the anterior chamber in one eye and was repositioned without sequelae; no other migration, dislocation or extrusion was recorded. Sustained IOP elevation occurred in 8.6% of eyes, hyphema > 2 mm in 1.5% and clinically significant hypotony in 0.3%, and most events were self-limiting or managed medically. In the subset with paired specular microscopy, endothelial cell loss was larger in eyes undergoing concurrent phacoemulsification than in standalone eyes and was of the magnitude that cataract surgery alone would predict, though the standalone subgroup is small and testing was elective. These findings do not establish comparative safety: the study has no control arm and causality was assigned by unmasked treating investigators. Twelve months does not yet reach the interval over which an earlier supraciliary micro-stent diverged from controls, and the 24-month CREST analysis, with protocol-mandated endothelial monitoring, remains the decisive test.
