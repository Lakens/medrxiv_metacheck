To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 4 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Background: Despite antiretroviral therapy (ART), children living with HIV (HI) experience a disproportionate burden of oral diseases, including dental caries. It remains unclear whether this increased susceptibility is associated with altered local expression of inflammatory cytokines, and whether systemic inflammatory profiles reflect corresponding immune signals in saliva. Methods: We analyzed 10 plasma and 7 salivary cytokines in matched plasma and unstimulated saliva samples from 467 Nigerian children aged 4-11 years: HI (n = 144), HEU (n = 161), and HUU (n = 162). Correlation analyses assessed plasma-saliva concordance, PERMANOVA evaluated overall cytokine profiles, multivariable linear regression examined individual cytokines, and network density analysis quantified connectivity. Results: Cytokines correlated more strongly within than between compartments. HIV exposure and infection status were associated with overall cytokine profiles in plasma (R² = 0.008, p = 0.038) and saliva (R² = 0.017, p < 0.001), and pairwise differences between comparison groups were significant only in saliva. HI children had higher plasma IL-5 and MCP-1 and higher salivary IL-6 and MCP-1 than HEU and HUU children. Higher CD4⁺ T-cell count was associated with lower plasma IL-6 and MCP-1 and lower salivary IL-6. Caries status was not associated with overall cytokine profiles, but salivary IL-4 was higher in caries-free children. Conclusions: Plasma and salivary cytokines capture largely distinct immune signals in children affected by HIV, and saliva does not appear to serve as a surrogate for plasma with respect to cytokine expression. We did observe that HIV infection and immune status, not dental caries, were the main correlates of cytokine variation in plasma and saliva compartments.

Introduction

The oral cavity is a key immunologic site where local and systemic immune responses converge, and disruptions in immune homeostasis can manifest as clinically significant disease. In the context of HIV infection, progressive immune dysfunction alters cytokine signaling and may increase susceptibility to opportunistic oral conditions, including oropharyngeal candidiasis, oral hairy leukoplakia, 1, 2 and other inflammatory lesions. 1, [3] [4] [5] [6] [7] [8] [9] Dental caries and other common oral diseases are disproportionately prevalent among children living with HIV (HI) and those perinatally-exposed to HIV, 10 highlighting the broader consequences of immune perturbation during critical developmental periods.

In children living with HIV, HIV infection is most commonly acquired perinatally through mother-to-child transmission, which can occur during pregnancy, childbirth through exposure to maternal blood and vaginal secretions, or postnatally through breastfeeding. [11] [12] [13] [14] HIV primarily targets CD4 + helper T cells, using them as sites for replication and proliferation to generate new viral particles. These new virions go on to infect additional CD4 + cells, ultimately driving progressive immunosuppression. [15] [16] [17] CD4 + T cells are central to adaptive immunity, coordinating immune responses through cytokine secretion and interaction with other immune cells. 18, 19 HIVmediated depletion of these cells disrupts cytokine production and the regulation of immunemicrobiome homeostasis, 20 which may increase susceptibility to opportunistic infections and contribute to the development of chronic oral diseases like dental caries.

There is a growing body of work that has characterized immune function in the context of both HIV infection and various oral diseases, suggesting that immune profiles may be associated with both oral disease and HIV infection despite ART. 1, 2, 4, 7, 8, [21] [22] [23] Our team has previously shown that immune status was strongly associated with oral microbial profiles than HIV infection or exposure status. 24 Together, these studies underscore the importance of elucidating the drivers of immune function and response, and the potential indicators of disease risk.

Saliva is an attractive, non-invasive source of immune biomarkers, and salivary cytokines (soluble proteins secreted by both immune and somatic cells) have been shown to play important roles in immune regulation and the coordination of immune responses. Several studies have measured both plasma and salivary cytokines in the presence of systemic and oral disease. However, the relationship between systemic immune signaling and local, oral immune activity remains poorly defined, especially among children. In particular, it is unclear whether cytokine profiles measured in plasma reflect those observed in saliva, or whether these compartments capture distinct aspects of immune function associated with HIV and its clinical sequelae. Addressing this gap is critical for advancing our understanding of the oral-systemic immune axis and for identifying accessible, non-invasive biomarkers of disease risk.

Multiple studies have explored the use of salivary cytokines as a non-invasive surrogate for systemic immune signaling, which is typically assessed using plasma cytokines. [25] [26] [27] The coordination between local and systemic immune responses has been proposed to result from the diffusion of biomarkers from blood into the oral cavity. 28 However, this notion is challenged due to the weak plasma-salivary cytokine correlation observed among healthy adults, suggesting that systemic and local immunity are independently regulated in response to separate microenvironments. [25] [26] [27] [28] [29] This underscores the importance of measuring immune biomarkers in both plasma and saliva. To our knowledge, plasma and salivary cytokines have not been compared in matched samples from children perinatally exposed and/or infected with HIV. Assessing both plasma and salivary samples will enable us to observe how systemic and local immune responses may be altered in the context of host clinical phenotypes across children with different HIV exposure and infection status. It may provide immunological insight into the disproportionate oral disease burden among children living with HIV.

In this study, we characterized plasma and salivary cytokine profiles in a cohort of Nigerian children aged 4-11 years, including children living with HIV (HI), HIV-exposed uninfected (HEU), and HIV-unexposed uninfected (HUU) children. Our primary aim was to evaluate the concordance between plasma and salivary cytokine concentrations and characterize differences in cytokine profiles according to HIV infection and exposure status. By evaluating circulating and oral immune mediators within the same participants, this study seeks to clarify the relationship between systemic and oral inflammatory profiles and their potential relevance to dental caries in children affected by HIV.

Results

Patient demographics:

Matched plasma and saliva samples were analyzed from 467 children: 144 HI, 161 HEU, and 162 HUU children. Sex and mean age were similar across groups (84-87 months). The median CD4 + lymphocyte counts differed significantly by HIV status (Kruskal-Wallis test, p < 0.001) and was lower in HI children (837 cells/mm³) than in HEU (990 cells/mm³) and HUU children (1,015 cells/mm³; Benjamini-Hochberg [BH]-adjusted p < 0.001). A CD4⁺ T-cell count below 500 cells/mm³ was recorded in 37 HI (25.7%), 6 HEU (3.7%), and 11 HUU (6.8%) children. Out of the 144 children living with HIV, 98 were virally suppressed (<200 copies/mL; 68.0%) and 46 were not suppressed (≥200 copies/mL; 32.0%). Additional characteristics are presented in Table 1.

Cytokines are more strongly correlated within than between plasma and saliva An initial assessment of cytokine concordance across fluid compartments was performed by calculating pairwise Pearson correlation coefficients (r) between individual cytokines. Correlations among cytokines within the same compartment were also examined to compare within-compartment and between-compartment correlation patterns. Visual inspection of the resulting heatmap suggested that within-compartment correlations were generally stronger, as indicated by larger correlation coefficients, than those observed between plasma and salivary cytokines (Figure 1). Correlation coefficients calculated for each HIV-status group gave the same result (Supplementary Figures 1 A-C).

Cytokine profiles are associated with HIV status and CD4⁺ T-cell count but not with caries To characterize variation in profile-wide systemic and oral immune signaling, multivariate analyses were performed on both plasma and salivary cytokines. Principal component analysis (PCA) was used to visualize patterns of variation in overall plasma and salivary cytokine profiles, while PERMANOVA assessed the associations of HIV status, dental caries status, and CD4 + count with cytokine profile composition, adjusting for demographic covariates.

HIV status was significantly associated with overall cytokine profiles in both plasma (p = 0.038; R² = 0.008; Figure 2A) and saliva (p < 0.001; R² = 0.017; Figure 2B). Pairwise comparisons in plasma suggested differences between HI and HEU children and between HI and HUU children, although neither comparison reached statistical significance (Table 2A). In contrast, salivary cytokine profiles differed significantly between HI and both HEU and HUU children (Table 2B). CD4 + count was significantly associated with plasma cytokine profiles only (p = 0.029; R² = 0.005) and not salivary profiles.

Caries status was not independently associated with overall plasma or salivary cytokine profiles. However, significant associations emerged in plasma when caries status was considered in interaction with HIV status or CD4 + count. The HIV x caries interaction approached statistical significance (p = 0.076; R² = 0.007), whereas the CD4 + count x caries interaction was significantly associated with plasma cytokine profiles (p = 0.0449; R² = 0.005). Neither interaction was significantly associated with salivary cytokine profiles.

Differences in plasma and salivary cytokine profile dispersion between groups were evaluated using PERMDISP. In plasma, CD4 + count groups (≥500 cells/mm 3 and <500 cells/mm 3 ) had different group dispersions (p = 0.009). In saliva, HIV status groups had different group dispersions (p = 0.024). Sensitivity analysis using a subset of children with matching maternal education and employment status did not influence the outcomes of plasma or salivary cytokine profiles. Loadings for principal component 1 (PC1) and principal component 2 (PC2) are presented in Supplementary Tables 1 and 2.

Cytokine Network Organization differs by HIV Status and Biological Compartment

Cytokine correlation network analysis revealed differences in network density across groups, with patterns varying by biological compartment and correlation threshold. Each network comprised seven cytokines, with 21 possible edges. In plasma, HUU children generally exhibited greater network density than HI and HEU children at correlation thresholds of |ρ| ≥ 0.40-0.48, although these differences diminished at more stringent thresholds. In saliva, HEU children exhibited the highest network density at |ρ| ≥ 0.40 (0.381), followed by HI (0.333) and HUU (0.238) (Table 3A). However, salivary network density declined more markedly in HEU children as the threshold increased, whereas HI and HUU networks remained comparatively stable.

In contrast to the within-compartment cytokine networks observed in plasma and saliva, no inter-compartmental (plasma-saliva) connections were identified across the three HIV status groups when applying the same correlation magnitude thresholds (|ρ| ≥ 0.40-0.48). The maximum absolute correlation coefficients for plasma-saliva cytokine pairs were relatively weak in all groups: HI (0.16), HEU (0.17), and HUU (0.22) (Table 3B). These findings suggest limited correlation-based connectivity between circulating and salivary cytokine profiles, despite the presence of stronger associations within each biological compartment.

Node-level centrality analysis revealed compartment-specific patterns across HIV status groups (Supplementary Table 3). In plasma, IFN-γ was prominent in HI and HUU, exhibiting the highest eigenvector centrality (1.00 in both groups) and strength (1.59 and 2.40, respectively), whereas IL-4 was most central in HEU, with the highest strength (2.07), betweenness (0.40), closeness (0.31) and eigenvector centrality (1.00). In saliva, IL-6 and IL-8 were prominent in HI, with IL-6 exhibiting the highest betweenness (0.33) and closeness (0.36), and IL-8 exhibiting the highest strength (2.28). In HEU and HUU saliva networks, IL-5 was the most central cytokine, exhibiting the highest degree (6.00 in both groups), strength (2.41 and 2.37, respectively), betweenness (0.47 and 0.33, respectively) and eigenvector centrality (1.00 in both groups).

HIV Status and CD4 + Count are Associated with Variation in Plasma and Salivary Cytokines: Separate multivariable linear regression models assessed associations of individual plasma and salivary cytokines with HIV status, CD4 + count, caries status, current weight (kg), height (cm), age (months), sex, and breast-feeding duration (months). HIV status and CD4 + count were the predominant variables significantly associated with cytokine concentrations in both compartments.

HIV status was significantly associated with 4 plasma and 3 salivary cytokines. In plasma, the HI group had higher IL-5, MCP-1, and TNF-α concentrations than HUU children (Table 4). Compared with HEU children, the HI group had higher concentrations of IL-5, IL-6, and MCP-1. In saliva, HEU children had lower IL-6 concentrations than HUU children. The HI group had higher IL-6 and MCP-1 concentrations than HUU children, and higher IFN-γ, IL-6, and MCP-1 concentrations than HEU children (Table 5). All comparisons remained significant after BH adjustment.

Higher CD4 + count was significantly associated with lower plasma IL-6 (β = -4.70 × 10⁻⁴ per cell/mm³) and plasma MCP-1 (β = -2.67 × 10⁻⁴ per cell/mm³) (Table 4), and with lower salivary IL-6 (β = -4.18 × 10⁻⁴ per cell/mm³) (Table 5).

Caries was significantly associated with only one salivary cytokine; salivary IL-4 was higher in caries-free children than in children with active caries (β = 0.359, p = 0.008) (Table 5). Current height and sex were also associated with plasma and salivary cytokine concentrations in the cohort; however, neither consistently influenced the same cytokine in both fluid compartments (except between sex and IL-4, but their directions were opposites; Table 4 5). Sensitivity analysis using a subset of children with matching maternal education and employment status did not influence the outcomes of plasma or salivary cytokine multivariable linear models.

In a subset of 203 children with available salivary flow rate data, multivariable linear regression revealed significant inverse associations between salivary flow rate and salivary concentrations of IL-8 (p = 0.006) and MCP-1 (p < 0.001) (Supplementary Table 4).

Caries-cytokine associations differ by HIV status and CD4+ T-Cell count:

Caries interaction models with HIV and CD4 + count were fitted to investigate if there are significant interactions between caries and either HIV status or CD4 + count. Significant interactions between HIV exposure/infection status and caries status were observed for plasma IL-1α, with both HI and HUU differing significantly from HEU (Supplementary Table 5a). Evidence of interaction was more limited for plasma IL-1β, with a significant interaction observed only between HI and HEU groups in relation to caries status (Supplementary Table 5a). Stratified estimated marginal means comparisons showed that active caries was associated with lower IL-1α concentrations among HEU children (β = -1.253; BH-adjusted p = 0.008) (Supplementary Table 5b). In contrast, higher concentrations of IL-1α were observed among HI and HUU children with active caries, although these differences were not statistically significant (BH-adjusted p > 0.05) (Supplementary Table 5b). A similar trend was observed for IL-1β, but none of the caries comparisons reached statistical significance (BH-adjusted p > 0.05) (Supplementary Table 5b). For both IL-1α and IL-1β, only a few direct comparisons of caries-associated differences demonstrated some differences between HI and HEU, but they failed to reach statistical significance in either caries status (Supplementary Table 5c).

A significant interaction was observed between CD4 + count and caries status in the plasma IL-1α (β = 0.001; p = 0.011) and IFN-γ (β = 7.567 x 10 -4 ; p = 0.029) models (Supplementary Table 5d). Stratified analyses showed a significant inverse association between CD4 + count and IL-1α concentrations among children with active caries (β = -1.33 x 10 -3 ; 95% CI: -2.30 x 10 -3 --3.70 x 10 -3 , p = 0.007). Among caries-free children, the positive association between CD4 + count and IFN-γ was weak and borderline significant (β = 3.00 x 10 -4 , 95% CI: -2.44 x 10 -5 -6.24 x 10 - 4 , BH-adjusted p = 0.070) (Supplementary Table 5e).

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is among the first studies to characterize both plasma and salivary cytokine profiles in matched samples from children with perinatal HIV infection or exposure. There were three main findings from this investigation. First, cytokines were more strongly correlated within each fluid compartment but not between fluids. Second, HIV infection was associated with cytokine profiles in both fluids, more clearly in saliva, and CD4⁺ T-cell count was associated with plasma profiles and with IL-6 in both fluids. Third, caries status was not associated with overall cytokine profiles; its associations were limited to salivary IL-4 and to plasma IL-1α, IL-1β, and IFN-γ in interaction with immune phenotypes.

Saliva has been proposed as a non-invasive surrogate for plasma on the premise that circulating cytokines can translocate to the oral cavity. 27, 28 For this cohort of children, our data does not support this. Within-fluid correlations were moderate, whereas the strongest plasmasaliva correlation in any HIV-status group was 0.22. This aligns with several studies in adults, in which plasma-saliva correlations were absent or were modest and confined to a few cytokines 25, 28, 29 , and with the weak relationship reported between salivary cytokines and plasma measures in adults with HIV. 22 Salivary cytokines are produced locally by salivary glands, oral epithelium, and resident and recruited immune cells, and they respond to local conditions such as oral hygiene, gingival inflammation, plaque accumulation, dental caries, and the oral microbiota. 29, 30 The compartmentalization observed in our study was evident across all three HIVstatus groups. Consistent with this finding, node-level centrality analysis revealed distinct patterns of cytokine network organization between plasma and saliva. IFN-γ and IL-4 were prominent in plasma networks, whereas IL-5, IL-6 and IL-8 occupied prominent positions in salivary networks, with these patterns also varying by HIV status. Network density also varied by HIV status within each compartment, although these differences were not consistent across correlation thresholds, highlighting the need for further validation. Together, these findings indicate that plasma and salivary cytokines should be measured concurrently rather than treated as interchangeable indicators of inflammatory status.

Previous studies using multivariate methods have shown that plasma cytokine and chemokine profiles differ between people with HIV and uninfected controls irrespective of virologic status. 31, 32 For this study, we wanted to extend this approach to saliva and to HEU children, a unique but growing population. HIV status was associated with overall profiles in both fluids, but it explained little of the variation (R² < 0.02), and pairwise differences between HI children and the two uninfected groups were significant only in saliva. Because dispersion also differed between HIV-status groups in saliva, part of this difference may reflect greater heterogeneity among HI children rather than a uniform shift. The salivary difference may reflect both the systemic effects of HIV on immune activation and HIV-associated changes in the oral environment, including altered salivary properties. 33 CD4⁺ T-cell count was associated with plasma but not salivary profiles, consistent with a closer relationship between systemic immune status and circulating cytokines.

After examining differences in multivariate systemic and local cytokine profiles across HIV status groups, we used multiple linear regression models to assess the associations of individual cytokines with HIV groups while adjusting for breast-feeding duration, age, weight, height, and sex. We observed that some differences were specific to one fluid compartment. Plasma IL-5 was higher in HI than in HEU and HUU children, plasma TNF-α was higher in HI than in HUU children, and salivary IFN-γ was higher in HI than in HEU children. Notably, IL-6 and MCP-1 behaved similarly in both fluids. IL-6 was higher in HI than in HEU children in plasma and saliva; in saliva, it was also higher in HI than in HUU children and lower in HEU than in HUU children. MCP-1 was higher in HI children than in both uninfected groups in both fluids. Higher CD4⁺ T-cell count was associated with lower IL-6 in both fluids and with lower MCP-1 in plasma only. These parallel group differences occurred even though concentrations of the same cytokine were not correlated between fluids within individuals, which suggests that HIV infection might act on both compartments through separate local and systemic sources rather than through transfer from blood to saliva.

The lower salivary concentration of IL-6 in HEU compared to HUU children was unexpected. Reports of children in the HEU group describe both attenuated and enhanced cytokine responses depending on the stimulus and age, 34, 35 and recent cohorts found lower concentrations of several circulating cytokines in HEU compared to HUU that persisted beyond infancy. 36, 37 Our finding is in the same direction but was limited to one cytokine in one fluid and needs replication. Also, given we enrolled from a hospital setting, it is possible that HUU children might not reflect a truly healthy unexposed group compared to HEU. This is plausible because in this cohort, we have also observed a lower though non-significant odds of having carious lesions compared to HUU children.

Caries status had little independent association with cytokines. Salivary IL-4 was higher in caries-free children, but this was a single association while adjusting for breast-feeding duration, age, weight, height, and sex. In plasma, the association between caries and IL-1α depended on HIV status. We observed that caries was associated with lower IL-1α in HEU children, whereas IL-1α tended to be higher with caries burden in HI and HUU children, although neither withingroup difference was significant. IL-1β showed a weaker pattern in the same direction.

In our cohort, salivary IL-6 varied with HIV status and CD4⁺ T-cell count. The importance of IL-6 is reflected in multiple studies, where IL-6 was found to be a prevalent salivary cytokine among children and young adults with dental caries. 38 Some have suggested that an increase in salivary IL-6 concentration is an indicator of the presence and severity of dental caries, serving key roles in the body's response to infections by cariogenic bacteria (i.e. Streptococcus mutans and Lactobacillus). [38] [39] [40] For example, IL-6 is found to be produced by activated macrophages, monocytes, and fibroblasts in inflamed tissues. 40 In addition, IL-6 was increased among children with early childhood caries (ECC) compared to those without any caries in saliva; however, such observations were made among children without any systemic, chronic diseases. 41, 42 Unlike these previous studies, we did not observe significant associations between IL-6 and dental caries in either plasma or saliva. This may be partly explained by the stronger association of HIV status with cytokine levels in our cohort, potentially overshadowing associations with caries. Additionally, these previous studies excluded children with systemic/chronic illnesses. A future goal would be to investigate whether the upregulation in plasma and salivary IL-6 increases the likelihood of developing dental caries in children living with HIV. In terms of salivary MCP-1 levels, it was previously observed to be elevated among individuals with severe chronic periodontitis compared to healthy controls. MCP-1 has been implicated in the chemoattraction of monocytes to sites of inflammation. 43 Together, these findings indicate that cytokine variability across HIV status and CD4 + count may not entirely be uniform, and may differ by cytokine, biological compartment, and other demographic and clinical factors. This complexity is consistent with previous studies associating salivary cytokine expression with HIV status and CD4 + counts in the presence of opportunistic infections. 7, 21, 44 Viral load may also influence salivary cytokine expression among people living with HIV, 1,2 while antiretroviral therapy (ART), periodontitis, and CD4 + count have been reported to contribute to variations in salivary cytokine profiles. 1, 5, 7, [21] [22] [23] 44 These findings suggest that HIVassociated cytokine profiles vary according to clinical presentation and other contributing factors, even among individuals receiving ART. Salivary rate is another important factor, as higher flow rates have been associated with lower concentrations of IL-1α, IL-1β, IL-6, IL-8, IL-10, and TNFα, potentially reflecting a dilution effect among salivary cytokines. 30 However, in our main analyses, we did not normalize cytokine concentrations based on salivary flow rate because flow rates differ between HUU and HEU children in our cohort. 30 As HIV status may influence both salivary flow rate and cytokine concentrations, normalization could attenuate biologically relevant differences between groups. Therefore, cytokine concentrations were analyzed without flow-rate adjustment, and the findings should be interpreted in the context of the clinical and physiological factors that may contribute to variation across biological compartments.

Although the proposed biological mechanisms for caries development can vary, a common theme here is that alterations in cytokine expression may create conditions that favor the manifestation of opportunistic infections and oral pathology. By reducing CD4 + T-cell numbers and altering cytokine production, HIV may shift this balance and alter immune function within the oral cavity. 1, 2, 4, 7, 21, 44, 45 Consistent with this hypothesis, the present study does demonstrate that both HIV status and CD4 + count can influence both plasma and salivary cytokine expression.

Despite a higher prevalence of dental caries among children living with HIV, caries status was not significantly associated with overall multivariate cytokine profiles, neither was it widely associated with individual cytokines in plasma or saliva. However, associations between caries and individual plasma cytokines varied according to HIV exposure/infection status and CD4 + Tcell count. Our group previously reported associations between dental caries and inflammatory biomarkers in the context of perinatal HIV exposure, 46 the present findings suggest that these relationships may be influenced by the underlying immunological context.

Several limitations warrant consideration. First, the selected cytokine panel does not capture the full complexity of immune signaling. Measurements calculated from this panel on whole saliva and plasma samples reflect aggregate cytokine activity rather than responses from specific cell populations. Although the manufacturer indicates that the analytes are detectable in saliva, independent validation of assay performance in this biological matrix was not performed. Multiplex bead-based assays may also have lower analytical sensitivity than single-analyte immunoassays, particularly at low cytokine concentrations. Second, potential assay-related batch effects cannot be excluded, as between-run variability was not directly assessed or accounted for across all 467 participants. Although intra-assay CVs remained below 15% in both biological matrices, greater inter-assay variability suggests that differences between assay runs may have influenced cytokine measurements. Nevertheless, adjusting for batch effects using ComBat reduced inter-assay variability, allowing our primary results to be relatively robust. Accordingly, some of the profile patterns observed may reflect technical variation in addition to underlying biological differences.

In conclusion, plasma and salivary cytokines captured largely distinct immune signals in this cohort of children aged 4-11 years, and saliva does not appear to be a surrogate for plasma cytokine measurement in children affected by HIV. HIV infection and CD4 + T-cell count were the main correlates of cytokine variation, although they explained only a small proportion of the variation in overall profiles. IL-6 and MCP-1 were the cytokines most consistently elevated in children living with HIV across both compartments, and overall cytokine profiles distinguished these children from HEU and HUU children more clearly in saliva than in plasma. Caries status was not associated with overall cytokine profiles. However, its associations with IL-1α, IL-1β, and IFN-γ varied by HIV exposure/infection status and CD4+ T-cell count, but these exploratory findings require confirmation. Together, our findings underscore the importance of assessing systemic and oral immune compartments in parallel when studying oral disease risk in children affected by HIV. Future longitudinal studies are needed to determine whether these cytokine alterations causally lead to dental caries development in HI and HEU children rather than reflecting differences in HIV exposure, immune status, and oral health.

Materials and Methods

Study Design:

This cross-sectional analysis used samples and data from children (N=467; aged 4-11 years) from the Dental Caries and its Association with the Oral Microbiomes and HIV in Young Children-Nigeria (DOMHaIN) cohort 47 at the University of Benin Teaching Hospital (UBTH) between May and November 2019. Participants were recruited at UBTH by trained healthcare staff and were categorized into three groups: children living with HIV (HI), HIV-exposed but uninfected (HEU), and HIV-unexposed and uninfected (HUU). HI and HEU participants were recruited from the Pediatric Special Treatment HIV Clinic, while HUU children were recruited from the Pediatric Out-Patient Clinic. Groups comprised of 144 HI, 161 HEU, and 162 HUU children. Sociodemographic, feeding, and oral hygiene data were obtained with standardized caregiver questionnaires.

The guidelines governing the administration of ART among children living with HIV participating in this study follow the protocols outlined by the Nigerian Federal Ministry of Health. ART was initiated for all children living with HIV at the time of recruitment from UBTH and have remained on ART through the duration of the study. ART regimens provided to these children consisted of a combination of different inhibitor drug classes. Children in the HI group were given a Nucleoside/Nucleotide reverse Transcriptase Inhibitor (NRTIs) in combination with a Non-Nucleoside Reverse Transcriptase Inhibitor (NNRTIs), Protease Inhibitor (PI), and/or Integrase Strand Transfer Inhibitor (INSTI) according to the recommended ascribed first-line regimens outlined for children <3 yrs and children 3 -<10 yrs (Table 6). 48

Sample Collection:

All efforts were made to ensure that biospecimen collection and handling procedures were conducted aseptically. Blood and saliva samples are collected to measure the concentration of dissolved cytokines found in plasma and saliva (oral cavity) respectively.

Blood/Plasma: At enrollment, 10 mL of venous blood was collected from each child by venipuncture. Plasma was separated by centrifugation at 4000 rpm revolution for 10 mins, and the clear plasma separated and stored for future cytokine profile assays within 1 hour of collection, aliquoted, and stored at -80 °C. Plasma aliquots were stored and subsequently shipped on dry ice to the US for further processing as described below.

Saliva: Unstimulated whole saliva was collected at the same visit as the blood sample. The child laid on a dental chair with the head raised, saliva was allowed to pool in the mouth for 5 minutes and was then aspirated with sterile plastic Pasteur pipettes into two pre-labelled 2 mL cryogenic vials kept on ice. Samples were stored at -80 °C and shipped as described for plasma. Processing of these samples are previously outlined in the study protocol. 47 Salivary flow rate was measured by dividing the recorded volume of saliva collected by the duration of saliva production, typically five minutes. These calculated values were then documented as the salivary flow rate for each participant in the study.

Before the assays, thawed plasma and saliva samples were brought to room temperature and centrifuged at 2,000 × g for 5 minutes. Plasma supernatants were used directly for the assay. Saliva supernatants were treated with protease inhibitor cocktail at a 1:500 volume ratio (protease inhibitor cocktail to saliva), centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 10 minutes, and diluted 1:2 with the kitprovided assay buffer.

Milliplex Multiplex Immunoassays:

Plasma and salivary cytokine concentrations were quantified using the MILLIPLEX Human Cytokine/Chemokine/Growth Factor Panel A Magnetic Bead Assay (MilliporeSigma), which measures 11 cytokines: IFN-γ, IL-1α, IL-1β, IL-2, IL-4, IL-5, IL-6, IL-8, IL-10, MCP-1, and TNF-α. The assay was performed according to the manufacturer's protocol, with samples analyzed in duplicate and the mean concentration calculated.

Assay precision:

Mean intra-assay coefficients of variation (CVs) ranged from 6.13% to 11.08% in plasma and 5.56% to 10.44% in saliva, below the recommended 15% threshold (Supplementary Table 6 7). ComBat (BatchCorrector) was used to help minimize/eliminate inter-assay related batch effects. 49 Cytokine concentrations for the standards were log2(x+1) transformed prior to ComBat adjustment, then back transformed after adjustment. 49 Inter-assay precision was more variable (Supplementary Table 8). In plasma and saliva, IL-1α, IL-10, MCP-1, and TNF-α are the only cytokines with batch-adjusted inter-assay CV% >20%. All other cytokines had a batch-adjusted inter-assay CV% <20%.

Data processing:

Following quantification using the Luminex xMAP Intelliflex system, cytokine concentrations below the lower limit of detection (LLOD) were imputed as ½ the cytokine-specific LLOD, defined as the minimum detectable concentration (MinDC) + 2 standard deviations (SD) for the overnight incubation protocol. 50 Plasma IL-2 and salivary IL-1β, IL-2, IL-10, and TNF-α were excluded from the analyses because more than 30% of their measured concentrations fell below the LLOD (Supplementary Table 9). This 30% threshold was used in other studies. 29, 30 Cytokine concentrations were then adjusted for batch effects using ComBat. Raw cytokine concentrations were log2(x+1) transformed prior to ComBat adjustment, then back transformed after adjustment. 49 The adjusted cytokine distributions were assessed using boxplots. Because adjusted concentrations were highly right-skewed, values were log2(x+1)-transformed to reduce right skewness and accommodate zero or near-zero values. 49, 51, 52 HIV status and viral load:

HIV infection and exposure status were determined from the medical records of the mother and child and confirmed by HIV testing of the child at enrollment. 47 Serial rapid antibody tests were used: Determine as the first test, Uni-Gold to confirm a reactive result, and Stat-Pak as the tie-breaker for discordant results. 53 For HEU children, records of early infant diagnosis by HIV DNA PCR (COBAS AmpliPrep/COBAS TaqMan HIV-1 Qualitative Test, version 2.0) were reviewed; under national guidelines this test is performed at 6 weeks of age, at 4 months for nonbreastfed infants, or 2 months after cessation of breastfeeding. 48, 54 HIV viral detection and viral load quantification were performed by UBTH laboratory technicians using Real-Time Polymerase Chain Reaction (RT-PCR). Viral suppression was determined based on a cutoff of 200 copies/mL. Viral suppression is any viral load <200 copies/mL, while ≥200 copies/mL is considered not virally suppressed. This cutoff had been used in previous studies. 55, 56 Antiretroviral therapy (ART) followed the guidelines of the Nigerian Federal Ministry of Health. 48 All HI children were receiving ART at enrollment and remained on ART during the study. Regimens combined a nucleoside or nucleotide reverse transcriptase inhibitor backbone with a non-nucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitor, a protease inhibitor, or an integrase strand transfer inhibitor (Table 6).

Quantification of CD4 and CD8 lymphocytes: CD4⁺ and CD8⁺ T-cell counts were measured for all children (regardless of group) by flow cytometry on a Sysmex/Partec CyFlow Counter II with fluorescently labelled monoclonal antibodies and a 532 nm solid-state laser. 57, 58 CD4⁺ T-cell count was analyzed as a continuous variable (cells/mm³) and, for dispersion analyses, in two groups (≥500 and <500 cells/mm³).

Oral health examination: The presence and absence of dental caries were evaluated during a dental examination performed by a qualified dentist who was unaware of the HIV exposure status of the child whom they are examining. The WHO criteria for the assessment of caries and International Caries Detection and Assessment (ICDAS) scores were used to determine if the child's dentition had active caries (CA) or were caries-free (CF). 59, 60 Children were classified as having active caries or as caries-free.

Statistical Analysis:

All analyses were performed in R (version 4.2.1). Multiple linear regression assessed associations of HIV status, CD4⁺ T-cell count (cells/mm³), and caries status in the presence of other covariates (sex, age (months), height (cm), weight (kg), and breast-feeding duration (months)) for each cytokine. Estimated marginal means (EMMs) were used for pairwise comparisons between factor levels. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. BH-adjusted p-values were reported for pairwise comparisons involving HIV status.

Overall cytokine correlations.

Pairwise Pearson correlation coefficients (r) were calculated using complete observations to generally assess correlations between cytokines measured in plasma and saliva. The resulting correlation matrix was used to generate a heatmap illustrating the direction and magnitude of within-and between-compartment correlations.

Overall cytokine profiles.

Using the vegan package, Permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA) tested differences in multivariate cytokine profiles between groups, while Permutational analysis of multivariate dispersion (PERMDISP) tested for differences in within-group dispersion around group centroids. PERMANOVA p-values were calculated using distances derived from log2(x+1)-transformed adjusted cytokine concentrations, adjusting for sex, age (months), height (cm), weight (kg), and breast-feeding duration (months) with 9,999 permutations. PERMANOVA R² values quantified the proportion of distance-matrix variation explained by group membership. Principal components analysis (PCA) was used to visualize variation in the profiles.

Multivariable models for individual cytokines.

For each cytokine, a multivariable regression model assessed associations with HIV status, CD4⁺ T-cell count (cells/mm³), and caries status, adjusting for sex, age (months), height (cm), weight (kg), and breastfeeding duration (months). Estimated marginal means (EMMs) were used for pairwise comparisons between factor levels. BH-adjusted p-values were reported for pairwise comparisons involving HIV status. P-values for the other predictors were unadjusted.

Plasma-saliva concordance. Partial spearman correlation analyses were performed separately for saliva and plasma cytokine concentrations within the HI, HEU, and HUU groups, adjusting for age and sex. Cytokine concentrations were log2(x+1)-transformed prior to analysis. Pairwise correlation coefficients (rho) and corresponding p-values were calculated, with multiple comparisons controlled using the Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) procedure. Associations with FDR-adjusted p-values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Correlation networks comprising seven matching cytokines (IFN-γ, IL-1α, IL-4, IL-5, IL-6, IL-8, and MCP-1) between plasma and saliva were constructed separately for each clinical group and biological compartment. Cytokines were represented as nodes, with edges representing statistically significant positive or negative partial Spearman correlations. Positive and negative edges indicated direct and inverse associations, respectively. Network density was evaluated across absolute correlation magnitude thresholds ranging from |ρ| ≥ 0.40 to |ρ| ≥ 0.50, in increments of 0.01, to assess the sensitivity of network connectivity to threshold selection. Network density was calculated as the ratio of observed to maximum possible edges in each undirected network. 61, 62 With seven cytokines, each network comprised a maximum of 21 possible edges (n(n-1)/2). The numbers of total, positive, and negative edges, together with network density, were summarized descriptively across clinical groups, biological compartments, and correlation thresholds.

For node-level centrality analysis, networks were constructed using all statistically significant cytokine associations (FDR < 0.05), without imposing an additional minimum correlation coefficient threshold. Edge weights were defined by the absolute magnitude of the partial Spearman correlation coefficients. Network centrality measures included degree (number of direct connections), strength (sum of the absolute weights of connections), betweenness (frequency with which a node lies on the shortest paths between other nodes), closeness, eigenvector centrality (importance of a node based on its connections to other highly connected nodes) and clustering coefficient (extent to which a node's neighboring nodes are interconnected). [63] [64] [65] Effect modification. To explore whether caries-cytokine associations differed by HIV status or immune status, HIV status × caries and CD4⁺ T-cell count × caries interaction terms were added to the cytokine-specific models. Where an interaction was significant, stratum-specific estimates were obtained from estimated marginal means and trends, with BH adjustment.

Sensitivity analyses:

The multivariate and regression analyses were repeated with maternal education and employment status as additional covariates in the children with these data (n = 400). In the children with salivary flow rate data (n = 203), multiple linear regression assessed associations between salivary flow rate and salivary cytokine concentrations, adjusting for sex, age (months), height (cm), weight (kg), and breastfeeding duration (months).
