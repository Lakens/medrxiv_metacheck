To fix Exact P-Values

What we found

Show details

Reporting p values imprecisely (e.g., p < .05) reduces transparency, reproducibility, and re-use (e.g., in p value meta-analyses). Best practice is to report exact p-values with three decimal places (e.g., p = .032) unless p values are smaller than 0.001, in which case you can use p < .001.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (e.g., “decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

P-Value Text
p>0.05 Liver palpability was similar in children and adults (2.6% vs 2.5%; χ²=0.005, p>0.05), whereas liver consistency differed by age, (Fisher’s exact test, p<0.001), as did liver tenderness (χ²=8.03, p=0.005) (Supplementary Table S5).
p>0.05 In contrast, PPF was present in 36.5% (19/52) of participants with liver tenderness compared with 28.6% (8/28) of participants without tenderness, with no significant association between liver tenderness and PPF (χ²=0.52, p>0.05).

The APA manual states: Report exact p values (e.g., p = .031) to two or three decimal places. However, report p values less than .001 as p < .001. However, 2 decimals is too imprecise for many use-cases (e.g., a p value meta-analysis), so report p values with three digits.

American Psychological Association (2020). Publication manual of the American Psychological Association, 7 edition. American Psychological Association.

To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 2 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Metacheck does not yet analyze automatically whether sentences which include non-significant p-values are correct, but we recommend manually checking the sentences below for possible misinterpreted non-significant p values.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Background: Ultrasound is commonly used to assess hepatosplenic morbidity in schistosomiasis, but access may be limited in resource-constrained settings. Abdominal palpation is simple and inexpensive, yet its clinical value and diagnostic performance for detecting ultrasound-defined organ enlargement remains uncertain. We evaluated standardised abdominal palpation against point-of-care ultrasound in a schistosomiasisendemic setting in rural Uganda. Methods: We conducted a cross-sectional study in January-February 2024 of 3177 participants aged 5-92 years from Pakwach, Buliisa and Mayuge districts in the SchistoTrack cohort. Trained nurses who also had midwifery skills performed standardised liver and spleen palpation, assessing palpability, extension, consistency and tenderness. Palpation agreement was assessed against point-of-care B-mode ultrasound performed by sonographers with organ dimensions classified using height-standardised internal reference values. We estimated sensitivity, specificity, predictive values and Cohen's κ for palpation against ultrasound-defined liver and spleen enlargement and assessed associations between liver consistency and tenderness, and schistosomal periportal fibrosis examined from ultrasound.

Results: Spleen palpation had high specificity (98.4%), with sensitivity ranging from 10.9% for moderate splenomegaly to 48.6% for severe splenomegaly. INTRODUCTION

Hepatosplenic schistosomiasis (HSS) is a chronic manifestation of current or previous infection with Schistosoma species that inhabit blood vessels around the intestines (1). HSS can cause hepatic fibrosis and portal hypertension, leading to splenomegaly, ascites, portosystemic collaterals and potentially life-threatening variceal bleeding (2, 3). A rough estimate of 200,000 deaths annually in sub-Saharan Africa have been attributed to these complications (3), however, the true burden may be higher because disability and mortality data from the region remain limited (4). Detecting hepatosplenic morbidity is important in schistosomiasisendemic settings. Yet, currently there are no World Health Organization (WHO) guidelines for surveying schistosomiasis-related morbidity, and no clinical guidelines exist in most endemic countries for use in primary healthcare facilities.

Point-of-care B-mode ultrasound is the primary technique for the diagnosis of HSS (5), and can identify organ lesions, periportal fibrosis (PPF) patterns, hepatosplenomegaly, abnormal liver texture and ascites (6). However, access to ultrasound machines and the expertise to scan individuals for hepatosplenic abnormalities remains extremely limited in rural, resourceconstrained sub-Saharan African settings (7, 8). Physical examination through abdominal palpation is inexpensive and requires minimal clinical expertise. Palpation can provide information on organ palpability, extension, consistency and tenderness (9). However, clinical definitions of hepatosplenomegaly vary considerably (10). Hepatomegaly has been defined as liver extension of >5 cm below the costal margin along the sternal line or >4 cm along the midclavicular line (11), while other definitions use >2 cm (12) below the costal margin or simply the presence of a palpable liver (13). METHODS

Study context

This study was undertaken within the SchistoTrack Cohort, a prospective, community-based cohort established in rural Uganda to investigate schistosome transmission and associated morbidity across Pakwach, Buliisa, and Mayuge districts. Further details of the cohort have been described elsewhere (22).

. This was a cross-sectional study conducted in January-February 2024 among 3177 participants aged 5-92 years from Pakwach, Buliisa, and Mayuge districts in rural Uganda. Schistosoma mansoni, which is associated with hepatosplenic disease in sub-Saharan Africa, is highly prevalent in the study districts, ranging from 22.05 -46.64% in 2024 (21). In 2024, schistosomal periportal fibrosis was detected in 18.51% of cohort participants, with a higher prevalence among adults (27.14%) than children (8.53%). Malaria prevalence was estimated at 46.04% in children and 14.12% in adults (21), while Human Immunodeficiency Virus (HIV) and Hepatitis B virus (HBV) occurred at slightly lower prevalence in adults (HIV: 1.19% in children and 6.94% in adults; HBV: 1.49% and 8.46%, respectively) (23), in 2024.

Physical examination

The standardised palpation protocol used in SchistoTrack was adapted from Vennervald et al. (24) as follows: Four nurses with qualifications in midwifery and experience conducting abdominal palpations conducted the clinical examinations. Each participant was palpated by one nurse. The examination involved initial observation of the abdomen, followed by superficial and deep palpation. Superficial palpation was performed gently, starting in the right lower abdominal quadrant and moving towards the left in a circular motion beneath the ribcage. This initial assessment was used to identify possible organomegaly and tenderness, although findings were recorded only during deep palpation.

For the liver, deep palpation was performed in the right upper abdominal quadrant, with examiners applying pressure to a depth of at least 1 cm unless the liver was markedly enlarged. Tenderness was assessed by observing the participant's facial response during palpation rather than directly asking about pain. Liver consistency was classified as soft, firm or hard using standardised tactile references corresponding to the lips, nose cartilage and . forehead bone, respectively. Extension of the left and right liver lobes below the ribcage was measured separately from the mid-sternal line.

For the spleen, palpation was performed from the left lower abdominal quadrant. Examiners recorded whether the spleen was palpable and assessed tenderness using the same approach as for the liver, applying pressure to a depth of at least 1 cm and observing the participant's facial response. Splenic extension below the ribcage was measured separately along the left mid-clavicular and mid-axillary lines.

Organ extension was measured in centimetres using a flexible measuring tape, with examiners instructed to look for approximately 2 cm of extension beyond the lower ribcage. A value of 0 cm was recorded when no extension could be measured or the organ was not palpable. In addition to liver and spleen palpation, nurses assessed other clinical findings, including suspected ascites, umbilical collaterals, fever, abdominal swelling, and abdominal or hypochondrial pain and tenderness. Examination findings were recorded electronically using a standardised SchistoTrack nurse palpation survey. A second trained nurse who was working at the same site could be consulted when findings were uncertain. Anthropometric measurements including weight and height also were recorded by nurses.

Ultrasound assessment

Point-of-care B-mode ultrasound examinations were performed by sonographers trained in the WHO Niamey Protocol using a Philips C5-2 curvilinear transducer and a standardised SchistoTrack protocol. Standardised liver and spleen sweeps were obtained to assess liver patterns, periportal fibrosis (PPF), organ dimensions, ascites and portosystemic collaterals, including splenic, gastro-oesophageal and pancreaticoduodenal varices and splenorenal . shunts. PPF was assessed using the WHO Niamey classification of ultrasound liver patterns (5), with patterns C-F classified as indicative of periportal fibrosis.

The left and right liver lobes were measured separately; the left lobe was measured in a parasternal view with the aorta visualised, while the right-lobe measurement incorporated estimation of the upper liver border behind the ribs, with the gallbladder fissure used to delineate the lobes. Spleen length was measured pole-to-pole through the splenic hilum.

Before field data collection, sonographers were trained to achieve inter-sonographer agreement within 2 cm for organ-size measurements.

Organ dimensions were classified using previously published height-standardised internal reference values derived from a healthy reference population within the SchistoTrack Cohort (21). The reference population included only participants who were negative for S. mansoni, malaria, HBV and HIV, were not pregnant, reported no alcohol consumption within the previous 12 months, and had no ultrasound-detected liver fibrosis. Participants were grouped into height deciles, and organ measurements were standardised against the mean and standard deviation (SD) within the corresponding height decile. For the left and right liver lobes and spleen, measurements were classified as normal, moderately enlarged (≥1 to <2 SD above the reference mean), or severely enlarged (≥2 SD above the reference mean).

Statistical analysis

Diagnostic performance of abdominal palpation was assessed using 2×2 contingency tables, with ultrasound as the reference standard. Sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV) and negative predictive value (NPV) (25) were calculated for splenomegaly and hepatomegaly of the left and right liver lobes. Ultrasound-defined splenomegaly and hepatomegaly were classified as moderate or severe enlargement, with either category . considered positive for any organomegaly. Diagnostic performance was also assessed separately for moderate and severe splenomegaly and hepatomegaly, with severe cases excluded from the moderate-enlargement analysis and moderate cases excluded from the severe-enlargement analysis. Overall hepatomegaly was defined as moderate or severe enlargement of either the left or right liver lobe.

Analyses were additionally stratified by age (<15 years and ≥15 years), and sex. The age categories were selected to reflect differences in organ size and palpability related to growth and development in children compared with adults (20). Unlike in adults, for whom normal organ size ranges are well established (20) assessing organ enlargement in children can be more challenging. For example, the spleen may be palpable in up to 10% of healthy children under 15 years of age, yet in most children, it must enlarge to two to three times its normal size before becoming palpable (20).

Confidence intervals for sensitivity, specificity, PPV and NPV were calculated using Wilson's method, which provides improved interval estimates for proportions, particularly with small samples and proportions close to 0 or 1 (26). Agreement between nurse palpation and ultrasound-defined organ enlargement was assessed using unweighted Cohen's κ with 95% confidence intervals (27).

Associations between clinical examination and ultrasound findings, as defined in the physical examination and ultrasound assessment sections, were assessed using 2 × 2 contingency tables. Among participants with a palpable liver, associations between ultrasound-defined periportal fibrosis (PPF) and liver consistency (firm/hard versus soft) and liver tenderness (present versus absent) were examined separately. Associations between liver and spleen palpability and between ultrasound-defined liver and spleen enlargement were also assessed.

RESULTS

A total of 3222 out of 3385 participants had complete data. Of these, 45 were excluded because abdominal palpation could not be performed due to pain (28), a muscular/bone-like abdomen which could not be palpated (10), or other reasons (7), leaving 3177 participants for analysis (Figure 1).

Study population characteristics

Participants ranged in age from 5 to 92 years, with a mean age of 27.0 years (SD 18.6). Of the 3177 participants, 40.3% (n=1279) were children aged <15 years and 59.7% (n=1898) were . aged ≥15 years. Overall, 55.3% (n=1756) were female and 44.7% (n=1421) were male.

Participants were recruited from Pakwach (43.6%, n=1384), Buliisa (33.0%, n=1049) and Mayuge (23.4%, n=744). The proportion of female participants varied across districts, being highest in Mayuge (63.6%, 473/744), followed by Buliisa (56.9%, 597/1049) and Pakwach (49.6%, 686/1384).

Splenomegaly

Overall, 8.1% of participants (256/3177) had a palpable spleen. Among participants with a palpable spleen, 43.8% (112/256) had a firm spleen, 24.6% (63/256) had a hard spleen, and 31.6% (81/256) had a soft spleen. Median spleen extension was 3.0 cm (interquartile range (IQR) 0 -5.0) along the left axillary line and 6.0 cm (IQR 4.0 -8.0) along the left clavicular line.

Spleen palpability was more frequent among children than adults (9.6% vs 7.0%; χ² = 6.68, p=0.010). Among participants with a palpable spleen, adults more frequently had firm or hard spleens and had greater spleen extension than children (all p<0.001; Supplementary Table S1).

Ultrasound-defined splenomegaly was present in 26.8% (n=852) of participants, including 16.4% (n=521) with moderate and 10.4% (n=331) with severe splenomegaly. The overall prevalence of splenomegaly was higher among children than adults (30.3%, n=387; vs 24.5%, n=465). Moderate splenomegaly was more common in children (19.5%, n=250) than adults (14.3%, n=271). Among participants with ultrasound-defined splenomegaly, severe splenomegaly accounted for a greater proportion of cases in adults than children (41.7% vs 35.4%; Supplementary Table S2).

Overlap of portal hypertension signs on ultrasound and splenomegaly

Portosystemic collaterals were identified in 36 participants overall, of whom 63.9% (23/36) had a palpable spleen. Among the 852 participants with ultrasound-defined splenomegaly, 3.6% (31/852) had portosystemic collaterals, which were more frequently observed in adults than children (6.0% (28/465) vs 0.8% (3/387); p<0.001, Fisher's exact test).

Ascites was uncommon (6 participants), with five cases occurring among participants with a palpable spleen.

Nurses independently identified umbilical collaterals in four participants. Of the six participants with ultrasound-detected ascites, two were correctly identified clinically by nurses, while one of 36 participants with ultrasound-detected portosystemic collaterals had umbilical collaterals recorded by nurses. Additional clinical findings recorded by nurses included fever (3.3%; 104/3177) and abdominal swelling (1.4%; 43/3177). Association between liver consistency/ tenderness with schistosomal PPF

Among participants with a palpable liver (n=80), schistosomal PPF was more frequent in participants with a firm or hard liver than in participants with a soft liver (53.3%, 24/45 vs 8.6%, 3/35; χ²=17.64, p<0.001). In contrast, PPF was present in 36.5% (19/52) of participants with liver tenderness compared with 28.6% (8/28) of participants without tenderness, with no significant association between liver tenderness and PPF (χ²=0.52, p>0.05).

Association between liver and spleen palpability and ultrasound-defined enlargement

Liver and spleen palpability frequently co-occurred, with 51.3% (41/80) of participants with a palpable liver also having a palpable spleen (χ²=206.65, p<0.001; Supplementary Table S8).

Similarly, among participants with ultrasound-defined hepatomegaly, 35.1% (289/824) also had ultrasound-defined splenomegaly (χ²=38.07, p<0.001).

DISCUSSION

There is a need to identify complementary strategies to ultrasound in areas that lack such equipment. Abdominal palpation is commonly applied in resource poor settings with several highly trained staff due to the availability of nurses and midwives for prenatal care. Here we assessed the agreement of abdominal palpation with point-of-care B-mode ultrasound for hepatosplenomegaly across 3177 individuals from communities endemic with intestinal schistosomiasis in rural Uganda. The diagnostic performance of standardised abdominal palpation varied by organ and severity. Spleen palpation was substantially more sensitive for severe than moderate splenomegaly, whereas liver palpation had low sensitivity for both liver lobes. Despite this limited diagnostic performance, clinical examination provided information beyond organ size; firm or hard liver consistency was positively associated with schistosomal PPF, while umbilical collaterals and other clinically relevant findings also were identified.

Spleen palpation showed consistently high specificity (98.4%) but limited sensitivity, which varied by the severity of ultrasound-defined splenomegaly. Sensitivity was higher for severe (48.6%) than moderate splenomegaly (10.9%), with a similar pattern observed in children and adults. The limited sensitivity observed in our study is broadly consistent with previous studies showing that physical examination may fail to detect ultrasound-defined splenomegaly (28) (29) (30). The limited diagnostic performance may partly reflect variation in how splenomegaly is . defined by palpation and ultrasound. In our study, splenic extension below the costal margin was measured in centimetres, and nearly all participants with a palpable spleen (95.3%, 244/256) had an extension of at least 2 cm at one or both measurement lines. We therefore used palpability as a simple binary clinical indicator rather than applying a specific extension threshold in the diagnostic-performance analyses. In contrast, ultrasound-defined splenomegaly was determined using spleen length relative to height-standardised reference values derived from a healthy population within the same cohort (21).

Hackett's classification, one of the most widely used clinical grading systems for palpable splenomegaly in field surveys (31, 32), is an ordinal system that incorporates palpability and the position of the lowest palpable point relative to anatomical landmarks, including the costal margin, umbilicus and pubic symphysis. Although this provides a standardised approach to describing palpable splenomegaly, Hackett grades do not necessarily correspond directly to ultrasound-defined spleen size (33). The importance of how palpable splenomegaly is defined has been demonstrated previously by Gerspacher-Lara et al. (15) in an S. mansoni-endemic population. Considering any palpable spleen as positive yielded 72.2% sensitivity and 90.5% specificity for ultrasound-defined splenomegaly, whereas requiring >4 cm extension below the costal margin reduced sensitivity to 27.8% but increased specificity to 98.0% (15). These findings show that the clinical threshold used to define splenomegaly influences diagnostic performance. There is a need to understand whether any palpable spleen is sufficient to raise clinical suspicion. Although, previous studies have shown that clinically palpable spleens are not necessarily enlarged or pathological (34) . As such, additional standardised studies focusing on measuring extension below the costal margin as done here are needed to identify relevant cut-offs that best reflect long-term patient outcomes focused on the study or clinical context. Focusing on patient outcomes is important as there may also be discrepancies from.

ultrasound due to probe positioning during examination, variations in spleen position and shape, respiratory movements and acoustic shadowing from ribs and bowel gas (35, 36).

Although spleen palpation had low sensitivity overall in the present study, its high specificity may help identify individuals with more pronounced splenic abnormalities who could be prioritised for further assessment. This prioritisation is particularly relevant to hepatosplenic schistosomiasis, where severe splenomegaly may signal more advanced disease and warrant assessment for portal-hypertension-related complications, including portosystemic collaterals and gastro-oesophageal varices, which can be life-threatening (1, 37, 38). In our study, of the 36 participants with ultrasound-detected portosystemic collaterals, 23 had a palpable spleen, of whom 21 had severe splenomegaly. These findings suggest that spleen palpation may have some value for clinical case identification and referral including capturing other underlying pathologies, rather than as a stand-alone method for screening or excluding splenomegaly.

Liver palpation had low sensitivity (3.4-7.3%) though high specificity (97.7%) for ultrasoundassessed hepatomegaly. These findings are consistent with previous studies showing that liver palpability does not reliably reflect ultrasound-assessed hepatomegaly (7, 30). Several factors may contribute to the low sensitivity of liver palpation. Although a problem with other studies (7) due to inconsistently defined reference populations, SchistoTrack used an internally defined healthy reference population to assess the hepatomegaly via ultrasound (21). Liver consistency may influence palpation, especially since a recently enlarged liver tends to be of soft consistency and more difficult to palpate than a firm or hard liver (39). In cases of marked enlargement, the liver may extend into the lower abdomen, making its lower border difficult to identify if palpation does not begin sufficiently low (39). Conversely, deep inspiration may.

bring a normal-sized liver below the costal margin, making it palpable (40). Anatomical variations, such as Riedel's lobe, where part of the right liver lobe extends below the costal margin, may also cause a normal-sized liver to be palpable and mistaken for an enlarged liver (41). Taken together, these factors may contribute to both missed cases of hepatomegaly and the incorrect identification of normal-sized livers as enlarged.

Age-stratified analyses showed a similar overall pattern in children and adults, with spleen palpation more sensitive for severe than moderate splenomegaly and liver palpation having low sensitivity for both children and adults. The greater proportion of moderate splenomegaly among children reported in this study may partly reflect malaria exposure, which is also known to exacerbate S. mansoni-associated splenomegaly in children (42). Given that malaria is routinely treated in the study area (43), including through SchistoTrack, treatment could limit malaria-associated splenomegaly and may partly explain the lower frequency of severe splenomegaly among children. Age-related anatomical differences may also influence spleen palpability, as a palpable spleen can occur in otherwise healthy children and does not necessarily indicate pathological enlargement (44). In adults, severe splenomegaly may reflect longer-term exposure to S. mansoni alongside other causes of splenic enlargement, including HIV (21) and malaria (45). Though contextually interesting, the relative contribution of these infections to splenic enlargement was beyond the scope of this agreement analysis and already studied thoroughly elsewhere for ultrasound (21).

Although palpation had low sensitivity for detecting ultrasound-defined liver and spleen enlargement, it may still provide important information about the presence and severity of hepatosplenic morbidity. In particular, severe splenomegaly is more likely to be detected by . palpation and is an important clinical feature of advanced hepatosplenic schistosomiasis. Liver consistency may also provide useful information. Among participants with a palpable liver, schistosomal PPF was present in 53.3% of individuals with a firm or hard liver compared with 8.6% of those with a soft liver. Evidence from other S. mansoni-endemic settings has been mixed. In Brazil, clinical findings including a hard or nodular liver were associated with ultrasound-detected schistosomal PPF (10), while in Venezuela, hard liver consistency was associated with S. mansoni infection (46). In contrast, Marinho et al. reported moderate agreement between hardened liver consistency and ultrasound-detected fibrosis in Brazil (30). These findings suggest that liver consistency may provide information about underlying schistosomal PPF, but further studies are needed to establish its reliability as a clinical marker.

Clinical examination may also identify other features of advanced disease. In addition to severe splenomegaly and changes in liver consistency, examination in our study identified umbilical collaterals, which may occur with portal hypertension (1, 47); as well as fever, abdominal swelling, and abdominal or hypochondrial pain and tenderness. These findings highlight the complementary value of bedside examination in capturing clinical information beyond ultrasound-defined hepatosplenic abnormalities. This finding is consistent with evidence from other clinical settings showing that physical examination provides immediate, low-cost clinical information and, despite the limited sensitivity of some individual findings, can increase clinical suspicion and guide further diagnostic investigation (48) (49) (50). In schistosomiasis-endemic settings with limited access to imaging, combining palpation findings, other clinical signs and presenting symptoms may therefore help identify individuals who warrant further assessment or referral. Future studies should evaluate whether integrating these features into a standardised clinical assessment algorithm improves case identification and prioritisation for further investigation.

. There were several limitations to this study. Both palpation and ultrasound are examinerdependent and may be influenced by examination technique and participant characteristics.

Although sonographers were trained to achieve agreement within 2 cm for organ-size measurements, comparable inter-examiner reproducibility was not formally assessed for palpation, despite the use of a standardised protocol. Nurses also identified cases of suspected ascites that were not confirmed by ultrasound. Given the high sensitivity of ultrasound for detecting free abdominal fluid, these findings were likely to represent falsepositive clinical assessments rather than ascites missed on ultrasound. This may partly reflect a lack of standardisation in the use of palpation and percussion for assessing ascites. Other causes of abdominal distension, including adiposity, flatulence, and pregnancy, may also have been mistaken for ascites. Further, only 80 participants had a palpable liver, limiting analyses on consistency or clinical findings.

CONCLUSIONS

Overall, palpation cannot replace point-of-care ultrasound or reliably exclude hepatosplenic morbidity but may increase clinical suspicion, supporting case identification and referral by identifying individuals who warrant further assessment where access to ultrasound is limited.

Bedside assessment also captures clinical history and presenting symptoms, including fever, abdominal pain and abdominal swelling, which, when considered alongside physical examination findings, may strengthen clinical suspicion and guide further investigation.

If validated in other settings, our findings might begin to provide a basis for developing and validating a simple, low-cost clinical assessment algorithm that combines palpation findings, clinical signs and presenting symptoms to identify individuals who should be prioritised for . further investigation or referral for ultrasound or other investigations in schistosomiasisendemic settings.
