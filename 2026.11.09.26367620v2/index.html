To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 3 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Metacheck does not yet analyze automatically whether sentences which include non-significant p-values are correct, but we recommend manually checking the sentences below for possible misinterpreted non-significant p values.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Spontaneous language offers a scalable, non-invasive approach for identifying biological indicators of preclinical Alzheimer's disease (AD). We examined prediction of APOE4 carrier status and cerebral amyloid-β (Aβ) burden using Cookie Theft picture-descriptions from two cohorts, including Hebrew-and English-speakers (IRAP, N=134; and WRAP, N=1,541). Aβ burden was classified across lowest-quartile, median, and highest-quartile cutoffs, capturing accumulation below conventional amyloid-positivity thresholds. We compared embeddings from pretrained large language models (LLMs) to standard cognitive test scores, reflecting current clinical assessment, and theory-driven linguistic features, reflecting traditional language analysis. Embedding-based models achieve the highest F1 among constituent models in five of eight tasks for APOE4 status and global Aβ. High prediction disagreement suggests complementary signals between approaches. An any-positive ensemble significantly outperforms every constituent model and a prior-informed random baseline across all eight tasks, and is also significantly superior to all constituent models in 21 of 24 regional Aβ tasks. These findings highlight the complementarity of LLM-based, theory-driven, and cognitive measures for predicting AD-related genetic risk and pathology, and supports development of early screening approaches.

1 Introduction

Alzheimer's disease (AD) is a neurodegenerative disorder and the most common cause of dementia. Its pathological processes can begin many years before clinically apparent symptoms, creating an important challenge for early detection and intervention. Early identification is particularly important because available treatments and preventive measures are most effective before substantial cognitive deterioration has occurred [1] [2] [3] . However, current diagnostic pathways are often initiated only after symptoms become noticeable, when the underlying pathology may already have been developing for decades 4 . This motivates the search for accessible, non-invasive methods that can identify signals associated with AD risk and pathology at early stages.

Language is a particularly promising source of information for this purpose. Language production involves multiple cognitive processes, and changes in linguistic abilities have long been recognized as characteristic of AD progression. Indeed, language-related tasks are central components of standard neuropsychological assessments, (e.g., verbal fluency, naming, and verbal memory tests [5] [6] [7] ). Beyond these structured assessments, spontaneous speech provides an opportunity to examine how individuals produce language in a more naturalistic setting. One widely used approach is the Cookie Theft picture-description task 8 , in which participants describe a standardized visual scene. The simplicity of collecting such speech samples, together with advances in Artificial Intelligence (AI), has motivated substantial research into languagebased indicators of cognitive impairment and dementia.

Earlier studies investigated linguistic changes through manual annotation and relatively simple quantitative measures [9] [10] [11] . More recent work has employed Natural Language Processing (NLP) and machine learning to extract linguistic information automatically and predict cognitive status from speech. These approaches include models based on predefined, theory-driven linguistic features, such as lexical diversity, syntactic complexity, and word frequency [12] [13] [14] [15] [16] [17] , as well as models that learn representations of language directly from data [18] [19] [20] [21] [22] [23] . The emergence of Large Language Models (LLMs) has substantially expanded the possibilities for the latter approach. Trained on very large text corpora to predict and generate language and perform a broad range of linguistic tasks, LLMs develop internal representations that capture rich aspects of linguistic meaning and context. These representations offer a promising means of identifying subtle language patterns that may be difficult to specify in advance. LLMs can also support automatic extraction of complex linguistic features that typically require human judgment, reducing the need for extensive manual annotation.

Despite this progress, an important scientific and clinical challenge remains. Much of the research on language-based AD prediction studies have focused on distinguishing individuals with dementia or Mild Cognitive Impairment (MCI) from cognitively healthy controls 24 . Although these studies have established that language contains useful information about cognitive impairment, they leave open a more fundamental question: Can language provide information about AD-related biological risk and pathology before clear clinical symptoms emerge? Addressing this question requires moving beyond the prediction of cognitive status toward biological indicators associated with the earliest stages of the disease.

In this work, we focus on two such indicators: apolipoprotein E ε4 (APOE4) carrier status and cerebral amyloid-β (Aβ) burden. APOE4 is the strongest common genetic risk factor for lateonset AD, with carriers facing an elevated risk of developing the disease 3, 25 . Aβ accumulation, in turn, is a central feature of AD neuropathology and can begin long before the onset of overt cognitive impairment 26 . These indicators capture different aspects of AD: APOE4 reflects inherited susceptibility, whereas Aβ burden provides a measure of underlying pathological processes. Investigating their relationships with language therefore offers an opportunity to examine whether linguistic behavior contains information associated with both biological vulnerability and disease pathology at early stages.

Our central hypothesis is that different representations of language and cognition capture distinct, potentially complementary signals associated with these biological indicators. In particular, we consider three sources of information: traditional cognitive assessment scores, which serve as our primary baseline; theory-driven (top-down) linguistic features extracted from spontaneous speech, which reflect the traditional approach to language analysis and serve as a second baseline; and language representations derived from pretrained LLMs. These approaches characterize individuals from different perspectives. Cognitive assessments measure performance on established neuropsychological tasks; predefined linguistic features capture specific aspects of language production and content; and LLM representations provide a broader, data-driven characterization of linguistic information. Although all three may be informative about AD-related risk and pathology, there is no reason to assume that they capture the same information or succeed and fail on the same individuals.

We investigate this hypothesis using data from two longitudinal cohorts: the Wisconsin Registry for Alzheimer's Prevention (WRAP) 27 and the Israel Registry for Alzheimer's Prevention (IRAP) 28 . Both cohorts include individuals at elevated risk for AD and provide cognitive assessments alongside genetic and neuroimaging information. We analyze spontaneous speech elicited through the Cookie Theft task, using English-language data from WRAP and Hebrewlanguage data collected in IRAP specifically for this study. The inclusion of these two cohorts allows us to examine the proposed approach across different languages and populations, while also extending computational language analysis to a Hebrew-speaking population that remains underrepresented in the AD literature [29] [30] [31].

We develop separate predictive models based on each of the three sources of information using Hierarchical Multi-Domain Regression (HMDR), a linear modeling framework that estimates shared and domain-specific effects. Importantly, rather than considering amyloid pathology only through a conventional positive-versus-negative distinction, we also examine multiple levels of amyloid accumulation, including levels below established clinical positivity thresholds. This allows us to investigate whether the available linguistic and cognitive signals are informative about variation in Aβ burden during earlier stages of the pathological process. We apply this approach to APOE4 carrier status and to global amyloid measures, and further extend the amyloid analysis to regional burden in the cingulate, frontal, parietal and temporal cortices to assess whether predictive patterns differ across brain regions.

Our results reveal that the three models achieve broadly comparable predictive performance, but their predictions differ substantially at the individual level. Across tasks and cohorts, observations correctly classified by one model are frequently misclassified by another, and no single representation consistently dominates. These findings provide evidence that the three sources of information capture complementary signals associated with APOE4 status and Aβ burden. Motivated by this complementarity, we investigate whether the different signals can mutually enhance prediction. We combine the three models using a simple ensemble that identifies a positive case whenever at least one constituent model predicts a positive outcome. This ensemble is the best-performing model, significantly improving F1 relative to each individual model for both APOE4 and global amyloid in both cohorts, demonstrating the predictive value of integrating information from language and cognitive assessments. The ensemble benefit also extends to regional amyloid outcomes, with significant advantages over all three constituent models in 21 of 24 tasks defined across the four cortical regions.

Together, these findings suggest that spontaneous language contains information associated with AD-related genetic risk and amyloid pathology that is not fully captured by standard . cognitive assessments. They also demonstrate that theory-driven linguistic features and LLMbased representations provide complementary information, despite being derived from the same speech samples. More broadly, our work provides evidence for the potential of combining modern language-based AI methods with established cognitive measures to improve the identification of biological indicators associated with early AD. This offers a foundation for developing accessible, low-burden screening approaches and for enriching existing clinical assessments with computational analysis of spontaneous speech.

Aggregate performance metrics, such as F1 scores, do not reveal whether models correctly classify or misclassify the same observations. No single constituent model consistently dominated the uniquely correct predictions among positive observations. 3 Discussion

This study examined whether language-based representations capture signals associated with preclinical AD risk and pathology, and whether these signals can enhance traditional cognitive assessments. We compared three sets of features: cognitive test scores, serving as the primary baseline for current clinical assessment; top-down, theory-driven linguistic features, representing the traditional approach to language analysis, and bottom-up linguistic embeddings derived from pretrained language models. Three main findings emerged. First, substantial individual-level disagreement among the constituent models indicated that each representation captured information not consistently recovered by the others. Second, embedding-based representations generally performed best among the constituent models, although no single representation consistently outperformed the others across all tasks. Third, combining the representations through an any-positive ensemble produced the best-performing model, significantly outperforming each constituent model and a prior-informed random baseline, and consistently yielding the highest F1 scores across both cohorts for APOE4 carrier status and global amyloid burden. This advantage also extended to regional amyloid burden, with the ensemble significantly outperforming all three constituent models in 21 of the 24 regional prediction tasks across four brain regions. Together, these findings suggest that linguistic representations and traditional cognitive measures capture complementary information associated with AD-related genetic risk and amyloid pathology, and that their integration improves predictive performance beyond that of individual representations.

This study provides a proof of concept for the potential utility of computational language analysis in preclinical AD research, with several implications for future clinical applications. First, because the Cookie Theft picture-description task is already incorporated into many cognitive assessments, our approach could extract additional information from an existing assessment with minimal additional effort. Second, language analysis could offer an accessible, low-burden screening approach to help identify individuals who may benefit from further clinical evaluation. Third, repeated speech collection could enable more frequent monitoring of linguistic changes, potentially even in home-based settings. This possibility is particularly relevant with the emergence of anti-amyloid treatments and the growing need for accessible methods of longitudinal monitoring. Although the present study did not examine within-person changes or treatment response, it provides a foundation for investigating whether languagebased measures could serve as indicators of disease progression or response to treatment. Importantly, the clinical use of such automated tools should be accompanied by healthcare professionals who can explain their clinical meaning and uncertainty, provide appropriate support, and guide individuals toward confirmatory assessment or available preventive and therapeutic options when indicated. Language-based tools may therefore be most valuable as decision-support or screening instruments rather than as systems that communicate biological risk independently of clinical context.

This study shows consistent findings across two independent cohorts with substantially different linguistic and sociocultural characteristics. This consistency is particularly meaningful given the predominance of English-speaking populations in research on speech and language markers of neurodegeneration 31 , alongside evidence that linguistic alterations associated with AD and MCI may vary across languages 17, [32] [33] [34] [35] . By including Hebrew, an underrepresented language in this field, our study contributes to broadening the linguistic diversity of AD research and supporting the development of more linguistically inclusive and equitable screening tools. Although differences in data collection and biomarker measurement complicate direct comparisons, the convergence of our central findings despite these provide strong evidence for their generalizability across diverse populations and settings.

Our findings add to growing evidence linking language characteristics to Aβ pathology in cognitively unimpaired and mildly impaired individuals 14, [36] [37] [38] [39] , including previous work in the WRAP cohort 37 . Previous studies have also employed pretrained language models to predict amyloid positivity, either in samples including individuals with clinically manifest cognitive impairment 40, 41 , and among cognitively unimpaired individuals 39 . However, these studies have framed amyloid prediction as binary classification based on predefined positivity criteria. Other studies have examined associations between language Aβ burden without relying on a binary clinical threshold, but have primarily focused on predefined linguistic or acoustic measures and did not employ LLM approaches 38, 42 . Therefore, a key contribution of our study is the evaluation of amyloid burden across multiple distribution-based thresholds using LLM representations, rather than relying exclusively on a single clinical cutoff. This approach is particularly relevant given growing evidence that Aβ accumulation below conventional positivity thresholds may precede more rapid biomarker progression and subtle cognitive changes [43] [44] [45] . Our findings extend this evidence by suggesting that language-based representations capture signals associated with amyloid burden even below conventional thresholds for amyloid positivity.

Notably, disagreement in amyloid prediction was highest for the median-based classification tasks in both cohorts. This pattern is consistent with the possibility that more pronounced amyloid-related patterns are captured more similarly across the three approaches, whereas subtler signals lead to more divergent predictions. However, because median-based classification includes the full range of amyloid values and differs from quartile-based tasks in class balance, these findings do not establish that disagreement is concentrated specifically among individuals with intermediate amyloid burden. Future analyses examining disagreement as a function of continuous amyloid burden could help clarify these patterns. This work also contributes to the relatively limited literature on spontaneous speech and APOE4 carrier status. Previous studies have often treated APOE4 as a covariate rather than a direct prediction target, including investigations of cognitive decline and AD-related biomarkers incorporating language or speech measures 12, 37, 42, 46 . APOE4-related linguistic differences have also been examined using structured verbal neuropsychological tasks [46] [47] [48] . In contrast, attempts to infer APOE4 carrier status directly from spontaneous speech remain scarce. A recent study classified cognitively healthy APOE4 carriers and non-carriers using Cookie Theft recordings, but relied exclusively on acoustic characteristics 49 . By contrast, our study examines embeddingbased and top-down linguistic representations, capturing multiple dimensions of language, including lexical, syntactic, and content-related characteristics.

Beyond the range of linguistic dimensions represented, another strength of this study lies in the quality of the underlying transcripts and annotations. Transcripts were carefully prepared by trained annotators following rigorous protocols. The IRAP transcripts were produced and meticulously annotated specifically for the present research, rather than relying on pre-existing transcriptions or automated transcription alone. This substantial data preparation effort involved independent annotation by two trained annotators, followed by adjudication of disagreements by a third annotator. Unlike approaches relying on readily available transcripts or uncorrected Automatic Speech Recognition (ASR) outputs, this procedure allowed careful preservation of subtle linguistic phenomena that may be particularly informative in preclinical AD research. The same emphasis on quality extended to the extraction of top-down linguistic features, which combined manual, automated, and LLM-based approaches. Disfluencies were manually annotated, an approach particularly valuable in clinical contexts, where subtle disruptions in speech may carry important signals of cognitive changes that off-the-shelf ASR systems may fail to capture reliably. Lexical measures were computed using language-specific NLP tools and corpus-based frequency norms, while higher-level, theory-driven features were annotated . using a validated LLM-as-a-judge framework. These procedures allowed us to preserve linguistic phenomena that could otherwise be lost during preprocessing and to examine their potential relevance to AD-related biological measures.

Importantly, the predictive linguistic signals identified in this study were obtained using relatively simple and computationally accessible approaches. LLM embeddings were extracted from pretrained, off-the-shelf language models with frozen weights, without additional finetuning. The top-down approach relied on only 13 theory-driven linguistic features, selected to represent diverse dimensions of language, including lexical, syntactic, and content-related characteristics. Both representations were used as inputs to HMDR, a linear classifier. Similarly, our ensemble employed a simple decision rule, classifying an observation as positive if at least one constituent model predicted a positive outcome, without additional training or parameter optimization. The ability to combine lightweight approaches to predict AD-related indicators has important implications for future clinical applications, as their modest computational demands not only safeguard patient privacy through local deployment, but also ensure accessibility and reproducibility in resource-limited settings.

These results establish a foundation for further investigating the predictive value of spontaneous speech in preclinical AD, while demonstrating that meaningful signals can be extracted using relatively simple linguistic representations. At the same time, the current approaches should not be considered an upper bound on achievable performance. Future work could examine whether larger or task-adapted language models, fine-tuning with sufficiently large datasets, or broader sets of linguistically informed features provide additional predictive value. Such developments should be evaluated against the interpretability, efficiency, and accessibility of the relatively lightweight approaches demonstrated here.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, although WRAP includes repeated speech observations for some participants, the present analyses do not model withinperson linguistic change over time. Accordingly, the threshold-specific patterns cannot be interpreted as longitudinal trajectories of language change as Aβ accumulates. Future repeatedmeasures analyses linking within-person changes in language and biomarkers are needed to test this directly.

Second, methodological and cohort-specific differences between IRAP and WRAP limit our ability to separate the observed associations between linguistic and cognitive measures and the target variables from potential influences of differences in data collection, biomarker measurement, and social and cultural characteristics. IRAP and WRAP are active longitudinal cohorts whose protocols continue to evolve, creating differences in available measures and participant overlap across modalities. We therefore focused on APOE4 and Aβ, the two ADrelated biological indicators currently available in both cohorts with sufficient overlap with Cookie Theft speech samples. Other potentially informative markers are either available in only one cohort or do not yet have sufficient overlap with the speech data. Particularly relevant future targets include tau and blood-based biomarkers such as GFAP and NfL, which are being collected as these cohorts continue to expand.

Although cognitive inputs were initially defined according to the established literature for each cohort, an additional evaluation using a harmonized set of cognitive measures available in both cohorts showed that the pattern remained largely unchanged, with no consistently superior constituent model and the ensemble outperforming all constituent models across tasks (Supplementary Methods 1.2 and Supplementary Tables 3 4 5). Nevertheless, other sources of cross-cohort variability remain, including differences in amyloid tracers and processing procedures, cultural and sociolinguistic factors that may shape language use, and potential differences in how embedding models represent Hebrew and English. Harmonizing amyloid . measures using Centiloid scaling, together with validation across additional languages and cohorts, could help disentangle these sources of variability. Importantly, the consistency of these findings across two independent cohorts, despite linguistic, sociocultural, and methodological differences, further strengthens the evidence for the complementarity of linguistic and cognitive measures and the predictive benefits of integrated approaches.

4 Methods

4.1 Participants

Participants were drawn from two longitudinal cohorts focused on preclinical AD, IRAP and WRAP. The cohorts comprised, respectively, middle-aged Hebrew-and English-speakers with a parental history of AD. Available data included speech recordings, cognitive assessments, neuroimaging, genetic information, and electronic health records. IRAP data were consolidated from several separate data files spanning June to September 2025; WRAP data were obtained from the November 30, 2025 data freeze. All participants provided written informed consent. Data collection was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Sheba Medical Center for IRAP and by the University of Wisconsin-Madison Institutional Review Board for WRAP.

To compare the language and cognitive models, we included observations for which both language and cognitive data were available, together with APOE4 carrier status, Aβ burden, or both. The cognitive assessment and Cookie Theft task were typically completed on the same day. However, these assessments could be separated in time from the Positron Emission Tomography (PET) scan used to quantify amyloid burden. In both cohorts, PET scans were restricted to within ±8 years of the assessment, limiting the temporal gap while allowing to retain all available IRAP observations.

In both IRAP and WRAP cohorts, participants contributed speech samples across multiple visits. For amyloid analyses, each speech sample was paired with the temporally closest eligible PET/PiB scan, allowing individual scans to serve multiple observations (IRAP: 59 observations across 58 unique scans; WRAP: 1,182 observations across 559 unique scans). Because APOE4 genotype is time-invariant, single genotype calls were mapped across all repeated visits for each participant (IRAP: 126 observations from 125 unique calls; WRAP: 1,538 observations from 538 unique calls).

In IRAP, participants were classified using the Clinical Dementia Rating (CDR) 50 into Healthy Control (HC; n = 120) and MCI (n = 14) groups. In WRAP, cognitive status was determined through a two-tiered review process, in which visit data were initially screened by an algorithm, followed by multidisciplinary consensus review of flagged cases 51, 52 . This process resulted in one of five classifications: Cognitively Unimpaired-Stable (CU-S; n=1,265), Cognitively Unimpaired-Declining (CU-D; n=222), MCI (n=46), impaired but not MCI (n=5), and dementia (n=3). CU-S denotes individuals with normal cognition and no evidence of meaningful decline, whereas CU-D denotes individuals with evidence of cognitive decline that is not sufficient to meet criteria for MCI. The impaired-but-not-MCI category includes individuals with cognitive impairment that was not classified as either CU-D or MCI, including cases in which impairment was considered longstanding rather than a decline from a previous level of functioning. For cross-cohort comparison, cognitive status was harmonized into two categories: cognitively unimpaired (IRAP: HC; WRAP: CU-S and CU-D) and cognitively impaired (IRAP: MCI; WRAP: MCI, impaired but not MCI, and dementia).

4.2 Target variables

4.2.1 APOE4

APOE4 carrier status was treated as a binary classification task, defined as the presence of at least one ε4 allele of the APOE gene. In both cohorts, APOE genotype information was obtained from blood samples.

4.2.2 Amyloid-β

Both cohorts used PET combined with Computed Tomography (PET-CT) scans to quantify amyloid tracer accumulation: the SUVR in IRAP, and PiB Index in WRAP. Further details are provided later in this section.

To examine whether language and cognitive measures captured signals across different levels of amyloid accumulation, three binary classification tasks were defined separately for each cohort using cohort-specific thresholds: membership in the lowest quartile, values above the median, and membership in the highest quartile. These tasks were defined for the global amyloid measure in each cohort and, separately, for each of four regional amyloid measures (cingulate, frontal, parietal, and temporal). For the regional tasks, the thresholds were determined independently from the distribution of the corresponding region within each cohort, rather than reusing the global biomarker cutoffs. Each cohort therefore contributed three global amyloid tasks and 12 regional amyloid tasks.

Clinical practice routinely relies on expert interpretation to determine the presence or absence of amyloid pathology. Therefore, we additionally evaluated the prediction of this binary status in WRAP (Supplementary Table 8). IRAP was excluded from this analysis due to severe class imbalance, with only two positive cases. 4.2.2.1 IRAP As previously described 28 , SUVR values were derived using PET-CT. SUVR reflected concentration of the 18 F tracer in cortical target regions (cingulate gyrus, frontal, parietal, temporal cortices) relative to the cerebellum, a reference region typically spared from amyloid deposition even in advanced stages of AD. Global SUVR was calculated as a weighted average of the SUVR values across these four cortical regions. The three binary classification tasks were defined using the global IRAP SUVR distribution: membership in the first quartile (SUVR < 0.910), the upper median (SUVR > 0.941), and the fourth quartile (SUVR > 0.980). For the regional analyses, the four cortical SUVR values (cingulate, frontal, parietal, and temporal) were used as separate outcomes. The Q1, median, and Q4 thresholds were calculated independently for each regional SUVR distribution, rather than using the global SUVR thresholds, resulting in the following thresholds: cingulate, Q1 < 0.998, median > 1.043, Q4 > 1.089; frontal, Q1 < 0.907, median > 0.938, Q4 > 0.982; parietal, Q1 < 0.920, median > 0.945, Q4 > 0.995; temporal, Q1 < 0.880, median > 0.923, Q4 > 0.949. Due to differences in data availability, the regional analyses included fewer scans than the global SUVR analysis (N = 59), with 41 observations available for the cingulate, parietal, and temporal regions and 40 for the frontal region. 4.2.2.2 WRAP Amyloid burden was quantified via 70-minute [¹¹C]PiB PET scans. The dynamic PET data were transformed into voxel-wise distribution volume ratio (DVR) maps using the cerebellar cortex as the reference region. Cortical DVR values from eight regions-anterior and posterior cingulate, medial orbitofrontal, supramarginal, angular, middle temporal, superior temporal, and precuneus-were then averaged into the composite PiB Index per the methodology detailed in [53] [54] [55] . The three binary classification tasks were then defined from the distribution of PiB . Index values in WRAP: membership in the first quartile (PiB Index < 1.046), values above the median (PiB Index > 1.092), and membership in the fourth quartile (PiB Index > 1.236).

In WRAP, regional DVR values were extracted using the Automated Anatomical Labeling (AAL) atlas and grouped into the four broad cortical regions examined in IRAP: frontal (superior and middle frontal gyri; opercular and triangular parts of the inferior frontal gyrus; medial superior frontal gyrus; orbital portions of the superior, middle, and inferior frontal gyri; medial orbitofrontal cortex; and gyrus rectus), parietal (superior and inferior parietal lobules, angular and supramarginal gyri, and precuneus), temporal (superior, middle, and inferior temporal gyri), and cingulate (anterior, middle, and posterior cingulate gyri). These regional measures incorporated additional cortical DVR values beyond the eight regions used to calculate the global PiB Index. The Q1, median, and Q4 thresholds were calculated independently for each regional DVR distribution, resulting in the following thresholds: cingulate, Q1 < 1.124, median > 1.176, Q4 > 1.319; frontal, Q1 < 1.002, median > 1.041, Q4 > 1.161; parietal, Q1 < 0.981, median > 1.021, Q4 > 1.147; and temporal, Q1 < 1.004, median > 1.038, Q4 > 1.123.

4.3 Experimental setup

We compared three constituent prediction models-cognitive, embedding-based, and top-downeach combining a distinct feature representation with the same HMDR framework. An analysis of agreement and disagreement among their predictions motivated evaluation of an "anypositive" ensemble strategy, which classified an observation as positive if at least one constituent model predicted a positive outcome. The model comparison was carried out for APOE4 and global amyloid outcomes and was extended to the four regional amyloid measures, using the cohort-specific classification thresholds defined above.

The following subsections describe the speech task and preprocessing procedure (4.3.1), the three constituent model input representations (4.3.2), constituent model-development procedure (4.3.3), and performance evaluation (4.3.4).

4.3.1 Speech Task and Preprocessing

Participants completed the Boston Cookie Theft picture-description task 8 , a narrative elicitation task widely used in cognitive assessment. IRAP participants were shown the picture presented in Supplementary Figure 2 and instructed to "describe the picture with as many details as possible" (translated from Hebrew). WRAP participants completed the same picturedescription task with the instruction "Tell me everything you see going on in this picture". IRAP recordings were transcribed using Subtitle Edit 4.0.11 by two blinded annotators, achieving substantial inter-annotator agreement (Cohen's κ = 0.758). Discrepancies were resolved by a third adjudicator. WRAP transcripts were annotated in CHAT format by trained speech-language pathology graduate students from the Mueller Lab at the Center for Communication and Neurodegenerative Disease and Language (CCANDL), University of Wisconsin-Madison, as described in 13.

Interviewer speech, and non-verbal vocalizations (e.g., laughter, coughing) and unintelligible words were removed. Filler words, repetitions, and false starts were retained to preserve spontaneous speech characteristics.

4.3.2 Constituent Model Inputs

4.3.2.1 Cognitive Model

The cognitive model was based on neuropsychological test batteries administered in the IRAP and WRAP cohorts. Raw test scores were standardized to z-scores prior to modeling. Previous studies within each cohort have also defined composite scores that aggregate individual tests into cognitive domains, as well as global measures of cognitive performance. Accordingly, we evaluated cognitive features at three levels of representation: individual standardized test scores, domain-level composite scores, and global cognitive scores. The specific tests and composite definitions included at each level are detailed in Supplementary Methods 1.1.

4.3.2.2 Embedding Model

For each transcript, embeddings were extracted independently from each language model included for the corresponding cohort, yielding multiple candidate representations of the same text. These representations were treated as a hyperparameter and selected within the modeltraining procedure, detailed in Supplementary Methods 3.2. All models were downloaded and executed locally to protect participant privacy and data security. The embedding models spanned multilingual, English-specific, and Hebrew-specific architectures and varied in pretraining objectives, model size, and embedding dimensionality, as described in Supplementary Methods 2.1. By incorporating both multilingual and language-specific architectures, this framework facilitates direct cross-cohort comparisons between English and Hebrew while maximizing representation quality within the Hebrew-speaking cohort.

4.3.2.3 Top-Down Model

In contrast to embedding-based representations, which encode text through latent semantic patterns that are not directly interpretable, the top-down approach relies on predefined, theorydriven features with explicit meaning, grounded in the clinical and linguistic literature. Table 5 outlines this feature set, including extraction methods for the IRAP and WRAP cohorts, across five categories: disfluencies, lexical and syntactic properties, objective content, subjective interpretation, and human experience.

Disfluencies captured disruptions in language production and were derived from manual transcript annotation followed by automated counting. Lexical and syntactic properties captured word-and sentence-level characteristics and were extracted primarily using conventional computational methods, including automatic linguistic analysis and languagespecific lexical resources: For IRAP, tokenization and Part-of-Speech (POS) tagging were performed using DictaBERT 56 , an open-source NLP toolkit that provides linguistic analyses including tokenization, POS tagging, and dependency parsing. For WRAP, corresponding features were derived using CLAN (Computerized Language ANalysis) 57, 58 pipelines under the transcription and analysis conventions maintained by TalkBank. Word-frequency in both Hebrew and English was derived using the Python wordfreq library (version 3.1.1) 59 , which provides corpus-based estimates of word frequency across multiple languages.

In addition, using a previously developed LLM-based annotation framework 60, 61 , we extracted a set of high-level binary features defined across four categories: lexical and syntactic properties, objective content, subjective interpretation, and human experience. These features assess the description as a whole rather than isolated words, sentences, or directly computed counts. Developed in consultation with experts and grounded in previous dementia research, they characterize not only what information the speaker reports, but also how the speaker interprets and responds to the depicted situation. Therefore, this approach complements conventional quantitative language measures by capturing complex, interpretable behaviors that are difficult to operationalize through simple counts or rule-based algorithms.

. To enable these high-level features to be annotated at scale, we used Qwen3.5-9B 62 as the LLM annotator. The quality of the LLM annotations was evaluated using the Alternative Annotator Test (AAT) 63 , a statistical method for assessing whether annotations produced by an LLM can serve as a substitute for human annotations (Supplementary Methods 2.4 and Supplementary Table 11). WRAP Correct Information Units (CIU) were already available from manual annotation, obviating the need for LLM annotation.

Detailed feature definitions and annotation prompts are provided in Supplementary Methods 2.2. Additional preliminary analyses, including descriptive statistics and exclusion criteria for features are provided in Supplementary Methods 2.3, Supplementary Table 12, and Supplementary Figure 3.

4.3.3 Model Development

Model development for all classification tasks was conducted using stratified MCCV and HMDR, which captures shared population trends and domain-specific variations. The HMDR formulation is detailed in Supplementary Methods 3.1. In each iteration, 20% of the samples were randomly held out as an independent test set, while the remaining 80% were used for hyperparameter tuning. All observations belonging to the same participant were assigned to the same partition. In the APOE4 analyses, the feature vector contained either cognitive, embedding, or top-down features. In amyloid-related tasks, both in IRAP and WRAP, the interval in years between the speech or cognitive assessment and the matched PET-CT scan was appended to the feature vector. All features, including the scan-speech gap, were standardized within the training data before model fitting. Hyperparameter selection was performed exclusively within the training set in two sequential phases (Supplementary Methods 3.2), each . using a separate stratified five-fold cross-validation procedure, with hyperparameters selected to maximize the positive-class F1 score.

4.3.4 Evaluation

Using F1 as the primary evaluation metric, we compare the constituent models with the anypositive ensemble to assess whether combining multiple representations yields a significant improvement in predictive performance. As a reference for chance-level performance, all approaches were also compared with a prior-informed random baseline, which assigned class labels according to their proportions in the task's full sample. The F1 score is defined as: (1) 𝐹1 = 2 × 𝑃𝑟𝑒𝑐𝑖𝑠𝑖𝑜𝑛 × 𝑅𝑒𝑐𝑎𝑙𝑙 𝑃𝑟𝑒𝑐𝑖𝑠𝑖𝑜𝑛 + 𝑅𝑒𝑐𝑎𝑙𝑙

Where: (2) 𝑃𝑟𝑒𝑐𝑖𝑠𝑖𝑜𝑛 = 𝑇𝑃 𝑇𝑃 + 𝐹𝑃 (3) 𝑅𝑒𝑐𝑎𝑙𝑙 = 𝑇𝑃 𝑇𝑃 + 𝐹𝑁 TP, FP, and FN denote true positives, false positives, and false negatives, respectively.

Precision, also known as Positive Predictive Value (PPV), measures how many of all cases predicted as positive are truly positive. Recall, also known as sensitivity or True Positive Rate (TPR), measures how many of all actual positive cases are correctly identified by the model.

The F1 score combines precision and recall into a single measure, thereby accounting for both false-positive and false-negative errors. By definition, it is their harmonic mean, therefore a low value for either precision or recall substantially reduces the overall F1 score. Additional performance metrics, including precision, recall, area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (ROC-AUC), and area under the precision-recall curve (PR-AUC), are reported in Supplementary Methods 3.3 and Supplementary Tables 13 14 15 16.

4.4 Statistical analysis

Continuous demographic and clinical characteristics were summarized as mean (SD), and categorical variables as counts and percentages. Differences between cohorts and between positive and negative outcome groups within each classification task were assessed using twosided Welch's t-tests for continuous variables and Pearson's chi-square tests for categorical variables.

Predictive performance was evaluated across 1,000 MCCV repetitions for APOE4 and global Aβ classification tasks, and 100 repetitions for regional Aβ tasks. F1 scores were calculated separately for each outcome class on the held-out test sets and summarized as the mean across repetitions. Mean F1 scores were reported with 95% confidence intervals. Additional performance metrics included precision, recall, ROC-AUC, and PR-AUC (Supplementary Methods 3.3).

Pairwise comparisons of model performance were conducted using F1 scores obtained from matched MCCV test partitions. For APOE4 and global Aβ tasks, comparisons proceeded in two stages. First, the highest-performing constituent model was evaluated against the remaining two constituent models. Second, the top-performing overall approach (consistently the "anypositive" ensemble in both tasks) was compared against the other four modeling approaches (the three constituent models and the prior-informed random baseline). For regional Aβ tasks, only this second stage was conducted, comparing the best-performing approach with the remaining four approaches among the three constituent models, the ensemble, and the priorinformed random baseline. Differences were assessed using two-sided paired t-tests, with BH . FDR correction applied separately to the comparisons within each task and stage (two comparisons for constituent models, four for the full model comparison in APOE4, global Aβ, and regional Aβ tasks). Full test statistics are reported in Supplementary Tables 6, 9, and 10. Statistical significance was defined as q < 0.05.
