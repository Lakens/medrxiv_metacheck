How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 225 papers containing 405 instances of non-exact p-values, the module correctly detected 269 cases (true positives) and incorrectly identified 78 (false positives). It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

The Path to Prevention (P2P) platform trial is a first-in-class, phase 2A, randomized, doubleblind, placebo-controlled learning study. Its design expedites the assessment of investigational therapies for the treatment of early-stage Neuronal α-Synuclein Disease (NSD), a biological disease continuum that encompasses and often precedes clinically manifest Parkinson's Disease. P2P is embedded within the Parkinson's Precision Medicine Initiative (PPMI), a robust observational longitudinal cohort study on Parkinson's disease progression and a trial-facilitating enrollment framework.

A regimen, comprised of an active investigational therapy and a matched placebo control, evaluates a therapeutic candidate within the P2P perpetual platform framework. Multiple regimens can be enrolled, enabling efficient testing of multiple therapeutics. A two-stage randomization scheme determines regimen sample size with 125 participants assigned to a regimen's active therapy and a variable number to placebo control. The treated follow up period is a minimum of 96 weeks and a maximum of 144 weeks, with the last randomized participant followed for 96 weeks.

As a pioneering interventional trial in early-stage NSD, P2P endpoint selection was critically informed by modeling and analysis of available PPMI data. measured by the bilateral putamen Specific Binding Ratio (SBR) and 2) a clinical outcome measured by the Movement Disorder Society-Sponsored Revision of the Unified Parkinson's Disease Rating Scale (MDS-UPDRS) Part III score. Introduction

The Path to Prevention (P2P) platform trial is a first-in-class, phase 2A, randomized, doubleblind, placebo-controlled learning study investigating multiple disease-modifying therapeutic candidates in participants with early stage, biologically defined Stage 2B Neuronal α-Synuclein Disease (NSD). α-Synuclein pathology is present in several neurodegenerative syndromes including Parkinson's disease (PD) and dementia with Lewy bodies. [1] [2] [3] The current treatment options that exist for early stage PD treat only symptoms and cannot slow or halt disease progression. [4] [5] [6] Recent advances in a biological definition of NSD propose an integrated staging system (NSD-ISS) that captures the presence of pathological n-α-syn species and dopaminergic neuronal dysfunction, as well as the presence of clinical symptoms. 3 With measurable biomarker pathology but minimal clinical symptoms, the Stage 2B NSD population is an ideal target for assessing biologically targeted therapeutics ( BTTs) to slow or halt clinical progression. 7 Narrowing the trial population to this biologically defined early stage of disease reduces heterogeneity in an effort to increase the likelihood of demonstrating treatment efficacy, a precision medicine strategy that has successfully been used in other neurodegenerative diseases. [8] [9] [10] [11] The companion manuscript to this paper (Schneider et al) further details the landscape of BTTs for PD and the rationale for using the NSD population for P2P.

P2P is a pioneering trial testing potential disease-modifying therapeutics in the early stages of the pathological NSD process, when participants exhibit limited manifestation of clinical symptoms. Endpoint selection for this novel trial population of biologically defined NSD Stage 2B participants required careful consideration. We surveyed the landscape of outcome measures used in PD trials, with particular focus on disease-modifying trials. 12, 13 The Movement Disorder Society-Sponsored Revision of the Unified Parkinson's Disease Rating Scale (MDS-UPDRS) is a validated clinical outcome measure and is often considered a standard outcome assessment for PD. 14, 15 It has four Parts, which assess distinct domains of PD: non-motor experiences of daily living (Part I), motor experiences of daily living (Part II), motor examination (Part III), and motor complications (Part IV). 14 Individual MDS-UPDRS Part scores, in particular Part III, as well as composite scores derived from individual MDS-UPDRS Part scores have been used as primary efficacy endpoints in PD clinical trials. 12,13,16- 19 MDS-UPDRS Part scores are clinimetrically distinct with differential longitudinal progression in early stages of disease. 14, 20, 21 While these endpoint considerations in early PD may have some overlap with those for the Stage 2B NSD population, it was critically important to explore natural history study data to assess endpoints in the context of Stage 2B NSD, specifically. Outcome assessments of biological changes also play a role in PD trials, with Dopamine Transporter (DAT) imaging aiming to capture early dopaminergic neuronal dysfunction. 22 As an example, the CALM-PD sub study used longitudinal DAT imaging as an outcome measure to quantify neuronal degeneration. 23 In addition to capturing important biological information, DAT imaging captures pathological changes that may precede clinical changes. 24 Selecting appropriate endpoints in early-stage NSD is critical to well-powered trial design. 25 The P2P platform trial is embedded within the PPMI Clinical natural history study that is part of the longstanding Parkinson's Precision Medicine Initiative (PPMI), which provides a robust and efficient infrastructure for identifying and enrolling trial-eligible participants (Figure 1). 26 The PPMI natural history study contains longitudinal, observational data on the progression of numerous outcome measures across the stages of NSD. Within PPMI Clinical, participants receive in-clinic assessments and provide comprehensive longitudinal data on clinical, imaging, and biomarker endpoints. Participants are recruited into three phenotypically defined cohorts: Parkinson's disease, Prodromal and Healthy controls. 27 All participants undergo biological testing to assess the presence of neuronal α-synuclein and dopaminergic neuronal dysfunction, as well as extensive clinical characterization to define their NSD status (+/ -). NSD+ participants are staged using the NSD-ISS. The Clinical cohort data provides valuable insight into the natural progression and associated variability of biological and clinical markers of NSD, including how these markers differ for NSD+ versus NSD-participants. These data were extensively modeled to inform P2P endpoint selection. In addition to facilitating trial recruitment, embedding P2P within the infrastructure that supports the PPMI Clinical cohort enables a key innovative design feature: the incorporation of non-randomized control data from P2P-eligible participants in the ongoing PPMI study to help characterize the natural progression of early-stage NSD. Randomized and nonrandomized control data are collected under the same PPMI study umbrella and collected using the same standardized methods for data collection.

P2P provides a trial framework for industry partners to test therapeutic candidates within a regimen, which is comprised of an active investigational therapy and a matched placebo control. Multiple regimens can enroll in the platform at once, allowing the evaluation of multiple therapeutic candidates. The P2P primary analyses use longitudinal Bayesian mixed effects models to evaluate the disease progression rate for participants on active treatment versus shared placebo control as measured by the longitudinal primary endpoints, MDS-UPDRS Part III and DAT bilateral putamen specific binding ratio (SBR). We explored and refined statistical models using longitudinal data from participants in the PPMI Clinical cohort, selected to meet P2P inclusion criteria. Since P2P participants will be enrolled through PPMI, thorough analyses of these cohort data provided high-quality, evidence-based assumptions for platform trial simulation. With no prior precedent, novel endpoint exploration of the biologically defined trial population was a necessary part of the trial design process. Numerous trial simulations were conducted to evaluate the P2P platform trial design features and ensure a robust, well-powered, and efficient clinical trial design.

Materials and methods

Endpoint selection

We modeled PPMI natural history data to understand the rate, shape, and variability of disease progression in the NSD Stage 2B population, which has not previously been therapeutically targeted. Outcome assessments that capture early biological and clinical change are essential as endpoints in trials for early stage disease. 25

Trial design

A key objective of the P2P platform trial is the ongoing ability to efficiently assess multiple therapeutic candidates for early-stage NSD. The P2P Master Protocol outlines a platform trial design that can accommodate several treatments at a time, each enrolled in a separate regimen (Figure 2). 28, 29 Each regimen contains a single active treatment and matched placebo control. Trial participants randomize in a two-step randomization process: 1) first with equal probability to a regimen for which they are eligible in an unblinded fashion, then 2) within that regimen, to receive active treatment or control with a K:1 ratio in a blinded fashion. 30 Here, K denotes the number of actively enrolling regimens in the platform at the time of a participant's randomization. When there is more than one active regimen, this allocation ratio increases the overall likelihood that an individual participant receives an active treatment while maintaining 1:1 allocation of each regimen's active treatment participants to the platform's randomized shared placebo controls. This two-step randomization process both enables the platform study design and requires careful statistical handling of control populations in the primary analysis. The pre-specified randomization scheme assigns 125 participants to active therapy and a variable number to placebo control. For participants randomized in P2P, follow up continues for a minimum of 96 weeks and a maximum of 144 weeks, with the last randomized participant followed for 96 weeks. A regimen's primary analysis triggers 96 weeks after the last regimen participant is randomized. More details on the study design can be found in the companion manuscript by Schneider et al.

The P2P visit schedule encompasses the PPMI visit schedule. This longitudinal alignment enables natural incorporation of parallel observational data from participants in PPMI who are eligible but unwilling or unable to consent to enrollment in the P2P randomized trial. PPMI data collected on non-randomized PPMI participants supplements the randomized data collected on control participants in the trial to enable improved estimation of the natural progression of untreated participants during analysis. The statistical modeling framework discounts data from the non-randomized P2P-eligible participants in the PPMI Clinical cohort to account for systematic differences of these PPMI participants compared to the randomized placebo control group in P2P.

Primary analysis population

The primary analysis population for a regimen includes an active treatment group and a collective control group (Table 1). When conducting a regimen's primary analysis, the regimen of interest is referred to as the "analysis regimen." The collective control group comprises intra-analysis regimen matched placebo controls (concurrent), extra-analysis regimen placebo controls (concurrent and nonconcurrent), and nonrandomized controls (NRC) from P2P-eligible participants in the PPMI Clinical cohort. Appropriate considerations are given to the composition of these control groups over time. A sensitivity analysis to the primary analysis population will be conducted restricting to concurrently randomized placebo control participants only.

Handling control populations

The Bayesian modeling framework naturally enables borrowing strategies in the statistical design of the P2P platform trial. 29, 31 A dynamic borrowing strategy downweighs all extraanalysis regimen control data in a flexible way, borrowing from extra-regimen sources more heavily when the control data closely resembles the analysis regimen's and with lower weight when the data are dissimilar. The collective control comprises distinct groups when considering enrollment status by regimen within three overarching categories (Table 1).

1. Randomized Intra-Analysis Regimen Placebo Control Participants

Data is used with full weight in the primary analysis models. Placebo control participants are enrolled and randomized in the analysis regimen.

2. Randomized Extra-Analysis Regimen Placebo Control Participants

Control participants from extra-analysis regimens are expected to have similar NSD progression to those in the analysis regimen, although each primary analysis model allows for deviations from this expectation. For example, if an extra-analysis regimen has a different mode of administration (e.g. injection vs oral) than the analysis regimen, it is possible that placebo effects may differ. We expect potential differences to be quite small in the biomarker endpoint, which is an objective measure expected to be robust to placebo effects. Accurate characterization of the natural progression of disease has important ramifications for modeling and trial design, and, subsequently, for trial operating characteristics such as power and Type I error. We analyzed data from the PPMI Clinical cohort to characterize important biomarker and clinical features of NSD Stage 2B progression (See Supplemental Material). 32 Estimates from these models (Supplemental Table S1) were used to support simulations that inform clinical trial design and allow characterization of the trial's operating characteristics.

Clinical trial simulation

Clinical trial simulation is a powerful trial design tool used extensively in the development of the P2P Master Protocol. Accurate characterization of untreated disease progression based on data from the PPMI Clinical cohort provides robust estimates of endpoint trajectories and associated variability that are used to underpin trial simulation assumptions. Simulation assumptions for the primary endpoints as well as operational trial features such as accrual rate and dropout rate were evaluated by clinical and operational experts. The interplay of clinical, statistical, and operational expertise was critical to designing P2P, as the early-stage NSD population is only now being systematically characterized. 33 Natural history study estimates serve as the basis for virtual participant simulation. A participant simulator creates realistic endpoint trajectories for DAT bilateral putamen SBR and MDS-UPDRS Part III outcomes for virtual P2P participants. Simulated control participant trajectories are centered around the estimates provided in Supplemental Table S1. Imposing a treatment effect in the simulation model then enables simulation of data for active treatment participants. As in the real trial, virtual participants are randomized to a regimen and then subsequently assigned to active treatment or matched placebo control.

Primary endpoints for each virtual participant are simulated across time conditional on their regimen treatment assignment.

Covariance structures across time and endpoints were informed by analyses of the PPMI Clinical cohort data (Supplemental Table S2). Simulated participant data was generated by sampling multivariate standard normal outcomes for each participant for both longitudinal endpoints incorporating correlations within and between endpoints across time. Then, the standard normal deviates were transformed to the data scale by multiplying by the assumed standard deviation and then adding the assumed mean.

Simulated participant recruitment is assumed to be fast in the first year (300 participants per year), reflecting the P2P-ready participants already enrolled in the PPMI Clinical cohort at the outset of the P2P trial. In subsequent years, recruitment will rely on newly enrolled PPMI participants resulting in a slower rate (assumed to be 175 participants per year). Here, we assume regimen entry times of year 0, 0.5, 1, 1.8 years for regimens one through four, respectively.

Thousands of simulated trials were conducted with variations in sample size, untreated progression rate, data variability, regimen entry times, participant enrollment patterns, follow-up duration, randomization scheme, shared controls, and analysis strategies. Operating characteristics, averaged over many simulations, enabled the quantification and assessment of design performance and were used to iterate on design features before selecting a final design. Discussion

P2P is the first platform clinical trial designed to test disease modifying therapeutic candidates in a biologically defined NSD Stage 2B population that precedes clinical diagnosis. This biologically defined NSD population reduces the biological heterogeneity of the population to more precisely target disease and more effectively power a clinical study.

Lack of prior clinical trial data on such a population provides a challenge for a priori considerations of endpoint selection and corresponding power calculations. However, the available data and established infrastructure of the longstanding PPMI observational study addresses some of these challenges.

It is difficult to model progression in an early disease population, because there are few measurable changes that capture the disease process at that stage. PPMI data provides the largest and most robust dataset currently available to characterize the NSD population. We investigated clinical and biomarker data in biologically defined Stage 2B NSD to select endpoints with early sensitivity for phenoconversion. The data-informed recommendations for the P2P primary endpoints are based on substantial efforts to understand early and detectable changes in validated outcome assessments. Ongoing data collection and modeling efforts will continue to provide an iterative learning approach and will inform the final prespecified analysis. The MDS-UPDRS Part III endpoint was selected for statistical and clinical relevance as an early measure of manifesting disease. Selection of this clinical endpoint focuses on the motor domain but neglects non-motor diagnosis domains.

Statistically, the rationale for this endpoint was based on higher sensitivity to phenoconversion and a higher signal to noise ratio. Multiple measures of non-motor dysfunction and disability are included as exploratory measures in the P2P study, and data analysis will inform which of them have appropriate performance characteristics for future studies.

The design of the P2P platform trial provides several statistical efficiencies that benefit trial participants while maintaining scientific rigor. Within the platform setting, the shared placebo control group and K:1 randomization scheme reduces the number of participants assigned to placebo control while maintaining a 1:1 allocation ratio of active treatment to shared concurrent placebo controls to ensure well-powered analyses. The longitudinal primary analysis method leverages all available participant data, which increases power relative to other common methods such as a change from baseline analysis. The Bayesian primary analysis method facilitates dynamic borrowing of the different control groups comprising the collective control (including non-randomized control data from the PPMI study), which increases the effective sample size of the study. Bayesian modeling provides an intuitive, flexible framework that both handles regimen differences and elegantly borrows from the non-randomized cohort.

PPMI provided simulation and design-enabling longitudinal data that greatly improves the accuracy and precision of assumptions made when powering and sizing regimens for evaluation in P2P. The trial design process was highly iterative and allowed the P2P design team to fine tune design features, including the dynamic borrowing framework that underpins the handling of regimen and non-randomized cohort data in the primary analysis. Additional decisions related to understanding accrual assumptions, accumulation of trial data over time, and timing of regimens entering and exiting the platform. Clinical trial success often hinges not only on therapeutic efficacy but also on realistic assumptions about participant outcomes to ensure the detection of an effective treatment.

As an embedded trial, P2P gains statistical efficiencies from non-randomized control data.

Statistical methods are well-established and have been used to borrow from historical and external control data, including in platform trials. 34 Beyond methodological arguments, regulatory guidance has been issued on considerations for the use of external controls in clinical trials. 35 With the infrastructure provided by PPMI, we expected the borrowed control data in P2P to have substantial similarity to the randomized control data, providing a compelling use case for borrowing. 36 In difficult to enroll populations, this is an innovative and important supplement to randomized trial data. The PPMI observational study also provides a robust operational framework. Embedding P2P within PPMI provides operational efficiencies related to accrual and data collection, in addition to the discussed statistical efficiencies.

Despite the wealth of natural history data used to inform the P2P design, some limitations do exist. The trial population could progress at different than expected rates, and assumptions on accrual and dropout may deviate from those considered during trial design. For example, accrual is expected to be quick for participants already enrolled in the PPMI observational study at the start of P2P, followed by a slower enrollment rate after the initial bolus. Trial operating characteristics were calculated via simulation across a range of possibilities to mitigate these risks.

P2P is a learning platform that aims to assess potential disease-modifying therapies and increase knowledge across several trial dimensions. P2P is a testing ground for embedded trials and the advantages that we may gain from non-randomized cohort data. We expect to gain rich outcome data on biomarkers, clinical outcomes, including digital-health technologies. Additionally, we will learn about similarities and differences between natural history cohort accrual and retention compared to that in a randomized setting.
