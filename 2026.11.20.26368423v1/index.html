Reporting p values imprecisely (e.g., p < .05) reduces transparency, reproducibility, and re-use (e.g., in p value meta-analyses). Best practice is to report exact p-values with three decimal places (e.g., p = .032) unless p values are smaller than 0.001, in which case you can use p < .001.

To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 2 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Metacheck does not yet analyze automatically whether sentences which include non-significant p-values are correct, but we recommend manually checking the sentences below for possible misinterpreted non-significant p values.

P = 0.82 The groups did not differ in age (P = 0.82) or gender (P = 0.57).

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

How Effect Sizes in t-tests and F-tests works

The Effect Size module checks if effect sizes are correctly reported in t-tests and F-tests.

The Effect Size check searches for regular expressions that match typical ways in which effect sizes are reported. This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Hemiplegic cerebral palsy results from a unilateral prenatal or perinatal brain injury and causes motor impairments in the contralateral side of the body. Variable functional outcomes among children with hemiplegic cerebral palsy despite similar lesion extent suggest that developmental neuroplasticity and brain reorganization contribute to motor function. By using multimodal neuroimaging, we characterize the structural and functional reorganization of the motor system and examine its associations with lesion characteristics and upper-limb performance.

We examined 26 children with hemiplegic cerebral palsy and 26 typically developing controls using electromagnetic source imaging to map the primary motor cortex and characterize movement-related oscillations across physiologically relevant frequency bands, navigated transcranial magnetic stimulation to assess motor cortical excitability and lateralization, and functionally guided diffusion-weighted MRI to evaluate the structural integrity of descending motor and motor commissural fibers. We further assessed upper-limb function using Melbourne Assessment 2 and examined multimodal relationships using correlation, cross-validated latentvariable, and mediation analyses.

Children with hemiplegic cerebral palsy showed microstructural abnormalities in the descending motor and motor commissural fibers, characterized by lower fractional anisotropy and higher mean, axial, and radial diffusivity (all P < 0.05). The navigated transcranial magnetic stimulation identified ipsilateral corticospinal projections from the less affected hemisphere in 17 of 22 children (77%) with hemiplegic cerebral palsy, accompanied by reduced contralesional primary motor cortex lateralization (P < 0.05). The hemiplegic cerebral palsy group also exhibited hemisphere-specific abnormalities in movement-related oscillations, including reduced theta synchronization in the contralesional hemisphere and attenuated mu-and beta-band desynchronization in the ipsilesional hemisphere (all P < 0.05). Larger lesions were associated with poorer motor performance (Rho = -0.74 to -0.57, P < 0.05). Reduced descending motor fiber integrity was associated with impaired dexterity and attenuated mu-and beta-band desynchronization (Rho = -0.51 to 0.62, P < 0.05), while motor commissural fiber integrity was associated with corticospinal motor lateralization (Rho = -0.45 to 0.41, P < 0.05). A crossvalidated multimodal structural-functional model predicted motor function (Q² = 0.42, P ≤ 0.001) more accurately than structural measures alone (Q² = 0.25). Beta-band desynchronization mediated the association between descending motor fiber integrity and motor performance (indirect effect = 0.12 SD, 95% CI 0.03-0.24).

Developmental motor neuroplasticity in hemiplegic cerebral palsy reflects an integrated structure-physiology-behavior process rather than the direct consequence of lesion burden alone.

Multimodal biomarkers may improve prognostication and support individualized rehabilitation and neuromodulation.

Introduction

Hemiplegic cerebral palsy (HCP) is a common subtype of cerebral palsy caused by unilateral perinatal injury that produces contralateral motor impairments. [1] [2] [3] [4] [5] [6] Affected children often experience spasticity, weakness, impaired motor control, and reduced manual dexterity, limiting independence and quality of life. 2, 3, 6 Lesion characteristics influence the severity of these impairments, yet motor outcomes vary widely among children with comparable injuries.

Function depends not only on the primary lesion but also on developmental neuroplasticity, the brain's capacity to reorganize after early injury. [6] [7] [8] [9] [10] [11] The mechanisms of this reorganization remain poorly understood, limiting rehabilitation strategies tailored to individual neuroplastic potential.

This gap motivates identifying biomarkers of patient-specific motor reorganization. 7, 12, 13 The developing brain can undergo substantial structural and functional adaptations after brain injury, including remodeling of corticospinal projections, cortical representations, and interhemispheric connections. [7] [8] [9] [10] Such adaptations may preserve motor function through alternative pathways, but they can also produce atypical network reorganization, such as mirror movements that impair movement selectivity and coordination. 14, 15 Characterizing the neural mechanisms of voluntary and stimulation-evoked motor control is essential to distinguish adaptive from maladaptive neuroplasticity in HCP and to guide personalized rehabilitation.

Structural MRI has clarified the anatomical basis of motor dysfunction in HCP, particularly the distinct consequences of periventricular (PV) and cortical-subcortical (CSC) lesions. Arising at different prenatal developmental stages, these lesions are associated with distinct patterns of structural damage, corticospinal reorganization, and motor impairment. 16, 17 PV lesions precede the corticospinal pathway maturation and therefore often leave greater capacity for adaptive reorganization, 18, 19 whereas CSC lesions more often disrupt established motor networks and entail greater upper-extremity dysfunction. [5] [6] [7] [8] [9] [10] 16, 20 Conventional MRI defines lesion type and extent without assessing motor-pathway integrity. Diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) addresses this limitation and has revealed microstructural abnormalities in descending motor fibers (DMF) and motor commissural fibers (MCF) within the more affected (MA) hemisphere of children with HCP. [21] [22] [23] Reduced DMF integrity is associated with poorer motor execution, dexterity, and functional outcomes, 20, 21, 24 whereas MCF abnormalities, which reflect disrupted interhemispheric communication through the corpus callosum, relate to impaired bimanual coordination, reduced motor selectivity, and poorer rehabilitation response. 21, [24] [25] [26] Motor dysfunction in HCP thus reflects disruption across distributed pathways rather than injury confined to the primary lesion.

Structural imaging defines the anatomical substrate of motor dysfunction but cannot reveal how injury alters motor-system function. Functional neuroimaging, including magnetoencephalography (MEG) and high-density electroencephalography (HD-EEG), fills this gap by noninvasively measuring cortical movement-related responses (MRR). 27, 28 In cerebral palsy, these techniques have revealed altered sensorimotor oscillations and atypical cortical activation patterns. [29] [30] [31] [32] [33] [34] Disrupted transcallosal inhibition may compound these changes by promoting excessive bilateral cortical activation during unilateral movement, further reducing motor selectivity. 21, 24, 25 Functional reorganization therefore extends beyond the primary lesion to distributed cortical networks, although its contribution to voluntary upper-extremity motor control remains poorly characterized.

While MEG and HD-EEG capture motor-network activity during voluntary movement, navigated transcranial magnetic stimulation (nTMS) probes corticospinal organization and cortical excitability directly. [35] [36] [37] By eliciting motor-evoked potentials (MEPs) through focal stimulation of the primary motor cortex (M1), nTMS maps corticospinal projections and hemispheric motor organization. 38 Excitability threshold is elevated in the ipsilesional motor cortex of children with HCP, 22 and contralesional M1 stimulation can elicit MEPs in the affected limb, indicating the persistence of ipsilateral corticospinal projections. [39] [40] [41] This pattern supports the hypothesis that early unilateral injury disrupts developmental pruning of bilateral corticospinal connections. These findings indicate that motor impairment arises from interactions among cortical activation, corticospinal organization, and interhemispheric communication.

Behavioral, structural, and functional measures inform neuroplasticity in HCP but are rarely integrated, leaving knowledge fragmented across interdependent levels. Here, we employ multimodal neuroimaging to characterize motor-system reorganization in children with HCP and to relate structural and functional markers of neuroplasticity to lesion characteristics and upperextremity motor performance. We hypothesize that early unilateral brain injury initiates a cascade of structural and physiological adaptations, in which disruption of descending and commissural motor pathways constrains corticospinal organization and reshapes system-wide cortical motor activity, ultimately shaping motor outcome. We combined simultaneous MEG and HD-EEG recordings during a visuomotor task, nTMS assessment of corticospinal excitability and hemispheric organization, and diffusion tractography seeded from functionally localized motor cortex to quantify DMF and MCF microstructure. These measures were then related to standardized upper-extremity assessments using correlation, latent-variable, and mediation analyses.

Materials and Methods

Participants

We recruited 26 children with HCP (17 male; 10.8 ± 3.0 years) from the cerebral palsy clinic at Cook Children's Medical Center, Fort Worth, Texas, and 26 typically developing (TD) children (14 male; 11.0 ± 3.1 years) from the local community. The groups did not differ in age (P = 0.82) or gender (P = 0.57).

Children with HCP were eligible if they had: (1) diagnosis confirmed by a pediatric neurologist, developmental specialist, or neonatologist; (2) Gross Motor Function Classification System level I or II; and (3) cognitive ability to follow age-appropriate instructions. TD children were screened to exclude neurological, developmental, or sensory disorders. Exclusion criteria for both groups were: (1) genetic syndromes; (2) prior brain surgery or major traumatic brain injury; (3) inability to remain still during recordings; and (4) contraindications to MEG or MRI.

The Cook Children's Health Care System Institutional Review Board approved all procedures (2019-068; PI: C. Papadelis). Written parental consent and child assent, as appropriate, were obtained from all participants.

Movement quality

Upper limb motor function was assessed using the Melbourne Assessment 2 (MA2; Fig. 1A). 42 Participants completed standardized unilateral upper-extremity tasks evaluating range of motion (ROM), accuracy, dexterity, and fluency. Assessments were video-recorded and independently scored offline by two raters (E.A. and R.C.) according to MA2 administration and scoring guidelines. Standardized scores were used in subsequent analyses.

MEG and HD-EEG acquisition

Simultaneous MEG and HD-EEG were recorded during a visuomotor task (Fig. 1B) in a singlelayer magnetically shielded room in the Radiology suite at Cook Children's Medical Center.

MEG was acquired using a whole-head 306-channel VectorView® system (Elekta Neuromag, Helsinki, Finland; 102 magnetometers and 204 planar gradiometers). HD-EEG was recorded using an EGI Geodesic system (Electrical Geodesics, Eugene, OR) with a 128-or 256-channel saline-based net selected according to head size. MEG and HD-EEG data were sampled at 1 kHz, and electrode impedances were maintained below 50 kΩ.

Electrode locations were digitized relative to the head using the GeoScan Sensor Digitization system (Magstim EGI, USA). Five head-position indicator coils were placed on the scalp for MEG head-position tracking. Before each recording, the nasion, bilateral preauricular points, and ~500 scalp-reference points were digitized with a Polhemus Fastrak system. Peripheral signals were also recorded including: (1) the bilateral surface electromyography (EMG) from the first dorsal interosseous muscles to identify motor-response onset (Fig. 1B); (2) vertical electrooculography (EOG) from bipolar electrodes placed above and below the right eye to identify ocular artifacts; and (3) electrocardiography (ECG) from electrodes placed over the upper chest to identify cardiac artifacts for subsequent removal during preprocessing.

MRI and diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) acquisition

Structural T1-weighted MRI and DWI were acquired on a 3T Siemens Skyra MRI scanner using a 10-channel head coil. T1-weighted images were obtained with a 3D magnetization-prepared rapid-acquisition gradient-echo (MPRAGE) sequence (echo time=3.22 ms, inversion time=450 ms, repetition time=8.21 ms, flip angle=15 degrees, field of view=24 cm, matrix size=256×256, 184 slices, and resolution=1.0×1.0×1.0 mm³). These images were used for cortical surface reconstruction and forward modeling for MEG/HD-EEG source estimation. DWI was acquired using a multi-shell diffusion protocol with b-values of 1,000 and 2,000 s/mm², with 30 and 60 diffusion-encoding directions, respectively. Images had 2.05 mm in-plane resolution and 2 mm slice thickness.

Radiological findings

A pediatric neuroradiologist (M.B.) and pediatric neurologist (W.M.) reviewed MRI scans to classify lesion type, laterality, and involvement of cortical gray matter, periventricular white matter, basal ganglia, thalamus, and corpus callosum. In children with HCP, the hemisphere with the most pronounced structural abnormality was designated the MA hemisphere and the contralateral hemisphere the less affected (LA) hemisphere (Fig. 1C). The MA hemisphere was contralateral to the paretic hand (P-hand) in all participants. Participant demographic and clinical characteristics are provided in Table 1.

For TD participants, the hemisphere contralateral to the dominant hand (D-hand) was designated the dominant hemisphere (D-hemi).

Lesion and ventricular dilation volume

Lesion and ventricular dilatation volumes were quantified from T1-weighted MRI. 5 Lesion volume included cortical gray-matter loss and ex-vacuo ventricular dilatation attributable to white-matter volume loss. To account for normal ventricular volume, we subtracted the mean ventricular volume of the TD group from the combined lesion and enlarged ventricular volume in the MA hemisphere.

Visuomotor task

Participants completed a Go/NoGo visuomotor task during simultaneous MEG and HD-EEG recording to elicit cortical activity related to movement preparation, execution, and response inhibition (Fig. 1B). Visual stimuli were presented centrally on a back-projection screen placed ~150 cm in front of the participant. Stimulus presentation and behavioral responses were controlled with Stim2 software (Compumedics Neuroscan, Charlotte, NC, USA) with event triggers synchronized to the MEG and HD-EEG systems for subsequent epoching and analysis.

Each trial began with a 500-ms fixation cross followed by a 1,200-ms dog or cat Go cue (Fig. 1B). Participants were instructed to tap the right index finger in response to the dog and left index finger in response to the cat. On pseudorandomly selected trials, a cartoon light bulb appeared 230-440 ms after cue onset (serving as the NoGo cue) signaling participants to withhold the planned response. Each run included 120 trials (60 dog and 60 cat cues), including 30 NoGo trials equally distributed across cue types. Participants completed four 8-minute runs.

Preprocessing of MEG/HD-EEG data

Raw MEG data were processed using MaxFilter (version 2.2.10, MEGIN, Finland) with temporal signal-space separation to suppress noise and correct for head movement. MEG and HD-EEG data were subsequently spatially aligned and temporally synchronized using an inhouse algorithm. 43 MEG and HD-EEG channels with excessive artifacts were identified by visual inspection and removed. Data were baseline-corrected, band-pass filtered (1-100 Hz), and notch-filtered at 60 Hz using Brainstorm. 44 MEG artifacts related to cardiac or ocular activity were identified from ECG and EOG recordings and removed using signal-space projection. 33 HD-EEG artifacts arising from cardiac activity, eye movements, blinks, and muscle activity were removed using independent component analysis. 34 For datasets with prominent muscle artifacts, components most strongly associated with contaminated channels were removed. Remaining noisy data segments and their corresponding trials were excluded after visual inspection.

Bilateral first dorsal interosseous EMG signals were band-pass filtered (30-200 Hz) and notch-filtered at 60, 120, and 180 Hz. EMG onset following Go cues was manually identified and used for epoching.

Electromagnetic source localization

Continuous MEG/HD-EEG data were segmented into 3,500 ms epochs time-locked to EMGdefined movement onset (1,500 prior and 2,000 ms after movement). Trials were averaged within participant to derive movement-related fields and potentials, and channels with excessive premovement activity were excluded (Fig 1D).

We estimated cortical source activity using dynamic Statistical Parametric Mapping (dSPM). 45, 46 Individual T1-weighted MRI scans were used to generate cortical, skull, and scalp surfaces. MEG/HD-EEG data were coregistered to each participant's anatomy using digitized head points, and forward models were computed with boundary element modeling (BEM) in OpenMEEG. 47 Noise covariance matrices were estimated and used to solve the inverse model across ~15,000 cortical vertices using combined gradiometer, magnetometer, and HD-EEG data, with dipole orientations normal to the cortical surface.

Primary motor cortex (M1) was identified as the peak movement-related activity within Brodmann area 4 at 120-150 ms 48 after movement onset. The peak and adjacent vertices were expanded to define an ~3 cm 2 M1 region of interest for diffusion tractography (Fig. 1E). In two participants with severe cortical lesions, movement-related peaks for the P-hand did not align with the anatomical atlas; therefore, the M1 region was defined from the peak activity in the MA hemisphere.

Time-frequency analysis of movement-induced response

Single-trial time series extracted from virtual M1 vertex were decomposed into time-frequency representations using complex Morlet wavelets (central frequency of 1 Hz and a cycle count of three cycles). To quantify non-phase-locked, movement-induced activity, time-frequency maps were averaged across trials for each participant. Event-related synchronization (ERS) and desynchronization (ERD) were calculated as power changes relative to the premovement baseline (-1,500 to -1,000 ms). 49 12

Spectrotemporal components of movement-induced responses

Time-frequency maps were examined across five movement-related spectrotemporal components implicated in motor initiation, execution, and inhibition: theta band synchronization (ERS theta ; 4-8 Hz), mu desynchronization (ERD mu ; 8-12 Hz), beta desynchronization (ERD beta ; 13-30 Hz), post-movement beta rebound (PMBR beta ; 16-30 Hz), and gamma synchronization or burst (ERS gamma ; 30-80 Hz). For each participant, baseline-normalized power was averaged within each time-frequency window and compared between groups.

Navigated Transcranial Magnetic Stimulation

Individual T1-weighted MRI scans were reconstructed into three-dimensional head models and coregistered to each participant for navigated TMS (nTMS; Nexstim NBS 5.1, Helsinki, Finland; Fig. 1F). Bilateral EMG was recorded from the abductor pollicis brevis (APB), with reference electrodes placed on the tendon and a ground electrode on the hand dorsum. Signals were sampled at 3 kHz (10-500 Hz bandwidth, 0.3μV resolution).

Single-pulse nTMS was delivered using a figure-of-eight coil and frameless stereotactic neuronavigation. The precentral gyrus was mapped in each hemisphere to identify the APB motor hotspot. Resting motor threshold (rMT) was defined as the minimum intensity that elicited MEP of at least 50 μV in 10 of 20 trials. Motor mapping was then performed at 110% rMT with the coil-oriented perpendicular to the cortical gyrus in a dorsal-rostral direction to optimize corticospinal tract activation. Both hemispheres were mapped while recording bilateral APB EMG activity.

For LI TMS , A c and A i represent MEP amplitudes in the contralateral and ipsilateral APB, respectively. 50 The LI TMS was calculated for each stimulation site and averaged within hemisphere for each participant (Fig. 1F). LIs were also calculated for movement-related spectrotemporal components. Because these measures may have positive or negative values, a modified LI formula was used as described in the Supplementary materials.

ROI-based tractography

DWI data were preprocessed and analyzed in DSI-Studio (version October 10, 2024).

Preprocessing included visual quality control, motion correction, and white-matter mask generation. Fiber tracking used generalized Q-sampling imaging, which resolves complex fiber configurations, including crossing fibers. 51 Tracking parameters included a 60° angular threshold, 0.5-1.5 voxels step sizes, and 30-200 mm tract lengths. Topology-informed pruning was applied over 16 iterations to remove false-positive correlations from an initial set of 5,000 tracts. 52 Functionally defined M1 regions were imported into DSI studio for subject-specific tractography (Fig. 1E). DMFs were reconstructed using M1 and the ventral brainstem corticospinal tract as regions of interest. MCF were identified by seeding from unilateral M1 with the corpus callosum (CC) as an additional region of interest; tracts were then restricted to the ipsilateral hemisphere. Fractional anisotropy (FA), mean diffusivity (MD), axial diffusivity (AD), and radial diffusivity (RD) were calculated for each reconstructed tract to characterize white-matter microstructural integrity. The same tractography procedure was performed using the nTMS-defined ROIs.

Statistical analysis

Within-group comparisons across hands and hemispheres used Wilcoxon signed rank tests. These analyses included MA2 outcomes; response time and inhibition accuracy; movement-related and TMS-evoked LI (LI MRR and LI TMS ); latency and resting-state motor threshold; and diffusion metrics (FA, MD, AD, and RD).

Between-group comparisons used Mann-Whitney U tests with false-discovery-rate correction. We compared the TD nondominant hand/hemisphere with the HCP paretic hand/more affected hemisphere and the TD dominant hand/hemisphere with the HCP nonparetic hand/less affected hemisphere. Statistical significance was set at 0.05. Effect sizes are reported as the absolute rank-biserial correlation (0-1).

Inter-modality correlation

Spearman correlations assessed associations among behavioral, functional, and structural measures, focusing on functional-behavioral, structural-behavioral, and structural-functional relationships. Analyses were conducted across all hands and hemispheres in both groups and separately within the P-hand/MA hemisphere of the HCP group. Statistical significance was set at 0.05. The analyses were performed in R® version 4.5.0.

Latent structural-functional integrity score

We used partial least-squares (PLS) regression to test whether a multivariate structural-functional integrity score predicted overall motor outcome. PLS was selected because diffusion metrics within each tract were highly collinear (correlations up to 0.9 among FA, MD, AD, and RD).

The model integrated diffusion metrics from DMF and MCF with movement-related MEG/EEG oscillatory measures and their LIs. Model performance was evaluated using leave-one-out crossvalidation, with the number of PLS components selected by nested cross-validation within each training fold. Statistical significance was assessed using 1,000 permutation iterations. Separate PLS models incorporating DMF or MCF measures alone were used to determine whether each tract contributed independent predictive information.

Mediation analysis

To test whether structural alterations influence motor function through cortical motor physiology, we examined whether ERD beta mediated the association between DMF FA/MD/AD/RD and the logit-transformed composite MA2 score. Sequential regression with 5,000 bootstrap resamples tested the hypothesized intermediate role of cortical physiology in the structure-physiologybehavior relationship. ERD beta was selected a priori because it is closely associated with movement-related sensorimotor cortical engagement and motor performance. 28, 53 PLS and mediation analyses were performed in Python® version 3.14.

Results

Participant characteristics and lesion distribution

Structural MRI showed predominantly unilateral lesions in children with HCP. Based on neuroradiological classification, seven participants had cortical-subcortical (CSC) lesions (6 male, 11.0 ± 3.2 years), 14 had periventricular (PV) lesions (10.5 ± 3.4 years), and five had other lesion types (11.0 ± 1.6 years). Detailed lesion characteristics are reported in Table 1.

Upper-extremity motor performance

Children with HCP exhibited poorer P-hand performance than TD controls across all MA2 domains (Fig. 2A): range of motion (P < 0.001, r = 0.69), accuracy (P < 0.01, r = 0.41), dexterity (P < 0.001, r = 0.79), and fluency (P < 0.001, r = 0.65). Deficits were more pronounced among children with CSC lesions, who showed poorer motor performance than those with PV lesions, particularly with dexterity (P < 0.001, r = 0.91) (Fig. 2B). In contrast, the non-paretic hand (NPhand) performance was largely preserved.

Response time and inhibition accuracy

During the visuomotor task, response times were longer for the P-hand than the NP-hand in children with HCP (P < 0.01, r = 0.67) and longer than for the TD nondominant hand (P < 0.001, r = 0.68; Fig. 3A). The HCP NP-hand was also slower than the TD dominant hand (P < 0.05, r = 0.39). Inhibition accuracy was higher (P < 0.05, r = 0.65) for the P-hand than the NP-hand (Fig. 3B).

Movement-related lateralization index

Movement-related cortical activity indicated altered motor-cortical lateralization in HCP (Fig. 3C). During P-hand movement, the movement-related response lateralization index was lower than the TD group (P < 0.01, r = 0.50) and the HCP NP-hand (P < 0.05, r = 0.62), consistent with increased ipsilateral and/or reduced contralateral M1 activity.

Time-frequency analysis demonstrated altered motor-related oscillatory dynamics in children with HCP (Fig. 4). During NP-hand movement, ERS theta was reduced compared to controls (P < 0.05, Fig. 4A). Motor-pathway microstructure

Diffusion tractography demonstrated reduced DMF microstructural integrity in the MA hemisphere (Fig. 6A). Relative to the TD group and the LA hemisphere of the HCP group, the DMF of the MA hemisphere showed lower FA (P < 0.01, r > 0.71) and higher MD (P < 0.001, r > 0.85), AD (P < 0.01, r > 0.50), and RD (P < 0.001, r > 0.79). Within the MA hemisphere, the CSC group had higher AD than the PV group (P < 0.05, r = 0.83; Supplementary Fig. 3). MCF showed a similar pattern (Fig. 6B): the MA hemisphere had lower FA (P < 0.01, r > 0.67) and higher MD (P < 0.001, r > 0.76), AD (P < 0.001, r > 0.60), and RD (P < 0.001, r > 0.77) than the TD group and the LA hemisphere of the HCP group. MCF integrity did not differ between CSC and PV groups. However, within the PV group, MA vs. LA differences were observed for the MD, AD, and RD of the MCF (P < 0.05, r > 0.79; Supplementary Fig. 3).

Intermodality associations

Larger lesion/ventricular dilation volume was associated with higher rMT (Rho = 0.77, P < 0.05) and poorer range of motion, accuracy, and dexterity (-0.74 < Rho < -0.57, P < 0.05) (Table 2). Reduced contralateral ERD beta was associated with poorer P-hand movement accuracy (Rho = -0.67, P < 0.05).

A cross-validated partial least-squares structural-functional integrity score significantly predicted motor function (Q² = 0.42, P ≤ 0.001; Supplementary Table 1). DMF and MCF integrity also predicted motor performance (Q² = 0.25 and 0.19, P < 0.01) and were moderately correlated (r = 0.63, P < 0.01). The full structural-functional model outperformed the structural model.

Discussion

Using multimodal neuroimaging, we show that developmental motor neuroplasticity in children with HCP is a coordinated structure-physiology-behavior process rather than a direct consequence of structural injury alone. Early unilateral injury disrupts descending and commissural motor pathways, altering corticospinal organization, redistributing cortical motor recruitment, and impairing upper-extremity function. This reorganization is not confined to the MA hemisphere: each hemisphere shows distinct alterations in movement-related oscillatory dynamics, indicating that early injury reshapes the bilateral motor network. Mediation analysis further ordered these levels hierarchically, with structural remodeling constraining corticospinal physiology and thereby shaping cortical recruitment and motor behavior. These findings provide a mechanistic framework for understanding individual variability in motor outcomes and support rehabilitation and neuromodulation strategies accounting for structural constraints and patientspecific bilateral motor-network reorganization.

Structural remodeling establishes the substrate for developmental motor plasticity

Structural remodeling in HCP was evident at macroscopic and microstructural levels.

Macroscopically, lesion type was associated with distinct patterns of motor system organization. 8, 11 All children with PV lesions retained an anatomically preserved M1, 8 with movement-related source localization closely matching the Brodmann area 4 parcellation, despite DMFs and MCFs displacement around the enlarged ventricle. In contrast, CSC lesions directly involved the sensorimotor cortex in a subset of children, displacing functionally localized M1 and reorganizing descending motor pathways. Therefore, injury preceding corticospinal maturation appears to spare the representation while deforming the pathways around it, whereas later injury involving cortex relocates the representation itself and requires reconstruction of descending and interhemispheric tracts which may fail in some children with larger CSC lesions. 8, 11 Seeding tractography from functionally localized M1 rather than atlas-defined landmarks identified lesion-specific patterns. Conventional tractography reconstructs the DMFs from anatomically defined cortical and subcortical regions of interest and therefore assumes preserved cortical organization, 20, 21 an assumption that may not hold in HCP, where motor representations can be displaced after early injury. 5, 8, 54 TMS-defined M1 offers an alternative cortical seed, but reliable motor mapping is often difficult in children with extensive cortical lesions, because direct M1 involvement and elevated rMT can preclude a reproducible motor hotspot. 55 Seeding from M1 localized with electromagnetic source imaging instead allowed us to reconstruct the descending and commissural pathways engaged during voluntary upper-extremity movement. This strategy characterizes physiologically relevant pathway reorganization. 5, 29 Children with HCP also exhibited microstructural abnormalities in MA DMF and MCF, indicating effects on corticospinal output and interhemispheric connectivity. Reduced FA and increased MD, AD, and RD are compatible with disrupted fiber organization and tissue integrity, potentially reflecting axonal loss, gliosis, cystic degeneration, altered myelination, or cerebral edema. 21, Rather than attributing individual diffusion metrics to specific histopathology, we interpret them as convergent evidence of altered microstructural organization. These findings extend prior DWI studies 21, 24, 25 localizing such abnormalities to pathways that support voluntary movement. DMF abnormalities were present in the MA hemisphere of both lesion groups but were most pronounced after CSC injury, consistent with damage occurring at a more advanced corticospinal development stage. 5, 22 MCF metrics showed no overall lesion-type difference, although children with PV lesions demonstrated hemispheric asymmetry in diffusivity measures. Timing of injury may therefore influence descending and commissural pathways differently: earlier injury may permit asymmetric transcallosal connections development, whereas later cortical injury may compromise both corticospinal and transcallosal architecture more severely.

Cortical recruitment reflects bilateral motor-system reorganization

Movement-related cortical activity showed that developmental plasticity extends beyond the injured hemisphere. During P-hand movement, children with HCP showed reduced movementrelated lateralization (Fig. 3C), consistent with bilateral network recruitment. Unlike post-stroke bilateral activation in adults, this organization likely emerges during corticospinal maturation and becomes integrated into the developing motor system. 57 Abnormalities were also evident during NP-hand movement. Despite relatively preserved motor performance, children with HCP showed slower responses (Fig. 3A), reduced theta synchronization (Fig. 4A) and greater beta lateralization (Supplementary Fig. 2). The LA hemisphere is therefore not functionally typical; it appears to participate in a reorganized bilateral system supporting motor planning and execution of both upper extremities. 6 Oscillatory abnormalities further revealed frequency-and hemisphere-specific alterations in motor-system dynamics. Movement-related ERD mu and ERD beta are well-established electrophysiological signatures of sensorimotor cortical engagement during movement preparation and execution, reflecting ongoing rhythmic activity suppression within recruited cortical networks. 28, 53 In children with cerebral palsy, alterations in these oscillations have been linked to atypical motor planning, movement execution, and lower-extremity performance. 34, 58 Here, children with HCP showed attenuated ERD mu and ERD beta in the ipsilesional hemisphere during P-hand movement (Fig. 4B), and these alterations tracked reduced DMF integrity (Table 2). Thus, descending motor-pathway disruption accompanies altered modulation of ipsilesional sensorimotor cortical activity during voluntary movement. Reduced contralateral ERD beta was associated with poorer movement accuracy.

In contrast, ERS theta was selectively reduced during NP-hand movement in the contralesional hemisphere (Fig. 4A) and was unrelated to DMF or MCF integrity and to motor performance. Unlike mu and beta rhythms, which track movement-related sensorimotor activation, theta activity is implicated in motor planning and in integrating sensory information, such as visual feedback, with intended actions. 59, 60 Its selective alteration may therefore reflect a component of bilateral motor-network reorganization that is not directly determined by corticospinal structural integrity. Developmental motor reorganization after early unilateral brain injury thus involves frequency-specific changes across the bilateral motor network, spanning movement preparation, sensorimotor engagement, and execution. 61 Increased beta lateralization during NP-hand movement suggests greater reliance on the LA hemisphere for descending motor output, whereas reduced gamma lateralization during Phand movement indicates less contralaterally restricted execution-related processing (Supplementary Fig. 2). 62, 63 Gamma peak latency and frequency showed similar trends without reaching significance, likely reflecting substantial interindividual variability. 64 Slower response times for both hands suggest that bilateral reorganization affects movement preparation and initiation. 28 Higher inhibition accuracy during P-hand trials (Fig. 3B) may reflect slower motor preparation rather than superior inhibitory control, consistent with a speed-accuracy trade-off during developmental motor reorganization.

Corticospinal physiology links structural integrity to motor function

nTMS revealed physiological reorganization of corticospinal motor output after early unilateral injury. Most children with HCP retained ipsilateral corticospinal projections from the LA hemisphere (Fig. 5C), consistent with altered developmental pruning. 7, 8 The MA hemisphere showed higher rMT (Fig. 5B), indicating reduced corticospinal excitability, while ipsilateral MEPs showed prolonged latencies (Fig. 5A), suggesting less efficient conduction through reorganized descending pathways.

Corticospinal physiology tracked both lesion burden and motor function (Table 2). Larger lesions were associated with higher rMT, and reduced corticospinal lateralization correlated with poorer performance across all movement-quality domains. Corticospinal lateralization also correlated with MCF integrity, indicating that efficient descending motor output depends on both corticospinal preservation and interhemispheric connectivity. Commissural-pathway disruption therefore may contribute to interhemispheric physiological imbalance and impaired coordinated motor output.

Although movement-related cortical responses and nTMS both showed reduced hemispheric lateralization, their association was only moderate, likely because nTMS assesses corticospinal output capacity whereas movement-related responses reflect distributed cortical network recruitment during voluntary movement. 65, 66 Corticospinal organization therefore constrains, but does not fully determine, voluntary cortical recruitment, leaving higher-order motor networks partially free to adapt during development.

Structural and microstructural alterations constrain functional outcome

Relationships between structural measures and motor performance reveal constraints imposed by early brain injury. Lesion extent was associated with upper-extremity motor function (Table 2), indicating that macroscopic structural disruption influences motor capacity. DMF integrity was also associated with dexterity, indicating that preserved descending pathways provide an anatomical substrate for upper-extremity control. Motor outcome is therefore shaped by both lesion extent and descending pathway integrity, reinforcing corticospinal integrity as a major upper-extremity function determinant. 13, 22, 67 Preserved white-matter architecture within the LA hemisphere accompanied extensive physiological reorganization, indicating that structural integrity provides the anatomical framework for motor control and partially shapes functional organization. 68, 69 Together, these findings suggest that the developing nervous system adapts its function within the injury-imposed structural constraints.

An integrated structure-physiology-behavior cascade explains motor dysfunction

Although each modality identified motor-system abnormalities, this study demonstrates how these findings converge into a unified biological framework. Structural connectivity forms the anatomical level of this hierarchy, corticospinal physiology and cortical recruitment the successive functional levels, and motor behavior the resulting functional outcome. These levels appear interconnected within developmental motor reorganization rather than independent consequences of early brain injury. The correlation analyses, latent structural-functional model, and mediation analysis provide convergent evidence supporting this hierarchical organization.

Structural injury established the anatomical framework of the cascade. Larger lesion size and diminished DMF integrity were associated with poorer movement quality, and DMF microstructure correlated with ERD mu and ERD beta (Table 2). DMF integrity may therefore influence both performance and sensorimotor-cortical recruitment during voluntary movement. 70, 71 MCF integrity, in contrast, was more closely related to corticospinal lateralization (Table 2), supporting its role in interhemispheric physiological balance. 21, 72, 73 Corticospinal organizations link structural remodeling to cortical function. The association between movement-related and TMS-derived lateralization suggests that corticospinal organization constrains voluntary cortical recruitment while permitting higherorder cortical adaptation, 69 which may help explain why children with similar lesions show different cortical recruitment strategies and motor outcomes. 8, 68 Multivariate analyses reinforced this model. DMF and MCF integrity independently predicted motor performance, yet the combined structural-functional model predicted movement quality better than structural measures alone. Beta-band modulation also mediated the association between DMF integrity and overall motor performance, suggesting that white-matter disruption affects behavior partly through altered cortical recruitment during movement. Causal inference cannot be established in this cross-sectional design, but the results support this relationship as a biologically plausible mechanism.

Collectively, these findings support a model in which early unilateral injury disrupts descending and commissural pathways, prompting reorganization of corticospinal output and cortical motor recruitment, 8, 32, 34 that ultimately shapes upper-extremity performance bilaterally. 5, 16 Structural connectivity, corticospinal physiology, cortical activity, and behavior should therefore be viewed as complementary measures of a single developmental adaptive process.

Clinical implications

Our findings highlight a major gap between neuroimaging evidence on developmental plasticity and clinical care for children with HCP. Clinicians cannot reliably predict individual long-term rehabilitation approach. Lesion characteristics and corticospinal tract integrity are informative but capture only part of the motor function biology. Our results indicate that structural connectivity, corticospinal physiology, and cortical dynamics provide complementary information about motor performance: children with similar lesion profiles may differ in outcome because they differ in physiological adaptation and motor-network reorganization.

Multimodal assessment could identify individual neuroplastic profiles to inform prognostic counseling, rehabilitation selection, and treatment timing.

Our findings also have direct implications for development of therapeutic neuromodulation for people with early unilateral brain injury. Suppression of the LA hemisphere commonly assumes that contralesional recruitment is maladaptive. Yet, the LA hemisphere showed substantial physiological reorganization and often generated corticospinal output to both upper extremities (Fig. 5C). Although greater ipsilateral corticospinal contribution was associated with poorer upper-extremity motor performance, it may suggest that contralesional corticospinal recruitment may contribute to motor function rather than simply compete with the MA hemisphere. Interventions targeting interhemispheric balance should therefore be guided by each child's motor-network organization rather than lesion characteristics alone, and account for altered hemodynamic, metabolic, and electrophysiological activity. [74] [75] [76] [77] [78] Integrating diffusion MRI, nTMS, and functional neurophysiology could support patient stratification and individualized rehabilitation neuromodulation.

Limitations and future directions

This cross-sectional design cannot establish the developmental trajectory of, or causal relationships among, structural connectivity, corticospinal physiology, cortical recruitment, and motor function. Longitudinal studies are needed to determine how these biomarkers evolve with development and respond to rehabilitation. We did not directly assess interhemispheric inhibition or effective connectivity, which may influence motor reorganization; combining diffusion MRI with paired-pulse TMS, TMS-EEG, and network-connectivity analyses could better define how structural remodeling shapes bilateral motor-network communication. Some physiological findings require replication. Theta synchronization was largely unrelated to structural injury and motor performance (Fig. 4), suggesting that it may reflect higher-order motor planning not captured by conventional clinical assessments. Gamma oscillations showed altered lateralization with non-significant trends in peak latency and frequency (Supplementary Fig. 2), but substantial inter-individual variability limited statistical power. Adding quantitative mirror-movement measures, bimanual coordination, and motor learning may clarify the functional significance of these oscillatory biomarkers.

Conclusion

Developmental motor neuroplasticity in HCP is best understood as a coordinated structurephysiology-behavior process. Early unilateral injury disrupts descending and commissural motor pathways, constraining corticospinal organization and cortical recruitment during voluntary movement. The developing brain then redistributes motor processing across a bilateral network, partially preserving upper extremity motor function. By integrating diffusion MRI, movementrelated cortical activity, oscillatory dynamics, nTMS, and standardized motor assessments in the same cohort, this study identifies complementary multimodal candidate biomarkers that capture distinct motor-system reorganization levels and link structural injury to motor function. This multimodal approach moves beyond lesion burden alone by providing an individualized profile of structural connectivity, corticospinal organization, and cortical recruitment. Such profiles could improve prognostication, identify neuroplastic patterns relevant to treatment selection, and guide more targeted rehabilitation and neuromodulation strategies in children with HCP.
