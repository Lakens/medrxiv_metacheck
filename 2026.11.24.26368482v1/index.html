How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. Abstract

Background: Persistent facial erythema and episodic flushing are prominent features of erythematotelangiectatic rosacea (ETR). The efficacy of auricular acupuncture (AA) for ETR is uncertain. Methods: This multicenter, randomized, participant-and assessor-masked, shamcontrolled superiority trial was designed to enroll 108 adults aged 18 to 50 years with ETR and a baseline Clinician Erythema Assessment (CEA) score of at least 2 at 5 hospitals in China. Participants were allocated 1:1 to AA with semipermanent intradermal needles or visually matched, same-site nonpenetrating sham stimulation. Four auricular points (Shenmen, Sanjiao, Endocrine, and Heart) were treated on alternating ears every 3 days for 6 weeks, with self-pressure 3 times daily. Follow-up was scheduled through week 10. The primary outcome was clinician-rated erythema (CEA score) at week 6.

Secondary outcomes comprised other clinician-rated erythema assessments, patient-rated redness, flushing, migraine symptoms, sleep quality, depressive and anxiety symptoms, and adverse events. The primary treatment contrast was planned using baseline-adjusted analysis of covariance with multiple imputation for missing week 6 outcomes. Secondary longitudinal outcomes were planned for exploratory mixed-model analyses.

Discussion: The trial was designed to assess the incremental efficacy and tolerability of penetrating auricular needling compared with a matched nonpenetrating procedure.

Same-site pressure in both groups may have physiological effects, which should be considered when interpreting the results.

1.1 Background and Scientific Rationale

Rosacea is a chronic inflammatory disorder of the central face characterized by recurrent flushing, persistent erythema, telangiectasia, inflammatory lesions, and, in some patients, ocular or phymatous involvement. A systematic review estimated that rosacea affects 5.46% of adults worldwide 1 . ETR is distinguished by persistent centrofacial erythema with episodic intensification and visible telangiectasia, making facial redness the dominant clinical problem in this population.

The burden of ETR extends beyond visible skin changes. Rosacea is associated with impaired quality of life and increased risks of depression, anxiety and sleep disturbance, and epidemiologic evidence also supports an association with migraine 2, 3 . Available treatments for persistent erythema include topical alpha-adrenergic agonists and vascular laser or light-based procedures. Systemic agents such as beta-blockers are used off label in selected patients. These approaches may improve individual features, but responses are variable, recurrence is common, and treatment may not address the neurogenic or psychosocial symptoms that accompany ETR 4 . A treatment that can improve facial erythema without adding sustained systemic drug exposure would therefore address an important clinical need.

Current models of rosacea pathophysiology implicate interacting neurovascular and immune processes rather than isolated vascular dilatation. Environmental and emotional triggers can activate cutaneous sensory pathways, promote release of vasoactive neuropeptides, and amplify innate immune signaling, thereby linking flushing, persistent erythema, and inflammation 5 . This neuroimmune framework provides a rationale for testing neuromodulatory interventions, while recognizing that clinical efficacy cannot be inferred from mechanistic plausibility alone.

AA uses needles placed at defined points on the auricular pavilion and has developed within both traditional East Asian and modern European auricular treatment systems 6, 7 . The external ear has heterogeneous sensory innervation that includes the auricular branch of the vagus nerve, particularly within the conchal region 8, 9 . Experimental studies show that vagal stimulation can attenuate proinflammatory cytokine responses, and preclinical auricular stimulation studies support engagement of vagally mediated anti-inflammatory pathways 10, 11 . These findings make auricular neuromodulation a biologically plausible approach for ETR, but they do not establish that needling the selected auricular points will improve rosacea. Randomized evidence in depression indicates that repeated semipermanent auricular needling can be delivered in outpatient settings, although local pain is common and may lead to treatment discontinuation 12 . At the time this protocol was developed, the evidence reviewed for the study did not include a randomized trial of AA for ETR. A rigorously masked, sham-controlled trial was therefore required to estimate its clinical effects and harms.

1.2 Rationale for the Comparator

A nonpenetrating sham applied at the same auricular points was selected to match the procedural and contextual components of AA while estimating the incremental effect of penetrating needling. Because auricular pressure may itself produce sensory stimulation, the sham should not be regarded as physiologically inert. Device preparation and application are specified in Section 4.7, and masking procedures are specified in Section 6.4.

1.3 Benefit-Risk Assessment

Participants assigned to AA may experience improvement in persistent erythema, flushing, or associated patient-reported symptoms, but individual benefit is uncertain. Participants in both groups may benefit indirectly from structured dermatologic assessment, repeated follow-up, and prompt identification of clinical deterioration or AEs. The study may also provide evidence on a low-cost, nonpharmacologic intervention that could be delivered in outpatient care if efficacy and acceptable tolerability are demonstrated.

Foreseeable intervention-related risks included localized pain or intolerance, adhesive allergy, bleeding, and auricular infection. Participants were required to attend repeated treatment visits, wear the devices for 3 days, press each point 3 times daily, and complete clinical and questionnaire assessments. Risk mitigation included sterile single-use active devices, skin disinfection, trained practitioners, standardized procedures, auricular inspection at treatment visits, and active adverse event monitoring. For sham treatment, practitioners disinfected the trimmed contact surface with povidone-iodine before application without repackaging.

Treatment was discontinued if an allergic reaction, infection, or other clinically important event made continuation inappropriate.

1.4 Objectives and Hypotheses

Primary objective. To determine whether AA reduces clinician-rated facial erythema at week 6 compared with SS in ETR, as measured by the CEA score.

Secondary objectives.

To assess between-group differences in CEA scores at weeks 2, 4, and 10 and in PSA, GFSS, VAS, PSQI, HAMD, and HAMA scores at weeks 2, 4, 6, and 10. Treatment safety and adverse events will also be evaluated throughout follow-up.

Primary hypothesis.

Participants assigned to AA will have a lower mean CEA score at week 6 than participants assigned to SS after adjustment for baseline CEA score.

Secondary hypotheses.

AA will be associated with more favorable trajectories of clinician-rated and participant-reported erythema and related symptoms through week 10. Safety outcomes will be summarized descriptively, without a hypothesis of equivalence or noninferiority. Secondary efficacy analyses will be exploratory because no multiplicity adjustment is planned.

4 Interventions and Comparator

4.1 Intervention Overview

Both randomized groups will receive a fixed unilateral intervention at 4 auricular points in the following order: Shenmen (TF4), Sanjiao (CO17), Neifenmi (Endocrine, CO18), and Xin (Heart, CO15). These standard codes identify the triangular fossa (TF) and concha (CO) according to GB/T 13734-2008 (Nomenclature and Location of Auricular Points). In the AA group, the intact 2.0-mm sterile press needles will penetrate the selected points. In the SS group, visually matched nonpenetrating devices will be applied at the same points. Point selection will not be individualized. Device construction is described in Section 4.7.

Both groups will receive the same skin preparation, number of devices, unilateral application, 3day retention period, alternating-ear regimen, 6-week treatment course, participant self-pressing instructions, contact with the intervention provider, and safety surveillance. No study intervention will be delivered during the post-treatment follow-up from week 6 through week 10.

The intervention and comparator are specified below in accordance with the STRICTA reporting framework 14.

4.2 AA Rationale

The intervention is a standardized form of traditional Chinese AA using semipermanent intradermal needles. The fixed prescription was selected before trial initiation through discussion by the research team, informed by traditional auricular theory, published auricular therapy literature, and the proposed neuroimmune and neurovascular mechanisms of ETR. No point will be added, omitted, or substituted according to an individual participant's symptom pattern.

The biomedical rationale is that the auricle has heterogeneous sensory innervation, including the auricular branch of the vagus nerve, and that auricular stimulation may engage autonomic and anti-inflammatory pathways. Contemporary randomized trials have also shown that standardized auricular needling with multiday retention and participant self-stimulation can be delivered reproducibly in outpatient settings, although the indications and point prescriptions differ from the present trial 12, 15.

4.3 Needling Details

Four insertions will be made at each treatment application on 1 ear. Practitioners will identify the fixed auricular points using anatomic landmarks according to GB/T 13734-2008. The application order is Shenmen (TF4), Sanjiao (CO17), Neifenmi (Endocrine, CO18), and Xin (Heart, CO15). Shenmen is located in the upper posterior triangular fossa. Sanjiao is posteroinferior to the external acoustic meatus, between the Lung and Endocrine regions. Endocrine is located within the intertragic notch at the floor of the cavum conchae, and Heart is in the central depression of the cavum conchae. The same points and sequence will be used in both groups at every application (Figure 1). Participants will remain seated during device placement. The auricular skin will be cleaned with a moist swab, disinfected with Aner iodine antiseptic, and dried. For AA, a JIAJIAN sterile press needle (SPN; 0.22-mm diameter and 2.0-mm needle length; Wuxi Jiajian Medical Instrument Co, Ltd) will be inserted approximately perpendicular to the auricular surface to a depth of 2.0 mm and secured with its integral adhesive. According to the manufacturer, the single-use, ethylene-oxide-sterilized device contains 06Cr19Ni10 austenitic stainless steel and carries Jiangsu medical device registration certificate 20152200876 (Su Xie Zhu Zhun). No electrical or instrument-assisted stimulation will be used. The devices are illustrated in Figure 2. Immediately after placement, the acupuncturist will manually press each device to an intensity tolerated by the participant. The response sought is a de qi-like sensation, which may include soreness, numbness, heaviness, distension, or pain, together with visible auricular erythema and subjective warmth. Each device will remain in place for 3 days. Device diameter, length, lot information, treated ear, points used, and any procedural deviation will be recorded in the intervention record.

4.4 Treatment Regimen

Treatment will begin after randomization and will continue for 6 weeks. All participants will receive the first application on the left ear. A new set of 4 devices will be applied every 3 days, for 14 planned applications: odd-numbered applications on the left ear and even-numbered applications on the right ear. At each return visit, the acupuncturist will remove the devices retained from the preceding 3-day period before applying the next set to the contralateral ear.

The number of applications, device retention, point prescription, and ear alternation will be identical in both groups. Dates of application and removal and the treated ear will be recorded. Missed or incomplete applications will be documented as protocol deviations. The intervention will not be extended beyond the prespecified 6 weeks to replace missed applications.

4.5 Other Components, Setting, and Participant Instructions

Applications will be performed in designated outpatient treatment areas by qualified acupuncture practitioners. Clinical outcome assessments will be conducted separately by masked dermatologists. Participants will not be shown the device or application process. The procedural sequence, neutral explanation, application time, practitioner contact, and safety questions will be matched between groups.

Participants in both groups will be verbally instructed to press each of the 4 applied points 3 times daily, once in the morning, once at midday, and once in the evening. Each point will be pressed until the auricle becomes flushed and warm. Pressure will approximate the intensity experienced during the practitioner's initial pressing and remain within the participant's tolerance. The same verbal instructions will be used in both groups.

Participants will be instructed to keep the adhesive-covered points clean and as dry as possible during bathing and hair washing. If an adhesive becomes wet but remains attached, the participant should press it firmly back into place. Detached, contaminated, or damaged devices must not be reused or reinserted by participants. Participants will contact the study team for assessment, and any replacement by study personnel will be documented. Persistent pain, swelling, discharge, marked redness, or other local reactions should be reported promptly.

4.6 Practitioner Background and Standardization

Both AA and SS will be delivered by practitioners holding professional acupuncture qualifications and having 5 to 10 years of clinical acupuncture experience. All practitioners will follow the fixed-point prescription and procedures described in Sections 4.3 through 4.5. Application and treatment exposure will be documented as described in Section 4.4.

4.7 Comparator Rationale and Procedures

Sham units were prepared from the same commercial JIAJIAN SPN 0.22 × 2.0-mm product by cutting the protruding needle flush to form a flat contact surface that did not penetrate the skin. After trimming at the treatment site, the treating acupuncturist disinfected this surface with povidone-iodine and applied the device without repackaging. The commercial base, adhesive material, and outward appearance were unchanged. Four devices were applied using the same point sequence, treatment regimen, and participant instructions specified in Sections 4.3 through 4.5.

4.8 Criteria for Modifying or Discontinuing Allocated Intervention

The point prescription and device dimensions will not be individualized or adjusted. Pressure during placement and self-stimulation may be reduced to a tolerable level, but points and penetration depth will not be changed. Participants must not reinsert loose, damaged, or detached devices. Such events, and any replacement by study staff, will be documented.

The allocated intervention will be discontinued for intolerable pain, clinically important adhesive allergy, auricular infection, a serious or otherwise clinically important AE, clinical worsening or another condition that makes continued treatment unsafe, a subsequently identified eligibility error or major protocol violation, or the participant's request. The treating clinician will remove the devices, document the decision, provide or arrange appropriate care, and report the event through the trial safety pathway. Suspected infection will require device removal, local cleansing and wound care, and topical or other anti-infective treatment when clinically indicated.

Treatment may be temporarily interrupted while a local reaction or intercurrent condition is assessed. It may resume only when judged safe and within the planned 6-week treatment period. Follow-up and analysis after intervention discontinuation will follow Section 3.7.

4.9 Permitted and Prohibited Concomitant Care

Medication intended to treat rosacea or reasonably expected to alter rosacea symptoms will not be used during the 1-week period before baseline or during the 6-week intervention period. This restriction includes, but is not limited to, carvedilol, gabapentin, duloxetine, minocycline hydrochloride, and isotretinoin. Any washout or discontinuation must be clinically appropriate and completed under the direction of the participant's treating clinician; medication required for participant safety will not be withheld.

Stable treatment for unrelated medical conditions may continue if the investigator judges that it does not create a safety concern, violate an eligibility criterion, or materially affect rosacea outcomes. All prescription and nonprescription medicines and other rosacea-directed treatments used from screening through week 10 will be recorded, including the indication, dose or procedure, and start and stop dates.

5 Outcomes and Participant Timeline

5.1 Primary Outcome

The primary outcome is the CEA score at week 6, the prespecified end of the 6-week treatment period. The outcome variable is the single participant-level score recorded at that visit on the 5category CEA scale: 0, clear (no erythema); 1, almost clear (slight redness); 2, mild (definite redness); 3, moderate (marked redness); and 4, severe (fiery redness). Lower scores indicate less severe persistent facial erythema. A dermatologist who is masked to randomized assignment will perform the live clinical assessment. The CEA has shown high inter-rater and good intra-rater reliability when used by trained raters in patients with rosacea 16.

CEA was selected because persistent facial erythema is the defining treatment target in this ETR population and because the scale provides a standardized, clinically interpretable assessment suitable for masked dermatologist rating. Baseline CEA is collected for covariate adjustment, as specified in Section 8. The primary treatment contrast, missing-data procedures, and supportive analysis that respects the ordinal scale are also prespecified in Section 8.

5.2 Secondary Outcomes

The prespecified secondary outcomes are CEA scores at weeks 2, 4, and 10 and the following scale scores at weeks 2, 4, 6, and 10. Baseline values of every scale will also be collected. Each outcome is the participant-level total or global score at the specified visit. Unless otherwise stated, lower scores indicate a more favorable clinical state. Collectively, these outcomes characterize persistent erythema, episodic flushing, migraine symptoms, sleep quality, depression, and anxiety across treatment and post-treatment follow-up.

PSA of facial redness will be reported on a 0-to-4 ordinal scale (0, no redness; 1, slight redness; 2, mild redness; 3, moderate redness; and 4, severe redness) 17 . The PSA will be completed by the participant without access to the clinician's CEA rating.

GFSS will capture the participant's overall severity of facial flushing during the previous 7 days on a 0-to-10 scale, anchored at 0 (no flushing) and 10 (extremely severe flushing) 18 . The GFSS is kept distinct from the CEA: GFSS evaluates episodic flushing, whereas CEA evaluates persistent facial erythema. This feature-specific separation is consistent with global rosacea assessment recommendations 13.

Migraine severity will be assessed by the participant on the study's 0-to-10 VAS with reference to the previous 7 days, anchored at 0 (no migraine) and 10 (most severe migraine). The participant's single recorded value will define the visit-level VAS outcome 19.

Sleep quality will be assessed with the PSQI, which uses a 1-month recall period and yields 7 component scores and a global score from 0 to 21. Higher scores indicate poorer sleep quality 20.

Depression and anxiety symptom severity will be assessed with the HAMD and the HAMA, respectively, administered and scored by psychiatrists using the study versions of the instruments. Higher scores indicate greater symptom severity 21, 22.

5.3 Safety and Harms Outcomes

Safety outcomes will be assessed from the first application through week 10 and will include AEs, treatment-emergent AEs, intervention-related AEs, grade 3 or higher AEs, serious adverse events (SAEs), intervention-related SAEs, temporary treatment interruptions, and permanent intervention discontinuations because of AEs. Events of particular relevance include auricular pain, local erythema or inflammation, bleeding, adhesive-related skin reactions, infection, sleep disturbance, dizziness, headache, and nausea.

Severity will be graded using the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE) version 5.0, when an applicable term exists. Event definitions, ascertainment, documentation, and reporting procedures are specified in Section 9.3. Statistical summaries are specified in Section 8.7.

5.4 Outcome Measurement Properties and Standardization

Outcome definitions, instruments, scoring, assessors, and visit schedules will be standardized across all 5 sites. Masked dermatologists will receive training in the CEA grading anchors. CEA will be rated from the participant's current clinical appearance rather than reconstructed from questionnaires or photographs. CEA and PSA ratings will be completed independently.

Participant-reported measures will be completed before treatment-related discussion at the assessment visit whenever operationally feasible. Staff may explain item wording in a neutral and standardized manner but will not coach responses. HAMD and HAMA will be administered by psychiatrists using the same item wording and scoring rules at all visits.

Facial photographs will be obtained at baseline and weeks 2, 4, 6, and 10 under each site's standardized acquisition procedure as supporting source documentation.

The CEA, PSA, GFSS, migraine VAS, PSQI, HAMD, and HAMA rating scale are provided in Appendices A through G, respectively.

Eligibility, relevant medical history, concomitant treatments, and the required 1-week washout of prohibited rosacea-directed medication will be reviewed before baseline. 6 Assignment of Interventions

6.1 Random Sequence Generation

Eligible participants will be assigned in a 1:1 ratio to AA or SS through the central EDC system of the First Affiliated Hospital of Army Medical University. The EDC randomization module will use simple unrestricted randomization, with a probability of 0.50 for each group at each allocation. No stratification, permuted blocks, minimization, or site-specific allocation quota will be used. Thereafter, they will have no role in enrollment, allocation, outcome assessment, or statistical analysis and will not access accumulating allocations. Outcome assessors and the trial statistician will not see forthcoming assignments or be able to alter the allocation algorithm.

Assignments will be generated only at the time of randomization, with no advance lists distributed to sites.

6.2 Allocation Concealment

Allocation remained concealed until written informed consent had been obtained, eligibility had been confirmed, and baseline assessments had been completed. The central EDC system then generated the participant's assignment, which was communicated to the treating acupuncturist for implementation. No advance allocation list was distributed to participating sites, and the enrolling clinician could not select, reject, override, or repeat an assignment. Central, participantspecific release preserved allocation concealment before randomization.

6.3 Implementation

At each site, a dermatologist identified potential participants, obtained written informed consent, confirmed eligibility, and arranged baseline assessments. After these steps, the participant was referred to the treating acupuncturist, who initiated the central randomization request and implemented the assigned intervention.

The treating acupuncturist documented the allocation date, assigned intervention, and device application in the intervention record and did not perform efficacy outcome assessment or statistical analysis.

6.4 Masking

Participants, dermatologists, psychiatrists, and the trial statistician will remain masked to allocation until database lock and code release. Treating acupuncturists cannot be masked because they must apply the allocated device, but they will not assess efficacy outcomes or conduct statistical analyses.

Masking will be supported by the similar appearance and adhesive of AA and sham devices. The active needle will penetrate the skin, whereas the sham device will not. Point locations, skin preparation, visit schedule, retention, ear alternation, self-pressing instructions, and practitioner contact will be matched. Participants will not be shown the devices during placement and will be instructed not to discuss device sensations with outcome assessors.

6.5 Permissible Unmasking and Emergency Procedures

Because treating acupuncturists are already aware of assignment, most immediate intervention actions-including removal of the device, temporary interruption, or permanent discontinuation-can be taken without revealing assignment to masked personnel. Individual unmasking will be permitted only when knowledge of the assigned device is necessary for the urgent clinical management of a serious or otherwise clinically important AE and the required information cannot be obtained adequately from the unmasked treating acupuncturist. 8 Statistical Methods

8.1 Statistical Objectives and Estimands

This 2-group superiority trial will estimate the effect of assignment to AA versus SS on CEA at week 6. In the ICH E9(R1) estimand framework, the target population is all randomized participants, and the treatment conditions are assignment to AA or SS with the common study procedures. The outcome variable is the week 6 participant-level CEA score, summarized as the baseline-adjusted mean difference (AA minus SS). Treatment discontinuation, missed applications, nonadherence, and clinically required concomitant or rescue treatment will be handled using a treatment-policy strategy. A negative difference favors AA because lower CEA scores reflect less severe erythema.

Participants will be analyzed according to their assigned group, and observed outcomes collected after intercurrent events will remain relevant to the primary estimand. Missing week 6 CEA observations will be handled using the planned missing-data methods rather than by changing the analysis population. Secondary outcomes will use the same treatment-policy strategy at their scheduled visits. Safety will be summarized among participants receiving at least 1 intervention.

8.2 Sample Size Determination

The sample size was calculated based on the between-group difference in the week 6 CEA score.

A mean difference of 0.70 points was considered a clinically relevant treatment effect, with a common SD of 1.20. With a 2-sided alpha level of .05, 80% power, and a 1:1 allocation ratio, 48 participants per group were required. The calculation was performed using PASS 2023 software (NCSS, LLC).

Allowing for 10% attrition, the planned sample size was increased to 54 participants per group, for a total of 108 participants.

8.3 Analysis Populations

The ITT population will include every randomized participant, analyzed in the group assigned at randomization. The ITT population is the primary population for all efficacy analyses and includes participants who receive no intervention, discontinue treatment, miss applications, use concomitant or rescue treatment, or have a protocol deviation. A participant with no observed week 6 CEA score will remain in the ITT population and will be represented in the primary analysis through the prespecified missing-data procedure.

The safety population will include all randomized participants who receive at least 1 application of the trial intervention and will be analyzed according to the intervention actually received. A complete-case population will include participants in the ITT population with the observed baseline and week 6 CEA values required by the ANCOVA and will be used only for sensitivity analysis.

8.4 General Analysis Principles

Treatment groups will be identified by masked codes during data review and programming.

Analyses will be conducted after database lock with 2-sided tests. For the primary comparison, the significance level will be .05, with a 95% CI. Interpretation will emphasize effect estimates and CIs rather than P values alone. All contrasts will be expressed as AA minus SS, with the direction of benefit specified for each scale.

Continuous variables will be summarized using means and SDs and, when useful for skewed distributions, medians and interquartile ranges. Categorical variables will be summarized using counts and percentages with explicit denominators. Baseline characteristics will be described by randomized group without significance testing, because any baseline differences arise after randomization and do not require a test of the randomization process. Source-verified extreme observations will not be removed solely because they affect normality tests. The primary model will adjust for baseline CEA and randomized group.

8.5 Primary Outcome Analysis

Week 6 CEA will be analyzed using ANCOVA with randomized group and baseline CEA as model terms. The treatment effect will be reported as the adjusted mean difference (AA minus SS), with a 95% CI and 2-sided P value. Adjusted group means will also be reported. Missing data will be handled as specified in Section 8.9.

Model diagnostics will assess linearity with baseline CEA, residual variance, residual normality, and influential observations. Because CEA has 5 ordered categories, an adjusted proportionalodds ordinal logistic model will be used for support. The common odds ratio will describe the odds of a higher (worse) CEA category for AA versus SS. Values below 1 favor AA.

8.6 Secondary and Exploratory Outcome Analyses

Repeated CEA scores and the PSA, GFSS, migraine VAS, PSQI, HAMD, and HAMA scores will be analyzed using separate mixed models for repeated measures. For each outcome, the postbaseline score will be the dependent variable and the fixed terms will be randomized group, categorical visit, the group-by-visit interaction, the corresponding baseline score, and the baseline-by-visit interaction. Within-participant correlation will be modeled using an unstructured residual covariance matrix with visit-specific residual variances. Model-based adjusted means and adjusted mean differences (AA minus SS) with 95% CIs will be estimated at each scheduled visit.

The repeated-measures models will use all available postbaseline observations under a MAR assumption and will not use single imputation. CEA contrasts at weeks 2, 4, and 10 and all PSA, GFSS, migraine VAS, PSQI, HAMD, and HAMA contrasts at weeks 2, 4, 6, and 10 are secondary.

8.7 Analysis of Harms

Harms will be summarized in the safety population according to treatment actually received. AEs will be graded with the CTCAE version 5.0, when an applicable term exists. For each group, the number and percentage of participants with at least 1 AE, treatment-emergent AE, interventionrelated AE, grade 3 or higher event, SAE, intervention-related SAE, temporary treatment interruption, and permanent discontinuation because of an event will be reported.

Events will be summarized by their recorded adverse event names, maximum CTCAE version 5.0 grade, seriousness, investigator-assessed relatedness, action taken, and outcome. Both the number of affected participants and the number of events will be shown, and the denominator for percentages will be stated. Treatment-emergent events through week 6 will be distinguished from events first identified during follow-up through week 10.

8.8 Multiplicity

There is 1 confirmatory primary comparison: AA vs SS for the week 6 CEA score. Its 2-sided type I error rate will be controlled at 0.05, and no multiplicity adjustment is required for that single test. All comparisons for CEA at other visits and for PSA, GFSS, migraine VAS, PSQI, HAMD, and HAMA are secondary and exploratory in interpretation. Their P values and 95% CIs will be nominal and will not be adjusted for multiple outcomes or visits.

8.9 Missing Data

The amount, timing, pattern, and documented reasons for missing outcome data will be summarized by randomized group and visit. For the primary analysis, missing week 6 CEA scores will be handled by MI under a MAR assumption. Fifty imputed data sets will be created using predictive mean matching. The imputation model will include randomized group; baseline and all available postbaseline CEA scores; and baseline PSA, GFSS, migraine VAS, PSQI, HAMD, and HAMA scores. The ANCOVA will be fitted in each imputed data set and the estimates and standard errors combined using Rubin's rules. The likelihood-based repeatedmeasures analyses will use all observed values without separate imputation under their MAR assumption.

8.10 Sensitivity Analyses

Robustness of the primary conclusion will be evaluated using a complete-case ANCOVA with randomized group and baseline CEA, the baseline-adjusted proportional-odds model described above, and an extreme missing-data analysis. The extreme analysis will assign a CEA score of 4 to every missing AA week 6 outcome and a score of 0 to every missing SS week 6 outcome before fitting the baseline-adjusted ANCOVA.

8.11 Statistical Software and Reproducibility

Analyses will be conducted in R version 4.4.0. Multiple imputation will use mice version 3.16.0, and repeated-measures models will use nlme version 3.1-165. Package versions, R session information, model formulas, contrast direction, and random-number seeds used for imputation will be retained with the analysis programs.

The trial statistician will conduct the analyses from a read-only extract of the locked database after release of the treatment codes. Derived variables and analysis populations will be generated programmatically where feasible. The analysis code, execution log, model diagnostics, imputation diagnostics, and final tables and figures will be archived in the statistical trial file so that each reported estimate can be traced to the locked data extract.

9 Monitoring and Safety Oversight

9.1 Data Monitoring Committee (DMC)

No independent DMC will be established. The trial evaluates a short-duration, minimally invasive outpatient intervention in 108 participants, and the foreseeable intervention-related risks are predominantly local, promptly detectable, and clinically manageable. These features support direct safety oversight by the investigator team rather than a separate committee.

9.2 Interim Analyses and Stopping Guidelines

No interim analysis of efficacy, futility, or between-group safety outcomes is planned, and no accumulating comparative outcome results will be reviewed before database lock and unblinding. Consequently, no statistical alpha-spending function or efficacy or futility boundary is specified. Recruitment and follow-up will continue to the planned sample size unless a safety, ethical, regulatory, or trial-integrity concern requires suspension or termination.

9.3 AE and SAE Procedures

Safety surveillance will begin with the first application and continue through week 10. At each treatment contact and visit, adverse events will be identified through participant reports, nonleading questions, and clinical assessment. An adverse event is any unfavorable medical occurrence after treatment, regardless of relatedness. Treatment-emergent events are those that occur or worsen from the first application through week 6. Events first identified during weeks 6 to 10 will be distinguished in the safety records.

A SAE is an event that results in death, is life-threatening, requires or prolongs inpatient hospitalization, results in persistent or clinically important disability or incapacity, results in a congenital anomaly or birth defect, or is another important medical event that may jeopardize the participant or require intervention to prevent one of these outcomes. Seriousness will be assessed separately from severity. Severity will be graded using the CTCAE version 5.0, when an applicable term exists.

For each event, investigators will record its verbatim description, onset and resolution dates, severity, seriousness, treatment action, care provided, outcome, and assessed relatedness. Relevant events include auricular pain, local inflammation, bleeding, adhesive reactions, infection, sleep disturbance, dizziness, headache, and nausea. Unanticipated events will also be reported.

The treating clinician will provide or arrange appropriate care and may remove the device, interrupt treatment, or permanently discontinue the intervention when clinically indicated.

Suspected auricular infection requires device removal, local cleansing and wound care, and topical or systemic anti-infective treatment when clinically indicated. Emergency unmasking is permitted only when knowledge of the assigned device is necessary for urgent clinical management and cannot be obtained adequately from the unmasked treating practitioner.

A site investigator must report every SAE to the trial safety lead within 24 hours after becoming aware of the event, using the information then available. A complete follow-up report will be provided as additional clinical information becomes available. AEs will be followed until resolution, return to baseline, stabilization, establishment of a chronic condition, determination that further follow-up is not clinically indicated, loss to follow-up despite reasonable attempts, or death. The protocol and informed consent form will be reviewed and approved by the institutional review boards of the First Affiliated Hospital of Army Medical University and each participating center before enrollment begins at the respective center. Each center must retain its ethics approval documentation and implement the approved consent, intervention, safety, and dataprotection procedures. The principal investigator is responsible for maintaining ethics approval and responding to requests from the relevant institutional review boards throughout the trial.

Eligible participants must be adults capable of providing valid informed consent. Written consent will be obtained by an authorized investigator or delegate before any study-specific procedure. Refusal or subsequent withdrawal will not affect routine care, legal rights, or access to indicated treatment.

10.2 Privacy and Confidentiality

Participant confidentiality will be protected before enrollment, throughout follow-up, during analysis and publication, and throughout the record-retention period. Each participant will be identified in the EDC system and analysis files by a unique trial identifier. The site identification record and signed consent form will be stored separately from coded research data. Direct identifiers will not be included in the analysis data set or aggregate trial reports. Authorized ethics, institutional, or regulatory reviewers may inspect identifiable source records when required, subject to confidentiality obligations.

Facial photographs will be handled as potentially identifiable records and used only within this trial. No identifiable participant photograph will be published. Electronic data and photographs will be stored on the internal server of the First Affiliated Hospital of Army Medical University, and paper consent forms, CRFs, and other source documents will be kept in locked cabinets at the participating sites. Records will be retained for 5 years after trial completion, or longer if required by an ethics committee, institution, or applicable law.

The research team will use the minimum participant information necessary to conduct the trial and answer the prespecified research questions. Individual participant data and trial photographs will not be provided to external investigators for secondary research under the present consent. At the end of the required retention period, the records will be disposed of or retained further only in accordance with institutional, ethical, and legal requirements.
