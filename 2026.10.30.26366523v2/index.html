Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. Abstract

Early biomarkers of durable response to immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) are urgently needed. We identify lymphocyte stability (LS) -preservation of peripheral lymphocyte counts after one cycle of ICB -as an on-treatment predictor of overall survival (OS) in metastatic melanoma (MM): patients with unstable lymphocyte counts (LS-U; post-ICB/baseline ≤0.8) have markedly increased risk of death. Metabolic profiling of patient T cells demonstrates ICB enhancement of mitochondrial bioenergetics, whilst CD8+ T cells from LS-U patients mount an exaggerated transcriptional response to ICB incorporating upregulation of mitochondrial pathways. Given these findings we assessed the relationship of LS with mitochondrial haplogroup, revealing carriage of haplogroup T (HG-T), present in ~10% of white British individuals, is associated with both LS-U and impaired OS, with mediation analysis implicating LS mediation. HG-T carriers have higher CD8+ T-cell pre-treatment mitochondrially encoded transcript abundance and, in paired scRNAseq data, fewer progenitor-exhausted cells, ICB hyperresponsivity, apoptotic program induction and reduced clonal persistence -consistent with baseline mitochondrial priming. These findings position LS as a clinically accessible marker of ICB outcome linked to inherited mitochondrial variation.

Introduction

Immune checkpoint blockade (ICB), consisting of monoclonal antibodies that target the checkpoint proteins PD-1, CTLA-4 and LAG-3, forms the mainstay of treatment for patients with advanced unresectable or metastatic melanoma (MM) and is increasingly central to the management of many other cancers. Marked heterogeneity between patients in terms of clinical benefit from ICB is apparent however, with over half of patients treated with combination anti-PD-1 (nivolumab) and anti-CTLA-4 (ipilimumab) for MM having disease-specific survival at 10 years, the rest progressing and dying within five years 1 . A comprehensive understanding of the determinants of variation in response to ICB promises to advance treatment stratification and development and is of major clinical and immunological interest.

Although early efforts to explain this heterogeneity focused on tumour features that confer sensitivity or resistance to ICB, it is increasingly clear that patient systemic immunity is cardinal to anti-tumour activity. One such factor is the ability to mount a robust lymphopoietic response after ICB initiation. This was originally described in early clinical trials of ipilimumab for MM, where patients who maintained peripheral blood lymphocyte counts (LC) after two cycles of treatment had a reduced risk of death 2 . Subsequent small retrospective studies linked on-treatment lymphopenia to poorer oncological outcomes of ICB in melanoma and lung cancer 3, 4 . Analysis of large datasets revealed a subtle fall in lymphocyte count is typical between the first and second cycles of anti-PD-1 based treatment for cancer 5, 6 . We hypothesise that the lymphopoietic response to ICB represents effective re-invigoration of T-cell function, linked to T-cell clonal expansion and antigen recognition, together with parameters of overall immune fitness, including sufficient lymphotropic cytokines. In keeping with this, human genetic studies implicate IL-7, a cytokine central to lymphogenesis and peripheral T-cell homeostasis 7 , in mediating the lymphopoietic response to ICB. Carriers of an intronic polymorphism in IL7, associated with a cryptic splice-variant and increased IL7 expression, show a reduced ICB-induced LC fall 5, 6 . IL-7 is pleiotropic, antagonising the intrinsic mitochondrial apoptotic pathway through JAK-STAT5-induced BCL2 expression and promoting memory T-cell differentiation 8, 9 . Other crucial cytokines include IL-2 for T-cell expansion, and IL-15 for T-cell survival. Beyond lymphotropic factors, clinical benefit from ICB is linked to a sufficient population of stem-like progenitor exhausted T cells (TPEX), which mediate the proliferative response to ICB [10] [11] [12] [13] . Other favourable peripheral factors in patients with MM include greater diversity of expanded large CD8+ T-cell clones, with increased expression of genes linked to T cell receptor signalling and mitochondrial translation being observed in CD8+ T cells of patients with durable responses 14 . Notably, mitochondrial function is exquisitely linked to T-cell state, with dynamic changes in mitochondrial morphology being linked to T-cell activation 15 . In CAR-T cell therapy, mitochondrial fitness is tightly associated with cellular persistence, memory differentiation and resistance to exhaustion 16, 17 , while effective T-cell infiltration and sustained functionality within tumours are critically entwined with mitochondrial metabolic state and oxidative fitness 18, 19.

Previously, in a prospective cohort of patients with MM treated with anti-PD-1 based singlet or combination ICB, we observed that maintenance of LC after treatment, termed lymphocyte stability (LS), was associated with both progression-free survival (PFS) and overall survival (OS) 6 . ICB induction of CD8+ T cell mitochondrial metabolic programs

CD8 T cell transcriptomic correlates of LS

Given the reduced clinical benefit of ICB in LS-U patients, we reasoned that T cells from these patients might demonstrate intrinsic resistance to anti-PD-1 based ICB effects. Pairwise pre-ICB (C1) and post-ICB (C2) gene expression profiles in sorted CD8+ T cells from 147 MM patients (122 LS-S, 25 LS-U) were compared. To adjust for differences in sample sizes between the LS groups, paired differential expression (DE) analysis was restricted to 10 patients per group and performed across 100 iterations. Functional analysis links ICB treatment to enhanced T cell bioenergetic demand and capacity

Given the relationship between ICB and induced expression of mitochondrial pathway related transcripts, the functional metabolic correlates of treatment with ICB were explored. To date, the effect of ICB in primary patient T cells on mitochondrial activity has not been functionally assessed. We therefore used the Seahorse XF Tcell Persistence Assay to perform comparative paired analysis of patient sourced T cells pre and post ICB, using negatively selected T cells from patient PBMCs (n=35 paired samples). T-cell oxygen consumption rate (OCR) and extracellular acidification rate (ECAR) were measured, with sequential addition of drugs perturbing mitochondrial electron transport chain function (Oligomycin, BAM15 and rotenone with antimycin A respectively) and bioenergetic profiles assessed throughout (Figure 3A, Table S6).

This demonstrated an ICB induced global increase in T-cell bioenergetic demand, with an upward shift in both oxygen consumption rate (OCR) and extracellular acidification rate (ECAR) kinetic profiles (Figure 3B), from which the metrics ATPlinked respiration, spare respiratory capacity, basal respiration, maximal respiration and compensatory glycolysis could be derived. Post-ICB T-cells had higher total ATP production rates (Figure 3C, P = 0.048), which was driven by coordinated increases in both mitochondrial ATP-linked respiration (Figure S4A, P = 0.044) and glycolytic ATP production (Figure S4B, P = 0.027).

Intriguingly, ICB was observed to enhance T-cell metabolic adaptability, with both increased maximal respiration rate (Figure S4C, P = 0.012) and higher spare respiratory capacity (SRC), both in real terms and also relative to basal metabolic rates (Figure 3D, P = 0.004; Figure S4D, P = 2.6x10 -4 ). Notably SRC represents the mitochondrial reserve available to meet acute energetic demands, vital for T-cell survival and function. Upon inhibition of mitochondrial ATP production by oligomycin, post-ICB T cells demonstrated greater compensatory glycolysis, as reflected by upregulated ECAR (Figure 3E, P = 0.017). Together, ICB not only elevates overall energy demand but also enhances metabolic flexibility, enabling T cells to dynamically engage both oxidative and glycolytic pathways in response to stress. Coupled with the distinct hypermetabolic CD8+ T cell state observed in LS-U patients following ICB, these findings implicated mitochondrial function as a potential determinant of variation in lymphocyte stability and ICB outcomes.

LS partially mediates the inferior survival of HG-T carriers

Given that LS-U was associated with both HG-T carriage and shorter OS, we next asked whether the adverse clinical impact of HG-T is mediated through its effect on LS. To address this, we performed causal mediation analysis, modelling LS status by logistic regression, HG-T as the exposure and OS by a Weibull accelerated failure time (AFT) model. Both models were adjusted for age, sex, CMV serostatus, cancer type, and ICB regimen. In the full cohort, HG-T carriage was associated with shorter OS (time ratio [TR] 0.43, 95% CI 0.21-0.88, P = 0.021; n = 370). Among patients with available LS data (n = 294), in whom the mediation analysis was performed, the effect of HG-T on OS was of similar magnitude, albeit with borderline significance (TR 0.50, 95% CI 0.24-1.04, P = 0.063). Within this subset, HG-T carriers had approximately four-fold higher odds of LS-U (OR 4.08, 95% CI 1.80-9.22, P = 6.6×10 -4 ). In turn, LS-U was independently associated with shorter OS (TR 0.35, 95% CI 0.20-0.61, P = 1.9×10 -4 ). After LS status was included in the survival model, the association between HG-T and OS was attenuated and no longer significant (TR 0.65, 95% CI 0.31-1.37, P = 0.26).

Causal mediation analysis identified a significant indirect effect of HG-T on OS acting through LS. This effect corresponded to an average reduction of approximately 22 months in model-predicted survival time (average causal mediation effect [ACME] -21.7 months, 95% CI -47.0 to -5.5, P = 0.004), whereas the direct effect of HG-T was not significant (average direct effect [ADE] -38.2 months, 95% CI -97.7 to 39.1, P = 0.25) (Figure 4G). Consistent with this, the survival difference between HG-T and Non-T patients was attenuated after stratification by LS status, both among LS-U patients (HR 1.21, 95% CI 0.62-2.40, P = 0.57) and LS-S patients (HR 1.13, 95% CI 0.59-2.17, P = 0.72) (Figures S5H and S5I). Together, these results are consistent with LS-U forming an intermediate phenotype through which carriage of mitochondrial HG-T contributes, at least in part, to inferior outcomes following ICB.

Divergent CD8+ T-cell transcriptional states between HG-T and Non-T

To investigate whether mitochondrial haplogroup T influences transcriptional response to ICB, ICB-induced transcriptional changes in CD8+ T cells were separately characterised across HG-T and Non-T individuals. HG-T CD8+ T exhibits a transcriptional hypermetabolic state

We next investigated whether HG-T carriage was associated with transcriptional differences in CD8+ T cells. To maximise statistical power while accounting for treatment status and population structure, HG-T and Non-T samples were compared across all available CD8+ T cell bulk RNA-seq profiles, correcting for treatment timepoints. Although genome-wide differences were modest at FDR<0.05, the strongest association was increased expression of MTND2P28 in HG-T carriers (Figure S6A and Table S9). Analogous analyses of independently profiled B and NK cells identified the same association, albeit less significant, in other cell-types (Figure S6A), indicating the MTND2P28 signal with HG-T carriage was reproducible across immune cell lineages, as would be expected if directly linked to haplogroup. Given that MTND2P28 is a nuclear mitochondrial DNA segment (NUMT) homologous to mitochondrial MT-ND2, together with a reported susceptibility to read misalignment 33 , we reasoned that this signal might reflect haplogroup-dependent misassignment -reflecting genetic divergence from the reference sequence -rather than authentic pseudogene transcription. To test this a mitochondrial variant-aware reference genome was constructed, incorporating conservative haplogroupassociated mtDNA variations (allele frequency [AF] ≥ 0.01), and the RNA-seq data competitively re-aligned against the nuclear and mitochondrial genomes. Tracking fragments between the original and variant-aware alignments revealed substantial reassignment of reads originally mapping to MTND2P28 to mitochondrial MT-ND2, while the majority of reads originally assigned to MT-ND2 remained mitochondrial (Figure S6B). Thus, accounting for mitochondrial genetic variation substantially reduced systematic misassignment between mtDNA and homologous NUMTs.

To address whether resolving this mapping ambiguity uncovered a distinct HG-T associated transcriptional phenotype in patient-derived CD8+ T cells, the ratio of mitochondrial-to-nuclear transcripts was characterised. This demonstrated that, compared to other haplogroups, pre-treatment HG-T CD8+ T cells had an increased ratio of mitochondrial-to-nuclear transcripts (P = 0.012, Figure 5C). At the gene level, this difference extended beyond MT-ND2, with expression across the 13 mtDNAencoded protein-coding genes, spanning respiratory complexes I, III, IV, and V, consistently increased in HG-T carriers (Figure S6C and Table S10). Reflecting this, a prespecified mitochondrial core transcriptional score summarising expression of the 13 mtDNA-encoded protein-coding genes was significantly elevated in HG-T CD8+ T cells at baseline (P = 7.9x10 -3 , Figure 5D). Together, these findings indicate that, in patients with cancer, HG-T CD8+ T cells enter ICB treatment with increased mitochondrial transcriptional activity. We further explored this divergent expression at mitochondrial complex-level analysis of nuclear-encoded genes (Table S11), reasoning that these should also track mitochondrial encoded changes. This demonstrated increased expression of mitochondrial programs across haplogroups following ICB (Figure S6D), but the magnitude of this induction for electron transport chain complexes I, III, and IV, together with ATP synthase (complex V), was significantly greater in HG-T individuals 34 (Figure 5E). In summary, these results demonstrate that HG-T carriers -at least in the context of the immune exhaustion of cancer -demonstrate subtle but significant upregulation of the majority of mitochondrially encoded transcripts, reproducible across cell subsets, but most notable in CD8+ T cells. Moreover, ICB elicits induction of respiratory chain transcripts, but the effect of this is greater in HG-T carrying patients, consistent with an exaggerated mitochondrial transcriptional response to ICB in HG-T carriers.

HG-T Constrains Clonal Persistence and Reshapes the T Cell Repertoire

Given the transcriptional changes indicative of increased activation of apoptotic pathways in HG-T carriers, we examined the bulk CD8+ T-cell RNA-seq derived TCR repertoire data to assess the impact of HG-T carriage on T-cell clonotype dynamics. Using multivariable analysis, we observed that both age and CMV seropositivity were associated with higher Gini coefficients, reflecting the known effect of ageing as well as CMV related memory inflation 35 . Conversely, biological female sex and, most significantly, carriage of HG-T was associated with lower Gini coefficients, in keeping with reduced repertoire clonality (Figures 5F and 5G). Intriguingly, the effect of HG-T carriage remained significant after accounting for LS (Fig S6E, LS P = 0.27, HG-T P <0.05), indicating that mitochondrial HG-T carriage directly accounted for divergence in TCR clonality observed in patients, irrespective of LS. To assess whether the apoptotic signaling observed in HG-T carriers had discernible effects on T-cell clonal survival, longitudinal samples collected at each cycle of ICB were examined. This showed that HG-T carriers exhibited significantly fewer persistent clones after one cycle of ICB, with a corresponding increase in clones lost following treatment compared to Non-T carriers (Figures 5H and S6F). Thus, the enhanced apoptotic signaling observed in the HG-T transcriptional landscape translated to detectable effects on the TCR repertoire, characterised by reduced representation of expanded memory-like clones and reduced clonal stability, in keeping with impaired CD8+ T cell clonal stability in HG-T carriers.

HG-T-specific transcriptional and clonal features at single-cell resolution

HG-T CD8+ T Cells Exhibit a Selective Loss of TPEX States

To provide granularity to these observations and further characterise the CD8+ T-cell subsets showing most transcriptional sensitivity to HG-T carriage, a peripheral CD8+ T-cell atlas comprising 5' scRNA-seq with paired VDJ sequences was generated. Post-quality control, this comprised 220,060 cells across 195 pre-and post-treatment samples from 120 patients receiving ICB for melanoma (Figure 6A). CD8+ T cells were annotated into distinct functional subsets, including naïve (CD8. Naive), central memory (CD8. TCM), effector memory (CD8. TEM), terminally differentiated effector memory (CD8. TEMRA), stem-like progenitor exhausted (CD8. TPEX), proliferative (CD8. Pro), mucosal-associated invariant T (MAIT), gamma-delta T (gdT), and mitochondrial gene expression-high (CD8. Mitohi) populations (Figure 5A). Canonical marker expression and quality control metrics were consistent with established subset identities (Figures S7A -S7E). Comparative analysis of subset proportions between HG-T carriers versus non-carrier patients using a linear mixed-effects model, adjusting for treatment timepoints and inter-individual variance, showed that all subsets showed similar proportions across haplogroups except for CD8. TPEX cells, which were found at a reduced frequency in HG-T carriers (P = 0.03; Figure 6B). A similar result was obtained through per-sample Wilcoxon tests at baseline, with a consistent direction of effect noted post-treatment (C1, P = 0.029; C2, P = 0.066; Figure S8A and Table S12). To test whether this observation was driven by outlier individuals, a leave-one-donor-out sensitivity analysis was performed across the 12 HG-T individuals. For all iterations the odds ratio remained below one however, with 7 out of 12 iterations reaching statistical significance with this smaller sample and the remainder showing borderline significance (Figure S8B), demonstrating fewer CD8. TPEX cells across HG-T carriers irrespective of donor heterogeneity. It is increasingly clear that such stem-like TPEX cells are crucial for sustaining CD8+ T-cell responses upon ICB, and these results indicate HG-T carriers have a compromised reservoir for T-cell reinvigoration, mirroring the impaired LS in these patients 36, 37 . Finally, the molecular basis underlying reduced TPEX proportions was explored with single-cell regulatory network inference using pySCENIC. This identified five CD8. TPEX-specific regulons, with the MYB(+) regulon emerging as the most specific to this subset (Figure S8C), in keeping with the described crucial role of the MYB transcription factor in the regulation of selfrenewal and maintenance of stem-like CD8+ T cells 38, 39 . Thus, the association of HG-T carriage with reduced CD8+ T cell clonal maintenance was supported by specific reductions in the proportion of CD8. TPEX cells, characterised by expression of persistence-related regulatory networks and central to durable ICB responses (Figure S8D).

TEM Links Transcriptional Rewiring to Clonal Dynamics in HG-T

The bulk CD8+ T cell RNA-seq analysis indicated that, in keeping with effects of LS-U, HG-T carriage was associated with exaggerated transcriptional response to ICB. To determine whether this divergence was reflected across distinct CD8+ T-cell states, ICB-induced DE analysis was examined across naive, TCM, TEM and TEMRA subsets. To ensure balanced comparisons, equal numbers of cells from HG-T and Non-T carrying patients were subsampled within each subset and bootstrapbased DE analyses performed (C2 vs C1). This permitted non-parametric identification of DEGs while controlling for differences in statistical power across subsets. We complemented this approach with independent validation using donorbalanced, linear mixed-effects models. Across all CD8+ T-cell subsets, HG-T carriers consistently exhibited exaggerated transcriptional responses to ICB, with naïve and TEM subsets showing the greatest divergence from Non-T individuals, as reflected by the largest differences in the number of DEGs (Figures 6C and S9A, Table S13). Notably, naïve cells were characterized predominantly by transcriptional downregulation, consistent with disruption of baseline homeostatic programs, whereas TEM cells showed haplogroup T-specific upregulation of mitochondrial metabolic pathways (Figure 6D and Table S14). Examination of canonical markers further supported these observations with BCL2, a key anti-apoptotic factor, being preferentially downregulated in HG-T naïve cells, while HLA-DRA and IL7R exhibited increased and decreased expression, respectively, in HG-T TEM cells (Figure S9B), consistent with enhanced activation coupled with reduced memory potential.

We next revisited clonal persistence across subsets, defining clones through paired alpha-beta TCR sequences derived from single-cell V(D)J sequencing. In keeping with observations from bulk RNA-seq data, cells derived from persistent clones were significantly reduced in HG-T patients across TCM, TEM and TEMRA populations, with the strongest effect observed in TEM (Figure 6E). The robustness of these observations to outlier individuals was further assessed using repeated subsampling of paired samples (8 HG-T and 8 Non-T donors) across 1,000 bootstrap iterations. At the cell level, the reduction in persistent clones remained significant in ~82.4% of iterations for TEM, whereas less than 50% of iterations reached significance in TCM or TEMRA (Figure S9C), suggesting that the effect is most consistently driven by the reduced clonal persistence in the TEM compartment.

To determine whether clonal fate was influenced by transcriptional state, we compared baseline transcriptional profiles of clones that were resampled, consistent with clonal persistence, versus those that were not at C2. In Non-HG-T carriers, metabolic and apoptotic features did not distinguish between persistent and nonpersistent clones, indicating other pressures for clonal persistence, most likely antigen recognition. In contrast, HG-T clones that were lost to sampling post ICB exhibited markedly elevated expression of apoptosis, ROS, glycolysis, and hypoxia signatures pre-treatment (Figures 6F and S9D). These findings indicate that CD8+ T cells from HG-T carriers demonstrate proportionally increased metabolic stress prior to treatment, resultant in shorter clonal half-life.

Discussion

Many established correlates of response to ICB -including the maintenance of large peripheral CD8+ T-cell clones over treatment and an intact stem-like progenitor compartment crucial for sustaining effector activity 14, 40 -reflect properties of the host T-cell compartment. Treatment selection in MM however is based on clinical and tumour-intrinsic factors, and does not take account host T-cell fitness. Clinically, preemptive identification of ICB non-responders is attractive for its potential to identify non-responding patients early so as to enable treatment switching and escalation. Here we show that LS -lymphocyte stability -representing the ratio of lymphocytes derived from routine blood tests across the first cycle of ICB, identifies patients with impaired long-term survival in MM well in advance of standard radiological assessment timepoints. We further show that LS reflects underlying divergent CD8+ T-cell mitochondrial activity, with an exaggerated oxidative transcriptional response to treatment characterising poorer outcomes. Notably, LS is in part determined by inherited mitochondrial variation: carriage of mitochondrial haplogroup T (HG-T) being associated with ICB hyperresponsiveness, reduced progenitor-exhausted TPEX compartment, impaired T-cell clonal persistence and shortened OS.

Lymphocyte stability (LS), the preservation of peripheral lymphocyte counts after one cycle of ICB, is a dynamic on-treatment indicator of OS across anti-PD-1 and anti-CTLA-4 regimens. After adjustment for clinical covariates, LS remains prognostic and further stratifies patients with elevated baseline NLR, suggesting that it captures on-treatment prognostic information distinct from baseline indices. Paired CD8+ T cell RNA-seq pre-and post-ICB revealed a substantially greater response in LS-U patients (more DEGs, larger effects), preferentially involving oxidative phosphorylation and electron transport chain pathways. Conversely, LS-Sassociated pathways were characteristically anabolic, including positive regulation of mitochondrial translation and IL-7 signalling, consistent with preserved biosynthesis and homeostatic survival. In addition, although PD-1 signalling regulates T-cell glycolysis, fatty-acid oxidation and oxidative activity 44 , the bioenergetic effects of checkpoint blockade in patient T cells remain incompletely characterised 45 . In primary patient samples, ICB consistently increased OCR, ATP production, as well as maximal and spare respiratory capacity. These data provide direct evidence that ICB is accompanied by invigorated mitochondrial activity, raising the possibility that inter-individual differences in adapting to energetic demand contribute to LS.

Given the association between LS and mitochondrial pathways, we reasoned that germline mitochondrial variation might influence LS. Mitochondrial haplogroups comprise hundreds of linked, co-evolved variants that influence mitochondrial biology 20 . Testing haplogroup against LS revealed a strong association between HG-T carriage, present in ~10% of the white UK population 20 , and LS-U phenotype. Consistent with the recently reported impaired radiological response to ICB among HG-T carriers with MM 27 , we find that HG-T carriage was associated with reduced OS in our independent, prospective OxCITE cohort. This association was at least partly mediated through LS, with no haplogroup effect on survival among LSmatched patients, positioning LS as an intermediate phenotype linking inherited mitochondrial variation with treatment outcome. These findings support HG-T as an upstream contributor to lymphocyte instability, but not the only determinant of LS-U. Initial analyses identified MTND2P28 as the strongest HG-T-associated transcript across CD8+ T, B and NK cells (adjusted P = 7.1×10⁻²⁰, 4.0×10⁻⁵ and 1.6×10⁻¹⁹, respectively). However, MTND2P28 is a nuclear mitochondrial DNA segment (NUMT) homologous to mitochondrial MT-ND2, and we observed implausibly high read counts for a pseudogene when using a single reference mitochondrial genome. Because NUMTs create alignment ambiguity, particularly when inherited mtDNA variation increases divergence from a single mitochondrial reference sequence 46, 47 , we therefore realigned reads to a variant-aware reference. This reassigned many pseudogene-mapped reads to the mitochondrial genome across all haplogroups, demonstrating that inherited mtDNA variation can generate genotype-dependent alignment artefacts. After remapping, HG-T carriers showed coordinated baseline elevation of mtDNA-encoded protein-coding transcripts in CD8+ T cells. Because germline effects attenuate with molecular distance from the causal variant 48, 49 , a causal haplogroup effect should be most detectable in genes governing mitochondrial function, with immediate secondary effects on metabolic pathways. Consistent with this, beyond mtNDA-encoded transcripts, HG-T carriers showed differential expression of the nuclear-encoded metabolic genes SLC16A14 and NXN, supporting extension of the HG-T phenotype to metabolic and redox pathways through mitochondrial-nuclear crosstalk 50 . These baseline effects were nevertheless relatively modest, suggesting a subtle genotype influence that manifests under the immunological demands of metastatic cancer and ICB. Indeed, ICB induced nuclearencoded oxidative phosphorylation programmes across haplogroups, but more strongly upregulated in HG-T across respiratory-chain complexes I, III, IV and V.

Failure of homeostatic maintenance of lymphocyte populations after ICB may reflect increased cell death or impaired progenitor-derived replenishment. A single-cell RNA and VDJ atlas of >220,000 CD8+ T cells from 120 patients (75 paired pre-and post-ICB) revealed three further key attributes of HG-T carriage that were consistent with bulk RNA-seq observations. Primarily, HG-T was associated with an exaggerated response to ICB, particularly in CD8+ TEM cells, with over-representation of induction of genes implicated in mitochondrial electron transport. Secondly, while we did not replicate the expanded naïve T-cell populations reported in a previous pretreatment dataset where analyses were split according to clinical benefit defined by radiological response 27 , we did see an association between HG-T carriage and pretreatment CD8. TPEX proportion, with a similar post-ICB trend. Enrichment of a MYBdriven regulon in these cells, given the critical role of MYB in anti-PD-1-triggered TPEX proliferative bursts 38 , strongly supports impaired homeostatic proliferation. Finally, ICB specifically upregulated apoptosis-related pathways in HG-T CD8+ T cells, consistent with mitochondrial control of apoptosis 51 . Homeostatic T-cell turnover is a response-associated feature of ICB 52 ; sc-VDJ tracking showed baseline clones in HG-T carriers were less likely to be re-detected after treatment, and nonpersistent clones showed baseline enrichment of apoptosis and ROS pathways.

We propose a priming-activation-attrition model to integrate these observations. For HG-T, inherited mitochondrial variation establishes a primed baseline state, upon which ICB imposes increased bioenergetic demand; the greater induction of respiratory-chain programmes in HG-T cells may therefore reflect increased demand or compensatory adaptation rather than greater mitochondrial fitness. Baseline CD8. TPEX depletion with reduced MYB-associated programmes is consistent with this state being one of increased cellular stress. After ICB, increased apoptotic programmes and reduced clonotype persistence suggest that altered metabolic adaptation in HG-T favours activation and differentiation at the expense of progenitor maintenance and clonal persistence.

Our study thus identifies LS as an integrative on-treatment phenotype, linking clinical outcome with T-cell metabolism and clonal persistence during ICB, derivable from routine blood testing and therefore potentially scalable for dynamic monitoring during therapy. The association of HG-T with lymphocyte instability provides evidence that inherited mitochondrial variation can shape T-cell capacity to meet the metabolic demands of ICB. Metabolic adaptability, rather than activation alone, is a potential determinant of sustained immunity -which may help explain why strong effector activation does not necessarily translate into durable response. As inhibition of additional checkpoints has yielded limited improvement beyond PD-1-based therapy 53 , our findings build on recent work that suggests approaches to support Tcell mitochondrial function 54 , rather than further releasing inhibitory brakes, may represent a complementary strategy to enhance ICB.

Limitations of the study

First, the study remains predominantly observational. HG-T is present in only a subset of LS-U patients, indicating that it represents one inherited contributor rather than a deterministic cause of lymphocyte instability. Other host-, tumour-, and treatment-related factors likely contribute to LS. Second, although our transcriptional and cellular findings are consistent with altered mitochondrial adaptation in HG-T CD8+ T cells, they do not directly establish mitochondrial dysfunction. Seahorse experiments assessed ex vivo stimulated T cells, rather than tumour-reactive CD8+ T cells, and were underpowered for HG-T-specific phenotypes. Further functional comparisons are required to distinguish compensatory adaptation and intrinsic respiratory dysfunction. Finally, short-read sequencing cannot completely resolve . homologous mtDNA and NUMT sequences. Although our variant-aware alignment substantially reduced genotype-dependent read misassignment, residual ambiguous mapping is likely to remain. Long-read sequencing, ideally incorporating haplotyperesolved mitochondrial and nuclear genomes, will be important for validating haplogroup-associated mitochondrial transcription.

METHOD DETAILS

Sample collection

Peripheral blood samples (30-50 mL) were collected prior to initiation of immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) and longitudinally before each subsequent treatment cycle. Plasma and peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were isolated by Ficoll-Paque density gradient centrifugation. PBMCs were processed as follows: (i) cells were lysed in RLT Plus buffer (Qiagen) supplemented with dithiothreitol (DTT; Merck) and snap-frozen at -80 °C for RNA and DNA extraction; (ii) immune cell subsets were enriched using magnetic-activated cell sorting (MACS) (Miltenyi Biotec) for downstream bulk and single-cell RNA sequencing; and (iii) aliquots were cryopreserved in 90% fetal calf serum (FCS; Gibco) with 10% dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO; Merck) for future use. Peripheral blood plasma collected at baseline (day 0) was diluted 1:2 in HBSS and used to quantify CMV IgG titres on an Abbott Architect i2000 immunoassay platform. For all analyses, the blood sample obtained closest to treatment initiation (within -30 to 0 days) was used as the baseline time point.

Clinical outcomes and lymphocyte stability (LS) calculations

Patient demographic and clinical characteristics were extracted from the electronic patient record (EPR). Disease stage was assigned according to American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) 8th edition staging system, with Tumour Node Metastasis (TNM) 8th edition criteria for metastasis subclassification (M1a-d). All patients included had a minimum follow-up duration of 6 months for survival analyses. PFS was defined as time of ICB initiation to clinical or radiological evidence of progression (according to irRECIST.1.1 criteria), or death. OS was defined as time from ICB initiation to death from any cause. Median follow-up duration was determined using a reverse Kaplan-Meier method.

To calculate lymphocyte stability (LS) and neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), full blood counts were obtained from EPR, where they were generated via Sysmex XN series analyzers (Sysmex Corporation) in an NHS clinical diagnostic laboratory. Pretreatment (C1) samples were defined as the most recent sample prior to ICB initiation (from Day -30 to Day 0), and post-treatment (C2) samples were the most recent sample after the first treatment cycle (from Day 18 to Day 50). Patients with a prior diagnosis of lymphoid-lineage haematological malignancies (e.g. chronic lymphocytic leukemia, lymphoma) were excluded.

Lymphocyte stability (LS) is defined as: where LS-stable (LS-S) is defined as LS > 0.8, and LS-Unstable (LS-U) is defined as LS ≤ 0.8.

The optimal cutpoint for LS at 0.8 was determined by maximally selected log-rank statistics using surv_cutpoint() function from survminer (version 0.5.1). This was supported by time-dependent receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis using pROC (version 1.19.0.1), where the 24-month optimal cutoff was 0.804 based on maximal Youden index.

Bulk RNA-seq preprocessing

Poly(A)-selected RNA libraries were sequenced using either 150-bp paired-end reads on an Illumina NovaSeq platform or 75-bp paired-end reads on an Illumina HiSeq 4000, both performed at the Oxford Genome Centre. Sequencing reads were aligned to the GRCh38/hg38 reference genome using HISAT2 (version 2.2.1), and gene-level read counts were generated with HTSeq (version 2.0.9). High-quality alignments were retained based on mapping quality (MAPQ) scores using bamtools (version 2.

Competitive re-alignment with variant-aware reference genome

To account for sequence-dependent misalignment between mitochondrial transcripts and nuclear mitochondrial DNA segments (NUMTs), we generated a mitochondrial variant-aware alignment reference based on GRCh38. Mitochondrial variants associated with the haplogroups represented in the cohort, together with conservative mitochondrial variants (AF ≥ 0.01) catalogued in the Genome Aggregation Database (gnomAD) v3.1, were incorporated into the HISAT2 reference as SNP and haplotype information, while retaining the complete nuclear genome to permit competitive alignment between mitochondrial and homologous nuclear loci. HISAT2 indices were generated using hisat2-build with the --snp and --haplotype options, and paired-end RNA-seq reads were subsequently realigned to the resulting variant-aware reference using HISAT2. Gene-level expression was re-quantified from the variant-aware alignments using HTSeq-count with the same annotation and counting parameters applied to the original analysis. These re-derived counts were used for all downstream analyses involving mitochondrial and nuclear mitochondrial pseudogene expression. TPM values were calculated from the re-quantified gene counts using gene lengths derived from the corresponding GRCh38 annotation.

Differential expression analysis

Differential expression (DE) analysis was performed using DESeq2 (version 1.50.2).

Genes with low expression were filtered prior to analysis, retaining only those with a mean count > 10 across samples. For longitudinal comparisons between baseline (C1) and post-treatment cycle 2 (C2) samples, DE analysis was conducted in a paired manner using the subset of 174 matched samples, incorporating patient identity as a blocking factor in the design formula to account for inter-individual variability. For cross-sectional comparisons between HG-T and Non-T groups at each time point (baseline and post-treatment), the top three principal components derived from variance-stabilised expression data were included as covariates in the design matrix, unless otherwise specified, to mitigate potential technical variation.

We performed GSEA using fgsea (version 3.22) with MSigDB Gene Ontology Biological Process (GOBP) datasets. Genes were ranked by average log₂ fold change (FC) derived from DE analysis. To ensure comparability between contrasts, only overlapping genes between the two groups were retained as the background set. To minimise selection bias and enhance robustness, enriched pathways were ranked based on the difference in normalised enrichment scores (ΔNES) between groups, and the top-ranked pathways were selected for visualisation. Overrepresentation analysis was conducted using clusterprofiler (version 4.18.4) with MSigDB GOBP gene sets with DEGs from each comparison tested against the shared gene universe using a hypergeometric test with Benjamini-Hochberg correction for multiple testing. Enriched pathways were ranked by fold enrichment, and the highest ranking terms were visualised.

Mitochondrial complex transcriptional activity

Mitochondrial complex-specific gene sets were curated from MSigDB (C2:CP:WikiPathways; complexes I-IV) and the HUGO Gene Nomenclature Committee (HGNC; complex V). Normalised gene expression values were extracted from baseline and post-treatment samples, and pathway activity scores were calculated as the geometric mean of gene expression within each complex. To assess the interaction between mitochondrial haplogroup status and treatment over time, linear mixed-effects models were fitted using patient ID as a random effect to account for repeated measurements. Fixed effects included mitochondrial haplogroup, treatment time point, and their interaction term.

Bulk adaptive receptor analysis

T cell receptor (TCR) repertoire profiling was performed on bulk CD8+ RNA-seq data from paired pre-and post-treatment samples using MiXCR (version 3.0.13). Sequencing reads were aligned to reference V, D, and J gene segments to reconstruct TCR clonotypes based on complementarity-determining region 3 (CDR3) sequences. To improve clonotype assembly, the non-default parameter OallowPartialAlignments = true was enabled to retain partially aligned reads for downstream assembly. Three iterative rounds of partial read assembly (assemblePartial) were conducted to maximise recovery of CDR3-containing sequences. In addition, quality-aware error correction during clonotype assembly and clustering was applied using a threshold of ObadQualityThreshold = 15.

Samples with fewer than 500 detected TCR clones were excluded to ensure sufficient repertoire complexity, and one outlier C2 sample from patient ID 250 was removed from downstream analysis. Following quality control, a total of 431,709 TRA chains and 714,627 TRB chains were identified across 173 donors with paired samples, including 106 donors with C1/C2 pairs and 67 donors with C1/C2/C4 longitudinal sampling. Clonotypes were defined based on the amino acid sequence of the CDR3 region in combination with TRB V and J gene usage. Clone size was quantified using TRB chains, with clonal expansion measured as the proportion of total repertoire occupancy (clone read count / total TRB read count). Persistent clones were defined as clonotypes present in both pre-treatment (C1) and posttreatment (C2) samples from the same individual. Lost clones were defined as clonotypes present at C1 but absent at C2, whereas new clones were defined as clonotypes absent at C1 but detected at C2.

Single-cell RNA-seq preprocessing, integration and annotation

Positively selected CD8⁺ T cells, together with peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs), were quantified and partitioned into nanoliter-scale droplets using the 10X Genomics Chromium platform, followed by cell lysis and reverse transcription. Complementary DNA (cDNA) was used to generate 5′ gene expression (GEX) and V(D)J libraries using the Chromium Next GEM Single Cell 5′ Reagent Kit v2 (Dual Index). Droplet encapsulation and reverse transcription were performed on individual samples, whereas library preparation and sequencing were conducted in batches.

Sequencing was performed on an Illumina HiSeq 4000 platform, generating 75-bp paired-end reads for 5′ gene expression libraries and 150-bp paired-end reads for V(D)J libraries. Raw base call (BCL) files were converted to FASTQ format using Illumina software.

Single-cell RNA-seq reads were aligned to the GRCh38 human reference genome and quantified using Cell Ranger (version 8.0.1, 10X Genomics). To improve cell barcode recovery, droplets were additionally processed using the EmptyDrops algorithm implemented in the DropletUtils package (version 1.28.0), retaining both Cell Ranger-and EmptyDrops-identified cells. For sample demultiplexing, bi-allelic single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) were quantified at the single-cell level using cellsnp-lite (version 1.2.0), and donor identities were inferred using Vireo (version 0.5.8). Putative heterotypic doublets were identified using scDblFinder (version 1.22.0), while additional genotypic doublets were detected via Vireo. Ambient RNA contamination was corrected using SoupX (version 1.6.2). Genes detected in fewer than 10 cells were excluded from downstream analyses. Cell-level quality control criteria included (i) a mitochondrial transcript fraction <20% and (ii) detection of >300 genes per cell. Doublets identified by either scDblFinder or Vireo were removed prior to clustering and annotation.

Following preprocessing and initial annotation, 220,060 high-quality CD8+ T cells were selected from 195 samples across 120 donors (12 HG-T and 108 Non-T individuals), in which 150 samples were paired across pre-and post-treatment timepoints. Downstream analyses were performed using Scanpy (version 1.11.5), including log-normalisation and identification of top 3,000 highly variable genes (HVGs) within the CD8+ T population. To determine the optimal integration strategy, we benchmarked five widely used methods -BBKNN, Harmony, Scanorama, ComBat, and scVI -using the scIB-metrics framework (version 0.5.7). Based on batch correction scores and overall performance, scVI (version 1.1.5) was selected for batch correction, modelling each experimental pool as a batch covariate. Dimensionality reduction and clustering were performed using the scVI latent space, followed by nested Leiden clustering and visualisation using UMAP. For automated cell type annotation, scANVI (version 1.1.5) and CellTypist (version 1.7.1) were applied for cross-dataset label transfer using external references derived from Azimuth and COMBAT. In addition, the expression of canonical marker genes was also visualised to refine CD8+ T-cell cluster annotations.

Subset-specific differential expression analysis with downsampling

Differential gene expression analysis was performed using Seurat (version 5.4.0) with the Wilcoxon rank-sum test implemented in the FindMarkers() function, unless otherwise specified. For longitudinal comparisons, only pre-and post-treatment paired samples with more than 20 cells per subset were included in the analysis to ensure sufficient statistical power and representation. To control for differences in statistical power arising from unequal cell numbers across groups, we implemented a subsampling strategy whereby each group was downsampled to the size of the smallest group (n = 1,200 cells). This procedure was repeated 100 times, and results were aggregated to mitigate sampling variability and reduce bias. As an orthogonal validation, DE was additionally assessed using a donor-aware linear mixed-effects model. Genes with non-zero variance in the subsampled dataset were included, and donor identity was modelled as a random effect to account for inter-individual variability. For post-versus pre-treatment comparisons, six donors per group were randomly selected to ensure balanced representation across conditions.

Pathway enrichment analysis was performed using clusterProfiler (version 4.18.4) with GOBP gene sets. Up-and down-regulated genes were analysed separately using a hypergeometric test, with the background defined as all detected genes.

To compare pathway-level responses to immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) between HG-T and Non-T individuals across cell subsets, enrichment analyses were conducted on each subsampled dataset (n = 1,200 cells per group). Pathway-level differences were quantified as the delta frequency of enriched pathways between HG-T and Non-T groups across bootstrap iterations. This approach was adopted to maximise statistical power for pathway detection, particularly in subsets with limited DE signals.

Single-cell TCR repertoire

TCR chains were filtered based on productive rearrangements and chain identity (TRA or TRB). Cells were retained if they contained either (i) a single TCRβ chain, (ii) one TCRα paired with one TCRβ chain, or (iii) two TCRα chains paired with one TCRβ chain. Cells not meeting these criteria were excluded from downstream analyses. In addition, samples with fewer than 100 cells containing detectable TCR clonotypes were removed to ensure sufficient repertoire coverage. Clonotypes were defined based on the concatenated amino acid sequences of CDR3 regions present within each cell, with cells sharing identical CDR3 compositions assigned to the same clonotype. Clonal proportions were calculated as the number of cells belonging to each clonotype divided by the total number of cells per sample that passed the above filtering criteria.

In total, 165,040 cells from pre-and post-treatment paired samples across 75 patients had matched transcriptomic data. After quality control, 112,897 cells from 72 individuals retained high-confidence V(D)J sequencing information with valid chain configurations and were included in all downstream clonal analyses. For analyses of clonal phenotype, cells were grouped by clonotype, individual, and time point. The distribution of each clone across transcriptional subsets was quantified as the proportion of its constituent cells assigned to each subset. The subset with the highest proportion was designated as the dominant phenotype of the clone.

Definitions of persistent, new and lost clones were applied as described in the MiXCR-based bulk TCR repertoire analysis section.

Pathway scoring with UCell

Single-cell gene set activity was quantified using UCell (version 2.14.0), a rankbased scoring method robust to differences in sequencing depth and data sparsity. Gene sets were obtained from the MSigDB Hallmark collection. To ensure adequate representation, only gene sets with more than five overlapping genes detected in the dataset were retained for analysis. UCell scores were computed at the single-cell level based on gene expression rankings. To summarise pathway activity at the clonal level, cells were grouped by clonotype, and the median UCell score across all cells within each clone was calculated for each gene set.

Inference of transcription factor regulons

Gene regulatory network analysis was performed using the pySCENIC pipeline (version 0.12.2b0). The raw single-cell count matrix was used as input to infer transcription factor (TF)-target interactions. Co-expression relationships between TFs and candidate target genes were first estimated using the GRNBoost2 algorithm implemented in the grn step. TF-target pairs with importance scores >1 were retained for downstream analysis. Regulons were subsequently refined using the cisTarget (ctx) step, which integrates motif enrichment analysis to identify direct TF targets. Motif annotation was performed using the motifs-v10nr_clust-nr.hgnc-m0.001-o0.0.tbl database, and genomic rankings were derived from the hg38_10kbp_up_10kbp_down_full_tx_v10_clust.genes_vs_motifs.rankings.feather reference. Regulon activity at the single-cell level was quantified using AUCell, which computes enrichment scores based on the ranked expression of target genes. To assess cell type specificity, regulons were ranked within each CD8+ T-cell subset based on their regulon specificity scores (RSS).

Genotyping

The Illumina Global Screening Array 24 version 3 (Illumina) was used for genotyping. For sample deconvolution of pooled single-cell data, nuclear autosomal genotypes were aligned to hg37 and assessed for call frequency > 0.9, SNP missingness < 3%, Minor Allele Frequency (MAF) > 1%, and Hardy-Weinberg Equilibrium (HWE) P > 10-20 using GenomeStudio (version 2.0.5, Illumina) and PLINK (version 1.9b6.24); samples with call rates < 97% were removed from further analysis. Upon initial quality control, imputation was performed using the Michigan Imputation Server with Haplotype Reference Consortium (HRC) version 1.1 Build hg19 as a reference panel, as well as Eagle (version 2.4) and Minimac4 (version 1.7.4) for phasing and imputation. SNPs were further screened for MAF > 4%, HWE P > 10-10 and r 2 > 0.7 and lifted-over to hg38. Prediction of mitochondrial haplogroups For mitochondrial haplogroup prediction, mtDNA sites were aligned to the revised Cambridge Reference Sequence (rCRS). Initial QC was performed on mitochondrial variants, retaining SNPs with call rates > 90% and MAF > 0.1%. Alleles were flipped or switched as required to guarantee alignment with rCRS orientation. With samples designated as male, mitochondrial imputation was then carried out using a GenBank mitochondrial reference panel 82 within the IMPUTE2 (version 2.3.2) chromosome X framework. Post-imputation QC included filtering variants with an imputation INFO score > 0.7 and resolving reference/alternate allele discrepancies. HaploGrep3 (version 3.2.1) was used to assign mitochondrial haplogroups based on PhyloTree build 17.2, applying a heteroplasmy level threshold of 0.9.

Seahorse T cell Persistence Assay

Paired pre-and post-treatment PBMC samples were selected by an independent investigator prior to experimentation. The principal experimentalist remained blinded to patient cancer type, treatment regimen, lymphocyte stability and mitochondrial haplogroup status until completion of data quality control and normalisation.

Cryopreserved PBMCs were rapidly thawed in pre-warmed R10 (RPMI-1640 supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum) and rested for 4 hours at 37°C in a 5% CO2 incubator to allow metabolic recovery. Untouched T cells were isolated from PBMCs using negative selection (Pan T cell isolation kit, Miltenyi Biotec) according to the manufacturer's protocol. T cells were stimulated for 16 hours on anti-CD3 (10μg/ml, BioLegend) coated plates in the presence of soluble anti-CD28 (5μg/ml, BioLegend) to mimic antigen exposure and early activation-induced metabolic remodelling.

Activated T cells were resuspended in Seahorse XF RPMI medium supplemented with 10mM glucose, 1mM sodium pyruvate and 2mM L-glutamine as per manufacturer instructions (Agilent Technologies). Seahorse cell culture microplates were pre-coated with Poly-D-lysine (10μg/ml) for 1 hour at room temperature to facilitate cell adherence. Activated T cells were plated at 1 x 10 5 total cells per well, with the maximum feasible number of technical replicates acquired for each sample according to cell availability. Plates were centrifuged at 200 x g for 2 minutes, and wells were visually inspected by light microscopy to confirm uniform cell seeding. Oxygen consumption rate (OCR) and extracellular acidification rate (ECAR) measurements were measured using the Seahorse XF Pro Analyzer (Agilent Technologies) at baseline and following sequential injection of oligomycin (1.5μM), BAM15 (2.5μM) and rotenone / antimycin A (0.5μM each).

To minimize inter-plate batch effects, paired samples from the same patient were always analysed on the same plate together with a standardized inter-plate healthy donor control. Wells failing pre-defined quality control criteria (e.g. abnormal mitochondrial stress test profiles, injection failure, poor cell adherence) were excluded. OCR and ECAR values were normalized to 1 x 10 5 viable cells, and scaled relative to the basal respiration of the inter-plate control.

Correlation analyses were performed using Spearman's rank correlation coefficient.
