Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. Full text

Abstract

Organ scores can conceal recent instability. We studied confirmed six-hour deterioration in six organs with low recorded severity among 19,690 adults with sepsis in MIMIC-IV, eICU, CareVue and SICdb.

Using a 175-variable model reduced from 552, we compared 3,072 combinations of 12 historical sources.

With hourly predictions, at least four hours between alerts and 0.50 alerts per patient, the model using ten sources captured 319 eICU events versus 245 using current information alone (+30.2%; difference 74, 95% CI 49-106.5). Gains were 34.6% in CareVue (144 versus 107) and 81.5% in SICdb (49 versus 27); using six sources captured 312 eICU events. Training with incomplete records increased capture from 180 to 211 (+31) under 50% withholding. Retrieving one existing laboratory record improved timely correct organ selection from 145 to 159 (+9.7%). Repeated new low-severity measurements changed earlier history's value. Models were evaluated without retraining in 1,661 adults with pancreatitis-related conditions.

Introduction

Identical organ scores can conceal different clinical courses. 1, 2 An organ that has recently improved after dysfunction may carry a different risk from one that has remained stable. Current scores omit how long the abnormality lasted, the support required during recovery and the sequence of improvement. Recovery after acute kidney injury illustrates how apparent normalisation can coexist with continuing vulnerability to recurrent injury. 3 We asked whether these earlier changes help identify which of six organs currently recorded at low severity is most likely to deteriorate over the next six hours.

Models using physiological time series can anticipate circulatory failure and cardiorespiratory instability. 4, 5 A systematic review has examined the predictive value of repeated measurements in critical care. 6 Studies of organ trajectories also describe sepsis subphenotypes and predict deterioration. 7, 8 The remaining question is how much earlier history adds once current physiology, treatment and measurement timing are known and the organ is recorded at low severity. Clinical usefulness also depends on which organ is selected for reassessment, when an alert is issued and how repeat alerts are suppressed within the available workload. 9 Electronic health records contain detailed histories, but reviewing an entire ICU stay at every assessment is impractical. Differences in charting systems also make large sets of derived variables difficult to transfer between hospitals. A useful summary should improve prediction beyond current clinical information while requiring few additional record sources. The number of model variables alone does not describe this requirement: a derived organ state may rely on laboratory tests, ventilator settings and infusion records. 10, 11 We evaluated how much of the benefit of organ history could be retained using fewer record sources (Fig. 1). We compared all 3,072 distinct combinations, including a derived organ state only when the records needed to calculate it were available. We tested history beyond current physiology and measurement patterns and compared compact history from ten sources and brief history from six sources with the full 175-variable model. We then evaluated hourly organ reassessment, incomplete records, retrieval of existing laboratory results and changes in the value of earlier history as new measurements arrived.

Results

Historical information improves prediction beyond the current assessment

We studied 19,690 adults with sepsis across four databases: 12,905 in MIMIC-IV (8,990 fitting, 1,955 auxiliary development/calibration, and 1,960 held-out evaluation patients), 4,983 across 114 hospitals in eICU-CRD, 1,288 in MIMIC-III CareVue, and 514 in the European SICdb cohort (Fig. 1; Table 1; Supplementary Tables 1 2). At each assessment, we predicted confirmed deterioration (SOFA component >=2 sustained across three consecutive hourly positions) within six hours-with 12-and 24-hour extensions-in each of the six organ systems (respiratory, coagulation, hepatic, circulatory, central nervous, and renal) currently recorded below threshold (Fig. 1). MIMIC-IV supplied model training and internal evaluation, eICU supported selection using both databases, CareVue tested temporal shift across an earlier charting era, and SICdb provided held-out external validation.

Low current severity did not erase risk associated with prior organ involvement. Standardising for the active index organ and entry route, six-hour circulatory deterioration risk in MIMIC-IV was 15.69% with prior circulatory involvement versus 1.07% without it (6.33% versus 0.89% in eICU), and respiratory risk was 18.24% versus 7.44% in MIMIC-IV (11.55% versus 3.26% in eICU; Supplementary Fig. 1a; Supplementary Table 4). Excess risk also persisted in coagulation (8.49% versus 1.11% in MIMIC-IV; 6.08% versus 0.57% in eICU), renal (10.05% versus 1.09%; 3.40% versus 0.58%), and neurological systems (5.01% versus 1.55%; 7.44% versus 2.77%). Conditioning on each patient's eligible organ set and total number of organs that deteriorated within the prediction horizon at each assessment confirmed that prior history localised which organ would deteriorate within the same patient, reducing conditional log loss by 0.1396 in MIMIC-IV (95% confidence interval 0.1231-0.1547) and 0.1093 in eICU (0.0654-0.1554; Supplementary Fig. 1b; Supplementary Table 5).

Across nine scheduled origins with six-hour alert suppression, the full model with 175 variables (67 current plus 108 historical columns across 12 sources) increased six-hour AUROC from 0.8979 to 0.9145 in MIMIC-IV and from 0.8418 to 0.8706 in eICU, reducing log loss from 0.08219 to 0.07867 and from 0.05406 to 0.05203 (Table 2; Supplementary Table 6). The compact history (163 columns) achieved AUROCs of 0.9114 and 0.8695, log losses of 0.07927 and 0.05173, and lifted eICU within-patient organ discrimination from 0.8421 to 0.8770. Scheduled six-hour capture (0.50 alerts/patient) increased from 363 to 382 in MIMIC-IV, 219 to 272 in eICU (282 with full history), 108 to 126 in CareVue, and 26 to 46 in SICdb (Table 2; Supplementary Table 39). Consistent gains held at 12 and 24 hours, with eICU median onset lead times of 2 hours (interquartile range 1-3) at 6 hours, 5 hours (2-7) at 12 hours, and 7 hours (3-14) at 24 hours (Supplementary Fig. 3a,b,d; Supplementary Tables 6 and 37).

Historical gains persisted in three comparisons addressing current information and measurement frequency. First, expanding the current baseline from 67 to 91 variables yielded 205 eICU captures (log loss 0.05427), whereas adding compact history on top of the 91-variable baseline reduced log loss to 0.05265 and increased capture to 270 (274 with full history; Supplementary Tables 6 7). Second, restricting the index assessment to observations with a target-organ measurement within the preceding three hours preserved historical gains in MIMIC-IV (AUROC 0.8742 to 0.8950) and eICU (0.7936 to 0.8405; Supplementary Table 8). Third, decomposing the model with 175 variables in eICU showed that current values/states captured 238 events (log loss 0.05450), adding recording-process indicators captured 219 (0.05406), adding 90 numerical-history variables captured 240 (0.05378), and adding the 18 derived organ-state history variables lifted capture to 282 (0.05203; Supplementary Fig. 3c; Supplementary Table 31), surpassing simple summary statistics plus state durations (256 captures; Supplementary Fig. 2d; Supplementary Table 11).

Increasing training sample size did not replace patient history. Across nested MIMIC-IV training sets of 2,247 (25%), 4,495 (50%), and 8,990 (100%) patients, models using current information captured 191, 202, and 219 eICU episodes, whereas models with target-organ history captured 233, 243, and 282 (Supplementary Fig. 2c; Supplementary Tables 9 10). A 25%-sample history model (233 captures, AUROC 0.8563) exceeded the 100%-sample model using current information (219 captures, AUROC 0.8418).

Ten record sources retain substantial historical prediction gain

We began with 5,492 types of structured clinical records, yielding 272,043 derived variables. Selection left 17 record groups with 1,492 variables. We excluded 912 variables without an equivalent eICU mapping from training and removed 28 constant or unused variables from the remaining 580, leaving 552 candidates in 13 groups. We compared 27 model sizes, down to 38 variables, and selected 175 as the full reference (-68.3% derived variables). Compared with 552 variables, this model captured 381 rather than 387 MIMIC-IV events and 282 rather than 273 eICU events; 38 variables captured 361 and 254 events, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 2a,b; Supplementary Table 12). Within the full model, we considered 8,192 combinations of 12 historical sources and the pain-assessment group already included in the current baseline. A derived organ state could be used only when all records needed to calculate it were available.

After merging combinations with identical model inputs, 3,072 remained, all sharing the 67 variables for current information (Fig. 2a; Supplementary Table 3). Three-fold cross-validation in MIMIC-IV identified 33 combinations on the Pareto frontier with 0 to 12 sources for evaluation in eICU (Fig. 2b; Supplementary Table 14). Selecting record groups, mapping between databases and pruning derived variables were separate steps.

The smallest combination meeting the prespecified requirement to retain >=80% of the full model's logloss and capture gains in both MIMIC-IV and eICU used ten sources: blood pressure, Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS), bilirubin, platelets, vasoactive therapy, saline infusion, blood pO2, creatinine, urine output and FiO2 (96 historical variables; 163 total inputs; Fig. 2c). The brief model used six sources: blood pressure, GCS, vasoactive therapy, lactate, creatinine and urine output (63 historical variables; 130 inputs). The two combinations differed: brief history included lactate, whereas compact history included five other sources; together they covered 11 sources (Fig. 2c). Brief history retained 85.1% of the eICU log-loss gain and 90.5% of the scheduled capture gain. Its 75.0% capture retention in MIMIC-IV development was below the joint 80% requirement (Supplementary Table 14).

At six hours, compact history retained 82.8% of full-history log-loss gain in the MIMIC-IV holdout cohort (95% confidence interval 65.4-97.6%) and 114.8% in eICU (97.9-150.2%), with scheduled capture retentions of 105.6% and 84.1%, respectively (Supplementary Table 13). Brief history retained 94.7% of source development log-loss gain and 85.1% in eICU (90.5% capture retention, 276 versus 282). At 12 and 24 hours, eICU log-loss retention for the compact model selected at six hours moderated to 53.3% and 61.7%.

In eICU TreeSHAP explanations for the compact model (Fig. 2d), coupled circulatory state history (blood pressure plus vasoactive support) contributed the largest share of mean absolute SHAP magnitude (22.7%), followed by platelets (14.4%), GCS (14.0%), blood pressure (10.4%), urine output (8.7%), creatinine (8.0%), blood pO2 (7.0%), coupled respiratory state (4.8%), and coupled renal state (4.3%).

Variables requiring more than one source were counted once under the corresponding group of sources.

Compact histories increase capture at an unchanged alert budget

We updated risk every hour and allowed at least four hours between alerts for a patient. Each model had the same budget of 0.50 alerts per patient (980 alerts in MIMIC-IV, 2,492 in eICU, 644 in CareVue and 257 in SICdb; Fig. 1).

In eICU, compact history captured 319 distinct organ deteriorations versus 245 with current information (+30.2%; difference +74, patient 95% confidence interval 49 to 106.5; hospital interval 43.3 to 106.9; Fig. 3a,b; Table 2; Supplementary Table 16). Capture rose from 107 to 144 in CareVue (+34.6%; difference +37, 14 to 54), from 27 to 49 in SICdb (+81.5%; difference +22, 8 to 35), and from 433 to 448 in MIMIC-IV (+3.5%; difference +15, -4 to 38). Brief (6-source) and full (175-column) histories captured 312 and 314 events in eICU, 134 and 145 in CareVue, and 31 and 39 in SICdb (Fig. 3a; Table 2). Sequential active query policies requesting 1-2 additional sources on demand from the 6-source baseline captured 297-306 eICU events, compared with 312 (fixed 6-source) and 319 (fixed 10-source; Supplementary Fig. 4c; Supplementary Table 29). Similar total captures did not imply identical event coverage. In eICU, compact (319) and full (314) history shared 260 captures, with 59 unique to compact and 54 unique to full history (Fig. 3c; Supplementary Table 17). Compact history captured more unique events in coagulation (18 versus 10), respiratory (9 versus 3), circulatory (9 versus 5), and hepatic (3 versus 0) systems, matched neurological unique captures (11 versus 11), and captured fewer unique renal events (9 versus 25, alongside 78 shared renal captures; Fig. 3d; Supplementary Table 15). Non-circulatory organs accounted for 253 of the 319 compact-history captures (79.3%) in eICU. In the earlier scheduled own-organ-history comparison, 43 of 122 newly captured episodes had a higher rank for the affected organ within the patient, with a median paired risk increase of 10.29 percentage points across the newly captured group (Supplementary Table 42).

Adaptation and record retrieval recover different kinds of information loss

When 50% of historical record batches were withheld across three masking assignments in eICU, the standard compact model (D0) captured a mean of 180.0 episodes (175, 183, and 182; mean AUROC 0.7749), whereas training with incomplete records (Dm) increased mean capture to 211.0 (+31.0 events, +17.2%; 95% confidence interval 16.3 to 47.7; mean AUROC 0.8080; Fig. 4a; Supplementary Table 21).

Adapted training also recovered full-history capture under 50% withholding (173.7 to 210.3) and 1-3 hour history truncation (Supplementary Table 21). Conditional generated-history comparisons improved log loss in some settings without establishing additional event capture over matched visible-sequence models; effects varied by generator and reader (Supplementary Tables 33 34).

In arrival-time aligned evaluation across 2,492 eICU queries, immediately retrieving one existing historical laboratory group (collected 3-24 hours pre-index) increased timely correct six-hour organ nominations from 145 to 159 (+9.7%; net +14, patient 95% confidence interval 4 to 25; hospital interval 3.9 to 23.8), converting 20 incorrect nominations to correct and 6 in the opposite direction (Fig. 4b; Supplementary Tables 23 24). Waiting up to two hours for a newly collected laboratory test yielded 139 timely replies and 61 timely correct nominations.

New measurements change the contribution of earlier history

For a fixed future outcome window, incorporating newly arrived measurements improved capture beyond advancing the clock alone: capture rose from 288 to 324 (+36) in MIMIC-IV and from 182 to 203 (+21) in eICU for D0 (295 to 315 and 184 to 201 for Dm; Fig. 4c,d; Supplementary Table 22). Under 50% history withholding in eICU, the adapted model before new records arrived (160 captures, AUROC 0.7831) already exceeded the unadapted model after new records arrived (143 captures, AUROC 0.7531), and reached 170 captures (AUROC 0.7993) once new records were incorporated (Fig. 4c,d).

To separate earlier history from new information, we compared models receiving the same 225 variables describing current observations, recording patterns and post-index measurements; one also received history from before index, kept unchanged. Overall eICU capture after index increased from 177 without earlier history to 214 with compact earlier history (Supplementary Table 25). With zero new measurements indicating low severity, compact earlier history improved eICU AUROC by +0.0258 (capture 185 to 195; paired Δ log loss -3.04 × 10-3); after one new measurement, the AUROC gain was +0.0158 (86 to 93). After >=2 low-severity measurements spanning >=1 hour, however, it overpredicted risk (1.47% compact and 1.74% full versus 1.38% observed; Fig. 5b) and reduced capture from 62 to 54 (ΔAUROC +0.0004; paired Δ log loss +1.30 × 10-3; Fig. 5a,c).

We progressively reduced the weight of earlier history as new measurements accumulated: full weight at 0-1 low measurements, half at >=2 spanning >=1 hour, and zero at >=3 spanning >=3 hours. In eICU, capture after index increased from 214 to 222 (log loss 0.05196 to 0.05148). Capture changed from 119 to 121 in CareVue and from 28 to 29 in SICdb, and from 320 to 317 in MIMIC-IV (Fig. 5d; Supplementary Tables 25 26).

Local calibration improves the comparability of organ risks

Under matched local calibration opportunities (Fig. 6a,b; Supplementary Tables 18 19), organ-specific affine calibration across 91 eICU evaluation hospitals (adapted on 23 disjoint hospitals) reduced compactmodel log loss from 0.05159 to 0.05079 and lifted capture from 218 to 233. Organ-specific shrinkage reduced compact-model log loss from 0.09904 to 0.09284 in CareVue (captures 126 to 129) and from 0.09507 to 0.08556 in SICdb, lifting SICdb AUROC from 0.8742 to 0.8854 and capture from 46 to 63 (Fig. 6a). AUROC varied by organ in MIMIC-IV and eICU (Fig. 6b; Supplementary Table 15). Actionmatched decision curves nominating at most one organ per patient quantified net benefit across 0.5%-20% thresholds in MIMIC-IV and eICU (eICU net benefit at 5% threshold: 0.01224 versus 0.00913 for current information; Fig. 6c,d; Supplementary Table 20). In the hourly workflow, local calibration changed SICdb capture from 49 to 72 (difference 23, 95% confidence interval 8 to 38), compared with 263 to 269 in the 91 eICU evaluation hospitals, 144 to 140 in CareVue, and 448 to 448 in MIMIC-IV (Supplementary Table 44).

Recalculating GCS from observed components and refitting the models in MIMIC-IV preserved the benefit of compact history (MIMIC-IV capture 220 to 235; eICU evaluation with its native total-GCS definition, 210 to 260; Supplementary Fig. 4b; Supplementary Table 27). We also applied the models without retraining to pancreatitis-related cohorts of 1,043 MIMIC-IV and 618 eICU patients. In eICU, six-hour AUROC increased from 0.8012 to 0.8347 and capture from 20 to 29 (Supplementary Fig. 4a; Supplementary Tables 28 and 40). In the separate MIMIC-IV norepinephrine analysis, AUTOC for riskbased prioritisation increased from 20.02 to 22.75 µg/kg with compact history and to 24.62 µg/kg with full history; using current vasoactive treatment information to rank estimated treatment benefit gave an AUTOC of 23.33 µg/kg (Supplementary Fig. 4d; Supplementary Tables 35 and 38). In MIMIC-IV pancreatitis-related patients, 24-hour capture increased from 245 to 256 (Supplementary Table 28). The norepinephrine comparison used the same 813 first opportunities to reduce treatment. Adding history did not establish an advantage over the shared treatment-context comparator for ranking treatment benefit (Supplementary Tables 35 and 38).

Discussion

Earlier organ history helped prioritise reassessment when current scores indicated low severity. At the same hourly alert budget, compact history increased six-hour event capture in eICU (+30.2%), CareVue (+34.6%) and SICdb (+81.5%). Counting events brought to attention within a fixed workload connects prediction to a specific clinical task. 12 Patients with the same currently active organ may have reached that state after recovery from multiorgan involvement or through direct onset. Their histories help identify which other, currently low-severity organ deserves reassessment. This extends the question from a patient's overall recovery pathway to the organ that may deteriorate next.

Selecting record sources addresses a practical problem that counting model variables alone misses.

Established methods balance prediction, simplicity and interpretability, including the cost shared by variables obtained from the same source. 11, 13, 14 Work in omics has similarly sought signatures that transfer between measurement systems. 15 Here, each derived organ state was included only when its required records were available, and combinations were selected for the gains they retained in both development databases. Respiratory state, for example, requires arterial blood gases and FiO2; renal state requires creatinine and timed urine output. A single derived variable can therefore require several sources. 10, 11 Ten sources retained substantial predictive benefit, while six sources offered an alternative with fewer records.

The latter included lactate instead of the five additional sources used by the compact model. Reducing redundant variables and reducing the records needed to calculate them are different steps. Cross-database mapping is another constraint: training was restricted to variables that could be represented in both databases. The full 175-variable model consequently reflects both predictive selection and measurement compatibility, with unmapped variables excluded before the final comparison of source combinations.

Similar capture totals can cover different patients and organs. In eICU, compact and full history captured 319 and 314 events, respectively, but full history uniquely captured more renal events, while compact history uniquely captured more coagulation, respiratory and circulatory events. These differences matter when choosing which records to retrieve. The separate organ-ranking analysis also showed that history could change the organ selected for a patient's reassessment (Supplementary Table 42). The choice between six and ten sources was sensitive to the size of the development gain: the brief model retained 9 of the 12 additional events captured by full history, so one more capture would have exceeded the 80% requirement. These results describe the trade-off between information requirements and event coverage across the available combinations.

The usefulness of history depended on what was available and what arrived next. Training with incomplete records recovered 31 eICU events when half the historical batches were withheld. Retrieving one existing laboratory result corrected 20 organ choices without waiting for a new sample. After two or more new measurements consistent with low severity over at least one hour, however, earlier history overpredicted risk and its discrimination gain diminished. This prompted the rule that reduced its weight as new evidence accumulated. Choosing which record to retrieve also relates to work on explanationguided information acquisition. 16 Some methods for generating missing history improved probability accuracy but did not clearly increase capture over models using the available records (Supplementary Tables 33 34). Training on incomplete records helps the model use what is visible, retrieval supplies an actual observation, and generation estimates missing information. Their value depends on whether the missing information can be recovered from the chart or requires a new measurement.

New measurements and elapsed time provided different information. Reusing a carried-forward normal value adds no new observation, whereas repeated measurements consistent with low severity can change estimated deterioration risk. When six organ risks compete for one alert, differences in organ event rates between hospitals can also change which organ is selected, even if ranking within each organ is preserved. Local calibration improved this allocation and substantially increased capture in SICdb. 17 No tested threshold met the adjusted criteria for lower-priority reassessment at 1%, 2% or 5% failure targets (Supplementary Table 41). The model therefore supports prioritising review rather than deciding when monitoring can stop.

Routine ICU records reflect both illness and clinical decisions about when to measure it. 18, 19 Historical gains persisted when the 67-variable baseline included measurement timing and frequency and when the current-information comparison was expanded to 91 variables. In 26 catheter-removal episodes from 25 patients, urine collections decreased by 1.61 per six observed ICU hours after removal (95% confidence interval -2.25 to -0.96; Supplementary Table 43). This change in recording frequency explains why the absence of a new record should not itself count as evidence of recovery. Blood counts also vary with age and sex in community-dwelling older adults. 20 Outcomes were defined from routine records using three consecutive hourly states and were not prospectively adjudicated. eICU informed model selection; CareVue and SICdb assessed performance after selection, with CareVue representing an earlier recording system at the MIMIC institution. Source counts describe data requirements; clinician time and financial costs were not measured. The rule for reducing the weight of earlier history had small external capture differences with intervals spanning zero.

Estimates of treatment prioritisation rely on confounder adjustment and treatment overlap. Prospective evaluation should first assess alerts without affecting care, then test whether they prompt appropriate reassessment and improve management and outcomes. This should include the time required to obtain each record source.

Much of the predictive value of organ history could be retained with fewer record sources when selection accounted for the records required by derived variables. Compact history and the brief alternative support six-hour organ reassessment, while training with incomplete records, retrieval of existing results and local calibration address common differences in what information is available. New measurements can then change how much earlier history remains useful.

Methods

Cohorts and time-aligned organ states

Sepsis cohorts comprised MIMIC-IV v3.1 (12,905 adults), eICU-CRD v2.0 (4,983; 114 hospitals), MIMIC-III v1.4 CareVue (1,288) and SICdb v1.0.8 (514); pancreatitis was reserved for transfer testing. 21- 29 Sepsis ascertainment followed Sepsis-3 criteria in MIMIC-IV (suspected infection paired with rolling SOFA >=2), antimicrobial-treated infection with qualifying SOFA >=2 in eICU, antimicrobial-supported coded sepsis in CareVue, and recorded sepsis diagnosis in SICdb. Within each candidate observation period, we identified the first structural transition from two consecutive hours with either zero or >=2 active organs to three consecutive hours with the same single active organ. Index (t0) was the third singleorgan hour. The selected transition was then checked for eligibility; a later transition was not substituted if the first failed. In MIMIC-IV and eICU, all five defining hours had to satisfy the sepsis informationtime eligibility criterion. Across cohorts, t0 had to precede ICU discharge or death, with at least one other organ observed below the activity threshold.

Activity meant SOFA component >=2 in the six organ systems. Features and labels respected measurement times and, where recorded, availability times. Respiratory scoring paired arterial blood gas pO2 with FiO2 recorded within the preceding 2 h; vasoactive exposure followed documented infusion intervals; and neurological scoring used total GCS. In the MIMIC-IV main branch, recorded GCS components were carried forward for up to 6 h. If any component was present, missing companion components received normal values; an endotracheal or tracheostomy verbal code of zero mapped to total GCS 15 under the public concept convention. Fully unobserved hours remained missing. An observedcomponent sensitivity analysis propagated uncertainty rather than applying these normal defaults. Other lookback validity windows were 6 h for vital signs and FiO2, 12 h for blood gases and lactate, and 24 h for platelets, bilirubin, and creatinine; urine output required 22-30 h of covered intervals (none >6 h) normalised to 24 h.

Prediction task and matched information comparisons

Eligible organs had observed SOFA components 0-1. Deterioration required three consecutive active hourly states (SOFA >=2), with onset 1-4 h after prediction and confirmation within 6 h before death or ICU exit. Valid carried measurements could support adjacent states; unknown did not establish recovery.

Each distinct patient-organ-onset episode was credited at most once; 12-and 24-h extensions used identical confirmation rules.

Six LightGBM predictors used depth 5, 31 leaves, learning rate 0.06, minimum leaf 20, L2 penalty 5, feature fraction 0.9, and up to 400 trees with 30-round early stopping; eligible windows had equal total patient weight. 30 MIMIC-IV was partitioned into patient-disjoint fitting (n = 8,990), early-stopping (n = 390), temporary calibration (n = 293), selection (n = 294), final calibration (n = 978), and held-out evaluation (n = 1,960) splits. The baseline with 67 variables comprised current values, states and recording-process timing/coverage. Controls used 91 current columns, recent measurements (<=3 h), 25%/50%/100% fitting samples, and current-value (50), recording-process (+17), numerical-history (+90) and state-history (+18) components.

Reassessment allocation and probability evaluation

The main workflow refreshed risk hourly through hour 23, nominated the highest-risk eligible organ, and imposed a 4-h patient cooldown at 0.50 alerts per patient. Mechanistic experiments retained nine origins and 6-h cooldown. Probability accuracy was assessed via patient-weighted log loss, Brier score, AUROC, and within-patient organ AUROC. 32 Action-matched decision curves evaluated at most one nominated organ per patient at index across thresholds p in [0.5%, 20%], weighting false-positive nominations by p/(1-p). 33 Local calibration compared identity, affine, shrinkage of organ intercepts with a shared slope, beta 34 , and isotonic maps selected by patient-weighted log loss (using a 23-to-91 hospital split in eICU and patient-disjoint cross-validation in CareVue and SICdb).

Withheld records, retrieval and new evidence

In three assignments, we withheld 50% of historical record batches throughout follow-up. We compared standard training (D0) with training on paired complete and incomplete histories (Dm), including indicators of record availability. For retrieval, we hid groups of laboratory records collected 3-24 h before index and evaluated 2,492 eICU requests. Each request could reveal at most one existing group, and timely six-hour organ choices were counted only after results were available; the comparison allowed waiting <=2 h for a new test. Update experiments compared elapsed time and new measurements for the same future outcome, using complete histories and 50% withholding. To study earlier history, two models received the same 225 variables describing current and post-index information; one also received history recorded strictly before index and kept unchanged. We stratified by 0, 1 or >=2 new measurements supporting low severity over >=1 h. The rule gave earlier history weight 1 initially, 0.5 after >=2 measurements spanning >=1 h, and 0 after >=3 spanning >=3 h.

Both predictions share current and new information; n counts distinct new low-severity measurements since the last new abnormality, and s is their time span. Carried values do not increment n.
