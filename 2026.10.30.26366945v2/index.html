How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Background: Upper extremity impairment persists in many stroke survivors, and few treatments are effective for severe chronic deficits. Brain-computer interface (BCI) therapy, which pairs decoded motor-intention-associated electrophysiology with sensory feedback, has improved upper extremity impairment in randomized trials, including in patients with severe paresis. Whether these benefits translate to routine care has rarely been evaluated. This study examined upper extremity motor recovery through 1 year among patients prescribed at-home BCI therapy in clinical practice. Methods: This retrospective observational study included adults who were prescribed the IpsiHand System, an electroencephalography-based BCI, through a commercial US home therapy program, had a baseline Upper Extremity Fugl-Meyer (UEFM) assessment, and averaged >1 session per week through their last assessment. Occupational therapists administered the UEFM by video every 4 weeks. The primary outcome was UEFM change from baseline to week 12. Introduction

Stroke is highly debilitating and a leading cause of death, with an estimated 795,000 cases in the United States annually and an estimated 12 million globally each year. 1, 2 Upperlimb impairment persists in about 40% of survivors, significantly impacting their daily living and quality of life. 3, 4 Current treatment modalities for functional rehabilitation in chronic stroke survivors are limited. Few stroke survivors receive physical or occupational therapy in the chronic phase, 5 and while impairment can still be reduced with rehabilitation, [6] [7] [8] this may require therapy doses that are more intensive and not often feasible. [9] [10] [11] Further, accessibility of therapies can be difficult, particularly for patients who require at-home solutions. [12] [13] [14] [15] Brain-computer interface (BCI) technologies provide a promising solution for chronic stroke rehabilitation. 16, 17 BCI systems can be noninvasive, and in some cases use portable hardware elements that can be safely deployed for at-home use. [18] [19] [20] [21] Therapeutic BCIs operate by decoding target electrophysiological biomarkers and concurrently driving physiologically relevant neurofeedback to promote beneficial neural remodeling to promote recovery. 22 This form of BCI-mediated cortico-sensory coupling is thought to induce Hebbian learning by simultaneously activating pre-and post-synaptic neurons. In the context of stroke, concurrent reinforcement of physiological mechanisms related to motor intention through assisted motor feedback has been shown to promote motor function and lead to long-term changes in neurophysiology. 20, 23 Prior studies applying BCI systems for chronic stroke recovery have reported motor recovery benefits and have demonstrated reliable BCI control in patients with hemiparesis through decoding of motor-related electroencephalographic (EEG) signals from the contralesional hemisphere, ipsilateral to the paretic limb. 20 In a pooled analysis of two prospective single-arm studies, 12 weeks of BCI therapy increased patient Upper Extremity Fugl-Meyer (UEFM) scores by a mean of 8.1 points. 24 In the recent BCI-REHAB randomized clinical trial (Unique identifier: NCT05965713), patients receiving BCI therapy averaged a 6.0 UEFM improvement after 12 weeks compared to a 1.5 UEFM improvement in the homeexercise control group, with over half of participants assigned to BCI therapy achieving clinically meaningful outcomes. 25 The outcomes from these studies not only support the therapeutic efficacy of BCI systems for motor recovery but also demonstrate the feasibility and accessibility of using such systems in a home environment. Whether these gains are enduring, and whether benefits translate to non-clinical research settings, however, remains uncertain. These studies assessed primary outcomes at the end of 12 weeks of therapy. Across BCI therapies for stroke, a meta-analysis found immediate benefits, 26 but only two trials reported gains that were maintained 6 to 12 months after therapy ended. 27, 28 While such studies have provided promising evidence for the benefits of at-home BCI systems for chronic stroke motor rehabilitation, they were conducted using prospective protocols, and real-world evidence derived from data collected during standard clinical care remains limited. 29 Understanding whether the benefits observed in prospective trials carry over to routine clinical care is critical for guiding appropriate adoption of these systems of care and for setting realistic expectations of benefit for patients and providers. 30, 31 The present study provides a single-arm, observational characterization of upper extremity motor impairment changes in a population of chronic stroke survivors utilizing an at-home BCI therapy system embedded within routine care. The goal of this study is to provide real-world evidence of upper extremity motor recovery with at-home BCI therapy by (1) quantifying UEFM changes after 12 weeks of use and (2) characterizing the trajectory of change through one year.

Study design, ethics, and data governance

The deviceuse criterion was applied so that outcomes described patients who used the device regularly.

Every logged session with a duration greater than zero counted toward the average, and for patients without a follow-up assessment, the average extended to the data cutoff. No sample size was calculated, and all eligible patients were included.

Before initiating therapy, candidates completed an EEG signal test confirming that the system could detect a control signal over the contralesional hemisphere. Program clinical staff scheduled the test, which was performed either in a clinic or in the patient's home. The signals were evaluated as described previously. 20, 24, 25 In sum, a dry-electrode EEG headset was placed with electrodes at frontal, central, and parietal sites of the international 10-20 system, including C3 and C4 over the left and right sensorimotor cortex. Patients performed a visually cued task of rest and motor imagery trials and were instructed to imagine moving the affected hand.

Spectral power was estimated with an autoregressive method, and at each electrode and frequency, the coefficient of determination (r 2 ) quantified the proportion of variance in spectral power explained by the difference between imagery and rest trials. Specifically, this was the squared correlation between power and trial type. A usable control signal was defined as an event-related desynchronization in the mu (8-12 Hz) or beta (13-29 Hz) band at an electrode over the contralesional sensorimotor cortex, consistent across repeated recordings. The electrode and the frequency bin (width 1 Hz) with the strongest r 2 became the patient's control feature and remained fixed for the course of therapy. Candidates without a usable control signal did not proceed to therapy.

Intervention, setting, and care pathway

IpsiHand is a non-invasive BCI therapy for patients undergoing stroke rehabilitation to facilitate and to maintain or increase upper-extremity range of motion. 33 The system consists of a wireless EEG headset that records neural activity over both sensorimotor cortices, a tablet that runs the therapy software, and a motorized exoskeleton worn over the affected hand and wrist. Signal recordings are decoded in real time by estimating spectral power at the patient's control feature determined during EEG screening. When spectral features of imagined movement (event-related desynchronization in 8-12 Hz and 12-30 Hz power) are detected, a control signal is transmitted to the motorized exoskeleton, which opens and closes the hand in a three-finger pinch and which also provides proprioceptive feedback. A flow diagram of the BCI system is shown in Figure 1A.

Each BCI therapy session consisted of a calibration phase followed by five blocks of 60 trials, with a full session lasting roughly 1 hour. During calibration, patients first remained at rest for 3 minutes, then completed a set of motor imagery and rest trials while the exoskeleton remained stationary. Calibration was used to ensure data quality and to identify motor imagery activity. Each block comprised 30 motor imagery trials and 30 rest trials in a randomized order. Trials lasted 8 seconds and were separated by 3-second buffer intervals during which patients were encouraged to blink or make minor physical adjustments. Blocks were separated by pauses during which patients could rest before resuming therapy. During motor imagery trials, patients were instructed to imagine opening the affected hand. In rest trials, patients were instructed to keep the exoskeleton closed by resting quietly.

Patients received the device through a clinical program administered by Kandu, Inc.

(Van Nuys, Cam USA). Each patient first attended a consultation with the program's clinical team to confirm eligibility, which required a stroke at least six months prior to consultation, age of 18 years or older, persistent upper-extremity motor impairment, and the ability to participate in a home-based program. Patients who met these criteria then obtained a physician prescription. The prescription and supporting medical records were submitted for payer authorization, or patients purchased the device through self-pay. Before initiating therapy, patients were also required to pass a set of screening procedures to ensure they met device eligibility, which included a cognitive screen, a clinical upper-extremity assessment, and the EEG signal test described in Patient population. Patients without aphasia completed the Short Blessed Test, 34 and patients with aphasia were assessed with the Mississippi Aphasia Screening Test. 35 All patients were screened for visual inattention with an unstructured cancellation test. 36 Patients initiating therapy received training and guidance on how to don and use the device from a licensed occupational therapist. Therapy was self-administered at home, and patients chose when and how often to use the device. Patients were provided device technical support through the clinical program by video conference or video call.

Outcome and assessment

The primary outcome was the UEFM total score (range 0 to 66, where higher scores indicate less impairment) during follow-up assessments up to 52 weeks. Week 12 was specified as the primary outcome timepoint because it corresponded to the treatment duration and endpoint of interest in published prospective studies of this device. 20, 24, 25 Assessments were administered remotely using videoconference, by licensed occupational therapists trained and certified in the Fugl-Meyer assessment. 37, 38 Assessors knew patients were using the device and were therefore unblinded. Training and certification included completion of the standardized BlueCloud Fugl-Meyer training, followed by at least three remotely administered Fugl-Meyer assessments reviewed and independently scored by a mentor clinician. Initial baseline UEFM assessments were conducted during onboarding and training after patients received the device. Follow-up assessments were conducted on a 4-week schedule; however, variation in assessment dates occurred due to patient availability. For analysis, followup assessments were organized into 4-week bins (week ± 2 weeks). UEFM change was calculated as the difference between the follow-up score and the baseline score. A ≥ 5.25 change in UEFM was chosen as the minimal clinically important difference (MCID) threshold for this study based on existing literature. 39 Severity categories were determined as UEFM total score 0-28 for severe, 29-42 for moderate, and 43-66 for mild. 40

Statistical analysis

Analyses were performed in Python 3.9.6 (pandas 2. Overall UEFM trajectory across available follow-up assessments up to a year was modeled using a mixed model for repeated measures (MMRM) to characterize changes in UEFM after the primary follow-up date. This model included every patient with at least one follow-up assessment, and UEFM at each assessment week was the response. The model had two fixed effects: assessment week, entered as an unordered categorical variable, and baseline UEFM. Baseline UEFM was included to account for differences in baseline impairment among the patients assessed at each week. No random effects were included. Correlation between each patient's repeated assessments was modeled through the covariance of the within-patient residuals. The model was fitted by restricted maximum likelihood, and inference on the fixed effects used Kenward-Roger degrees of freedom. 41 Due to variance in follow-up trajectories, and patient factors, data were assumed missing at random and missing values were handled by direct likelihood. 42 For each patient i at follow-up visit t, the UEFM total score was modeled as where 𝑌 !"

Two additional sensitivity analyses were conducted to examine the missing-at-random assumption of the MMRM. Each analysis involved imputation of due but absent assessments 50 times and pooling the results by Rubin's rules. 43 First, a carry-forward multiple imputation replaced each due but absent assessment with the patient's last observed value plus a zeromean normal draw with the model's week-specific standard deviation. The MMRM was then refitted to each completed dataset. Second, a tipping-point analysis was used to impute each assessment under missing at random, from the model's conditional distribution given the patient's observed scores. A fixed penalty term (δ) was subtracted from the imputed values after week 12 before refitting. This was repeated over a range of δ to determine the smallest δ at which the 95% confidence interval of the modeled difference between week 12 and week 52 contained 0. This analysis allowed us to determine the magnitude of departure from missing at random required before the improvement from week 12 to week 52 was no longer supported.

Exploratory analyses

After the primary analyses, we conducted three post hoc exploratory analyses of baseline characteristics. First, we compared age, baseline UEFM, time since stroke, and social deprivation index between patients that improved by at least MCID and those who did not at weeks 12, 16, and 24. Second, we compared baseline characteristics between patients with a week 12 assessment and patients whose 12-week assessment was due but not recorded.

Third, we compared baseline characteristics between patients with at least one follow-up assessment and patients with only a baseline assessment. Statistical comparisons were made using a Wilcoxon rank-sum test, and p-values were adjusted using the BH procedure.

Given the primary results and the predominance of severe baseline impairment in our cohort, we conducted an additional post hoc exploratory analysis of patients with a baseline UEFM of 20-50, the inclusion range of previously reported clinical trials of upper extremity motor recovery after stroke. [44] [45] [46] This range excludes deficits mild enough to produce a ceiling effect and deficits too severe for meaningful recovery to be likely. [47] [48] [49] In these patients, we summarized UEFM change from baseline at weeks 12, 16, and 24 with the statistics used in the primary analysis and adjusted t-test p values across the three weeks using the BH procedure.

Patient characteristics

Out of 5107 patients who received an IpsiHand prescription, 1448 received a device and were cleared to initiated therapy. Of the 3659 patients prescribed IpsiHand, but did not initiate therapy, 2225 were denied payer coverage or faced a payment barrier, 144 failed EEG screening, 695 had their inquiry case closed due to withdrawal or other reasons (including lack of medical records, patient withdrawal, or clinical ineligibility), and 595 were awaiting next steps towards receiving the IpsiHand system. Of these, 16 lacked a documented age or were younger than 18 years, 493 did not have a baseline UEFM assessment, 190 had a stroke chronicity of less than six months or did not have a stroke date documented, 293 averaged one session per week or fewer (mean 0.25, SD 0.28, range [0.00, 1.00]), and 4 had a significant detrimental medical event.

Overall, the full patient cohort included in the analysis set consisted of 452 real-world device users who met our inclusion criteria and received baseline UEFM assessments (mean age 61.9, SD 13.5; 253 male, 185 female of 438 with recorded sex), with 369 of them receiving at least one follow-up assessment (mean age 61.6 SD 13.7; 201 male, 154 female of 355 with recorded sex). In these 369 patients, median follow-up from baseline to the last UEFM assessment was 16.6 weeks (IQ range [9.0, 28.1]). Discussion

This study provides a real-world observational characterization of an at-home, noninvasive BCI therapy for upper extremity motor recovery after chronic stroke. Among patients with a paired assessment, mean UEFM improved by 4. As an emerging technology, therapeutic BCI systems provide an alternative modality to conventional interventions. Users are required to dedicate cognitive effort and personal time to receive potential benefits from BCI use. As such, real-world evidence is valuable for assessing the potential for BCI adoption in routine care, especially in the context of debilitating pathologies affecting quality of life such as motor impairment following stroke. Currently, access to sustained rehabilitation remains a barrier for many survivors, particularly for those that depend on at-home solutions. 12, 15 While clinical trials are typically conducted at academic centers, with recruitment, oversight, and adherence incentives, the patients in this cohort self-administered BCI sessions at home under routine care. Understanding whether previously documented therapeutic outcomes from BCI therapies translate to real-world settings not only reinforces clinical findings but also provides quantitative evidence to support patient and provider expectations. The evidence provided in this study, while observational, suggests that real-world use of BCIs provides a promising and accessible option for chronic stroke survivors.

A defining feature of this study population is its motor impairment profile. Most patients (73.7%) had severe upper-extremity motor impairments at baseline. Greater baseline motor impairment is generally associated with limited recovery, 50, 51 and the magnitude of measured motor recovery varies with baseline severity. 37, 52 Despite the high proportion of severely impaired patients, a mean UEFM improvement exceeding MCID was observed by week 16, and this continued to increase out to one year. However, patients that reached MCID at weeks 12, 16, and 24 were significantly less impaired at baseline than patients that did not. It is possible that the mean week-12 improvement below MCID partly reflects the severity profile of this cohort, and that more severely impaired patients require a longer duration of BCI therapy to reach clinically meaningful improvement. Consistent with this possibility, patients with a baseline UEFM of 20-50, the inclusion range of previous clinical trials and inclusion recommendations, 44- 47 showed larger mean improvements than the full cohort at weeks 12, 16, and 24, with the mean improvement exceeding MCID by week 12. Future work evaluating how the rate, magnitude, and shape of improvement trajectories vary with baseline severity in a baseline UEFM-balanced cohort will be necessary to determine its association with at-home BCImediated motor recovery.

In addition to severe motor impairment, our cohort had a mean stroke chronicity (time from stroke onset to baseline assessment) of 5.4 years. Typically, in long-term stroke survivors, function and quality of life tend to decline rather than improve over longer intervals. [53] [54] [55] Instead, our results suggest that meaningful clinical improvements may be possible in the chronic stage after stroke. Further, this chronicity profile and the observed improvements in UEFM resemble those reported in the BCI-REHAB randomized trial, reinforcing previous findings of meaningful improvements in the chronic stage after BCI intervention in a substantial fraction of subjects. 25 Compared to previously reported UEFM outcomes in prospective and randomized clinical trials, the mean week-12 improvement in our cohort was smaller. 24, 25 At later timepoints, however, our observed and model-estimated improvements from baseline were comparable to the range reported by those trials, with the observed mean improvement reaching +5.6 by week 16 and +6.7 by week 24. It is possible that comparable gains appearing later are consistent with slower accrual of therapeutic benefits under the variable conditions of routine care. For example, greater scheduled therapy time has been associated with larger motor gains after stroke. 9 The BCI-REHAB trial instructed patients to complete five one-hour sessions per week.

In contrast, our cohort used the BCI for an average of 3.6 sessions per week, and we did not analyze session duration or other utilization metrics. It is possible that degree of the UEFM improvement observed in this study is associated with extent of BCI utilization. Future work characterizing and examining the relationship between BCI utilization and UEFM improvements will be necessary to establish how much use is required for clinically meaningful improvement and to inform dosing recommendations in routine care. Further, based on the characterized improvements from this study, future study designs, or recommendations for at-home BCImediated upper-extremity motor recovery should consider evaluating primary outcome dates beyond 12-weeks, as the present evidence suggests that improvement may continue past previously evaluated outcome dates.

Taken together with previous real-world evidence, 29 these findings support at-home BCI therapy as a treatment where upper-extremity improvements can emerge during routine care, not only in controlled trials. To our knowledge, this is the first characterization of therapeutic outcomes at this scale in prescribed users of an FDA-cleared BCI, analyzed under a prespecified plan, and followed from the start of therapy to one year. Understanding how novel BCI therapies perform as a rehabilitation option in real-world settings provides patients and providers with evidence to set realistic expectations and judge the therapy for routine care.

Notably, our results provide an encouraging signal for the future of BCI therapies for stroke.

Within our cohort, we found that clinically meaningful improvements in motor function can occur years after stroke, even in patients whose recovery is often assumed to have plateaued.

Although this study is observational, it establishes an initial real-world evidence base for larger, future prospective controlled trials that can clarify the efficacy and target population for BCI adoption into routine stroke care.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. It was observational and single-arm, without a randomized comparator. Therefore, the within-cohort change and the outcome characterizations are contextual; extent of rehabilitation therapy outside of study procedures was not measured and might have impacted the observed change over time, though such therapy is typically absent in patients with chronic stroke. 56 The UEFM was administered by unblinded occupational therapists remotely by video conference. While video administration of the Fugl-Meyer shows adequate agreement with in-person administration, 57, 58 it does not remove the expectation effects that may accompany unblinded assessment. 59 BCI therapy was self-administered at home, and the analysis could not document or correct for environmental factors that may have influenced therapy delivery, such as reliable internet access, 60 correct headset placement and upkeep, and adherence to the intended BCI task. Further, this study cohort consists of patients engaged with routine care, which includes routine follow-up assessments. As reflected in our cohort profile in Figure 1B, many real-world BCI users do not receive UEFM assessments, and therefore, our findings describe only patients who receive clinical follow-up services. Further, only patients with outcome assessments contributed to the analyses. If unassessed patients improved less, the estimates overstate improvement.
