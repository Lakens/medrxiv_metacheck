Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Metacheck does not yet analyze automatically whether sentences which include non-significant p-values are correct, but we recommend manually checking the sentences below for possible misinterpreted non-significant p values.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Abstract

Background: The double-hit hypothesis proposes that chronic fine particulate matter (PM2.5) exposure and acute nitrogen dioxide (NO2) exposure increase COVID-19 mortality. Spatially patterned pollution and mortality complicate interpretation of these associations.

Objective: To test whether pollution exposure was more strongly associated with COVID deaths than with other natural deaths in Cook County Medical Examiner records.

Methods: We linked death records with demographic characteristics, Census population data, and modeled and monitored pollution estimates. Adjusted logistic regression compared 10,887 COVID deaths with 4,123 contemporaneous non-COVID natural deaths during March 2020-August 2021; sensitivity analysis used 12,048 pre-pandemic non-COVID deaths. Population-weighted Monte Carlo analyses compared observed exposures with group-specific expectations. Separate spatial and temporal randomizations examined short-term NO2 exposure.

Results: Higher five-year PM2.5 was associated with lower odds that a death record was classified as COVID rather than non-COVID (odds ratio per 1 µg/m³, 0.63; 95% confidence interval, 0.55-0.72), with a similar prepandemic comparison (0.54; 0.48-0.60). Modeled PM2.5 exceeded population-weighted expectations in both death groups. Modeled short-term NO2 excesses were small for COVID deaths and consistently larger for contemporaneous non-COVID deaths. At actual death times, NO2 exposure at death locations exceeded the population-weighted spatial expectation. At actual death locations, exposure immediately before death did not exceed the expectation based on randomized nearby times.

Significance: Pollution associations in these records were not specific to COVID deaths. The NO2 comparisons supported a spatial interpretation without evidence of unusually elevated exposure immediately before death. The study does not estimate population-level mortality risk.

Introduction

Frontera et al. [1] described this as the "double-hit" hypothesis: long-term exposure to particulate aerosols followed by acute exposure to nitrogen dioxide. Ecological studies of long-term PM2.5 and NO2 exposure provide related evidence for its component pollution claims [2, 3].

In this model, persons chronically exposed to high PM2.5 concentrations overexpress angiotensin-converting enzyme 2 (ACE2), and this facilitates COVID-19 viral penetration. Experimental work in mice subsequently found that exposure to particulate matter increased pulmonary expression of ACE2 and TMPRSS2, providing biological plausibility for this pathway [4]. The reduction in normal ACE2 activity resulting from COVID-19 infection could then result in clinical deterioration by disrupting dependent cardiometabolic pathways. Once bound to ACE2, COVID-19 downregulates cellular expression of ACE2, also resulting in dysregulated Ang-II and proinflammatory lung response [5]. Once infected, the second hit comes from high-end episodic exposure to NO2, which causes an inflammatory response and oxidative stress that may induce respiratory distress [1].

The "double-hit" hypothesis is important to test, as it makes significant claims that would potentially alter both policy and treatment for future pandemics. The hypothesis would, for example, provide an additional justification for government-mandated stay-at-home orders, which are associated with short-term reductions in air pollution by lowering traffic and industrial activity. Previous workers have suggested that decreases in air pollution following the quarantine periods may have also decreased the total number of COVID-19 deaths during those periods [6].

Several ecological studies have presented evidence for an association between air pollution and increased COVID-19 mortality. In Europe as of March 19, 2020, 83% of all COVID-19 fatalities (n = 3,701) occurred in regions where the maximum NO2 concentration was above 100 µmol/m² and only 1.5% of all COVID-19 fatalities occurred where NO2 concentration was below 50 µmol/m² [2]. An ecological regression analysis of all counties in the United States used a negative binomial mixed model with daily county-level COVID-19 death counts through 18 June 2020 and surface level PM2.5 data from 2000 through 2016 to estimate the effect of PM2.5 on COVID-19 mortality. The model estimated that an increase of 1 µg/m³ in average PM2.5 is associated with a statistically significant 11% (95% CI, 6% to 17%) increase in a county's COVID-19 mortality rate [3].

Subsequent evidence has remained mixed. A systematic review of 116 studies found positive and statistically significant associations in 48.1% of the reported mortality estimates; all of the studies were observational and most had a high risk of confounding and outcome-measurement bias [7]. A nationwide cohort study of 3.7 million Danish adults reported positive associations between long-term PM2.5 and NO2 exposure and COVID-19 hospitalization and death [8]. These later studies provide stronger evidence that an association may exist, but they do not eliminate the problem of spatial confounding or establish that the double-hit mechanism explains COVID-19 mortality.

The air pollution hypothesis makes the prediction that particulate and NO2 pollution both have positive correlations with COVID-19 mortality. This makes it reasonable to test the hypothesis by comparing these pollution measures with deaths. But there are difficulties with doing this directly.

First, air pollution in cities can be tightly linked to location and varies both spatially and temporally. This creates a problem when pollution and mortality data are not measured at comparable scales. We reduce this problem by linking the times and locations in individual death records with modeled and observed PM2.5 and NO2 data, while recognizing that the assigned values remain spatial exposure proxies.

Second, air pollution might be acting as a proxy for an unobserved variable, such as demography, poverty, mobility, population density, or housing type [9]. These associated variables could well be the real cause of any observed correlation, which would be misleading as air quality would then have a correlative but not causative relationship with COVID-19 mortality [10].

There is a way around some of this confounding, as the COVID-19 mortality/air-quality hypothesis makes another, implicit prediction: air quality should be more important for COVID-19 mortality than for non-COVID mortality. Comparing COVID and non-COVID natural deaths from the same data source and study period places both groups under the same broad spatial and pandemic conditions. Although air quality has wellestablished effects on overall mortality [11, 12, 13], the proposed double-hit mechanism predicts an additional association with COVID-19 mortality. If that association is not stronger for COVID deaths, then the observed pollution pattern would not provide evidence specific to the proposed double-hit mechanism and could instead reflect other factors associated with air pollution and all-cause mortality.

The existing research and ecological studies that associate air pollution levels with COVID-19 outcomes have been criticized as lacking rigor [14, 10] because they rely on COVID-19 summary data that is not age-adjusted and use air pollution data that is not spatially or temporally aligned. These studies are not definitive because they have short durations (sometimes only covering one month of sample data - [15, 16]), small sample sizes [2], and may not account for important confounders [17, 18, 19, 20].

To help resolve these issues, we compare modeled and observed measures of PM2.5 and nitrogen dioxide among COVID and non-COVID natural deaths in Cook County, Illinois. By linking Cook County Medical Examiner records with pollution estimates, we can test whether air pollution distinguishes COVID from non-COVID deaths after accounting for age, sex, recorded race, and Latino ethnicity. The detailed times and locations in the death records also allow us to test whether short-term NO2 associations reflect unusually high exposure before death or are better explained by where deaths occurred.

Materials and methods

We used two complementary approaches to test whether pollution exposure patterns differed for COVID versus non-COVID deaths: a logistic regression comparing COVID and non-COVID deaths, and a Monte Carlo simulation comparing observed pollution exposure at death locations with an age-adjusted, population-weighted expectation.

Data sources and processing

A retrospective study was conducted using natural-cause death records from the Cook County Medical Examiner's Office. The primary analysis compared records classified as COVID and non-COVID deaths occurring during the same period, from March 2020 through August 2021. This contemporaneous comparison was used so that both groups experienced the same broad secular and pandemic-period conditions. To address the possibility that some contemporaneous deaths classified as non-COVID were undiagnosed COVID deaths, we repeated the same analysis using non-COVID natural deaths from 2015-2019 as a robustness check. Logistic regression was used to determine whether demographic characteristics and modeled long-term PM2.5 exposure distinguished COVID from non-COVID death records within the Medical Examiner's sample [21, 22].

The Cook County Medical Examiner's records contain incident-location information, including latitude and longitude coordinates and incident address fields. The analysis used the coordinates present in the processed records. These coordinates represent the location associated with the death record and do not necessarily represent the person's primary residence. We therefore interpret pollution assigned at these coordinates as a spatial exposure proxy at the incident or death location rather than as a complete residential exposure history.

The Medical Examiner's file contains demographic information for each death, including exact age at death, gender, race, and ethnicity. Ages were binned into ranges for comparison with Census tables and vital statistics reports, and the gender, race, and ethnicity fields were one-hot encoded to True/False values for ingestion into statistical tests.

Causes of death are listed in freeform text fields and are subject to misspellings and inconsistent transcription. Regular expressions were used to identify mentions of common disease categories across the primary and secondary cause fields. The COVID classification was derived from these same fields. Consequently, the disease indicators describe conditions mentioned in the death record rather than independently ascertained preexisting conditions. They were excluded from the primary adjusted model to avoid using variables derived from the outcome source as baseline covariates. Analyses using these disease mentions and incident-setting variables are treated separately as descriptive classification of Medical Examiner records and are not interpreted as estimates of pre-existing disease risk.

PM2.5 data WUSTL GEOS Chem model

The Atmospheric Composition Analysis Group at Washington University in St. Louis provides gridded estimates of surface-level fine particulate matter by combining satellite remote sensing with the GEOS-Chem chemical transport model and calibrating the estimates to ground-based observations [23, 24]. We used the V5. GL.02 monthly North American PM2.5 product from January 2000 through December 2020. The estimates are gridded at 0.01° × 0.01°, which in Cook County equates to cells roughly 1.1 km north-to-south by 0.8 km east-to-west. Each death was assigned the value for the grid cell containing its recorded coordinates, and longterm exposure for each look-back interval was calculated as the mean of the available monthly estimates [25].

To compare average PM2.5 exposure between persons who died from COVID-19 and the general population, an areal interpolation of the PM2.5 surface estimates to Census block group was conducted, because a simple spatial mean gives equal weight to unpopulated or less dense areas and so underestimates population exposure. The geographic centroid of each of the 3,993 Cook County Census block groups was assigned to the appropriate grid cell; this is efficient because block groups are small relative to grid cells and the PM2.5 gradient between cells is not steep within the study area [26] (Supplementary Figure S1).

NO2 data EPA CMAQ model and AQS monitors

The Community Multiscale Air Quality (CMAQ) modeling system is an open-source EPA project that provides hourly estimates of pollutant concentrations on the WRF 12 km simulation grid. In response to pandemic changes in activity and emissions, EPA's Office of Research and Development produced a specialized CMAQ model for the continental United States that adjusts the base model using INRIX vehicle-miles-traveled data [27]. The retained hourly CMAQ input covered January 1 through July 31, 2020 (local conversion begins December 31, 2019), while deaths were drawn from the March-July first-wave period. The grid was sliced to Cook County and the appropriate cell assigned to each death. To keep the centered ±14-day randomization window complete, deaths later than the maximum CMAQ timestamp minus 14 days were excluded. The January start provided complete coverage for the longest 168-hour look-back and the lower randomization boundary [28].

The Air Quality System (AQS) is EPA's national network of ground monitoring stations. In Cook County, the only urban-scale NO2 monitor (site #4201) was offline for most of 2020. Two neighborhood-scale monitors (sites #76 and #4002), each with a 4,000 m measurement scale, are the source of hourly observational NO2 data used here; observational data were restricted to residents within 4,000 m of a station, consistent with the calibrated monitoring scale. The remaining sites are near "trouble spots" (O'Hare, major highways) and are not independently and identically distributed, so CMAQ was used for county-wide NO2 estimates rather than interpolating those sites [29].

The AQS NO2 series showed strong diurnal, weekly, and seasonal structure, which informed the temporal randomization analysis.

Logistic model

The logistic model was used to test whether modeled long-term PM2.5 exposure distinguished records classified as COVID deaths from contemporaneous non-COVID natural deaths after accounting for demographic differences. The response variable indicated whether COVID was recorded in the cause-of-death fields. Predictors were age band, sex, recorded race, Latino ethnicity, and the modeled 2020 five-year mean PM2.5 concentration assigned to the incident or death location. Ages were divided into eight bands (<20, 20-29, 30-39, 40-49, 50-59, 60-69, 70-79, and ≥80 years); reference categories were 50-59 years, male sex, White race, and not Latino. The PM2.5 odds ratio was estimated per 1 µg/m³. For context, we also report the same coefficient per pooled within-group SD of 0.303 µg/m³; this is an algebraic rescaling, not a refitted model.

Models were fitted to complete cases using observed records, an intercept, and no resampling or feature selection. For records with non-missing PM2.5 exposure data, only 17 were excluded because of incomplete demographic covariates (8 COVID and 9 non-COVID; ~0.1% of records). The contemporaneous primary model included 15,010 deaths (10,887 COVID and 4,123 non-COVID). The sensitivity model used the same predictors and COVID cases but substituted non-COVID natural deaths from 2015-2019, yielding 22,935 complete records (10,887 COVID and 12,048 non-COVID). Results are reported as odds ratios with 95% confidence intervals and two-sided p-values; model fit is summarized using McFadden's pseudo-R². Mean PM2.5 exposure was also compared directly between COVID and non-COVID deaths using a two-sided Welch t-test. Analysis-specific cohorts, date windows, exposure coverage, and denominators are summarized in Supplementary Table S1. Denominators differ because exposure availability and complete-case requirements differ among analyses.

Monte Carlo simulation of exposure

We used a series of Monte Carlo analyses to compare observed pollution exposure in each death group with the expected exposure of its age-adjusted countywide population. Since Monte Carlo simulations approximate the full range and likelihood of possible outcomes, the results are superior to single-point estimates for risk assessments [30]. This approach requires us to spatially and temporally weight the population to match the demographic characteristics of the corresponding death group.

We used the Cook County Medical Examiner's data to construct a probabilistic age distribution for each death group. We then built the spatial probability distribution using areal interpolation of Census block groups to the grid cells in our air quality data, assigning a pro rata population proportion by age range to each grid cell.

A series of Monte Carlo analyses was conducted for each death group to compare actual NO2 exposure at various time intervals in the week prior to death versus hypothetical exposure using a randomly selected hour of death occurring in a 28-day period centered at the actual time of death. The NO2 data contain hourly observations for each cell, allowing us to estimate mean exposure in the 24, 48, 72, 96, 120, 144, and 168 hours prior to death. The inclusion of temporal randomness allows us to account for diurnal variability in NO2.

Comparing expected and observed PM2.5 is somewhat simpler, as PM2.5 does not have the periodic variability of NO2, and so only requires spatial weighting. The PM2.5 data contain monthly observations for each cell, allowing us to use the Monte Carlo analyses to estimate the mean exposure of each death group in the 12, 24, 36, 48, 60, 72, 84, 120, 180, and 240 months prior to death, which is then compared with the corresponding weighted population for the same period.

For deaths occurring after December 2020, WUSTL look-back means used the available monthly estimates through December 2020; unavailable later months were excluded from both the observed and simulated means.

We also calculated hourly average NO2 values for 2020 and 2021 from EPA site #4002. Annual AQS PM2.5 values were calculated from the available Cook County-area monitoring data and population-weighted by the monitoring areas used in the spatial workflow. The retained annual AQS series contains no values for 2011-2013. The corresponding WUSTL series gives population-weighted annual means across Cook County (Figure 1).

We used a simulated sample size equal to the number of deaths in the relevant group and conducted each Monte Carlo analysis with 10,000 trials to provide a practical balance between computational efficiency and stable estimation. Locations were drawn from the age-adjusted, population-weighted spatial distribution; the NO2 analyses also randomized time by drawing an hour from a uniform 28-day window centered on the actual time of death, whereas the PM2.5 analyses required only spatial randomization. Since the hypothesis predicts higher exposure, we calculated the empirical right-tail p-value as the proportion of simulated means at least as large as the observed mean, and calculated the Z-score as the difference between the observed and expected means divided by the standard deviation of the simulated means.

For the NO2 robustness analysis, observed exposure at the recorded death locations and times was compared with two simulated reference distributions. First, reference locations were randomized using population weights while each death's timing was retained, testing the spatial exposure contrast at the observed death times. Second, reference times were randomized within the centered 28-day window while each death's location was retained, testing whether exposure immediately before death was unusually high relative to nearby times at the same locations.

ChatGPT was used to assist with manuscript editing, formatting, and checks of internal consistency.

Results

Logistic model

Using natural-cause deaths occurring during the COVID-19 study period as the comparison group, mean fiveyear PM2.5 exposure was slightly lower among COVID-19 deaths than among non-COVID deaths (difference = -0.049 µg/m³, 95% CI -0.059 to -0.039; two-sided Welch p = 1.12 × 10⁻²¹). In the primary adjusted model containing only variables measured independently of the cause-of-death text-age, sex, recorded race, Latino ethnicity, and modeled PM2.5 exposure-higher PM2.5 was associated with lower odds that a Medical Examiner record was classified as a COVID rather than a non-COVID death (OR = 0.63 per 1 µg/m³, 95% CI 0.55-0.72; p < 0.001). The same coefficient corresponds to OR = 0.87 (95% CI 0.83-0.91) per pooled within-group SD of 0.303 µg/m³. As a robustness check addressing possible undiagnosed COVID deaths in the contemporaneous comparison group, we repeated the same analysis using natural-cause deaths from 2015-2019. The association remained negative (OR = 0.54, 95% CI 0.48-0.60; p < 0.001) (Table 1).

Monte Carlo simulation of exposure

The modeled WUSTL PM2.5 and CMAQ NO2 analyses showed exposures above the respective populationweighted expectations for COVID-19 deaths. However, similar analyses for non-COVID deaths showed equal or larger departures from expectation, indicating that the pattern was not specific to COVID-19.

Both COVID and non-COVID deaths were associated with locations above their population-weighted WUSTL expectations at all examined time periods (Figure 2, Table 2). The absolute observed-minus-expected differences were small (approximately 0.02-0.14 µg/m³), but the large group sizes produced tight simulated distributions of the group means and WUSTL z-scores above 5. The smaller AQS data set showed a less consistent pattern.

The long-term decline in expected PM2.5 concentrations (Table 2) is the result of a secular decline in PM2.5 across the study domain (Figure 1). The population-weighted WUSTL annual mean fell from 16.2 µg/m³ in 2000 to 8.8 µg/m³ in 2020, and the 2020 mean was below the lower bound of the approximate 95% exposure band for 2000. The population-weighted AQS series closely tracked WUSTL where available; the retained AQS annual series has no values for 2011-2013. The 12 µg/m³ annual primary standard shown for the study period was revised to 9 µg/m³ in 2024 [31].

The relationship between deaths and short-term NO2 exposure shows less of a pattern than that shown in the PM2.5 case (Figure 3). For COVID deaths, the AQS point estimates did not exceed the upper bound of the corresponding central 95% simulation interval at any time frame. Some AQS comparisons for non-COVID natural deaths did exceed the interval, including the 24-hour mean and the mean and maximum values for the 48-through 168-hour periods. The county-wide CMAQ results showed modest but statistically detectable excess exposure for COVID deaths across the 24-through 168-hour periods (mean differences of 0.13-0.19 ppb; empirical p = 0.043 to <0.001). However, excesses were also present, and were consistently larger, for contemporaneous non-COVID natural deaths (0.82-0.99 ppb; all empirical p < 0.0001) (Table 3).

Temporal variability

NO2 is periodic in the data at the daily, weekly and seasonal scales, as is observed in the AQS tower data. The average daily high occurs at 6am and is 19.9 ± 11.1 ppb; the average daily low occurs at 2pm and is 8.5 ± 5.1 ppb. Weekdays have higher values (14.6 ± 9.8 ppb) than weekends (12.0 ± 9.4 ppb). There is also a strong seasonal component, with winter values averaging 18.1 ± 11.0 ppb and summer values averaging 11.3 ± 8.2 ppb. These patterns describe general temporal variability in NO2; the temporal randomization analysis separately tested whether exposure immediately before death was unusually high relative to nearby times at the same locations.

The AQS observations show the same broad temporal pattern as WUSTL, with population-weighted annual means declining from 17.1 µg/m³ in 2000 to 8.8 µg/m³ in 2020 (Figure 1).

Two robustness checks indicate that the pollution results are not evidence of an effect specific to COVID deaths. First, the negative association between five-year PM2.5 exposure and classification as a COVID rather than non-COVID natural death was retained when the comparison group was restricted to natural-cause deaths from 2015-2019, eliminating possible contamination by undiagnosed COVID deaths in the contemporaneous comparison group (OR = 0.54, 95% CI 0.48-0.60; p < 0.001). Second, observed CMAQ NO2 exposure exceeded the reference exposure obtained by randomizing locations while retaining each death's timing (meanexposure z-scores 2.70-5.20 across the 24-through 168-hour periods). When reference times were randomized while each death's location was retained, observed mean exposure showed no positive excess (z = -1.80 to -0.44). These comparisons support a spatial exposure contrast rather than unusually high NO2 exposure immediately before death.

Discussion

Logistic model

The logistic model found that age was the clearest demographic factor distinguishing COVID from non-COVID natural deaths. The odds that a record was classified as COVID increased steadily with age, and were also higher for women and Latino decedents (Table 1). This is consistent with previous work showing that age and other demographic characteristics were important predictors of COVID-19 mortality [32]. It should be noted that our analysis differs from the earlier study in that it compares COVID and non-COVID natural deaths: it does not estimate the population-level risk of dying from COVID-19.

In contrast, our results did not find evidence that long-term PM2.5 exposure distinguished COVID from non-COVID natural deaths in the direction predicted by the double-hit hypothesis. The adjusted relationship was negative in both the contemporaneous analysis and the pre-pandemic robustness check. This does not imply that PM2.5 exposure is protective. Rather, within this sample, long-term PM2.5 was not more strongly associated with COVID deaths than with other natural deaths. This argues against the "double-hit" hypothesis, which privileges the role of PM2.5 in COVID-19 mortality.

Monte Carlo simulation of exposure

If the double-hit hypothesis is true, we would expect higher than expected long-term PM2.5 exposure and higher than expected short-term exposures to NO2. Modeled PM2.5 exposure values are higher than expected for COVID-19 deaths, with very high Z-scores regardless of which long-term time frame is considered most important. Considered alone, this is consistent with the double-hit hypothesis. But the same pattern occurs among non-COVID deaths, and the direct comparison found slightly lower five-year PM2.5 exposure among COVID deaths. Thus, particulate exposure does not have the stronger relationship with COVID deaths that the hypothesis predicts.

The NO2 exposure analysis is also not consistent with the double-hit hypothesis. The AQS observations did not show excess exposure among COVID deaths. The CMAQ model found a small positive excess for COVID deaths, but the excess was consistently larger for non-COVID deaths. These simulations compare each group with its own population-weighted expectation and are not a direct statistical test between the groups. We therefore treat the difference in excesses as descriptive rather than as a formal between-group test; even so, the pattern is not specific to COVID deaths.

The CMAQ robustness analysis helps distinguish spatial exposure contrasts from acute temporal elevations. At the observed death times, NO2 exposure at death locations exceeded the population-weighted expectation from randomized reference locations. At the observed death locations, however, exposure immediately before death did not exceed the expectation from randomized nearby reference times. The findings therefore support a spatial interpretation of the association and provide no evidence of unusually elevated NO2 exposure immediately before COVID deaths under this comparison. They do not establish whether the spatial association reflects pollution exposure, other characteristics of the death locations, or both.

The mortality associations with PM2.5 do not by themselves establish a special COVID-19 mechanism. It appears likely from our data that PM2.5 and NO2 exposure are spatial proxies for other health impacts. When we look at PM2.5 exposure alone we find a strong and consistent connection with mortality, but in the logistic model, after accounting for demographic differences, PM2.5 did not distinguish COVID from non-COVID deaths in the predicted direction. This does not imply that air pollution has no health effects. It indicates that the observed pollution associations do not provide evidence for the double-hit hypothesis.

This interpretation is also consistent with the descriptive exposure pattern for deaths not ordinarily attributed to long-term PM2.5 exposure: mean five-year PM2.5 at locations of accidental, gun-related, and opioid-related deaths was as high as or higher than at COVID death locations (Supplementary Figure S2).

The modeled PM2.5 and NO2 data (WUSTL and CMAQ) yield consistently stronger exposure/mortality relationships than the AQS observations do (Figures 2, 3). This is not surprising as the modeled area includes the entire domain while the AQS values are restricted to a small fraction of the area, and so capture fewer deaths (Supplementary Figure S1). The AQS analyses were included as observational complements to the spatially complete modeled products. AQS monitor availability resulted in missing data and limited geographic coverage, particularly for NO2 (Supplementary Table S2); consequently, the AQS results should be interpreted as sensitivity analyses rather than population-representative exposure estimates. Our experience highlights a challenge for environmental health studies: where the observational data is limited by issues of data quality and geographic coverage, such data lacks sufficient spatial and temporal resolution to find subtle effects.

Limitations

These data have properties that reduce their precision. Pollution values were assigned to incident or death-record locations and are spatial proxies rather than measurements of personal exposure. Time spent elsewhere, indoor exposure, and residential moves will therefore introduce error, particularly for long-term PM2.5 and short-term NO2 exposure. This is particularly relevant for NO2, as those who died from COVID-19 may have spent a final period of time indoors or in a hospital, where ambient values at the recorded location may not represent actual exposure. The available data for short-term CMAQ NO2 analysis was restricted to the first pandemic wave (March-July 2020) and may not generalize to subsequent conditions. The fixed five-year PM2.5 measure also varied over a narrow range within Cook County. This constrains interpretation of effect size and generalization to larger exposure contrasts, although the coefficient itself was estimated precisely.

The Medical Examiner's records are also not a complete sample of deaths in Cook County. The logistic model therefore estimates which characteristics distinguish COVID from non-COVID natural deaths within this sample; it does not estimate the population-level risk of dying from COVID-19. Some contemporaneous non-COVID deaths may have been undiagnosed COVID deaths, although the pre-pandemic robustness check produced the same negative PM2.5 relationship. The separate recent non-COVID PM Monte Carlo group (December 31, 2018-December 31, 2021) also overlaps the pandemic period and may include undiagnosed COVID deaths; it is treated as a negative-comparison group rather than the primary exchangeable control group. Finally, the Monte Carlo simulations compare each group with its own population-weighted expectation and are not a direct statistical test between the COVID and non-COVID groups.

We found no evidence that air pollution associations predicted by the "double-hit" hypothesis explained COVID-19 deaths among Cook County Medical Examiner records. Age and demographic characteristics clearly distinguished COVID from non-COVID natural deaths within the Medical Examiner's records, while long-term PM2.5 did not distinguish the groups in the predicted direction and the short-term CMAQ NO2 analysis showed a spatial exposure contrast without evidence of unusually high exposure immediately before death. These results do not imply that air pollution has no health effects, but rather reinforce the need for high-quality data in studies that search for acute and long-term air-quality effects. Future studies using verified residential histories, individual-level covariates, population-based or prospective cohorts, or observational air pollution data with greater spatial and temporal coverage could more directly test whether acute and chronic pollution exposures are associated with cause-specific mortality risk.
