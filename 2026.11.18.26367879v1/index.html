How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 128 papers with 246 instances of power statements, 203 were correctly detected (true positives), 22 were missed (false negatives) and 21 were incorrectly detected (false positives). Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Marginal Significance works

List all sentences that describe an effect as ‘marginally significant’.

The marginal module searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The list of terms is a subset of those listed in a blog post by Matthew Hankins. The module returns all sentences that match terms describing ‘marginally significant’ results.

Some of the terms identified might not be problematic in some contexts, and there are ways to describe ‘marginal significance’ that are not detected by the module.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

However, the module missed 42% of all true statements (false negative rate).

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

Abstract

Alcohol use disorder (AUD) is common, heritable (h2 ~50%) and polygenic, but the contribution of rare structural variation is largely unexamined. Using short-read whole-genome sequencing (srWGS) data from the All of Us Research Program, we tested the association of rare copy number variants (rCNV; minor allele frequency <1%) with AUD in 56,062 participants (5,410 cases, 50,652 controls) defined from electronic health records. Analyses were conducted within European-like (EUR; N=29,392), African-like (AFR; N=8,269) and Admixed American-like (AMR; N=8,049) samples and combined by cross-ancestry meta-analysis. We tested genome-wide rCNV burden, burden within AUD-related, mouse behavioral and neuropsychiatric gene sets, and individual rCNV regions. No genome-wide burden metric or individual rCNV region ≥10 kb was associated with AUD after false discovery rate (FDR) correction. Significant gene-set results were almost exclusively duplications associated with reduced AUD risk across biologically unrelated sets, suggesting a global duplication dosage effect rather than pathway-specific enrichment. In contrast, deletion count within a Neurof_UnionStringent (Neurofunction Union Stringent) gene set was associated with increased AUD risk (OR=1.04, FDR=0.044), and deletions in xenobiotic-response genes showed nominal associations in multiple ancestry groups. Among rCNV of all sizes, a 58 bp deletion at 16p12.1, a dosage-sensitive neuropsychiatric locus, was significant in the cross-ancestry analysis (OR=2.93, FDR=0.030) without significant heterogeneity. rCNV detectable by srWGS do not appear to contribute substantially to AUD liability at current sample sizes.

Introduction

Alcohol use disorder (AUD) is a chronic condition, where individuals continue to drink excessively despite negative health and social consequences (1). AUD is a major cause of both morbidity and mortality but often goes undiagnosed and untreated (2, 3). In 202https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?Axk5iA, approximately 29.5 million individuals in the United States aged 12 and older met the Diagnostic (https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?2EXYn6) and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (DSM-5) (1) criteria for AUD, leading to roughly 178,000 deaths (3). The lifetime prevalence of AUD is estimated at 29.1%, with nearly half of the cases classified as severe. Despite this high prevalence, <8.7% of those diagnosed received any form of treatment for AUD (5).

The genetic architecture of AUD is complex and polygenic. AUD has moderate twin-based heritability (h 2 ) of ~50% (https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?Yka9tn and large-scale genome-wide association studies (GWAS) have identified associated loci (7) (8) (9) (10). The largest GWAS of alcohol-related conditions, using a phenotype termed problematic alcohol use (PAU, including both self-report and formal diagnosis), identified more than 110 independent risk variants in 90 loci (11). Despite these successes, important gaps remain in elucidating the genetic etiology of AUD. First, even with large samples, the estimated single-nucleotide polymorphism (SNP)-based heritability (h²SNP) of AUD and PAU is relatively low (5.6% to 10.0%), indicating substantial missing heritability. Secondhttps://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?LxZqLp although samples of African ancestry are now large enough to yield significant within-ancestry signals (11, 12) , the majority of samples used in human genetic studies, including those focused on AUD, are derived from European ancestry. This lack of ancestral diversity limits both our understanding of the genetic basis of these traits and the generalizability of findings to global populations (8, 11). Third, most associated variants are noncoding and have small effect sizes, complicating the identification of causal genes (13). Finally, AUD samples are wellpowered for common variants, but lack power for rarer variation, potentially overlooking relevant genes (14).

Rare variant analyses of AUD and problematic drinking have also primarily focused on European populations. Whole-exome sequence (WES) data from the UK Biobank (UKB) identified 25 protein-coding variants and 13 genes associated with alcohol consumption (14). Gene-level analysis of the Alcohol Use Disorders Identification Test problem subscale (AUDIT-P) in the UKB WES data showed that rare variation in ADH1C and THRA is associated with AUDIT-P (15). A recent WES study identified additional genes enriched for ultrarare variation in AUD cases (16). However, analyses of copy number variants (CNV) in AUD have previously been limited to small samples and array-based CNV (17) (18) (19). Specific rare CNV (rCNV; MAF <1%) display large effect sizes in psychiatric and neurodevelopmental disorders (20https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?WsY7hS 21) including autism (22https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?nXteUa 23) https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?vmgFhC schizophrenia (24) https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?URbTRC epilepsy (25) , intellectual disability (26) , and brain structure (27). Most prior CNV studies used SNP microarray intensity data, but the release of biobank-scale whole-genome sequence (WGS) datasets allow for large-scale WGS-based CNV studies, including of previously understudied traits like AUD, as we present here.

Phenotypic heterogeneity remains a central challenge in genetic studies of AUD. As in other complex psychiatric traits (28) https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?23hwHv phenotypic definition heavily influences genetic discoveries (8). While most AUD GWAS participants were formally ascertained cases and controls based on DSM or International Classification of Diseases (ICD) criteria, the moderate h²SNP and limited number of associated genomic loci motivate the inclusion of population biobank samples to increase discovery power. For example, quantitative assessments such as the AUDIT are available for a subset of UKB participants, and GWAS of intake/frequency (AUDIT-C) and negative consequences (AUDIT-P) yield distinct genetic architectures (9, 29, 30). The phenotypic challenge is particularly evident in defining AUD using electronic health records (EHR), where formal diagnoses of AUD may be absent, but other EHR data (like prescription data) suggest an individual is affected, potentially leading to misclassification (31).

Here, we used EHR-based phenotyping and WGS data from the All of Us Research Program (AoU) V8 data release to investigate rCNV associated with AUD. We first performed whole-genome burden analyseshttps://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?FsEdvz followed by gene-set burden analyses using genes implicated in 1) AUD (11) , 2) behavioral phenotypes in mouse models (32) (33) (34) and 3) curated gene-sets from a study of CNV in post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) (35). We estimated associations of rCNV burden and individual rCNV with AUD within three genetic ancestry groups (European-like [EUR], African-like [AFR], and Admixed American-like [AMR]) followed by crossancestry meta-analyses of both burden and region-level summary statistics. We also performed rCNV metaanalyses of both burden and region-level summary statistics across ancestries to increase statistical power to detect significant biological pathways associated with AUD. Additionally, we performed an exploratory analysis using trans-ancestry assignments (https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?3OuflR) (Figure 1, Supplementary Figure 1). To our knowledge, this is the first large-scale, WGS-based rCNV analysis of AUD conducted across multiple ancestries.

Data and Methods

Participants and phenotyping AUD cases were defined by the International Classification of Diseases, 9th/10th Revision, Clinical Modification (ICD-9/10-CM) diagnostic codes for AUD and chronic alcohol-related conditions (Supplementary Table 1), or by prescriptions for acamprosate or disulfiram in the absence of such codes (n = 136) (37, 38). Controls had no AUD-related diagnoses or prescriptions and reported at least one lifetime alcoholic drink in the AoUs survey, thereby excluding individuals who reported lifetime abstinence. Individuals prescribed naltrexone without an AUD diagnosis (n = 2,695) were excluded to avoid confounding with opioid use disorder (39). After phenotypic and genomic quality control (QC), 22,385 cases and 347,195 controls remained. Of these, 56,062 (5410 cases and 50,652 controls) had CNV calls passing CNV-specific quality-control (Supplementary Methods for details about phenotyping, CNV curation and detection, Supplementary Figure 2).

Genetic Ancestry Assignment

We reassigned AoU participants to continental ancestry groups using the population grouping by Mahalanobis distance (POP-MaD) approach (https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?z9YCMx) with the same reference of merged 1000 Genomes Project and Human Genome Diversity Project data (36, 41) https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?9Z0pED assignment, outlier pruning, and computation of principal components (PCs) are described in detail in the Supplementary Methods. Because only a subset of AoU participants have CNV calls, the East Asian-like (EAS), Central and South Asian-like (CSA), Middle Eastern-like (MID), and Oceania-like (OCE) groups each had fewer than 1000 individuals, so within-ancestry analyses included only the EUR, AFR and AMR groups (Table 1) (Supplementary Figure 3, Supplementary Table 2). CNV burden analysis CNV burden was assessed at both the genome-wide and gene-set levels, following a previously published analytic framework (35). CNV burden was quantified using multiple complementary metrics, including: (1) the cumulative length in megabases (Mb) of all rare duplications and deletions analyzed separately; (2) the number of rare duplications and deletions considered separately; and (3) the aggregate length of all duplications and deletions combined, and evaluated for association with AUD using Firth's logistic regression.

We initially analyzed CNV ≥10 Kb due to their greater potential for pathogenicity, and then rCNV of any size.

We conducted genome-wide and gene-set burden analyses within the three largest ancestry groups (EUR, AFR, AMR), meta-analyzed the within-ancestry results, and performed a trans-ancestry analysis of all samples pooled together (rationale in Supplementary Methods).

Genome-wide burden analysis

We conducted whole-genome rCNV burden analyses for both length and count of deletions and duplications separately, and cumulative length of all rCNV metrics using Firth's logistic regression, adjusted for the first ten genetic principal components to control for population stratification. We tested three covariate models for genome-wide burden analyses: 1) the first ten PCs only; 2) the first ten PCs, age (at blood draw), sex; and 3) the first ten PCs, age, sex, total rCNV count, and average rCNV length (Supplementary Figure 4). Effect estimates were broadly consistent across models. Following previous studies (35, 42) we report Model 3 as the primary model in the main text because it is the most conservative, additionally accounting for individual differences in global rCNV load.

Gene-set burden analysis

At the gene-set level, analyses quantified the extent to which rCNV overlapped individual genes and predefined gene sets. To map genes to each identified CNV to calculate burden in Mb, we used the GENCODE Release 46 (GRCh38.p14) (https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?BMSwNk) genic intervals (Supplementary Table 10). Each CNV was assigned to any genes sharing at least one base pair of overlap (35).

Gene-set burden analysis included 5 AUD/PAU related gene sets from a previous PAU study (https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?93NH9q) plus a combination of all five setshttps://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?vKy5j9 146 abnormal behavior gene sets derived from mouse mutants with central nervous system (CNS) phenotypes (32) (33) (34) , 73 curated sets tested in the PGC PTSD study and one negative control cholesterol gene-set, previously shown to have no genetic association with PAU (11) (Supplementary Table 11). Each CNV was assigned to all genes with which it shared at least one base pair of overlap. For each gene, the total number of overlapping CNV and the extent of base pair overlap were calculated (Supplementary Table 10). Gene burden was encoded as a binary variable, where individuals were assigned a value of 1 if at least one CNV overlapped the gene and 0 otherwise.

We adjusted for average rCNV length and total rCNV count to reduce confounding by background genomic instability. Total rCNV count accounts for differences in the rate of rCNV formation between cases and controls, whereas average rCNV length accounts for differences in the size of the events themselves (https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?nxko1R) (Supplementary Methods). Our model followed previously established frameworks (35) and tested five geneset level burden metrics: deletion/duplication count, deletion/duplication length, and total length within each gene set. For each metric, we fitted Firth's logistic regression model of the following form, adjusting for genome-wide average rCNV length, genome-wide total rCNV count, the first ten within-ancestry genetic PCs, age, and sex.

Individual rCNV meta-analysis

Association results for individual CNV regions in the EUR, AFR, and AMR ancestry groups were metaanalyzed using METAL (https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?rnbzWb) with the sample-size weighted scheme. To account for imbalanced case-control ratios, the effective sample size (𝑁 𝑒𝑓𝑓 ; Supplementary Methods) was used as the sample size for each study. Genomic control correction was applied. Between-ancestry heterogeneity was assessed using Cochran's Q and I². Significance was assessed after FDR correction across all regions tested within CNV size thresholds (all CNV sizes and ≥10kb CNV).

Results

We analyzed rCNV called from AoU srWGS data on 56,062 participants passing CNV QC, comprising 5,410 AUD cases and 50,652 controls. Ancestry-stratified analyses were conducted in the three largest groups, EUR (N = 29,392; 2,304 cases and 27,088 controls), AFR (N = 8,269; 1,276 cases and 6,993 controls) and AMR (N = 8,049; 805 cases and 7,244 controls), together with a trans-ancestry analysis of all pooled samples (N = 56,062; 5,410 AUD cases and 50,652 controls). Because of their greater potential for pathogenicity and because short calls are the most sensitive to breakpoint uncertainty in short-read data, rCNV of at least 10 kb were treated as the primary analysis set, and all CNV sizes were analyzed secondarily. We evaluated rCNV at five levels of resolution: 1) genome-wide rCNV burden, and burden within 2) six curated AUD and PAU gene sets, 3) 146 abnormal mouse behavior gene sets, and 4) 73 curated gene sets from a previous PGC PTSD study of CNV; and 5) association of individual rCNV regions with AUD.

Genome-wide rCNV burden

First we asked whether the total genome-wide load of rCNV differed between cases and controls, using five burden metrics (deletion, duplication and total burden in Mb, and deletion and duplication counts) adjusted for the first ten within-ancestry principal components. For rCNV of ≥10 kb, no metric was associated with AUD in any ancestry group or in the trans-ancestry analysis (all FDR > 0.40), and the cross-ancestry meta-analysis was also null (Supplementary Tables 3 and 4). When CNV of all sizes were included, deletion count was associated with AUD in the EUR sample (OR = 1.004, 95% CI 1.003-1.005, P = 9.7 × 10 -9 , FDR = 4.9 × 10 -8 ) and in the trans-ancestry analysis (OR = 1.0014, 95% CI 1.0007-1.0021, P = 4.4 × 10 -5 , FDR = 2.2 × 10 -4 ), with no corresponding signal in the AFR (P = 0.90) or AMR (P = 0.54) samples. The meta-analysis of deletion count across the three ancestries was significant (Stouffer Z = 4.90, Pmeta = 9.4 × 10 -7 , FDR = 0.002) but highly heterogeneous (I² = 90.1%, P heterogeneity < 0.001), indicating that the result was driven by the EUR sample. Because this signal was confined to the unrestricted size category, in which short calls predominate, and disappeared when the analysis was limited to rCNV ≥10 kb, we regard it as most consistent with ancestryspecific differences in the detection of short deletions rather than with a genuine excess of genome-wide deletion load in cases (Supplementary Table 5). In the EUR-like sample, duplication count for rCNV of all sizes was nominally significant before, but not after, multiple-testing correction (OR = 1.0011, 95% CI 1.0002 to 1.0021, P = 2.3 × 10 -2 , FDR = 5.7 × 10 -2 ). We did not find any significant burden length variables in any test (Table 2).

Burden across abnormal mouse behavior and curated gene sets from PGC PTSD CNV study

Each of the gene sets was tested with five burden metrics per ancestry group and CNV size category. For rCNV of at least 10 kb in size, no test reached FDR significance in the EUR, AFR, or AMR samples analyzed separately. The strongest results in the EUR sample were inverse associations with duplication metrics in the abnormal mouse behavior sets: human postsynaptic density core set (burden OR = 0.60, 95% CI 0.47-0.77, P = 7.4 × 10 -5 , FDR = 0.057; count OR = 0.89, 95% CI 0.83 to 0.94, P = 1.5 × 10 -4 , FDR = 0.057), the postsynaptic density Bayesian set (count OR = 0.93, 95% CI 0.90-0.97, P = 1.4 × 10 -4 , FDR = 0.057) and the abnormal nervous system development set (burden OR = 0.65, 95% CI 0.51-0.81, P = 2.1 × 10 -4 , FDR = 0.061) (Table 3). No test approached significance in the AFR or AMR samples, where the smallest FDR values were 0.9999 and 0.998, respectively.

In the trans-ancestry analysis, which carries approximately twice the case count of the EUR-like sample, 91 of 1,165 tests were significant after FDR correction at the 10 kb threshold, distributed across 74 gene sets (Supplementary Tables 6 and 7). The signal was largely driven by duplication metrics with uniformly betas uniformly <0 (inverse in direction), with duplication count significant in 69 sets and duplication burden in 21.

The strongest associations were with abnormal nervous system development (burden OR = 0.75, 95% CI 0.65 to 0.86, P = 2.8 × 10 -5 , FDR = 0.012; count OR = 0.93, 95% CI 0.90-0.96, P = 3.1 × 10 -5 , FDR = 0.012), genes with high-to-medium Brain-Span expression (BspanHM_lg2rpkm3.21: Genes with at least 5 BrainSpan data points log2 (rpkm) >= 3.21, but not in BspanVH_lg2rpkm4.74) (count OR = 0.98, 95% CI 0.97 to 0.99, P = 2.9 × 10 -5 , FDR = 0.012), abnormal associative learning (count OR = 0.83, 95% CI 0.76 to 0.91, P = 1.3 × 10 -4 , FDR = 0.017) and abnormal social or conspecific interaction (count OR = 0.88, 95% CI 0.83 to 0.94, P = 7.0 × 10 -5 , FDR = 0.017). Only one deletion metric passed correction: deletion count within the stringent neurofunction union set was associated with increased odds of AUD (OR = 1.04, 95% CI 1.01 to 1.06, P = 2.9 × 10 -3 , FDR = 0.044) (Supplementary Figure 5).

For deletions it was null (trans-ancestry deletion burden OR = 1.00, P = 0.93 for rCNVs ≥10 kb) . For duplications it was also non-significant, although its estimates lay in the same inverse direction and within the range of the sets that reached significance (duplication count OR = 0.89, P = 0.16); however, at 104 genes, it is an order of magnitude smaller than the largest sets tested.

The deletion results followed a narrower and more interpretable pattern. Deletion count within the abnormal behavioural response to xenobiotic set, a category that captures organismal responses to foreign chemical compounds including alcohol, was nominally associated with increased odds of AUD in the EUR sample (OR = 1.32, 95% CI 1.09 to 1.60, P = 4.9 × 10 -3 , FDR = 0.095), the AMR sample (OR = 1.46, 95% CI 1.10 to 1.95, P = 8.5 × 10 -3 , FDR = 0.998) and the trans-ancestry analysis (OR = 1.16, 95% CI 1.03 to 1.30, P = 0.015, FDR = 0.113) (Table 3, Supplementary Table 6).

Association of individual rCNV with AUD

No rCNV region of at least 10 kb was associated with AUD after FDR correction in the EUR, AFR or AMR samples, in the trans-ancestry analysis, or in the cross-ancestry meta-analysis. When rCNV of any size were considered, two deletions reached FDR significance in the trans-ancestry analysis. A 58 bp deletion at 16p12.1 overlapping ENSG00000260580 gave the smallest P value genome-wide (OR = 2.93, 95% CI 1.94 to 4.42, P = 3.06 × 10-7, FDR = 0.030), and a 171 bp intergenic deletion at 2p11.2 was also significant (OR = 14.99, 95% CI 5.15 to 43.65, P = 6.90 × 10-7, FDR = 0.034). The 16p12.1 deletion was likewise the top-ranked region in the EUR sample (OR = 4.18, 95% CI 2.28 to 7.65, P = 3.70 × 10-6, FDR = 0.064), where three further regions approached significance: a 582 bp intergenic duplication at 2p11.2 (OR = 3.42, 95% CI 2.05 to 5.71, P = 2.38 × 10-6, FDR = 0.064), a 735 bp deletion at 2q37.3 overlapping ESPNL (OR = 3.38, 95% CI 2.03 to 5.63, P = 2.99 × 10-6, FDR = 0.064) and a 159 bp deletion at 22q13.31 overlapping TBC1D22A (OR = 7.41, 95% CI 3.13 to 17.56, P = 5.39 × 10-6, FDR = 0.070). No region survived FDR correction in the AFR-like or AMR-like samples, possibly consistent with their smaller case counts. In the cross-ancestry meta-analysis of the three groups, the 16p12.1 deletion again ranked first (Z = 4.81, P = 1.54 × 10⁻⁶, FDR = 0.10), although no region passed correction (FDR < 0.05). There was no significant between-ancestry heterogeneity at this locus (I² = 33.9%, P heterogeneity = 0.22). All implicated events were very short and uncommon, and each conferred a roughly three-fold to fifteen-fold increase in the odds of AUD among carriers (Table 5, Supplementary Figure 8).

The 16p12.1 deletion is of particular interest because it was recovered independently in the trans-ancestry, EUR and meta-analytic results and falls within an established dosage-sensitive locus in which deletions are associated with developmental delay and with a broad range of psychiatric outcomes in both clinical and population-based samples (46) (47) (48). Its convergence across analytic levels, together with the absence of heterogeneity between ancestries, supports prioritising this locus for follow-up in larger AUD samples, although the event tested here is short and the association did not survive correction in any within-ancestry analysis.

Test statistics from the region-level analyses were well calibrated. For rCNV of all sizes, the λ was 1.03 in the trans-ancestry analysis and 1.05, 1.03 and 1.04 in the EUR, AFR and AMR samples, respectively. Restricting to rCNV of at least 10 kb yielded 1.05 in the trans-ancestry analysis, higher values in the EUR (1.21) and AFR (1.16) samples, and no inflation in the AMR sample (0.93); the smaller number of tests and the lower carrier frequency of large events make λ less stable in this category. The cross-ancestry meta-analyses gave λ = 1.05 for all CNV sizes and λ = 1.11 for rCNV of at least 10 kb, with λ1000 of 1.01 in both cases, indicating that the residual deviation from the null reflects sample size rather than uncontrolled population stratification. Taken together, all analyses converge on a consistent account. At the resolution of individual regions, no rCNV of at least 10 kb was associated with AUD after correction in any ancestry group, and the two deletions that reached FDR significance in the trans-ancestry analysis of all CNV sizes were both short events, at 16p12.1 and 2p11.2. At the level of burden, the associations that survived correction were almost exclusively inverse duplication effects recurring across biologically unrelated gene sets. The single deletion result that passed correction, together with the directionally consistent but uncorrected deletion signal in the xenobiotic response set, represents a possible candidate for an AUD-relevant effect. Both require replication in larger and more ancestrally diverse samples before firm conclusions can be drawn.

Discussion

To our knowledge this is the first biobank-scale, WGS-based rCNV analysis of AUD using multi-level EHR phenotyping in 56,062 All of Us participants (5,410 cases, 50,652 controls). For rCNV ≥10 kb no genome-wide burden metric, or individual region was associated with AUD in any single or combined ancestry group after correction for multiple testing. Significant signals were modest and of two kinds. First, at the gene-set level, duplication burden and count were weakly associated with reduced risk of AUD across many gene sets, at both size thresholds, in the trans-ancestry analysis and the cross-ancestry meta-analysis but not within any single ancestry at ≥10 kb. Second, two short deletions (<200 bp) and genome-wide deletion count were associated with AUD only when rCNV of all sizes were included.

Duplications were associated with reduced AUD risk across biologically unrelated gene sets in the transancestry analysis and the cross-ancestry meta-analysis, and three BrainSpan expression sets were significant under both the Stouffer and inverse-variance-weighted frameworks without heterogeneity. The cholesterol negative control was not significant for any metric (minimum FDR = 0.55), but its duplication count estimates fall in the same inverse direction as the significant sets, and it is underpowered to distinguish a global from a specific effect. The most simplest interpretation is that these results reflect a genome-wide property of duplication dosage, or residual confounding with duplication detection, rather than enrichment in AUD-relevant pathways.

The findings for deletions were narrower and more interpretable. Deletion burden was nominally elevated within the combined AUD/PAU gene set and the H-MAGMA brain chromatin-interaction set in the transancestry analysis, deletion count within the Neurof_UnionStringent set survived correction, and deletion count within the abnormal behavioural response to xenobiotic set was nominally associated with AUD in the EUR, AMR and trans-ancestry analyses (Supplementary Tables 6 and 7). This is the pattern expected if deletions confer risk through haploinsufficiency of dosage-sensitive genes, as reported for other psychiatric phenotypes (21, 27, 50, 51) https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?gJwGik however, with one exception, these results were not significant after correction.

The absence of FDR-significant regions is consistent with a highly polygenic disorder in which rare structural variationhttps://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?AmcpQJ if contributory, is distributed across many loci of small effect (11, 24) , and with limited power at the current case count. Two short deletions passed correction in the trans-ancestry analysis of rCNV of all sizes. The 58 bp deletion at 16p12.1 is the more compelling of the two: it also ranked first in the EUR-like and metaanalytic results, showed no between-ancestry heterogeneity, and falls within an established dosage-sensitive locus in which deletions are associated with developmental delay and a broad range of psychiatric outcomes (46) (47) (48). The EUR-like regions that approached significance also lie in or near genes of plausible neurobiological relevance, including TBC1D22A at 22q13.31 (52, 53) and ESPNL at 2q37.3 (54, 55). However all these individual rCNV are short, uncommon events, carrier counts are limiting, and replication in independent, adequately powered samples is required before they can be regarded as AUD loci.

While the current results suggest a modest contribution of rCNVs to AUD, they should be viewed in the context of several limitations. First, phenotyping of AUD from EHR in large-scale biobanks remains a developing area to address differences in healthcare utilization generally, and under-diagnosis and underreporting of AUD specifically. Second, CNV calls passing QC were available for only 56,062 participants in AUD case:control cohort (out of total CDRv8 srWGS 414,830 participants, of whom only 97,061 [23%] had srWGS-SV calls available). Additional verification of rCNVs across differing QC protocols is needed to confirm the robustness of small rCNVs previously undetectable in array data. Finally, the AFR and AMR samples remained underpowered and the EAS, CSA and MID groups were too small to analyse, so our results are still driven largely by EUR participants.

Conclusion

rCNV detectable by short-read WGS do not appear to make a large contribution to AUD liability at this sample size. The 16p12.1 deletion and the xenobiotic-response deletion signal are the most promising candidates for follow-up, and distinguishing genuine pathway enrichment from global duplication load will require larger, more ancestrally diverse CNV datasets with harmonised calling and QC.

Methods

Participants and phenotyping

A major challenge in studying AUD in biobanks is accurately phenotyping participants to select true cases and controls. AUD cases were defined based on the International Classification of Diseases, 9/10th Revision Clinical Modification (ICD-9/10-CM) diagnostic codes corresponding to SNOMED Clinical Terms (CT) concepts related to AUD and chronic alcohol-related conditions (Supplementary Table 1). We additionally identified 136 participants who lacked documented AUD-related diagnostic codes but had received prescriptions for acamprosate or disulfiram, both of which are specifically indicated for the treatment of AUD (https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?S9FuKk. Controls were selected from participants with no recorded AUD-related diagnoses or prescriptions and evidence of alcohol exposure based on the AoUs survey, thereby excluding individuals who reported lifetime abstinence. Individuals who had been prescribed naltrexone in the absence of a corresponding AUD diagnosis (n = 2,695) were excluded to minimize potential misclassification arising from its use in the treatment of opioid use disorder (https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?p5rJbE. Following application of all phenotypic inclusion and exclusion criteria using EHR data in AoU, 29,485 individuals met the criteria for AUD and 483,907 individuals are classified as controls. Subsequent genomic quality-control procedures, including assessment of sex concordance and exclusion of ancestry outliers, yielded 22,385 AUD cases and 347,195 controls. Because CNV data were available for only a subset of participants with qualifying genomic data, the final CNV analysis included 56,062 participants (5410 cases and 50,652 controls) after applying CNV-specific quality-control criteria (Supplementary Figure 2).

Genetic Ancestry Assignment

We reassigned AoU participants to continental ancestry groups using the population grouping by Mahalanobis distance (POP-MaD) approach (https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?CfOg9S) with the same reference of merged 1000 Genomes Project and Human Genome Diversity Project (5, 6) data as used by AoU. An individual was first assigned to the closest reference population defined by minimum Mahalanobis distance from the reference median calculated using the first 10 genetic principal components (PCs). Outliers in each initial group >3 S.D. from the median standardized withingroup Mahalanobis distance were pruned. QC and LD-pruning was done within each assigned ancestry group. PCs were computed with FlashPCA2 (https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?o0v6vrhttps://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?t7raA3 using the unrelated (<3 rd degree, estimated using KING ( 8 )) subset of assigned ancestry groups. Within-ancestry related individuals and ancestry outliers were then projected onto the PCs. For trans-ancestry PCs, the union of unrelated individuals from each assigned ancestry group was used to compute PCs onto which remaining individuals were projected. We identified European-like (EUR), African-like (AFR), Admixed American-like (AMR), East Asian-like (EAS), Central and South Asian-like (CSA), Middle Eastern-like (MID), and Oceania-like (OCE) ancestries. All models included the first 10 within-ancestry PCs, age, and sex at birth. Because only a subset of AoU participants have CNV calls, the subsamples of EAS, CSA, MID, and OCE ancestries were all less than 1000 individuals, so we included only EUR, AFR and AMR groups (Table 1) for within-ancestry analyses (Supplementary Figure 3).

All of Us WGS and CNV detection

This study used data from the All of Us (AoU) Curated Data Repository (CDR) release C2024Q3R3 ("CDRv8"), which includes short-read whole-genome sequencing (srWGS) data for 414,830 participants, produced at the Broad Institute, Baylor College of Medicine, and the University of Washington using Illumina NovaSeq 6000 sequencers. Data processing pipelines and data QC procedures (https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?1GBUP4) (https://support.researchallofus.org/hc/en-us/articles/29390274413716-All-of-Us-Genomic-Quality-Report) have been published. Our analyses were based on the srWGS structural variant (SV) callset, which comprises 97,061 participants and is an updated version of the CDRv7 off-cycle srWGS SV release (97,940 samples). For CDRv8, variant calling was not rerun; instead, dropped samples were removed and additional refinements were applied. Notably, the SV callset was generated from CRAMs aligned with DRAGEN v3.4.12, whereas other srWGS data in CDRv8 used DRAGEN v3.7.8. All samples followed the Consistency across Genome Centers and Single Sample QC processes in the srWGS QC pipeline prior to SV calling. SVs were called with GATK-SV using the previously described pipeline (10) https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?p0Smyz which combines Manta and Wham (paired-end/split-read evidence), MELT (mobile elements), and GATK-gCNV and cn.MOPS (read-depth CNVs). During callset generation, samples were removed for insert size outside 320-700 bp, assessed using Picard (CollectInsertSizeMetrics within GATK's CollectMultipleMetrics; N = 30); contamination ≥1% (N = 296); whole-genome dosage (WGD) scores >6× MAD, because coverage variability affects CNV calling (N = 1,337); >500 non-diploid 1 Mb bins (N = 1,508); and outlier SV counts across ≥30 categories or abnormal chromosome ploidy (copy ratio outside 1.8-2.3; N = 61). We then applied additional filters. Samples flagged for srWGS QC issues (N = 4,044) were excluded prior to all further filtering. We compared the DRAGEN sex-chromosome constitution against self-reported sex assigned at birth, retaining only individuals who self-reported "male" or "female" and had a matching constitution (XY or XX), and removed samples with potential sex-chromosome aneuploidy. Related individuals were excluded using the AoU kinship matrix, so that only unrelated individuals were retained. Finally, we restricted analyses to rare (MAF < 1%) deletions and duplications and excluded all other SV types.

Individual rCNV filtering

CNV region-based association testing (duplications and deletions analyzed separately) was performed in PLINK 2.0 using Firth's logistic regression, adjusting for age, sex, and PCs 1-10. Because regions observed in only a few individuals yield unstable association metrics and inflated test statistics, inference was restricted to regions with a minor allele count (MAC) > 20; regions below this threshold were excluded prior to multipletesting correction. Region-level p-values for the retained tests were corrected using the Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate, and associations passing FDR < [0.05] were reported. The genomic inflation factor was evaluated on the MAC-filtered test set. A total of 863,195 CNVs (DELs plus DUPs) are excluded due to MAC <= 20. To comply with the All of Us Research Program Data and Statistics Dissemination Policy, which prohibits reporting statistics derived from ≤ 20 individuals, we further confirmed that every reported region was supported by more than 20 unique carrier individuals, counting each heterozygous or homozygous carrier once [https://support.researchallofus.org/hc/en-us/articles/360043016291-How-to-comply-with-the-All-of-Us-Data-and-Statistics-Dissemination-Policy].

CNV burden analysis CNV burden was assessed at both the genome-wide and gene-set levels, following a previously published analytic framework (11). CNV burden was quantified using multiple complementary metrics, including: (1) the cumulative length in megabases (Mb) of all rare duplications and deletions analyzed separately; (2) the number of rare duplications and deletions considered separately; and (3) the aggregate length of all duplications and deletions combined, and evaluated for association with AUD using Firth's logistic regression.

We initially analyzed CNV >10 Kb in size due to their greater potential for pathogenicity, and then analyzed rCNV of any size. We conducted both genome-wide and gene-set burden analyses within ancestry for the three largest ancestry groups (EUR, AFR, AMR), we performed meta-analysis of the within-ancestry results, and we performed a trans-ancestry analysis of all samples pooled together. Within-ancestry meta-analysis and trans-ancestry analysis have different strengths and limitations. Because CNV boundaries vary by ancestry, meta-analysis of within-ancestry summary statistics across groups controls for possible population stratification by keeping null models calibrated within each ancestral group. However, AoU is a population cohort and, like the US population, displays substantial admixture. Trans-ancestry analysis increases both sample size and power, and appears to better address this admixture.

Genome-wide burden analysis

We conducted whole-genome rCNV burden analyses for both deletions and duplications across count, length, and the cumulative length metrics using Firth's logistic regression, adjusted for the first ten genetic principal components to control for population stratification. We used three covariate models for genome-wide burden tests: 1) ten PCs only and 2) ten PCs, age, sex and 3) ten PCs, age, sex, total rCNV count, and average rCNV length. (Supplementary Figure 4). Effect estimates were broadly consistent across models. Following previous studies (35, 42) we report Model 3 as the primary model in the main text because it is the most conservative, additionally accounting for individual differences in global rCNV load.

Gene-set burden analysis

At the gene-set level, analyses quantified the extent to which rCNV overlapped individual genes and predefined gene sets. To map genes to each identified CNV to calculate burden in Mb, we used the GENCODE Release 46 (GRCh38.p14) (https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?BMSwNk) genic intervals. Each CNV was assigned to any genes sharing at least one base pair of overlap (11).

We also conducted gene-set rCNV analyses of both deletions and duplications across count (representing the number of genes within the set overlapped by CNV in each individual), length and the cumulative length metrics using Firth's logistic regression. Gene-set burden analysis included 5 AUD/problematic alcohol use (PAU) related gene sets from a previous PAU study (https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?wSBmLB) plus a combination of all five sets, 146 abnormal behavior gene sets derived from mouse mutants with central nervous system (CNS) phenotypes (https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?vKy5j9 73 curated sets studied for CNV from PGC PTSD study and one negative control cholesterol gene-set, previously shown to have no genetic association with PAU (https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?93NH9q. For each gene, the total number of overlapping CNV and the extent of base pair overlap were calculated. Gene burden was encoded as a binary variable, where individuals were assigned a value of 1 if at least one CNV overlapped the gene and 0 otherwise.

We adjusted for average rCNV length and total rCNV count to reduce confounding by background genomic instability. Total rCNV count accounts for differences in the rate of rCNV formation between cases and controls, while average rCNV length accounts for differences in the size of the events themselves (17). Together, these covariates capture genome-wide rCNV burden, so that associations reflect the specific loci of interest rather than a global tendency toward structural variation. We evaluated multiple covariate models to identify the optimal predictors of CNV burden [Supplementary method]; however, our primary model followed previously established frameworks (11) and tested the five gene-set level burden metrics: deletion/duplication count, deletion/duplication burden length, and total burden length within each gene set. First, a sample-size-weighted Stouffer's Z (https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?Yli3w8) was computed because it weights each ancestry by the square root of its sample size (𝑤 𝑖 = √𝑁 𝑖 ), making it more robust to imbalance in cohort size and to instability in per-ancestry standard-error estimates.

Second, an inverse-variance-weighted (IVW) estimate was obtained as 𝑍 𝑚𝑒𝑡𝑎 = 𝛽 𝑚𝑒𝑡𝑎 𝑆𝐸 𝑚𝑒𝑡𝑎 , where 𝛽 𝑚𝑒𝑡𝑎 = 𝛴 𝑖 𝛽 𝑖 /𝑆𝐸 𝑖 2 𝛴 𝑖 1/𝑆𝐸 𝑖 2 , and 𝑆𝐸 𝑚𝑒𝑡𝑎 = (∑ 𝑖 1 𝑆𝐸 𝑖 2 ) -1/2 , with a corresponding pooled odds ratio (OR) and 95% confidence interval derived by exponentiating 𝛽 𝑚𝑒𝑡𝑎 and its interval.

Two-sided P values were computed from the standard normal distribution for both frameworks.

Individual rCNV analysis

rCNV associations with AUD were evaluated using Firth's logistic regression to account for sparse data and low-frequency events. Subsequent ancestry-stratified analyses were conducted within the EUR, AFR, and AMR groups. The CSA, EAS, and MID groups were excluded from ancestry-specific analyses because limited sample sizes reduced statistical power for rare CNV association testing. We also performed an exploratory analysis in a trans-ancestry framework using all QC-passed samples from all ancestry groups (including EUR, AMR, AFR, and adding CSA, EAS, and MID) and using transancestry PCs for assignment, as described above. Within-and trans-ancestry ancestry association analyses were performed using PLINK 2.0 (Chang et al., 2015). For each model, age at blood collection, sex, and the first 10 within-ancestry PCs (for withinancestry analyses) or trans-ancestry PCs (for trans-ancestry analyses) were utilized as covariates.

Associations between rCNV and AUD were evaluated using p-values (P), regression coefficients (β), OR, and standard errors (SE) derived from the regression models. We analyzed CNV regions with two filters of ≥10 kb and all CNV sizes Individual rCNV meta-analysis Association results for individual CNV regions in the EUR, AFR, and AMR ancestry groups were metaanalyzed using METAL (19) with the sample-size weighted scheme (SCHEME SAMPLESIZE), which combines per-ancestry P values into a weighted Stouffer's Z, weighting each study by the square root of its sample size and incorporating the direction of effect. This matches the sample-size weighted framework used for the gene-set burden meta-analysis above. To account for imbalanced case-control ratios, the effective sample size (𝑁 𝑒𝑓𝑓 ) was used as the sample size for each study.

Multiple test correction

Statistical significance was determined using the Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR), with an adjusted p-value threshold of <0.05 applied within each family of tests and adjusting within ancestry group and CNV size filters.

Genomic inflation evaluation

To assess potential inflation of association statistics, we first calculated the genomic inflation factor (λ), defined as the ratio of the median observed chi-square association test statistic to the median expected under the null hypothesis of no association. Because λ is influenced by sample size, we additionally calculated the standardized genomic inflation factor λ₁₀₀₀ (https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?M3TYmO which adjusts λ to an equivalent study with 1,000 cases and 1,000 controls using the following formula.

Values greater than 1 for either metric indicate inflation of the test statistics, whereas values close to 1 suggest adequate control of population stratification and other confounding factors (21).

Association of individual rCNV with AUD

No rCNV region of at least 10 kb was associated with AUD after FDR correction in the EUR, AFR or AMR samples, in the trans-ancestry analysis, or in the cross-ancestry meta-analysis. When rCNV of any size were considered, two deletions reached FDR significance in the trans-ancestry analysis. A 58 bp deletion at 16p12.1 overlapping ENSG00000260580 gave the smallest P value genome-wide (OR = 2.93, 95% CI 1.94 to 4.42, P = 3.06 × 10-7, FDR = 0.030), and a 171 bp intergenic deletion at 2p11.2 was also significant (OR = 14.99, 95% CI 5.15 to 43.65, P = 6.90 × 10-7, FDR = 0.034). The 16p12.1 deletion was likewise the top-ranked region in the EUR sample (OR = 4.18, 95% CI 2.28 to 7.65, P = 3.70 × 10-6, FDR = 0.064), where three further regions approached significance: a 582 bp intergenic duplication at 2p11.2 (OR = 3.42, 95% CI 2.05 to 5.71, P = 2.38 × 10-6, FDR = 0.064), a 735 bp deletion at 2q37.3 overlapping ESPNL (OR = 3.38, 95% CI 2.03 to 5.63, P = 2.99 × 10-6, FDR = 0.064) and a 159 bp deletion at 22q13.31 overlapping TBC1D22A (OR = 7.41, 95% CI 3.13 to 17.56, P = 5.39 × 10-6, FDR = 0.070). No region survived FDR correction in the AFR-like or AMR-like samples, possibly consistent with their smaller case counts. In the cross-ancestry meta-analysis of the three groups, the 16p12.1 deletion again ranked first (Z = 4.81, P = 1.54 × 10⁻⁶, FDR = 0.10), although no region passed correction (FDR < 0.05). There was no significant between-ancestry heterogeneity at this locus (I² = 33.9%, P heterogeneity = 0.22). All implicated events were very short and uncommon, and each conferred a roughly three-fold to fifteen-fold increase in the odds of AUD among carriers.

The 16p12.1 deletion is of particular interest because it was recovered independently in the trans-ancestry, EUR and meta-analytic results and falls within an established dosage-sensitive locus in which deletions are associated with developmental delay and with a broad range of psychiatric outcomes in both clinical and population-based samples (22) (23) (24). Its convergence across analytic levels, together with the absence of heterogeneity between ancestries, supports prioritising this locus for follow-up in larger AUD samples, although the event tested here is short and the association did not survive correction in any within-ancestry analysis.

Test statistics from the region-level analyses were well calibrated. For rCNV of all sizes, the λ was 1.03 in the trans-ancestry analysis and 1.05, 1.03 and 1.04 in the EUR, AFR and AMR samples, respectively. Restricting to rCNV of at least 10 kb yielded 1.05 in the trans-ancestry analysis, higher values in the EUR (1.21) and AFR (1.16) samples, and no inflation in the AMR sample (0.93); the smaller number of tests and the lower carrier frequency of large events make λ less stable in this category. The cross-ancestry meta-analyses gave λ = 1.05 for all CNV sizes and λ = 1.11 for rCNV of at least 10 kb, with λ1000 of 1.01 in both cases, indicating that the residual deviation from the null reflects sample size rather than uncontrolled population stratification (see the table below for the complete λ values).

The current sample size may be underpowered to detect large rCNV associations, or alternatively, large rCNV may have little impact in AUD, while the functional role of small rCNV remains uncertain (25).

Taken together, all analyses converge on a consistent account. At the resolution of individual regions, no rCNV of at least 10 kb was associated with AUD after correction in any ancestry group, and the two deletions that reached FDR significance in the trans-ancestry analysis of all CNV sizes were both short events, at 16p12.1 and 2p11.2. At the level of burden, the associations that survived correction were almost exclusively inverse duplication effects recurring across biologically unrelated gene sets. The single deletion result that passed correction, together with the directionally consistent but uncorrected deletion signal in the xenobiotic response set, represents a possible candidate for an AUD-relevant effect. Both require replication in larger and more ancestrally diverse samples before firm conclusions can be drawn.
