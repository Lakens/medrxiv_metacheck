What we found

To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 1 non-significant p value that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Metacheck does not yet analyze automatically whether sentences which include non-significant p-values are correct, but we recommend manually checking the sentences below for possible misinterpreted non-significant p values.

p-corrected>0.05 Subjects with Crouzon syndrome presented volumes (TBV and regional brain) similar to those of the control group (p-corrected>0.05) except for increased lateral ventricles volumes (p<10 -8 ).

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. How StatCheck works

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Craniostenosis, a cranial malformation due to premature suture fusion, includes rare FGFRrelated conditions such as Crouzon (CS), Muenke (MS), and Apert (AS) syndromes. These 30 mutations affect skull but also brain development and are associated with variable neurodevelopmental outcomes. This brain impairment is still poorly described and understood, prompting for neuroimaging anatomical studies. We thus investigated regional brain growth and volumetric scaling in patients with these three syndromes and nonsyndromic non-FGFR-related craniostenoses, using a normative modelling framework. We 35 analyzed a rare set of brain MRI from 65 patients close to first cranial surgery time (30 CS among which 23 pre-surgery, 4 AS, 6 MS, 23 non-syndromic) and 130 typically developing controls. Millimetric 3D T1-weighted images were segmented into 6 cortical and 3 subcortical regions (AssemblyNet, Morphologist). After harmonization (ComBat), normative growth curves (Turner model) and static scaling curves (power-law model, effect of brain 40 size regardless of age) were derived in controls. Patient deviations were assessed using permutation tests on mean normalized distance to typical model and abnormality rates (10th/90th percentiles). Patients with CS showed typical brain volumes for age except for enlarged ventricles, but slightly overscaled frontal volumes for brain size. Non-syndromic patients showed very similar volume distribution for age and brain size than CS ones. 45

Patients with AS displayed increased total brain, temporal, cerebellar and central volumes for age, with overscaled temporal and cerebellar regions but underscaled frontal volumes for brain size. Patients with MS showed a similar, though milder, pattern: increased temporal volume for age, with temporal overscaling but frontal underscaling for frain size.

Our findings reveal genotype-dependent brain anomalies in FGFR-related 50 craniosynostoses, with a convergent pattern of temporal-frontal disproportions in AS and MS, associated with very specific mutations in the FGFR2 and 3 genes respectively, contrasting with a much more typical brain organization in more heterogeneous CS and nonsyndromic patients. Beyond the pathophysiological meaning of these results, which call for a larger similar study covering as wide a range of patient variability as possible, our work 55 highlights the relevance of normative modelling approach for characterizing rare craniofacial disorders using clinical MRI datasets.

1 Introduction

Craniosynostoses are congenital craniofacial malformations resulting from the premature 60 fusion of one or more cranial and/or facial sutures, leading to abnormal skull shape and potentially impaired brain development and growth (Yasaroglu & Demir, 2023). Some forms of craniosynostoses are associated with mutations in the fibroblast growth factor receptor (FGFR) gene group (Webster & Donoghue, 1997), such as Crouzon syndrome, Apert syndrome, and Muenke syndrome. Their prevalence varies, with Crouzon syndrome 65 occurring in 1/50,000 to 1/60,000 live births (Helman et al., 2014), Apert syndrome in 1/65,000 (Breik et al., 2016), and Muenke syndrome being more common at 1/10,000 births (Sabatino et al., 2004). Besides these rare syndromic forms, non-syndromic craniosynostoses are much more common and occur in 5.2 of every 10,000 live births. In these conditions, the skull malformation occurs in isolation, with no identified mutation or 70 evident extra-cranial phenotypic expression in most cases, particularly without known specific cerebral phenotype (Alperovich et al., 2025).

Syndromic craniosynostoses are characterized by extensive phenotypic features including cranial, facial, cerebral and extracephalic features. Each syndrome presents with distinctive cranial abnormalities. Crouzon syndrome is characterized by midface 75 hypoplasia (Levesque et al., 2019) and is associated with variability in suture closure, with different sutures affected to different degrees depending on the patient (Azoury et al., 2017) leading to variable levels of skull growth anomalies (Delassus, 2025). Apert syndrome also features midface hypoplasia and is generally associated with bicoronal craniosynostosis (Giancotti et al., 2014). Muenke syndrome generally has a less severe facial involvement and 80 is associated with bicoronal craniosynostosis in most cases (Mathijssen, 2015).

The cerebral phenotype of syndromic craniosynostoses is less clearly defined that the skeletal phenotype and varies in severity regarding brain structure and cognitive functions. Surgical interventions may influence outcomes (Mathijssen, 2015), but a cognitive and behavioral phenotype is frequently observed despite optimal management that may lead to 85 severe neurodevelopmental impairment (Mathijssen, 2015). The functional phenotype in Crouzon syndrome is varied and imprecisely documented, visual impairment affecting up to 40% of patients (Bowling & Burstein, 2006), and hearing loss in 74% (Agochukwu et al., 2014). Intellectual impairment is reported in around 50% of patients with Apert syndrome (Mathijssen, 2015). In Muenke syndrome, intellectual disability occurs in around 35% of 90 cases (Mathijssen, 2015). In addition, recurrent neurological abnormalities are associated with these conditions. In Crouzon syndrome, intracranial hypertension occurs in 40-63% of cases (Levesque et al., 2019), (Spruijt et al., 2016), as well as ventriculomegaly (38-70%) (Doerga et al., 2022), hydrocephalus (29%) (Coll et al., 2015), and Chiari I malformations (70%) (Cinalli et al., 2005). Apert syndrome is associated with ventriculomegaly in 95 approximately 48.5% of cases (Breik et al., 2016), hydrocephalus in 9% (Giancotti et al., 2014), temporal cortical dysgyria (Breik et al., 2016 , (Cohen & Kreiborg, 1990), abnormalities of the corpus callosum and limbic system (de León et al., 1987 , Maksem & Roessmann, 1979), and generalized white matter reduction (Raybaud & Di Rocco, 2007). Muenke syndrome has been related to an increase in the volume of the posterior fossa and 100 cerebellum (Rijken et al., 2015).

Nevertheless, although these syndromes differ clinically, they share a close genetic relationship through activating mutations affecting FGFR genes (FGFR2 and FGFR3). These mutations notably lead to bone abnormalities and premature fusion of cranial sutures (Azoury et al., 2017). Both FGFR2 and FGFR3 alternatively splice their mRNA, producing 105 isoforms that bind different fibroblast growth factors (FGFs) (Galzie et al., 1997), this ligandbinding specificity being crucial for embryonic development and organogenesis. Most FGFR2 mutations occur in exons IIIa and IIIc, essential regions for receptor function (Chim et al., 2011), the FGFR2c isoform (alternative exon IIIc) being particularly expressed in the mesenchymal tissues forming the flat bones of the developing skull (Chim et al., 2011). A 110 large diversity of pathogenic FGFR2 variants is responsible for Crouzon syndrome while in Apert syndrome, p.S252W and p.P253R elective mutations alter the ligand affinity of FGFR2c (Chim et al., 2011), enabling abnormal binding to specific FGFs. In addition to osteogenesis, FGFs are also involved in brain development, neuronal maturation, neuroprotection and neurometabolic regulation. For example, FGF1 and FGF2 contribute to neuronal growth and 115 plasticity, with FGF2 also stimulating osteogenesis (Reuss et al., 2003). Therefore, activating FGFR mutations is expected to affect both skull morphogenesis and brain development, underlying the intricate phenotypes observed in these syndromes.

Despite these insights, the cerebral phenotype associated with FGFR-related craniosynostoses remains poorly understood, and epidemiological and clinical data are 120 rare. Neuroimaging research based on magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) has just begun to explore brain structure with more details. A recent study reported that patients with FGFR mutations had increased intracranial volume compared to controls, while displaying relatively reduced cortical surface area, particularly in the parietal and occipital lobes (Wilson et al., 2022). The existing literature, however, reveals contradictory results. Studies 125 of intracranial and cerebral volumes in craniosynostosis have reported heterogeneous results, ranging from increased volumes in some syndromes (Dubois et al., 2008) to normal volumes in others (Junn et al., 2023). These divergences may be attributed to variations in the types of syndromes, sample sizes and imaging methodologies. Overall, these observations highlight important lacunae in our understanding of the brain patterns 130 associated with FGFR mutations. Indeed, understanding the mechanisms of brain disruption in FGFR-related craniosynostosis syndromes presents several challenges. Firstly, it is essential to improve our knowledge and description of observable brain abnormalities associated with the different syndromes. Secondly, it is crucial to distinguish the congenital cerebral alterations from secondary complications linked to the progression 135 of the disease or the side-effects of therapeutic wandering and surgical treatments. Finally, identifying correlations between genotype and brain phenotype remains an essential objective for advancing diagnosis and personalized management.

Today, tertiary expert centers are able to gather relatively large clinical series of genotyped and perisurgically-imaged patients, allowing the study of the neuroanatomical phenotype of 140 these syndromes in greater depth, while carefully controlling for confounding factors.

Object-based neuroimaging strategies relying on a limited set of robust regions of interest and anatomic parameters (volumes, surfaces), combined with normative analysis methods (Marquand, 2016), are expected to be sensitive enough to detect individual brain abnormalities associated with FGFR2 or 3 pathogenic variants, such as global or focal 145 abnormalities in brain growth (size-for-age), or even abnormalities in brain scaling (size distribution among regions) independent of a global sizing (Fraize et al. 2023; Kerdreux et al., 2025).

Using a clinical MRI dataset of patients with craniosynostosis, including patients with Crouzon, Apert and Muenke syndromes but also patients with non-syndromic 150 presentations, imaged before or close to first surgical step, and a large group of typically developing controls, we segmented, quality-checked and harmonized 8 cerebral volumes of interest at the scale of large cerebral regions. Firstly, we established typical normograms on controls, based on growth (cross-sectional effect of age) and static scaling models (size effect independent from age). Secondly, we tested whether the patient groups showed 155 global or regional deviations from the volumes expected for their age. In a third step, we regressed out the age parameter, under the assumption of z-score stability during growth, and tested whether the patient groups showed regional deviations from the expected volume for brain size.

T1-weighted brain MRI were segmented into 8 regions using the following pipeline combining BrainVISA (Rivière et al., 2009), VolBrain (Manjón et al., 2022) and AssemblyNet (Coupé et al., 2019). The six cortical surface parcels were ultimately back-projected into the brain volume and expanded within the hemispheres to the braincore boundaries (multilabel front propagation) to define 6 volumetric hemispheric ROIs. All the 210 morphological operations were performed with the BrainVISA toolbox (see Figure 1 for an overview of the processing pipeline).

2.4 Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were carried out using Python and its packages pycombat (v0.20; 220 (Behdenna et al., 2020)), scikit-learn (v1.2.2; (Pedregosa et al., 2011)) scipy (v1.11.4; (Virtanen et al., 2020)) and statsmodels (v0.14.0; (Seabold & Perktold, 2010)). We used the Benjamini and Yekutieli (Benjamini & Yekutieli, 2001) false discovery rate (FDR) control method to adjust p-values, to control the proportion of false positives among significant results in a context of multiple dependent comparisons. 225

2.4.1 Adjusting for confounders factors or variables of noninterest

Although the NEM site included several sequences and scanners, it was treated as a single site for batch correction as patients and controls were acquired using the same panel of sequences. To ensure that this approach did not compromise the effectiveness of the harmonization and that no residual subgroup structure remained after harmonization, a 230 post hoc analysis (Sup. After harmonization of the whole control group, we made a 4fold partition of the NEM control group (subsets of 8 subjects) and fitted 4 times the normative models for age including only 3 of the 4 NEM subsets into the whole control group. For each fold, a shuffle and split permutation test (see below) was performed to assess for 235 the presence of a significant mean deviation for the normative model of age in the subset not included in the model fit. In none of the 4 folds did we did not observeddeviation in any of the 4 folds, testifying for a good correction of the site effect on the anatomical parameters studied, despite the heterogeneous nature of the NEM site.

The native volumes were adjusted using ComBat (Behdenna et al., 2020), for the effect of 240 acquisition site and sex in order to perform normative analyses of the age effect and static scaling as proposed in previous studies (Fraize et al. 2023; Kerdreux et al., 2025). Sex was adjusted a priori to avoid dividing the study groups and to preserve maximum statistical power notably when establishing the reference curves. Combat was based on a linear model with empirical Bayesian estimation to harmonize batch effects (Behdenna et al., 2020). The 245 site effect was corrected first, followed by the sex effect, while preserving age and group (patients, controls).

4 Discussion

Our study proposed a normative approach based on a rare set of clinical MRI to analyze cerebral volumetric regional profiles in syndromic craniosynostosis linked to FGFR2 and FGFR3 mutations. Our results highlighted syndrome specific cerebral alterations, particularly a contrasting pattern of growth and sizing (static-scaling) anomalies common to Apert and Muenke syndromes, albeit with a smaller effect size for the latter. Conversely, both patients with Crouzon syndrome and non-syndromic patients showed brain volume distributions close to typical individuals, with the mild singularities identified being similar in both conditions. In addition to a contribution to a better understanding of craniosynostosis linked to FGFR variants, our results also illustrate the potential of clinical data inscribed into a methodological framework exploiting the current reference datasets and harmonization tools.

4.1 Small groups but rather contrasted syndromic effects 410

The results indicated that Apert syndrome was associated with significant global and regional volumetric abnormalities. Apart from an enlargement of the lateral ventricles, the total brain parenchyma was overgrown so as the temporal lobes, consistent with some rare reports from the literature (increased intracranial volume (Wilson et al., 2022), temporal lobe abnormalities (Raybaud & Di Rocco, 2007)). Notably, this overgrowth also concerned other brain regions, such as particularly the cerebellum, but spared the frontal lobes. These differences were ultimately reflected in an atypical distribution of brain volume for brain size, with oversized temporal and cerebellar regions and relatively undersized frontal regions (for a brain of this size). As such, it may be interesting to look at this uneven distribution from another point of view, taking the frontal lobe -of expected size for age -as a reference, which highlights a gradient of oversizing for the rest of the cerebral regions in Apert syndrome, of which the temporal and, to a lesser extent, the cerebellar regions constitute the extrema (see this representation in Supplementary Material, Sup. Figure S5). Indeed, the very small number of patients with Apert syndrome, (n=4) constituting more a small case series than a representative group of the Apert syndrome population, tends to weaken these results. Their significance was supported by large effect sizes, but a selection bias is difficult to rule out. However, it should be remembered that the only active selection made was on the quality of the MRI scans and their compatibility with the proposed computational treatments. With this in mind, the intra-group consistency of the anomalies and their concordance with the literature reinforced their reliability. More unexpectedly, as suggested by no preliminary data, our analyses revealed a rather similar, albeit less intense, pattern of volumetric anomalies in Muenke syndrome, with temporal « overgrowth » and oversizing, associated with frontal undersizing for brain size. Here again, the small size of the group (n=6) makes the result prone to an even « random » selection bias , but the consistency over the 6 subjects seems an element of conviction. Interestingly, in a context where we could not detect any global « overgrowth » of the brain, the static-scaling analysis revealed an atypical pattern of volume distribution similar to the one observed in Apert syndrome.

By contrast, patients with Crouzon syndrome showed only ventricular abnormalities, with almost no significant cortical or subcortical volumetric alterations for age and a mild uneven distribution of brain volumes for brain size, a configuration eventually very similar to the one observed in non-syndromic craniosynostoses. The size of these two groups might suggest a lower risk of insufficient sampling of the population, Yet, given the heterogeneity at least of Crouzon syndrome compared with Apert and Muenke syndromes both genetically (large diversity of FGFR2 variants in Crouzon syndrome, virtually unknown determinism in non-445 syndromic craniosynostosis) and phenotypically (among other things, diversity of sutural anomalies) -we could have under-sampled subgroups and masked their specific effects. Qualitative observation of the data or distribution tests did not support this hypothesis, which would nevertheless require larger numbers to be effectively tested. Furthermore, complementary analysis of the Crouzon syndrome and non-syndromic groups restricted to patients with bicoronal anomalies did not support an effect of sutural phenotype on cerebral parameters in our patients. Yet this pattern of regional brain growth and scaling rather similar to typically developing controls in patients with Crouzon syndrome might look surprising given the high frequency of neurological symptoms reported in this condition, such as raised intracranial pressure (Spruijt et al., 2016), Chiari (Cinalli et al., 2005) or neurodevelopment impairments (Wenger et al., 2020 , Wilson, 2020). The ventricle enlargement was almost expected and of course limited in amplitude by the fact that we excluded the patient with more than mild ventriculomegaly. The mild frontal oversizing for brain size common with non-syndromic forms -probably at the expense of the temporaloccipital regions according to the pattern trending in the two average z-score maps -might be more informative and an avenue for further studies, though it could only be the residual of an abusive averaging between more contrasted subgroup effects.

4.2 Relevance of a genotype-dependent brain phenotype

From a clinical perspective, it makes sense that we found the main growth and sizing regional anomalies in the brain of the patients with the two syndromes more associated with neurodevelopmental disorders (50% of intellectual development disorder in Apert syndrome, 40% in Muenke syndrome with 20% epilepsy (Kruszka et al., 2016). In the more heterogeneous Crouzon syndrome, it is considered difficult to dissociate the mild cognitive and behavioral impairments from the complications associated to prolonged increased intracranial pressure, the variations in therapeutic sequences or the psychosocial repercussions (Rostamzad et al., 2024). In this respect, it seems consistent that the mild pattern of cerebral missizing observed in Crouzon syndrome was identical to that of nonsyndromic patients, who typically have few associated neurodevelopmental disorders.

Yet, recent investigations in the neuropsychological profiles of patients with non-syndromic craniosynostoses seem to show that mild cognitive alterations are more prevalent than in control groups, independently from surgical management and thus potentially caused by intrinsic brain anomalies that may not be detectable by current imaging protocols (Olsson et al., 2023). In addition, even though few studies are available to assess the cognitive phenotype in Crouzon syndrome (Fernandes et al., 2016), neuropsychological signs in this syndrome seem to be highly variable with no current valid genotype-phenotype correlation.

Here we excluded Crouzon syndrome patients with severe initial presentations such as cloverleaf skulls, where brain malformations may be present. For instance, a neuropathological investigation of fetuses with FGFR2-related syndromic craniosynostoses diagnosed as Pfeiffer syndrome (now considered as a variant of Crouzon syndrome) have reported temporal lobe malformations that reminded the malformations observed in FGFR3-related thanatophoric dysplasia, a lethal form of chondrodysplasia (Hevner et al., 2005 , Khonsari et al., 2012). The absence of brain phenotype we report may thus be related to a selection bias. Nevertheless, extremely severe presentations such as cloverleaf skulls are not the most common clinical situation in this syndrome and our results, both for Crouzon syndrome and non-syndromic craniosynostoses, are in line with the fact that these two conditions have generally milder cognitive alterations than Apert and Muenke syndrome patients.

Genetically speaking, the converging pattern observed in Apert and Muenke syndromes, if confirmed on a larger number of subjects, is both unexpected and questioning from a mechanistic point of view, given that these syndromes are linked to very specific variants but in two different genes of the FGFR family. Although it is not possible to consider this type of correlation in our dataset, it is now possible to test whether greater deviations from typical brain growth or regional dimensioning is associated with greater severity of the neurodevelopmental disorders associated with these two syndromes. Conversely, the apparent consistency of the absence of any major neuroanatomical abnormality in the Crouzon syndrome spectrum shall not hinder from questioning the relevance of this finding. Indeed, the high heterogeneity of genetic variants in Crouzon syndrome compared with syndromes such as Apert syndrome (2 causal variants) or Muenke syndrome (only one) could account for the absence of a homogeneous trend towards regionalized brain volumetric dysgenesis. It is also possible that all the FGFR2 variants involved in Crouzon syndrome share a relative lack of effect on brain growth (which would not exclude them having common or specific effects on other aspects of brain development or maturation), compared to the two Apert syndrome variants for instance.

To better understand this point, the spectrum of Crouzon syndrome shall probably described regarding the cognitive phenotype and its correlations with neuroanatomy and genotype. As mentioned above, the most severe variants of Crouzon syndrome in our cohort have been excluded from this study. Crouzon syndrome patients with cloverleaf skull and/or severe and early hydrocephalus do not have a specific genetic background reported to date. Despite this current limitation in the characterization of the cohort, a stratification of Crouzon syndrome patients according to cognitive status could still contribute to tackle an important question raised by our study. In fact, the brain malformations we report in Apert and Muenke syndromes could be: (1) common to all patients with FGFR mutations and severe brain involvement, including the excluded forms of severe Crouzon syndrome patients, or (2) relate to specific, still undescribed, mechanisms common to Muenke and Apert syndromes but not present in Crouzon syndrome. According to the current, very limited, available date in the literature, the first hypothesis seems the most relevant. In fact, as mentioned above, severe prenatal forms of FGFR2-related Crouzon syndrome (labeled as Pfeiffer syndrome) have been associated with major brain malformations resembling malformation in other conditions due to activating FGFR mutations such as FGFR3-related thanathophoric dysplasia. Furthermore, the very uncommon subtype of Crouzon syndrome due to FGFR3 mutations (Crouzon with acanthosis nigricans) seems to be associated with specific clinical severity and brain anomalies (Cornille et al., 2022 , de Planque et al., 2021). These phenotypic overlaps in conditions due to different mutations may be related to the expression overlap of FGFR genes in the craniofacial region, both in humans and in mouse models. In brief, as there is no current argument to relate the mechanisms underlying Apert and Muenke syndromes, the proximity of their brain phenotype may correspond to the characterization of the severe side of a common spectrum of brain anomalies due to all activating FGFR mutations (Delezoide et al., 1998 , Paek et al., 2009).

Furthermore, even though the genetics causes of the vast majority of non-syndromic craniosynostosis cases are still to be uncovered, several mutations have been associated with specific risks of cognitive impairment and potential brain anomalies (Timberlake et al., 2022). The non-syndromic group also shall benefit from more precise neuroanatomical characterization in relation with functional severity before being considered as a control group where the sole source of anomalies is the skull deformation.

Finally, our results also suggest that the genotype has a predominant influence on cerebral organization in craniosynostoses compared with the sutural phenotype, which could have played a direct role (by simple deformation or even developmental stress), or else be an indirect marker of cerebral dysgenesis. Indeed, even when restricting the analysis to patients with bicoronal synostosis, no convergence in volumetric pattern emerged between the Crouzon syndrome group (and the non-syndromic patients) and the Apert syndrome or Muenke syndrome groups. Also, the post-hoc analysis performed on the Crouzon syndrome patients did not show any major differences between the Crouzon syndrome subgroups according to the suture affected.

4.3 Interest, means and limits of normative analyses in clinical datasets

Neuroimaging research into rare conditions such as syndromic craniosynostosis is complicated by the lack of access to sufficient quality data in sufficient quantity. Our approach has been to combine a number of methodological tools implemented in recent years for neuroanatomical analysis in computational imaging, in order to make the most of the rare retrospective data set at our disposal. The main strategy had been to generate normograms reflecting the effect of age (cross-sectional) and size (static-scaling) in the context of typical development, which would be accurate enough and compatible with our patient data. To achieve this, we combined data harmonization tools, whose effectiveness in computational neuroanatomy has been widely documented, with explicit modeling of normative curves by rather simple biomathematical functions (few parameters to fit), which are relatively data-saving.

In terms of harmonizing data by correcting for batch effects, the main weakness of our approach lay in the heterogeneous nature of the patient acquisition site (NEM site). Indeed, even though the same panel of 3DT1 sequences was used for patients and controls from the NEM site, it was not possible to divide this site into as many sequences for the sake of parametrization. This is why we sought to verify whether this choice did not bias the analysis of mean divergence from the normative model obtained on all controls by a post-hoc test based on a partition of controls from the NEM site: none of the four partitions of eight controls considered as a group of interest (pseudo-patient) showed significant deviation from the norm for any of the anatomical metrics studied, validating the hypothesis that, within the precision limits of the study, our simplification of the batch effect model remained acceptable.

More generally, it would have been preferable to have more homogeneous data in terms of 3DT1 sequence parameterization, over and above the multisite character, even though the large neuroanatomical objects we studied are probably little sensitive to sequence effects due to the dilution of the small differences induced in the total volume of the object (unlike cortical objects, for example). Despite our reassuring post-hoc verifications, taking into account the sequence effect through the site effect is probably not optimal, but it is important to bear in mind the size of the main effects finally observed, particularly in the Apert and Muenke syndromes group, which it is hardly debatable and exceeds the magnitude of the noise or even the residual biases linked to the production of the normograms.

Regarding the modelling of age and size effects, the power law choice for scaling analysis rather consensual and its use was highly documented, including from our previous work. Modeling age effect forms a 130 subject large control group over a 16 years window with a rather simple (parsimonious parametrization) function is a less well-documented task. Interestingly, classical growth curves such as von Bertalanffy didn't show convincing fits, with nearly asymptotic values reached as early as 4 to 5 years of age, which was not consistent with the literature (Peterson et al., 2018). We chose to rely on a more general model published by Turner and providing sufficient degree of freedom to fit a more convincing "growth" dynamic from our data set at the extent of only 3 parameters to fit.

Eventually, within the limits of the available power, we found the residual from our models to show no heteroscedasticity, allowing for rather easy estimate of z-score and proper uses of the proposed statistics.

4.4 Other built-in limitations

Concerns about the sample size, particularly for Apert and Muenke syndromes, and about the setting-up of normative charts have been already mentionned but two important built-in limitations have still to be taken into.

First, we wanted to study the brains of patients with the least impact on development from mechanical complications such as hydrocephalus, or from the often-cumbersome therapeutic sequences, which also degrades the quality of brain images. For the sake of recruitment, we did include data from MRI performed before surgery, but also just in the aftermath of the first surgical step (may the only). This pragmatic and developmentally relevant choice nevertheless reduces the representativeness of the data, associated with the de facto exclusion of the large number of children who required early surgery, at an age when only T2 weighted images were available, which is often not usable (clinical 2D imaging) and in any case requires very different analysis strategies. Besides, potential effect of the first step of surgical procedures cannot be totally ruled out, even if a comparison between pre-and post-operative Crouzon syndrome (see supplementary material Sup. Table S5) revealed no significant difference, suggesting a limited effect in the short term, but a longitudinal study would be needed to validate this point.

Second, as in previous normative studies with medium sized control group (150-250), we favored statistical power by fitting the reference in the overall typically developing sample, while controlling for the effect of sex with data harmonization using ComBat (Fraize et al., 2023; Kerdreux et al. 2025). Indeed, many studies have shown that total brain volume is generally higher for men even if these differences were more limited at regional level, 615 particularly when volumes were corrected for total brain size (TBV) (Mills et al., 2016). The exploration of any interactions at the brain level between sex and FGFR-related craniosynostosis, that definitely have been described in many developmental conditions (Postic et al., 2024 , Smith et al., 2014), was then not considered.
