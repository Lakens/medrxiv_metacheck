Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. Some differences come from the ground truth: it lacks p-values with a correction name (“p Bonf = .061”), and counts partial eta squared written with its subscript after it (“η 2 p = .05”), “p = .05”, and some table notes and figure legends (“† p < .10, *p < .05”), which this module leaves out, as non-significant p-values. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

Full text

Abstract

Polygenic indices (PGIs) are downwardly biased in their predictive accuracies in independent samples due to the presence of estimation error in Genome-Wide Association Studies (GWAS) from which they are constructed. By performing GWAS of the same phenotype in independent samples, we obtain to error-corrected estimates of PGI prediction accuracy, which we use to estimate the extent to which different PGI construction methods (unadjusted, Clumping, LDpred2inf, LDpred2-auto, SBayesR, PRS-CS-auto) index total single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) heritability. To ensure practicality of this approach when individual-level data from the original GWAS are not available, we provide a means of synthetically splitting existing GWAS summary statistics into independent halves. In both simulated and empirical data, we find that advanced PGI construction methods that produce enhanced uncorrected prediction accuracies (often by shrinking individual SNP effects towards zero or exactly to zero) also produce deflated error-corrected prediction accuracies. LDpred2-inf, which most closely resembles a multiple regression estimator to account for linkage disequilibrium without further aggressive shrinkage, consistently produces PGIs with relatively lower uncorrected prediction accuracies but higher error-corrected prediction accuracies that nearly fully recover SNP heritability. We show that PGI construction methods that implement aggressive shrinkage may also provide biased patterns of associations with collateral phenotypes, irrespective of error correction. By providing a means of straightforwardly obtaining error-corrected estimates of prediction accuracy using PGIs derived from standard GWAS summary statistics, we have highlighted an uncommonly recognized bias-variance tradeoff associated with the choice of PGI construction methods.

Introduction

A polygenic index (PGI; also commonly referred to as a polygenic score or a polygenic risk score) is a weighted combination of allele counts for many genetic variants into a single quantitative estimate of an individual's genetic propensity for a trait or disease (Choi et al., 2020; Wray et al., 2007; Purcell et al., 2009). PGIs are most valid when applied to individuals from the population and environmental contexts matching the Genome-Wide Association Studies (GWAS) from which the weights used to construct them are estimated (Furrer & Turkheimer, 2026; Wang et al., 2026). However, even in such ideal situations, because of estimation error in the GWAS, PGIs are inherently noisy proxies for underlying genetic liability, and PGI prediction accuracy is substantially attenuated relative to the SNP heritability of the trait (Becker et al., 2021; Tucker-Drob, 2017; DiPrete et al., 2018). Further complicating PGI construction is that GWAS estimates represent marginal associations that do not account for linkage disequilibrium (LD) among variants. GWAS estimates are therefore ill-suited for direct use as PGI weights, for which conditional associations are more appropriate.

A variety of strategies have been developed to construct PGIs that both mitigate the effects of estimation error and account for LD among GWAS variants. The strategies differ from one another in their assumptions, mechanics, and degrees of sophistication, but can be generally classified as either sparsity-inducing (lasso-like; Tibshirani, 1996) or continuous (ridge-like; Hoerl & Kennard, 1970) shrinkage methods. Sparsity-inducing methods create PGI weights by shrinking GWAS estimates for a subset of variants to exactly zero, in effect creating a sparser set of genetic predictors. In addition to removing variants for which estimates are trivial relative to their uncertainty, sparsity-inducing methods remove highly correlated, effectively redundant, variants. Clumping and thresholding (e.g. PRSice-2; Choi & O'Reilly, 2019) is perhaps the most straightforward approach to inducing sparsity, whereas Bayesian mixture-based approaches (e.g. SBayesR; Lloyd-Pierce et al., 2019) that include a prior distribution composed of a point mass zero and one or more continuous distributions of effect sizes represent more sophisticated sparsityinducing methods. Continuous shrinkage methods create PGI weights by shrinking GWAS estimates toward, but not exactly to, zero. One such continuous shrinkage method is PRS-CS which uses shrinkage to both adjust for LD and "adaptively squelch small noisy estimates towards zero, while leaving data-supported large signals unshrunk" (Ge et al., 2019, p. 2). In contrast, LDpred2-inf (Privé et al., 2020) assumes an infinitesimal model in which all variants have causal effects, and shrinkage largely functions to adjust for LD based on an LD matrix that is regularized to stabilize estimates.

Here, we identify an uncommonly recognized bias-variance tradeoff in PGI construction methods. Put simply, we find that methods that produce enhanced prediction accuracies (reduce error) also produce some of the most biased representations of genetic architecture. We reveal this phenomenon using a split-half method that divides the discovery GWAS sample into two independent halves in order to obtain and correct for measurement error in the PGIs, thereby producing an error-corrected estimate of PGI prediction accuracy. We also derive a pipeline for synthetically splitting existing GWAS summary statistics into two independent sets that both decreases computational burden and obviates the need for access to individual level data from the original GWAS or multiple independent GWAS of a trait of interest. Applications of these error correction approaches to both real and simulated data consistently demonstrates that the "best" PGI construction methods in terms of uncorrected prediction accuracy produce some of the most downwardly biased error-corrected estimates of prediction accuracy. We demonstrate that biased PGI construction methods may also evince biased patterns of associations with external phenotypes, even when implemented in standard settings (without error correction).

Results of Simulations using Artificial Genotypes

As an initial test of the validity of the split-half method, we conducted a simple simulation with artificial biallelic variants in LD. By simulating using independent variants, this initial simulation focuses specifically on the problem of measurement error in the PGIs, and we construct PGIs directly from the marginal GWAS estimates. Our later simulations, which compare the performance of different PGI construction methods, are based on real genotypes, thus adding to the complexity associated with developing weights in the presence of both estimation error and LD. We drew m variants drawn from a two-trial binomial distribution with probabilities randomly drawn from minor allele frequency (MAF) = 0.05 to 0.50, resulting in m biallelic variants conforming to Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium with allele counts 0, 1, or 2 for N individuals. Standardized effect sizes for each variant on the phenotype were drawn from a normal distribution with mean 0 and variance = SNP h 2 true/m. We choose m = 60,000, corresponding to the effective number of independent markers in a standard GWAS (Wray et al., 2013), and selected SNP h 2 true = 0.20. Prediction sample size was fixed at 10,000 to produce stable estimates (prediction sample size affects uncertainty in estimates of prediction accuracy but does not affect the prediction accuracy expectation). We varied the discovery sample size from 20K to 3M (i.e., 10K to 1.5M per half) computing 5 replications per sample size condition. For comparison, we computed the average of the uncorrected R² for the PGIs based on each half within each replication for each condition.

Results of Simulations using Real Genotypes

Having established that the error-corrected estimates closely recover SNP h 2 in the absence of LD among variants, we next conducted a series of simulations using real genotype data from the UK Biobank (UKB). Simulations were based on unrelated White British participants, and 749,619 HapMap3 variants present in UKB which passed standard genetic quality-control procedures, including filters on MAF, call rate, Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium, and imputation quality, and were also present in the LD reference panels used by the PGI construction methods. For each simulation replicate, a random subset of variants was designated as causal and used to generate a 100% heritable phenotype, to which normally distributed random variation then added to achieve the target level of heritability (e.g., 20%). Simulations were performed using genotypes from a discovery sample of 150,901 participants from UKB and genotypes from an independent prediction sample of 33,196 participants from UKB. The discovery sample was subsequently divided into two non-overlapping halves, and separate GWASs were conducted within each half to generate independent summary statistics for the split-half analyses. We compared uncorrected R² and error-corrected R² estimates across PGIs constructed using unadjusted marginal GWAS estimates, Clumping, LDpred2-inf, LDpred2-auto, SBayesR, and PRS-CS-auto (see Methods section for details). We simulated the phenotype with SNP h 2 true = 20% and 20,000 causal SNPs. For each of the 5 replications, the discovery sample was divided into two non-overlapping halves and separate GWAS were conducted within each half, yielding ten GWAS in total. PGIs were subsequently constructed using each method, evaluated in an independent prediction sample, and the resulting estimates were averaged across replications. We do not explicitly consider the most recent methods that use functional annotations to further increase precision of estimates (e.g., SBayesRC, Zeng et al., 2024), or which combine GWAS estimates for a trait from multiple populations into a single PGI (e.g. PRS-CSx, Ruan et al., 2022). While such methods represent exciting progress in PGI construction, they retain the same core features of interest as are represented by the PGI construction methods considered here.

Results Across Simulation Conditions Representing a wider range of Polygenicity and Genetic Architecture

We went on to examine the generalizability of the obtained pattern of results, focusing this more expansive set of conditions on SBayesR, LDpred2-inf, and Clumping. SBayesR was selected as an aggressive shrinkage-based estimator expected to yield the highest uncorrected R² but to be more biased after correction. Results of Simulations of a Mixture Architecture using Real Genotypes

When a PGI is used to predict the same phenotype on which it was trained within an independent sample drawn from the same population, downward bias in its error-corrected R² relative to the total SNP h 2 of that phenotype may indicate that the PGI disproportionately represents different components of that trait's genetic architecture. We simulated a scenario in which such bias might become apparent. Specifically, we generated a phenotype (AB) that was equally affected by a mixture of two distributions (50% of its heritability was attributable to each distribution) of causal effects, a relatively concentrated set of 10,000 causal variants (A) and a relatively diffuse set of 100,000 causal variants (B). Such a scenario broadly resembles that which might be expected for educational attainment (which has been modeled as arising from genetic components corresponding to cognitive and noncognitive factors; Demange et al., 2021) or molecular traits (e.g., testosterone, for which the genetic architecture is thought to reflect core genetic pathways superimposed on a far more highly polygenic background; Sinnot-Armstrong et al., 2021). We also generated phenotypes with genetic architectures arising from only one of these distributions each (A or B). We developed PGIs using both SBayesR or LDpred2-inf based on GWAS performed on the mixture phenotype (AB), which we then used to predict the nonmixture phenotypes A and B. The SNP h 2 true of each of the three phenotypes was set to 20%. If a PGI trained on the mixture phenotype (AB) is not disproportionately biased with respect to the two distributions of causal effects composing its genetic architecture, its associations with the non-mixture phenotypes (A and B) should be equal.

As hypothesized, we found that the SBayesR derived PGI for the mixture phenotype (AB) evinced a biased pattern of associations (both error-corrected and uncorrected) with the non-mixture (A and B) phenotypes (Figure 5 and Supplementary Table S5). SBayesR derived PGIs were appreciably more strongly related to the phenotype created from the more concentrated polygenic architecture (A) than to the phenotype arising from the more diffuse polygenic architecture (B), despite both A and B phenotypes sharing exactly half of their respective genetic architecture with the mixture phenotype AB. In comparison, the LDpred2-inf derived PGI evinced an unbiased patterning of associations with the A and B phenotypes. Moreover, for LDpred2-inf, the error-corrected R² for both the concentrated phenotype A (0.093) and the diffuse phenotype B (0.093) each approached the theoretical maximum of 0.10. Of particular note is that the SBayesR model of genetic architecture produced biased patterns of association despite that it is explicitly a mixture model that subsumes the genetic architecture of the AB phenotype. In contrast, LDPred2-inf produced an unbiased pattern of associations despite the fact that its infinitesimal model of genetic architecture was a misspecification relative to the true model.

Discussion

In standard GWAS, associations for millions of SNPs with a phenotype are individually estimated with error. This presents two challenges for constructing PGIs. First, even small amounts of estimation error will compound across the myriad estimates, diluting or in some cases nearly completely overshadowing true signal. Second, the marginal associations obtained directly from GWAS do not account for LD among variants and must be weighted to approximate conditional estimates. PGI construction methods typically attempt to surmount both of these challenges.

Methods that aggressively shrink individual SNP effects towards, or exactly to, zero can often increase prediction accuracy by reducing or removing SNPs whose true conditional effects are null or trivial relative to their estimation error. However, they may also produce biased representations of genetic signal. Using a combination of simulation studies and empirical applications, we find that after correcting for measurement error, many of the most predictive PGI estimation methods (LDpred2-auto, SBayesR, PRS-CS-auto) leave substantial portions of SNP h 2 unaccounted for. We find that LDpred2-inf, which most closely resembles a multiple regression estimator to account for LD, produces PGIs with lower realized prediction accuracy but nearly fully recovers SNP h 2 after error correction. By providing a means of obtaining error-corrected estimates of prediction accuracy, we have highlighted what we believe to be an uncommonly recognized bias-variance tradeoff in the derivation of PGIs. Researchers are likely widely aware of this trade-off when implementing clumping and p-values thresholding methods that explicitly remove all but one SNP within a locus and all SNPs falling above a particular p-value significance threshold. For instance, it is wellknown that when constructing PGIs exclusively from independent genome-wide significant loci, the resulting index will only represent a portion of genetic propensity for polygenic traits and that weakly associated SNPs may exhibit different patterns of association with collateral traits than is exhibited by the PGI. Such limitations may be more difficult to intuit when using an advanced method that relies, for example on a mixture distribution. Nevertheless, a straightforward way to understand the results is as follows: construction methods that rely on aggressive shrinkage to obtain enhanced prediction accuracies, do so by discounting or eliminating the contributions from some components of polygenic architecture, thereby providing biased indices of polygenetic effects on the phenotype that the PGI was trained to index.

Our simulation results highlight that the same general pattern of bias-variance tradeoff applies across a wide range of trait polygenicities. One might expect bias to occur when sparsityinducing PGI construction methods are applied to traits with a near infinitesimal polygenic architecture. However, we find that mixture-model based PGI construction methods suffer from the same limitations even when a relatively smaller number of causal variants contribute to polygenic signal. In fact, even though the mixture model assumed by the SBayesR model subsumes the model used to generate the data in our simulations (i.e., we generated SNP effects from a mixture of a normal distribution of causal effects and a point mass of noncausal effects), PGIs produced using "incorrect" LDpred2-inf model were produced error-corrected estimates of prediction accuracy that were higher, and closer to total SNP h 2 . We attribute this to the inability of the mixture model to perfectly determine the identity of causal variants, thereby setting estimates for some causal variants to 0 rather than including their noisy signal in the PGI. In contrast, LDpred2-inf operates primarily a multiple regression estimator that includes all variants while accounting for their LD, with regularization performed on the LD matrix to stabilize estimates without otherwise aggressively shrinking estimates. While LDpred2-inf bears its name to reflect its characterization as an infinitesimal model in which all variants are assumed to have a nonzero effect, it might be best to view it less as an infinitesimal model, but as a model that allows each variant to have a nonzero estimate without imposing aggressive shrinkage. Importantly, our findings illustrate that the optimal PGI construction method depends on its intended application. When the primary objective is to maximize prediction accuracy, methods that introduce systematic bias may nevertheless be preferable because of their lower variance. However, when the objective is to capture the full genetic architecture of a trait, minimizing systematic bias may be more important than maximizing prediction accuracy.

Our correction of prediction accuracy for measurement error in the PGIs builds directly on work by Tucker-Drob (2017) and DiPrete et al. (2018) and on classic methods from psychometrics and econometrics (Spearman, 1904). In recent years, genetic research implementing various approaches to error-correction methods has become increasingly common (Becker et al., 2021; Frach et al., 2026; Malawsky et al., 2026; Pingault et al., 2021). However, previous applications have not considered the important role that the choice of PGI construction method plays. In fact, rather than treating the error-corrected estimate of prediction accuracy as a quantity to be estimated independently of SNP h 2 (as we have done here), some methods (e.g. Becker et al., 2021 , Pingault et al., 2021 , Frach et al., 2026) use an external estimate of SNP h 2 as a fixed value to identify the error correction model. Our results indicate that this choice may bias downstream estimates, especially when using aggressive shrinkage-based PGI construction methods. An advantage of methods that use an external of SNP h 2 has been that they can implement existing GWAS summary statistics and do not require access to individual-level data from the GWAS sample. Such data are often difficult or impossible to access, such that directly splitting the discovery sample can be impractical. Here, we have derived method for synthetically splitting GWAS summary statistics to provide a means of obtaining error-corrected estimates of prediction accuracy without relying on the strong assumption that the external estimate of SNP h 2 generalizes to the PGI method(s) employed or to the prediction sample. We expect that there may be wide-spread applications of the synthetic split approach that we have introduced here beyond the estimation of obtaining errorcorrected estimates of prediction accuracy. To facilitate implementation of synthetic split approach, we are making publicly available noise GWAS summary statistics generated using genotypes from the UKB (n = 49,879) and the HRS (n = 9,878).

SNP h 2 estimates are often interpreted as representing the theoretical upper asymptote of PGI prediction with increasing GWAS discovery sample sizes (Wang et al., 2023). Error-corrected estimates of PGI prediction cannot be universally interpreted in this way. For many PGI construction methods, the extent of shrinkage may vary systematically with GWAS discovery sample sizes, such that PGIs derived using larger GWAS exceed the error-corrected prediction accuracies of PGIs derived for the same trait using smaller sample sizes. For many PGIs for which error-corrected prediction accuracies appreciably differ when implementing moderately-size GWAS discovery samples, prediction may converge with one another and with SNP h 2 as GWAS sample sizes approach levels needed for saturation. Of course, PGI prediction may also exceed SNP h 2 for other reasons, such as the inclusion of rare variants in the PGI that are not included in the estimate of SNP h 2 , estimation of SNP h 2 using GWAS meta-analysis of heterogeneous samples, and employment of a more reliable measure of the phenotype in the prediction sample than was used in the discovery sample.

In summary, we have used both simulation and empirical application to obtain errorcorrected estimates of prediction accuracy of PGIs constructed using a variety of construction methods. Our results highlight an uncommonly recognized bias-variance tradeoff in the choice of PGIs. PGIs produced by methods that aggressively shrink information from a subset of variants (LDpred2-auto, SBayesR, and PRS-CS-auto) may not represent the broader genetic architecture of the phenotype and may evince biased patterns of associations with collateral phenotypes. LDpred2-inf, which most closely resembles a multiple regression estimator to account for LD, produces PGIs with relatively lower prediction accuracy but after error correction nearly fully recovers SNP h 2 and produces unbiased patterns of associations with collateral phenotypes. Importantly, while we have used error correction to index and highlight this phenomenon, this limitation applies to results from standard applications of PGIs within statistical genetics, even when error correction is not of interest. Nevertheless, when the primary goal is to maximize individual-level prediction, aggressive shrinkage-based methods may still be the most appropriate choice.

Methods Protocol for Synthetically Splitting GWAS Summary Statistics

In many cases it may be difficult or impossible to access and process individual-level data from the original GWAS in order to compute GWAS into independent halves. We derived a pipeline for synthetically splitting existing GWAS summary statistics into two independent sets that both decreases computationally burden and obviates the need for access to individual-level data from the original GWAS (see Extended Data Figure 1 for a conceptual overview of the protocol). Here, the noise GWAS refers to a GWAS of a pseudo-phenotype randomly sampled independently for each individual from a standard normal distribution (M = 0, SD = 1). Empirical Analyses

Population-level GWAS

The primary GWAS discovery sample was a subset of White British participants from the UK Biobank (n = 237,944).

UKB Prediction Sample

Our primary prediction sample was a subset of white British UKB participants not included in the discovery sample (n = 33,195). To prevent sample overlap and cryptic relatedness, we excluded any prediction-sample participant with a kinship coefficient > 0.05 with any participant in either discovery half, which corresponds roughly to third-degree relatives or closer. Kinship was estimated with the UKB relatedness file (Bycroft et al., 2018).

UKB Discovery Sample

The primary GWAS discovery sample was a subset of White British participants from the UK Biobank (n = 237,944) who were not included in the prediction subsample. Genetically related individuals were identified using UK Biobank's pre-computed pairwise kinship coefficients. Participants were clustered into family units by grouping all individuals connected through kinship relationships (kinship > 0.05), such that individuals related either directly or indirectly (via intermediate relatives) were assigned to the same family cluster. Individuals with no detected relatives were treated as single-member families and assigned unique family identifiers. The same kinship information was additionally used to ensure that no individual included in the discovery sample was related (kinship > 0.05) to any individual in the prediction sample, thereby preventing cross-sample genetic relatedness.

To construct independent split-half discovery samples while preserving family structure, the split was performed at the family level rather than the individual level. First, one row per family was created and the number of individuals in each family was recorded. The order of families was then randomly shuffled to prevent any systematic differences (e.g., due to recruitment order or family size) from influencing the split. Following this randomization step, families were sequentially assigned to the first half in their shuffled order until the cumulative number of individuals in half 1 reached approximately 50% of the total sample size. All remaining families were assigned to half 2. This ensured that no related individuals were split across halves, and the two halves were approximately equal in sample size, while the initial random shuffling ensured that assignment to half 1 versus half 2 was not systematically structured.

GWAS were then conducted separately for each phenotype in each split-half sample using REGENIE, with sample size ranging between 49,596 and 118,671 across halves (see Table 1), respectively. LDSC confirmed near-perfect genetic correlations across the two split-half GWAS for each (mean rg = 1.014), and independence of estimates (mean cross-trait intercept = 0.012).

UKB GWAS Phenotypes

Within UKB, we curated educational attainment (EA; UKB data-fields 6138 and 845), standing height (data-field 50), body mass index (BMI; UKB data-field 21001), verbal numerical reasoning (VNR; labelled as "fluid intelligence score" in the UKB; UKB data-field 20016), and neuroticism (UKB data-field 20127) in UKB. For each phenotype, the discovery sample was split into two halves, and separate GWAS were conducted in each half.

For each trait of interest, we regressed the phenotype on each available autosomal biallelic SNP, controlling for chromosomal sex, age, age 2 , birth cohort, and 10 genetic principal components (PCs) for ancestry. All phenotypes were standardized prior to association testing.

SBayesR

SBayesR performs Bayesian multiple regression using GWAS summary statistics and an external LD reference panel, modeling SNP effect sizes under a finite mixture prior consisting of a point mass at zero and multiple normal distributions with increasing variances. We specified mixture proportions of 0.95, 0.02, 0.02, and 0.01 across four components, with corresponding variance scaling parameters of 0, 0.01, 0.1, and 1, such that the majority of SNPs were assumed to have null effects while allowing a small proportion to have progressively larger non-zero effects. Posterior SNP effect sizes were estimated via Markov chain Monte Carlo sampling, and PGIs were constructed using the posterior mean effect estimates. LD was modeled using the UK Biobank 10K HapMap3 shrunk sparse LD reference panel based on 1,094,841 variants. Clumping PGI weights were constructed using LD-based clumping. Clumping did not use a separate precomputed LD reference panel; LD was estimated directly from the genotype data used in the clumping procedure via PLINK2. For each discovery half, GWAS summary statistics were subjected to LD clumping, in which SNPs were ranked by statistical significance and the most significant variant within a given genomic window was retained while nearby variants in LD above a specified r 2 threshold were removed. No p-value threshold was imposed at the clumping stage, such that all SNPs were eligible for selection and retention was determined by LD structure rather than significance filtering.

We used data on educational attainment, height, and verbal-numeric reasoning within the UKB dataset to evaluate the sensitivity of results to clumping parameters. We examined multiple combinations of physical window sizes (1 Mb, 3 Mb, and 5 Mb) and LD thresholds (r² = 0.1, 0.05, 0.01; see Supplementary Table S4). Across parameter combinations, the highest error-corrected prediction accuracies for educational attainment and height were obtained from PGIs constructed using a 5 Mb window and an LD threshold of r² = 0.01. Although this specification produced the lowest error-corrected prediction accuracy for verbal-numeric reasoning, estimates were very similar across parameter combinations. We therefore retained the 5 Mb window and LD threshold of r² = 0.01 for all clumping-based PGIs reported throughout the main text, including those constructed for other phenotypes and simulated phenotypes. Results from the alternative parameter specifications are reported in Supplementary Table S4.

LDpred2-auto and LDPred2-inf

For LDpred2, we used LD estimates for 1,054,330 HapMap3 variants derived from 362,320 individuals of European-like ancestry in the UKB. Whereas LDpred2-inf assumes an infinitesimal genetic architecture in which all variants contribute to the phenotype, LDpred2-auto uses a Bayesian spike-and-slab model that allows some variants to have zero effects. Specifically, LDpred2-auto estimates the proportion of causal variants and SNP heritability directly from the GWAS summary statistics using a Gibbs sampling procedure, rather than requiring these parameters to be specified or tuned using an external validation sample (Privé et al., 2020). PGIs were constructed using the resulting posterior mean SNP effect estimates.

Simulations

We conducted a series of simulation analyses using individual-level genotype data from the UKB and the HRS to evaluate the performance of PGI construction methods. Across all conditions, we simulated quantitative phenotypes under a genetic architecture with varying numbers of causal SNPs (500, 1K, 5K, 10K, 20K, 30K, 100K, and a 20K condition restricted to functional annotations) and two levels of SNP heritability (SNP h 2 true = 0.20 and SNP h 2 true = 0.05).

For each condition, we ran five independent replications, resulting in a total of 120 simulations. In each replication, causal SNPs were randomly sampled from the available genotype data, and their effect sizes were generated using GCTA. Individual-level genetic values were computed as the sum of allele dosages weighted by these true effects. Phenotypes were constructed by adding normally distributed environmental noise such that the target heritability was achieved. All phenotypes were subsequently standardized.

For the functional annotation condition, causal variants were restricted to protein-coding regions using the baselineLD annotation model (Gazal et al., 2017). Specifically, we first selected SNPs annotated as coding in the baselineLD files (approximately 39,000 SNPs), then intersected this set with SNPs present in our genotype data (resulting in a total of 21,190 SNPs). From this subset, 20,000 SNPs were randomly sampled as causal variants in each replication.

UKB GWAS

Genome-wide association studies (GWAS) were conducted in UKB using a total of 150,901 White British unrelated participants using the simulated phenotypes. Prior to analyses we implemented quality-control (QC) specifications such as excluding SNPs with Minor Allele Frequency (MAF) < 1%, call rate < 95%, and those that deviated from Hardy-Weinberg Equilibrium (p < 1x10 -6 ). SNPs with poor imputation quality (INFO < .40) were removed, while all SNPs were restricted to HapMap3. The sample was split into two non-overlapping halves, and GWAS were performed separately within each half while controlling for covariates (sex, age, age squared, birth cohort, and genetic principal components). This produced two independent sets of summary statistics per replication.

LD Score Regression

SNP h 2 was estimated using the version of LDSC (Bulik-Sullivan et al., 2015) implemented in the Genomic SEM R package (Grotzinger et al., 2019). Because, the majority of our simulations and empirical data analyses capitalized on genotypes from individuals of European-like ancestry from UKB, we used European-like ancestry LD scores from the Pan-UK Biobank reference panel (Karczewski et al., 2025). We selected this reference panel following preliminary simulation analyses using UKB genotypes in which application of LDSC using the European-like ancestry 1000 Genomes reference panel (1000 Genomes Project Consortium, 2015) produced modest overestimates of SNP h 2 . Specifically, across 15 simulation replications with a SNP h 2 true of 0.20, the mean LDSC estimate was 0.219 using the 1000 Genomes reference panel, compared with 0.197 using Pan-UK Biobank. We therefore used Pan-UK Biobank LD scores for subsequent analyses.

PGI Construction Methods

Below we describe each PGI method employed and the associated LD reference panel, when relevant. To ensure comparability across PGI construction methods, we constructed PGIs from the 749,619 SNPs that were common across all LD reference panels and available in UKB after merging. Unadjusted PGI Unadjusted was constructed by weighting each allele by its corresponding marginal GWAS effect estimate, without LD modeling, clumping, or shrinkage.

PRS-CS-auto

PRS-CS-auto constructs PGIs using a Bayesian regression framework that applies continuous shrinkage priors to SNP effect sizes while explicitly accounting for LD among variants. The method uses GWAS summary statistics together with an external LD reference panel to estimate posterior SNP effect sizes under a global-local shrinkage prior, allowing effect sizes to be adaptively shrunk according to the strength of evidence for association. We used the UKB European reference panel provided with PRS-CS, which is based on 1,120,697 HapMap3 variants and captures LD patterns across the genome. In the auto implementation, the global shrinkage parameter controlling the overall sparsity of the genetic architecture is estimated directly from the data, eliminating the need for an external validation sample or parameter tuning.
