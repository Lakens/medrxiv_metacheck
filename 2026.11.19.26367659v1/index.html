What we found

We found 14 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Seeded aggregation of α-synuclein and tau in the gastrointestinal tract is a potential biomarker and window into disease pathogenesis, but its quantitative relationship to the brain is unknown. Here, various regions of the gut and brain from autopsies of Lewy body disease and other diagnoses were tested for seeded aggregation of α-synuclein and an Alzheimer's disease-related tau species, tau-dGAE. Gut seeding of αsynuclein (51.8 ± 16.8 vs 112.6 ± 23.0 hours) and tau-dGAE (28.5 ± 2.6 vs 31.7 ± 2.9 hours) was faster in Lewy body disease than non-Lewy cases and was correlated with seeding in frontal cortex and midbrain/medulla (Pearson's r from 0.75 -0.63). Seeding of α-synuclein in gut tissue was also correlated with loss of nigral pigment, pigmented neurons, Lewy bodies, and tyrosine hydroxylase-positive neurons as well as α-synuclein in the dorsal motor nucleus of the vagus (r values from 0.76 -0.49).

Seeding of tau-dGAE in duodenum (but not brain) was faster in Lewy body disease compared to non-Lewy cases (28.5 ± 2.6 vs 31.7 ± 2.9 hours). Tau seeded aggregation in duodenum was correlated with tau-dGAE seeded aggregation in hippocampus and frontal cortex (r values from 0.58 -0.55) and with tau and amyloid immunohistochemistry in the hippocampus, mainly for cases of Lewy body disease. Seeding for αsynuclein and tau-dGAE in the gut correlated with seeding in the brain regardless of disease status. Overall data show a quantitative mirroring of peripheral and central seeded aggregation and neuropathology and demonstrate its utility for studies of pathogenesis and clinical biomarkers.

INTRODUCTION

Seeded aggregation of pathologic proteins offers a view into the prion-like pathogenesis of neurodegenerative disease and serves as a clinical biomarker. The gastrointestinal (GI) system is a useful peripheral target for studying seeding in Lewy body disease (LBD) by having early and prevalent symptoms, routine biopsy in the general healthy population, and alterations to the enteric nervous system that mirror central pathology. Prior research using seeding assays in peripheral body sites have typically tuned the method for binary classification. Consequently, there are functional knowledge gaps about how peripheral and central pathologic proteins relate to each other and to core neuropathologic features. For example, in terms of α-synuclein the quantitative relationship between peripheral (e.g. gut) and central seeding is unknown and it is unclear how it changes in the context of LBD and if it relates to central loss of dopaminergic neurons and Lewy pathology. Furthermore, the rostro-caudal gradient of α-synuclein abundance, where immunohistochemical α-synuclein burden is greater in the upper gastrointestinal tract (e.g. duodenum) versus lower tract (e.g. colon) has had limited testing in terms of functional seeded aggregation. Less is known about tau, which has not been measured in humans in terms of seeding in the gastrointestinal tract and neurodegenerative disease. Such data would help clarify if peripheral seeding of tau relates to Alzheimer's Disease (AD) co-pathology in the context of LBD. Finally, it is unresolved if archived formalin-fixed and paraffin embedded tissues could be used to address these questions given their technical challenges with seeding assays. Overall, such data would help clarify the functional interdependencies of pathologic proteins throughout the body in neurodegenerative diseases.

To address these knowledge gaps, the current study includes subjects at autopsy with or without LBD and measures seeding of α-synuclein and AD-related tau (tau-dGAE) in archival GI tract and brain tissues. Data were collected at multiple GI sites and seeding was compared to diagnosis, other sites, and to histologic features of neurodegeneration and diagnostic neuropathology.

METHODS

Patient Samples

Tissues from forty-two participants were obtained sequentially at autopsy from The Oregon Brain Bank at Oregon Health and Science University (OHSU IRB No. 1623). Samples of duodenum, appendix, large intestine, midbrain/medulla, hippocampus, and frontal cortex were collected, fixed in formalin, and embedded in paraffin. All subjects underwent neuropathologic evaluation by a board-certified neuropathologist (RW). Twenty-three subjects received a neuropathologic diagnosis of Lewy Body Disease (LBD), and 19 control subjects did not (non-LBD). The LBD group included subjects with clinical diagnoses of Parkinson's disease (PD), Dementia with Lewy Bodies (DLB), Lewy Body Disease, Not Otherwise Specified (LBD-NOS). The control group included subjects with Alzheimer's Disease (AD), Hippocampal Sclerosis (HS), Progressive Supranuclear Palsy (PSP), mild ischemic injury (MII), or no diagnostic pathology (Table 1).

Real-Time Quaking-Induced Conversion (RT-QuIC) -Deparaffinization

A previously described protocol [16] was altered to deparaffinize tissue. For each sample, two 15μm sections were subjected to four 10-minute xylene washes with centrifugation (25°C for 5 minutes at 17,000 x g), fresh xylene overnight, and two more xylene washes in the morning. Two brief washes using 100% ethanol followed, with 10 seconds and 1 minute of vortexing respectively. Samples were shaken in a Thermomixer (Eppendorf ThermoMixer F1.5, Fisher Scientific 05-412-500) at 56°C for 3 minutes (300 rmp), centrifuged at 25°C for 5 minutes (17,000 x g), then subjected to three, 10-minute washes with 100% ethanol. During washes, samples were on a rocker and briefly vortexed every 2 minutes. Samples were spun between washes as described above. After the final wash, ~80% of supernatant was removed, and the samples were left at 4°C overnight. The next day, samples were dried for 30min in a SpeedVac (Savant SPD1010, Thermo Fisher Scientific) set to 10 Torr/minute and ambient temperature.

RT-QuIC -Tissue Processing

Samples were processed based on dry tissue weight as previously described [2, 16, 18] in TSB (pH 8.0, 500mM TRIS, 0.08% SDS, protease inhibitor (MilliporeSigma 11697498001, concentration per vendor). 500μl of TSB and fifteen, 0.7mm zirconia grinding beads (BioSpec 11079107ZX) were added to sample tubes. Samples were rocked for 30 seconds and vortexed for 30 seconds. Tubes were filled halfway with equal parts 0.7mm zirconia grinding beads and 0.4mm zirconia grinding beads (OPS Diagnostics BLBZ 400-250-37) and the remainder of the tube was filled with TSB. Samples underwent ten rounds of bead-beating (Mini-Beadbeater-Plus, BioSpec 1018), each round was 1 minute of homogenization and 1 minute in an ice water bath. Next, three 5-minute freeze/thaw cycles were performed by alternating samples between a -80°C ethanol bath and a 37°C water bath. Samples were then rocked for 10 minutes with intermittent vortexing, centrifuged at 25°C for 5 minutes (17,000 x g), old TSB was replaced, and then rocked for another 10 minutes. A 10% w/v homogenate in TSB was prepared in 0.2ml tubes. Shaking and heating of samples was performed at 90°C (1500rmp) for 20 minutes (Eppendorf Thermomixer F1.5, Fisher Scientific 05-412-500), then again at 60°C for another 90 minutes. Samples were briefly vortexed and then sonicated at 15°C using a cup-horn attachment and chiller (Qsonica Q700A-110, 431C2, 445, and 4905-110) (amp 30, on 3 seconds, off 2 seconds, 15 minutes of sonication). Samples were clarified 3-4 times by centrifugation at 4°C for 5 minutes (5000 x g) and stored at -80°C.

RT-QuIC -Assay

Previously published RT-QuIC protocols [2, 16, 18] were adapted for FFPE tissue. Each well of a Nunc black, clear-bottomed, 96-well plate (Thermo Fisher Scientific 265301) was loaded with six 0.8 mm silica beads (OPS Diag BLBG 800-200-03). Reaction buffer consisted of 40 mM NaPO4, 170 mM NaCl, 20um ThT (filtered with a 0.22um syringe filter (MilliporeSigma SLGPM33RS)), and 0.1 mg/ml recombinant monomer. The recombinant α-synuclein monomer (StressMarq catalog code SAA-100, batch# NPD-R091.30, NPD-R091.31, and NPD-R091.36) for brain tissue and Proteos RP-003 or Tau dGAE C322A monomer (StressMarq cat# SAA-500, batch# NPD-R145.04) for gastrointestinal tissue, was thawed, filtered with Amicon 100kDa, 0.5ml filters (MilliporeSigma UFC510024), and added prior to plating. For the α-synuclein assay, initial testing included tissue concentrations from 10 -3 to 10 -5 of GI tissue homogenate and 10 -1 and 10 -2 dilutions of brain tissue homogenate. For the tau assay, a 10 -3 dilution was used for both GI and brain tissue homogenates. After testing, 10 -3 of GI tissue and 10 -1 of brain tissue was used. 2μl of sample and 98μl of reaction buffer were plated in each well. The assay was run in a FLUOstar Omega plate reader (BMG Labtech) at 42°C (gastrointestinal tissue) or 37°C (brain tissue) and consisted of intermittent shaking (1 minute, 400rpm (gastrointestinal tissue) or 700rpm (brain tissue), double orbital) and rest (1 minute) as well as plate reading every 45 minutes (bottom read, 450nm excitation, 480nm emission). A baseline of 20,000 relative fluorescence units (rfu) was used to set optic gain. Onset of aggregation/lag time was defined as the time fluorescence exceeded 4x baseline and mean lag times were calculated across four technical replicates for each sample. Excluded replicates were those with nearinstantaneous aggregation; a known artifact in RT-QuIC. For the main manuscript, all technical replicates were used for quantitative comparisons. An exception was a demonstration in the supplemental of use of duodenum RT-QuIC data for conventional binary classification of LBD vs non-LBD, which was performed using the 3-of-4 replicates rule, where a case is called positive if three of four replicates show fluorescence 4x above a baseline threshold by a certain time and the minority slower aggregating technical replicates are ignored.

Microscopy and Image analysis

Paraffin sections (6μm) were deparaffinized and stained using eosin/luxol fast blue (ELFB) to identify histologic features or used in immunostaining preparations. Immunostaining was done after heat-and citratebased antigen retrieval and used antibodies to α-synuclein (MJFR1; Abcam; Catalog No. ab138501), tau (PHF1, kind gift from lab of Peter Davies and Aβ (4G8; BioLegend; Catalog No. 800701). Stains were developed using horseradish peroxidase (Vector ELITE HRP kit). Slides were digitized at 40x resolution on an automated brightfield slide scanner (Aperio GT 450; Leica Biosystems). Sections of FFPE midbrain and medulla were prepared as described above and photobleached for 96 hours to remove autofluorescence. Immunofluorescence staining was performed using antibodies against protein gene product 9.5 (PGP 9.5) (Sigma-Aldrich; Catalog No. AB1761-I) and Chromogranin A (1:200) in duodenum tissues and tyrosine hydroxylase (TH) (F-11; Santa Cruz Biotechnology; Catalog No. sc-25269), 8-hydroxy-guanosine (OHG-6) (N45.1; OriGene Technologies; Catalog No. AM20226PU-S), and PGP 9.5 in midbrain and medulla tissues. AlexaFluor secondary antibodies were applied (ThermoFisher; Catalog No. A-21244 and A-21137) and (Jackson ImmunoResearch Laboratories Inc.; 115545205) and washed in 4,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI; Invitrogen; Catalog No. 62248). Mounting media (Invitrogen; S369175) was added to tissue before cover slipping (Fisher Scientific; Catalog No. 12541021). Full slide scans at 20x resolution were performed on an automated scanner (Axioscan 7; Zeiss). Quantification of Lewy Bodies and degenerative foci of pigment in macrophages were performed by manual counting in ELFB-stained slides within six high-power fields. Histologic evaluation of autolysis in gastrointestinal specimens was performed by a single blinded gastrointestinal pathologist (GS) for each tissue layer (10 = complete,0 = none) and the estimated proportion of nuclear basophilia, lamina propria disruption/hyalinization, crypt base loss and submucosal gland dilation/architectural disruption. Histologic evaluation of morphologic features in brain sections was blinded and performed by the same single pathologist rater (GS) in randomly selected high-power fields after initial training and ongoing QA by a board-certified neuropathologist (RW). For image analysis of pigment and immunostaining, whole slide image files were imported into image analysis software, Visiopharm (Versions 2025.02.2x64, Visiopharm A/S, Hørsholm, Denmark). ROIs were manually identified for gray matter, white matter, and substantia nigra. Image segmentation of pigment, Lewy pathology, tau pathology and amyloid pathology was performed through manual annotation of all pixels in a blinded manner by a single rater (AQ) in 10+ representative high-power fields followed by training an automated detector in Visiopharm that could be deployed across all fields of all slides. Results were spot checked in randomly selected slides by a board-certified neuropathologist (RW). TH expression in the midbrain was measured by identifying neuron-associated pixels using whole-slide intensity thresholding of PGP 9.5 and OHG-8 (same threshold for all subjects) and mean neuronal TH intensity was normalized to PGP 9.5.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analysis was performed in RStudio (Posit Software, Boston, MA). Groupwise lag times were compared using a student's t-test and ANOVA (functions t.test and aov). Pearson correlation was calculated using linear regression (function lm) and outliers were identified using standardized residuals (function rstandard) greater than 3. Results are reported as Pearson's r correlation coefficient. Results were evaluated for the entire cohort and then subsequently in just LBD and non-LBD subgroups. A secondary analysis divided LBD into those with (LBD+AD) and without (LBD-no-AD) Alzheimer's disease co-pathology based on neuropathologist evaluation at the time of autopsy.

RESULTS

Baseline characteristics and tissue quality

There were no significant differences of baseline characteristics (Table 1, sFigure 1) between LBD and non-LBD in terms of age (77.3 ±7.4 vs. 80.2 ±15.7 years), sex (69.6 vs. 57.9 % male), and post-mortem interval (17 ±9.5 vs. 17.4 ±16.3 hours). Seven LBD subjects (30%) had concomitant neuropathologic diagnosis of AD pathology. GI tissue autolysis was present, typically confined to the mucosa, and was not significantly different between groups (sFigure 1).

Validation of seeded aggregation assay in brain tissue versus corresponding neuropathology

Seeding in brain and GI tissues was measured and lag times are shown in Figure 1A. To validate seeding against neuropathology, brain seeding was compared to neuropathologic features (sFigure 2). In the LBD group, seeding of α-synuclein in the frontal cortex was correlated with both α-synuclein immunohistochemistry (r = 0.63) and Lewy bodies in the substantia nigra (r = 0.51). Seeding of tau-dGAE was correlated in the non-LBD group with Braak tau stage in the frontal cortex (r = 0.73) and hippocampus (r = 0.79).

Seeded aggregation of α-synuclein and tau-dGAE in the gastrointestinal tract

To test the dependency of gut seeding on the presence of neurodegenerative disease, seeding in gastrointestinal tissues was compared between LBD and non-LBD groups (Figure 1B). Seeding in duodenal tissues was significantly faster in LBD versus non-LBD for α-synuclein (51.8 ± 16.8 vs 112.6 ± 23.0 hours) and tau-dGAE (28.5 ± 2.6 vs 31.7 ± 2.9 hours) (Figure 1B) and there were progressively smaller albeit-still-significant groupwise differences for α-synuclein seeding in the appendix (63.7 ± 30.9 vs 105.9 ± 28.9 hours) and large intestine (74.3 ± 36.5 vs 108.7 ± 33.5) (sFigure 3). Duodenal α-synuclein seeding was correlated with duodenal tau-dGAE seeding in all subjects (r = 0.54) and just the LBD subgroup (r = 0.5) (data not shown). Seeded aggregation in gastrointestinal tissue was not correlated with participant age, post-mortem interval, atherosclerosis, or autolysis (sFigure 4).

As an aside, to demonstrate the use of seeding as a binary test in this specimen type, the fastest 3-of-4 replicates rule was used where a positive test has 3 of 4 technical replicates that aggregate before a determined time. Duodenal α-synuclein seeding showed sensitivity of 91.3% and specificity of 100% for binary classification of LBD versus non-LBD (sFigure 5). Additional testing was not performed including testing of earlier disease stage, performing endpoint dilution, and blinded external validation.

Seeded aggregation in the brain versus seeded aggregation in the gastrointestinal tract

To compare seeding in the gut and brain, lag times for seeding in the brain were measured and then correlated to seeding in gastrointestinal tissues Seeded aggregation was measured in various brain regions and compared between LBD and non-LBD (Figure 1B). For α-synuclein, lag times were shorter in LBD for the frontal cortex (18.0 ± 8.6 vs 44.4 ± 18.4 hours) and midbrain/medulla (12.0 ± 3.3 vs 28.4 ± 13.0 hours). For tau-dGAE, lag times were not different between LBD and non-LBD in frontal cortex (23.2 ± 2.5 vs 24.2 ± 2.5 hours) or hippocampus (21.9 ± 2.3 vs 21.7 ± 2.2 hours). Seeding in the brain was compared to seeding in gastrointestinal tissues through linear correlation (Figure 2). For α-synuclein, the duodenum was positively correlated with frontal cortex for all subjects (r = 0.75) and borderline for both LBD (r = 0.42, p = 0.079) and non-LBD (r = 0.43, p = 0.079) subgroups. Duodenum was correlated with midbrain/medulla for all subjects (r = 0.55) but only borderline for LBD (r = 0.35, p = 0.10). The large intestine was more strongly correlated with midbrain/medulla for all subjects (r = 0.63) and the non-LBD subgroup (r = 0.64). For the LBD subgroup there was range restriction of assay results (mean lag time = 12.0 ± 3.3 hours) and ten-fold dilution of midbrain/medulla demonstrated increased inter-subject variability (mean lag time = 48.8 ± 14.6 hours). At this lower input concentration of midbrain/medulla tissue, significant correlation with α-synuclein seeding in the large intestine was seen for the LBD subgroup (Figure 2, dark orange, r = 0.61) in addition to all subjects (r = 0.61) and the non-LBD subgroup (r = 0.40).

For tau-dGAE seeding in brain versus gastrointestinal tissues, duodenum was correlated with hippocampus (r = 0.55) and frontal cortex (r = 0.58). In the LBD subgroup, duodenum was correlated with hippocampus (r = 0.78) and frontal cortex (r = 0.62). In non-LBD, duodenum correlated with hippocampus (r = 0.64) and borderline for frontal cortex (r = 0.58, p = 0.07).

Seeding across brain and gut and across α-synuclein and tau-dGAE were compared and there was one positive correlation for tau-dGAE in the duodenum and α-synuclein in the cortex (r = 0.44) and a negative correlation for just LBD between α-synuclein in the large intestine and tau-dGAE in the cortex (r = -0.63).

Brain Lewy pathology versus α-synuclein seeded aggregation in the gastrointestinal tract

To measure how seeding of α-synuclein in the gut relates to central LBD neuropathology, histologic features were counted in the brain and correlated to gut lag times. Lewy pathology was measured using quantification of immunostained α-synuclein and manual identification of histologic Lewy bodies. The LBD group had markedly elevated α-synuclein-positive pixels in the substantia nigra (0.035 ± 0.023 vs 0.0022 ± 0.0019 % tissue area) and the dorsal motor nucleus of the vagus (0.0026 ± 0.0023 vs 0.0000011 ± 0.0000045 % tissue area) and the LBD group had Lewy bodies in the ventrolateral substantia nigra (0.34 ± 0.39 vs 0 ± 0 per highpower field) and medial substantia nigra (0.27 ± 0.34 vs 0 ± 0 per high-power field).

Lewy pathology in the LBD group was compared to gastrointestinal seeding (Figure 3A). Lewy bodies in the medial substantia nigra correlated with α-synuclein seeding in both the duodenum (r = -0.45) and borderline for large intestine (r = -0.38, p = 0.09). α-synuclein-positive pixels in the DMV (r = -0.50) were correlated with αsynuclein seeding just in the large intestine. No correlation was seen with Lewy bodies in the ventrolateral substantia nigra, and no correlation was seen between tau-dGAE seeding and Lewy pathology.

Tau and amyloid neuropathology compared to tau-dGAE seeded aggregation in the gastrointestinal tract and brain

To measure how seeding of tau in the gut relates to central tau and amyloid pathology, Lewy-related histologic features were counted in the brain and correlated to gut lag times. Tau and amyloid were measured in immunostained sections of hippocampus. There were no groupwise differences between LBD and non-LBD for amyloid-positive pixels (3.1 ± 3.2 vs 3.1 ± 2.6 % area, p = 0.9) or tau (2.3 ± 2.7 vs 3.1 ± 3.9 % area, p = 0.4).

Amyloid immunostaining in the hippocampus was correlated to tau-dGAE seeding in the duodenum for the LBD subgroup (r = 0.57) and borderline for the entire cohort (r = 0.25, p = 0.11) (Figure 3B). No correlation existed in the non-LBD group. Similar correlation, strongest in LBD, was present for tau-dGAE seeding in the hippocampus versus amyloid immunostaining in the hippocampus (sFigure 7). Tau immunostaining in the hippocampus was borderline correlated with tau-dGAE seeding in the duodenum just for the LBD subgroup (r = -0.34, p = 0.12).

No significant correlation was present between tau and amyloid in the hippocampus versus α-synuclein seeding in the duodenum or large intestine.

Nigral neurodegeneration versus seeded aggregation in the gastrointestinal tract

To measure how seeding in the gut relates to central neurodegenerative pathology, nigral pathologic features were counted in the brain and correlated to gut lag times. Degeneration of dopaminergic neurons was measured in histologic sections of the substantia nigra by image analysis of total neuromelanin pigment and manual count of pigmented neurons and neuromelanin-laden macrophages. The LBD group had less total pigment in the substantia nigra (0.25 ± 0.18 vs 0.38 ± 0.19 % total SN area), fewer pigmented foci (586 ± 337.2 vs 1041 ± 400.6 no. foci), and fewer pigmented neurons (6.0 ± 3.0 vs. 9.1 ± 3.2 per high-power field). There were borderline increased neuromelanin-laded macrophages in the ventrolateral substantia nigra (3.7 ± 2.1 vs 2.9 ± 1.3 per high-power field, p = 0.19) and increased numbers but no groupwise differences in medial substantia nigra (4.0 ± 2.5 vs. 4.2 ± 2.7 per high-power field, p = 0.8). In the dorsal motor nucleus of the vagus, the LBD and non-LBD groups did not have significantly different numbers of pigmented neurons (1.5 ± 1.9 vs. 1.0 ± 1.5, p = 0.3) or neuromelanin-laded macrophages (2.1 ± 7.3 vs 1.3 ± 3.1, p = 0.6).

Seeding in the gastrointestinal tract was compared to histologic measures of neurodegeneration (Figure 4A). Nigral pigment loss correlated strongly with α-synuclein seeding in the large intestine (r = 0.76) and less so with the duodenum (r = 0.45, p < 0.05). For the large intestine this correlation was significant in subgroup analysis of both LBD (r = 0.67) and non-LBD (r = 0.65). Using partial correlation, findings were replicated after adjusting for subject age (sFigure 6). Findings were similar when using manual counts of pigmented neurons with strongest correlation to α-synuclein seeding in the large intestine (r = 0.56) that was significant in the LBD subgroup (r = 0.42) but not non-LBD (data not shown). Neuromelanin-laded macrophages in ventrolateral and not medial substantia nigra were correlated with the duodenum in the entire cohort (r = -0.37) and the non-LBD subgroup (r = -0.58). In the LBD subgroup, increased seeding speed in the large intestine was also correlated with decreased neuronal Tyrosine hydroxlase expression (r = 0.49) in immunolabeled neurons within the midbrain with the substantia nigra (Figure 4A).

Mucosal enteroendocrine cells, seeded aggregation, and nigral neurodegeneration

To pilot test the relationship of seeding and pathology to an implicated cell type in the gut, enteroendocrine cells were counted in a subset of GI specimens and compared to seeding and neuropathology. In a subset of cases, enteroendocrine cells were immunolabeled and manually counted in the duodenal mucosa. There were no significant differences in counts of enteroendocrine cells in LBD versus non-LBD (18.2 ± 12.3 vs. 13.7 ± 10.4). When comparing counts of enteroendocrine cells to seeded aggregation of α-synuclein in the same duodenal tissue, results show increased enteroendocrine cells with faster seeded aggregation (r = 0.49 for entire cohort, r = 0.61 for LBD only) (Figure 4B). Note that a lower 10 -4 concentration of duodenum tissue was used with more intersubject variability (sFigure 3). Increased counts of enteroendocrine cells were also correlated with loss of pigment in the substantia nigra (r = 0.49 for entire cohort, r = 0.59 for LBD only) (Figure 4B).

Comparison of seeded aggregation in Lewy Body Disease with versus without Alzheimer's co-pathology

To test the relationship of seeding to Alzheimer's disease co-pathology, neuropathologist diagnosis was compared to lag times in the gut for both α-synuclein and tau. The LBD cohort was split based on the presence of concurrent Alzheimer's disease pathology determined by neuropathologist diagnosis (LBD+AD) versus without (LBD-no-AD). No differences in seeding were present between LBD+AD versus LBD-no-AD in the gut or brain (sFigure 8). LBD-no-AD had borderline faster α-synuclein aggregation in the large intestine versus non-LBD (66.1 ± 31.4 vs 108.7 ± 33.5 hours, p = 0.08) and LBD+AD did not (83.9 ± 45.6 hours). In contrast, only LBD+AD had faster tau-dGAE aggregation in the duodenum versus non-LBD (27.8 ± 1.9 vs 31.7 ± 2.9 hours) and LBD-no-AD did not (28.9 ± 2.8 hours).

DISCUSSION

The current study demonstrates seeded aggregation in the gut and brain along with histologic measures of neuropathology. Data show that seeding in the gastrointestinal tract mirrors central seeding, Lewy-, and AD copathology and mirrors neurodegeneration independent of aging and the presence of a synucleinopathy. Preliminary results show mucosal enteroendocrine cells provide overlapping data to seeded aggregation. Overall, the GI tract provides an accessible window for studies of pathogenesis and clinical biomarkers. Data using archival tissues opens the benefits of large well-characterized retrospective cohorts with neuropathologic assessment.

Seeded aggregation of α-synuclein in the absence of Lewy Body Disease

Data here demonstrate that seeding in the periphery (gut) mirrors seeding in the brain regardless of the presence of LBD. The speed of seeding was faster in LBD but even in non-LBD controls (a mixture of healthy controls and non-LBD neurodegenerative disease) seeding in the gut still strongly correlated with the brain. There is limited prior data for comparison and technical differences. Two previous studies from experienced labs tested seeded aggregation in the gut of PD versus controls and found excellent specificity [13, 24]. Perhaps most importantly, the reaction time limit in these studies was shorter at 40 hours which data above suggest is too short to capture most non-LBD aggregation. In support of this, data from both previous studies showed occasional technical replicates and aggregate data where controls were beginning to aggregate at the end of their time window. Other technical variables may also play a role such as use of fresh/frozen tissue and different monomer protein substrate. In terms of the magnitude of association of peripheral and central seeding, when data did not have a restricted range there did not appear to be differences in the slope of linear regression between non-LBD and LBD. Testing of explicit subgroups such as Alzheimer's disease and input of additional tissue concentrations is needed to further explore brain-gut seeding relationships.

Seeded aggregation of α-synuclein in Lewy body disease

The finding in the current study demonstrating increased seeding of α-synuclein in LBD in the gut and association with severity of synucleinopathy aligns with prior studies using other measures and supports the use of seeding assays alongside the benefits of retrospective studies. Numerous (immuno-) histochemical studies have identified Lewy bodies and abnormal (phospho-)α-synuclein in gut nerve plexi and nerves [4, 9, 23, 25]. In these studies, gut staining was highly specific and weakly-to-moderately sensitive for LBD, with sensitivity improving in more developed and severe disease. Our lab's efforts using α-synuclein immunofluorescence were unsuccessful to obtain specificity for LBD in GI nerves or enteroendocrine cells which others have ascribed to use of formalin fixative rather than paraformaldehyde-based fixatives (e.g. Zamboni's) along with limited sampling [12]. For seeded aggregation there have been fewer studies but they show similar trends to histology where there is moderate sensitivity and high specificity for discerning LBD and the assay sensitivity increases with more advanced disease and larger/deeper tissue sampling [13, 24]. Our data also aligns with mapping of αsynuclein in the gut. The decreasing gradient of sensitivity in the current study from duodenum to appendix to large intestine aligns with the rostro-caudal gradient of synuclein seen by immunohistochemistry and positivity rates in other seeded aggregation assays [3, 15]. The concordance of data is encouraging for the use of seeded aggregation in archival tissues for retrospective studies ideally with paired neuropathologic assessment. Such studies can circumvent time course bottlenecks by spanning the decades of preclinical/prodromal disease for research on pathophysiology and neuroprotective treatments and spanning the decades of LBD progression for research on disease modification.

Seeded aggregation of tau-dGAE in the gut and brain

Seeded aggregation of tau in the gastrointestinal tract has not been measured in human neurodegenerative disease but can be informed by limited corresponding data from animal studies and human data in the skin. Animal studies have shown pathologic tau in the enteric nervous system is related to models of neurodegeneration. In mouse and rat models of AD and aging, enteric neurons and myenteric plexi have been shown to harbor increased phosphorylated tau, total tau, and Aβ with a subset colocalized to α-synuclein [14, 19]. Furthermore, tau was suggested in mouse models to be induced by gut inflammation and travel rostrally along the vagus in association with worse learning and memory that is rescued by vagotomy [8]. In humans, tau is expressed in enteric neurons but immunohistochemical studies of its relationship with neurodegenerative disease have been negative or inconclusive [11]. Prior human studies of seeded aggregation in the periphery have used skin and generally focused on discerning controls from pure and mixed tauopathies such as progressive supranuclear palsy (PSP), corticobasal degeneration (CBD), and Alzheimer's Disease with moderate-to-excellent accuracy [10, 26]. A recent detailed study included cases of LBD and found elevated tau seeding in the skin, agreeing with data here showing elevated tau seeding in the gastrointestinal tract in LBD. An interesting difference in findings is that tau seeding in the skin did not show a relationship to AD copathology in the brain. This study included neuropathologic evaluation and reported on a subset of four DLB participants with AD co-pathology, but seeding was not visibly elevated in four participants within the DLB subgroup and was also not elevated in DLB versus PD. This gut versus skin difference in the relationship of tau seeding to central AD co-pathology could have entirely technical or biologic explanations such as different tau monomers and LBD enrollment or differences in the enteric and cutaneous nervous systems [1, 17, [20] [21] [22]. For example, the tau-dGAE monomer may be an ideal substrate to model Alzheimer's co-pathology given 1) its isolation from the core of paired helical filaments in neurofibrillary tangles, 2) aggregation without the need for anionic additives like heparin, and 3) prion-like seeding of full-length tau into disease-associated conformations [17]. Further human studies are needed to directly compare seeding at different peripheral sites.

Enteroendocrine cells are a possible link to seeding

Current data and previous studies suggest enteroendocrine cells are a potentially useful indicator of enteric neuropathology. Enteroendocrine cells are a rare lining cell of the gastrointestinal tract and play a combined role sampling luminal contents, making and secreting hormones, and interacting with the enteric nervous system. Enteroendocrine cells, like seeding, are potentially poised for clinical translation as these are mucosalbased cells that can be routinely biopsied. Transgenic-and exposure-based mouse models suggest that these cells have similar properties to neurons and synapse basally with enteric neurons where in PD models crosssectional data suggest they transynaptically transfer α-synuclein to the enteric nervous system [6]. Human data shows expression of (phospho-)α-synuclein and (phospho-)tau in enteroendocrine cells [5, 7] and diseases such as inflammatory bowel disease are associated with PD risk and have increased enteroendocrine cells [27]. As noted above, we attempted α-synuclein immunohistochemistry in GI tissue and did not see LBD specificity, possibly due to use of formalin and not paraformaldehyde-based fixative [12]. For cell counts of enteroendocrine cells, early data here are in line with these prior findings by showing a quantitative link between counts of enteroendocrine cells and seeded aggregation.

Limitations

An important limitation of the current study is a lack of additional clinical measurements such as neurologic and gastrointestinal symptoms, disease duration, and standardized rating instruments (e.g. UPDRS, MOCA). The current study contained diagnosis, baseline patient characteristics, and neuropathologic evaluation and for the LBD group, pathologic staging is Braak stage 3+ which generally corresponds with clinical manifestation (tremor, slowness, stiffness, and/or postural instability). Explicit measurement of these and other ancillary data (e.g. UPSIT smell test, cardiac scintigraphy, DatScan, LRRK2/GCA genotype) will help connect peripheral seeding to studies of pathogenesis and in vivo data for clinical research. Encouragingly, findings are independent of aging, which typically is one of the largest effect confounders in neurodegenerative disease research. Another limitation was the lack of testing smaller superficial biopsy specimens. Full thickness specimens were used in the current study to sample more of the enteric nervous system and maximize sensitivity for quantitative comparisons. Use of GI seeding in vivo and longitudinally will require studies of a feasible smaller biopsy specimen that is limited to mucosa and submucosa. Data here show enteroendocrine cells located in the mucosa may offer a superficial surrogate for biopsy. For studies outside of screening biopsies, full thickness and deeper GI tissues are often available as they are some of the most common larger and deeper sampling procedures performed in the healthcare system (e.g. sleeve gastrectomy, appendectomy, colorectal resections, rectal suction biopsy).

Overall, the current study demonstrates an underlying quantitative mirroring of seeded aggregation in the gastrointestinal tract and central nervous system for both α-synuclein and tau (modeled in Figure 5). This relationship is present regardless of diagnosis and correlated with central neuropathology suggesting it is not sufficient for disease but can serve as a quantitative biomarker of LBD and AD co-pathology. Early data also show the possible utility of mucosal enteroendocrine cells to provide similar information about central neuropathology. Data above collectively show the potential of gastrointestinal tissues to inform clinical biomarkers and basic disease pathogenesis.
