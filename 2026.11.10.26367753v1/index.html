How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module retrieves information from repositories.

It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Objective: To develop and evaluate AToP (Attribution to Temporal Patterns), a post-hoc explainability framework that converts per-token Integrated Gradients (IG) from a Transformer into structured temporal pattern narratives.

AToP was applied to a single-stream Transformer trained on MIMIC-IV v3.1 (218,196 patients; 12.6% unplanned 30-day readmission). It mines frequent episodes from salient-token subsequences in the training cohort, aggregates attribution among patients whose records contain each token or pattern, and collapses near-duplicates by patient overlap. Validation used masking, reversed-order analysis, token-level faithfulness tests, and KernelSHAP comparison.

Results: The Transformer achieved AUROC 0.77 and PR-AUC 0.34. Population-weighted attribution emphasized high-prevalence codes with small per-patient impact. Carrier-conditional attribution, the mean among patients with the token, surfaced rare high-attribution psychiatric markers including psychosis, self-harm overdose, suicidal ideation (SI), and severe major depressive disorder (MDD). Pattern-level attribution identified within-visit co-occurrences and cross-visit trajectories not captured by token rankings. Risk-increasing patterns included psychiatric sequences ({MDD, SI} → SI) and oncologic progressions (cancer anemia → ascites). Obstetric and chest pain evaluation patterns were associated with lower predicted readmission.

Discussion: The framework provides two complementary views of model predictions: carrier-conditional aggregation highlights rare, highly attributed clinical features, while temporal pattern analysis identifies within-visit combinations and cross-visit trajectories missed by token-level rankings. Masking and visit-order reversal provided evidence of correspondence with model behavior, but pattern attributions do not represent unique effects or establish clinical validity.

Conclusion: AToP turns a Transformer's attributions into visit-anchored temporal pattern narratives, showing which events, in which order, contribute to its readmission predictions.

Transformer-based models have increasingly been used to learn directly from longitudinal electronic health record (EHR) data, from architectures such as BEHRT 1 and Med-BERT 2 to more recent longitudinal EHR foundation models. 3, 4 These approaches have been applied across diverse clinical prediction tasks. 5, 6 However, the same capability to model dependencies across heterogeneous clinical events creates an interpretability gap: how those events combine over time to produce a prediction remains difficult to discern.

As predictive models become more widely used in clinical care, clinicians, regulators, and institutional review processes increasingly emphasize explanations that are locally faithful, showing which inputs drove an individual prediction, and globally coherent, showing recurring patterns in model behavior across patients. Existing post-hoc attribution methods, including SHAP, 7 IG, 8 and DeepLIFT, 9 assign importance scores to individual input features.

Attention-based models such as RETAIN 13 offer a partial alternative by reflecting temporal order through visit-and feature-level importance. However, the faithfulness of attention weights as explanations remains debated. 14, 15 Interaction attribution methods, such as SHAP interaction values 16 and Integrated Hessians, 17 quantify pairwise feature interactions but do not represent ordered sequences of events across visits.

The lack of temporal and combinatorial structure in conventional attribution summaries is especially consequential in longitudinal clinical data, where the same clinical events may carry different meaning depending on whether they occur together within an encounter or sequentially across encounters. To address this limitation, we propose using sequential pattern mining to organize model-attributed clinical events into recurring within-visit combinations and ordered cross-visit sequences. Prior work has applied sequential pattern mining to longitudinal EHR and claims data to study disease progression, adverse drug events, treatment pathways, and diagnostic trajectories. [18] [19] [20] [21] [22] [23] In related work combining temporal pattern mining and explainability, sequential pattern mining has been used to construct inputs for Transformer-based disease trajectory prediction, with SHAP subsequently applied to explain individual predictions. 24 However, that approach uses mined patterns as predictive inputs rather than using model-derived attributions to guide temporal pattern discovery and explain model behavior.

We present AToP (Attribution to Temporal Patterns), a post-hoc explainability framework that combines token-level attribution from a trained Transformer with sequential pattern mining to probe how combinations and sequences of clinical events contribute to model predictions. We evaluate AToP on unplanned 30-day hospital readmission, a prediction task for which conventional risk models have historically shown modest discrimination.

Objective

To develop and evaluate AToP, a post-hoc explainability framework that uses token-level attribution to guide sequential pattern mining and examine how clinical events combine and unfold over time in Transformer-based EHR predictions.

Materials and Methods

Data and prediction task

The study cohort included 218,196 patients from MIMIC-IV v3.1, 26, 27 with an unplanned 30-day hospital readmission rate of 12.6%. The prediction target was unplanned readmission within 30 days of index discharge. The index admission was defined as the patient's penultimate hospitalization. All earlier admissions were used as longitudinal history. For patients with a single admission, that admission served as the index. These 119,874 patients (54.9%) had no subsequent admission recorded in MIMIC-IV and were labeled as not readmitted. Among patients with two or more admissions, the readmission rate was 28.0%. Each patient's longitudinal record through the index admission was represented as a single chronological token sequence. The sequence contained ICD-9 and ICD-10 diagnosis and procedure codes, along with prescription drugs. ICD-9 codes were mapped to ICD-10 using the CMS General Equivalence Mappings, 28 retaining only unambiguous one-to-one mappings. Codes without such a mapping were kept in their original ICD-9 form. Visit boundaries were marked with explicit [VISIT] tokens. Each admission formed one visit block, with blocks ordered chronologically. The sequence encodes the order of admissions, not the elapsed time between them. Within a block, tokens were grouped by type, and the pattern analysis treated events within a visit as unordered co-occurrences.

Chronic conditions were retained only at first appearance or when recorded as the primary diagnosis. Drugs were retained only at first recorded prescription, and ingredients prescribed in more than 25% of admissions were excluded to prevent routine inpatient treatments from dominating the mined patterns. Prescription drugs were mapped to RxNorm ingredient concepts using the RxNav API, by National Drug Code and then by drug name.

Tokens occurring fewer than twice in the training set were excluded, giving a 24,005-token vocabulary of diagnoses, procedures, RxNorm ingredients, and the [VISIT], [CLS], and [PAD] tokens (Supplementary Methods).

Unplanned readmissions included emergency, urgent, direct, and observation admissions within 30 days of index discharge. Elective and surgical same-day admissions were excluded. Admissions ending in in-hospital death (11,801; 2.2%) were removed before index selection and labeling. Among index admissions, 709 (0.32%) were followed only by an admission ending in death. These were labeled as not readmitted. This yielded 27,508 unplanned 30-day readmissions.

Single-stream Transformer

A Transformer encoder 29 with a 24,005-token vocabulary and approximately 3.5 million parameters was trained using class-weighted binary cross-entropy loss. The architecture included two encoder layers, four attention heads, 128-dimensional embeddings, and 512-dimensional feed-forward layers. Early stopping was based on validation AUROC. All hyperparameters are listed in Table S1.

Diagnoses, procedures, medications, and the [VISIT], [CLS], and [PAD] special tokens were represented in a shared embedding space. Token embeddings were summed with learned absolute positional embeddings to encode sequence order. No separate visit-level or elapsed-time embeddings were used. The [CLS] representation was passed through a two-layer feed-forward head (128 to 64 to 1 units) with sigmoid activation to produce the predicted readmission probability.

Patients were split at the patient level into training (70%, n=152,737), validation (10%, n=21,819), and test (20%, n=43,640) cohorts. IG attribution and AToP analyses included 43,602 test patients. The remaining 38 yielded no salient-token set.

Baselines and performance evaluation

We compared the Transformer with two baselines trained on seven structured predictors: the four components of the LACE readmission index 30 (length of stay, emergent admission, Charlson comorbidity index, and emergency department visits in the preceding six months), entered as raw values rather than LACE points, plus age, sex, and number of prior admissions. Logistic regression used standardized predictors with L2 regularization. XGBoost used 200 trees, a maximum depth of 4, a learning rate of 0.05, and positive-class weighting for class imbalance. Both baselines used fixed hyperparameters and were evaluated on the test cohort.

Discrimination was summarized by the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) and the area under the precision-recall curve (PR-AUC). Differences between models were estimated with a paired bootstrap of test-cohort patients (1,000 resamples), reporting percentile 95% confidence intervals and two-sided p values with add-one correction, for which the smallest attainable value is 0.002.

AToP explanation framework

AToP operates on the trained model in five steps (Figure 1): (1) compute token-level attribution using Integrated Gradients; (2) select salient tokens by cumulative share of each patient's absolute attribution; (3) mine frequent episodes from the training cohort's salient tokens: single-token events, within-visit co-occurrences, and cross-visit sequences; (4) aggregate attribution at the token and pattern levels over carriers; and (5) collapse near-redundant patterns by Jaccard similarity of their carrier sets.

We calculated Integrated Gradients on the model's pre-sigmoid logit. By the completeness property, attributions over all input positions, including the [CLS] and [VISIT] tokens, sum approximately to the difference between the patient's logit and that of an all-[PAD] reference sequence. 8, 31 Attribution signs are relative to this reference: positive values indicate a contribution toward a higher logit than the reference sequence, and negative (protective) values toward a lower one.

AToP explanations and pattern analyses used only non-special-token attributions. Details of the reference sequence, special-token attribution, and approximation error are provided in the Supplementary Methods.

For each patient, a token's attribution score was its signed IG value, and a multi-token pattern's attribution score was the sum of the signed IG values of its constituent tokens. Rankings used absolute attribution magnitude, with the sign retained.

At the pattern level, patterns were matched step by step to each carrier's full record, rather than only to the salient-token subsequence used for mining. Each step was assigned to the earliest visit containing all its tokens, with subsequent steps required to occur in strictly later visits. For each carrier, the pattern score was the sum of signed IG values for its tokens in the matched visits. These scores were averaged across carriers to obtain carrier-averaged ΣIG. For the per-patient display, each salient token occurrence was counted only once to avoid double-counting across overlapping patterns (Supplementary Methods).

Frequent-episode mining 18 was implemented using a level-wise candidate-generation algorithm and applied only to salient-token subsequences from the training cohort. Patterns were constructed by either adding a co-occurring token within the same visit or appending a token from a later visit, yielding three pattern types: single-token events, within-visit co-occurrences, and cross-visit sequences. Mining was conducted separately in each outcome class. The resulting patterns were then pooled into a single set of distinct patterns that served as candidates for global rankings and per-patient explanations.

To reduce near-duplicate patterns, we applied greedy clustering based on Jaccard similarity between test-cohort carrier sets. Patterns were processed in descending order of absolute carrier-averaged ΣIG, with the highest-ranked pattern retained as the representative of each group.

Test-cohort carrier counts were used to determine reporting eligibility, and carrier-set overlap was used for deduplication. Neither influenced model training or pattern discovery.

Parameter choices and sensitivity analyses

AToP parameter choices were guided by design and tractability constraints. We assessed sensitivity to several parameters, as described below and in the Supplementary Methods.

For mining, we retained for each patient the smallest set of tokens accounting for 80% of total absolute clinical-token IG, capped at 50 tokens. We assessed sensitivity to the attribution-mass threshold from 60% to 95%. Frequent-episode mining used a minimum support threshold of 0.1% of patients in each outcome class and a maximum of four tokens per pattern. Jaccard-based deduplication used a similarity threshold of 0.20, with sensitivity assessed across alternative thresholds.

After mining, separate eligibility criteria were applied to the all-pattern ranking, cross-visit ranking, and per-patient explanations. Cross-visit rankings were restricted to patterns spanning at least two visits and containing at least two distinct tokens. Minimum carrier counts for each ranking are provided in Table S2. Per-patient explanations used the subset of pooled patterns observed in at least 0.1% of patients in the full training cohort, a stricter floor than the class-specific mining threshold.

Additional implementation details are provided in the Supplementary Methods; parameter settings and their selection rationale are summarized in Table S2.

Validation analyses

We evaluated AToP using pattern masking to assess effects on predicted readmission risk, reversed-order analysis to assess sensitivity to temporal order, token-level faithfulness tests to evaluate the influence of highly attributed tokens, and cross-method comparison with KernelSHAP to assess whether rankings depended on the attribution method.

For pattern masking, we selected the reported patterns and additional high-ranked patterns with positive or negative attribution, based on absolute carrier-averaged ΣIG. For each pattern, matched token positions were replaced with [PAD], and the change in predicted readmission probability was calculated as Δŷ = ŷ_original -ŷ_masked.

We assessed sensitivity to temporal order using two approaches. First, we compared pattern attribution between patients whose salient visit blocks contained a pattern in its original order and those whose blocks contained the reversed sequence. This analysis included the reported cross-visit patterns and additional patterns meeting minimum carrier-count criteria (Supplementary Methods). Second, we reversed the order of matched visits within patient records, recomputed IG, and compared pattern attribution before and after reversal.

We assessed token-level attribution faithfulness by measuring changes in predicted readmission probability after either masking the highest-ranked salient tokens or retaining those tokens while masking the remaining salient tokens. 32 Comprehensiveness was measured as the absolute change in predicted readmission probability after masking the top k salient tokens ranked by absolute IG. For sufficiency, we retained the top k salient tokens, masked all remaining salient tokens, and measured the absolute change in predicted readmission probability. We varied k from 1 to 10, including only patients with at least k salient tokens. In both analyses, masking was restricted to salient clinical tokens, and non-salient and special tokens remained unchanged.

We compared both measures against a random baseline that selected the same number of salient tokens. Confidence intervals were estimated by bootstrapping patients. At k = 5, we compared comprehensiveness between IG-based and random token selection using a one-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test.

To assess whether the rankings depended on the attribution method, we compared Integrated Gradients with KernelSHAP in the analytic test cohort. We compared population-weighted and carrier-conditional token rankings, as well as all-pattern and cross-visit pattern rankings. Pattern-level comparisons used carrier-conditional averaging (Supplementary Methods).

Token-level attribution faithfulness

To assess whether highly attributed tokens had greater influence on model predictions, we compared IG-ranked tokens with randomly selected salient tokens. Masking the five highest-ranked tokens produced a larger mean absolute change in predicted readmission probability than masking five randomly selected salient tokens (0.079 vs 0.056; Wilcoxon signed-rank p < 0.001).

Across selections of 1 to 10 tokens, masking the highest-attributed tokens produced larger prediction changes than masking randomly selected salient tokens, and retaining them better preserved the original predictions, with non-overlapping 95% bootstrap intervals at every k (Figure S7).

Temporal-order analysis

For each cross-visit pattern, we compared attribution between two groups of patients: those whose records contained the pattern in its reported order and those whose records contained the same events in reverse order. Three of the 30 patterns in Table 4 read the same in both directions and were excluded. For 26 of the remaining 27 patterns (96%), attribution magnitude was lower in the reversed-order group, with a mean attenuation of 30% (Table S5). The effect was weaker in a broader set of 1,714 patterns. Attribution magnitude was lower in the reversed-order group for 54% of them, and attribution direction was the same in both groups for 95% (Figure S8).

Because the two groups contained different patients, these differences may also reflect patient characteristics rather than event order alone.

In the within-patient analysis, reversing the order of matched visits reduced attribution magnitude for 21 of the 27 patterns, with a mean attenuation of 14%. Among 722 patients, 59% showed a reduction in mean attribution magnitude, with a significant overall difference (one-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank p < 0.001; Table S6).

Discussion

This study introduces AToP, an explainability framework that combines token attribution with temporal pattern mining to explain Transformer predictions from longitudinal EHR data. AToP extends conventional feature rankings by identifying combinations of clinical events within visits and sequences across visits, and by summarizing their attribution at the patient and cohort levels. In our readmission application, carrier-conditional attribution, defined as the mean attribution among patients whose records contain a given token, highlighted less prevalent clinical features that were obscured in population-weighted rankings. Separating cross-visit patterns from within-visit combinations revealed psychiatric and oncologic trajectories that were less prominent in the all-pattern rankings.

Masking tokens in patterns with positive attribution lowered predicted readmission probabilities, whereas masking those with negative attribution raised them. These findings support correspondence between attribution and model behavior. Temporal-order analyses provided more qualified evidence: attribution magnitudes decreased under reversal for most reported patterns, but only about half of the broader pattern pool. IG and KernelSHAP showed substantial agreement on token rankings but limited agreement on cross-visit pattern rankings, consistent with documented disagreement between explanation methods. 33 Previous approaches have represented temporal information in different ways. Attention-based models such as RETAIN assign importance weights to visits and clinical features, although the faithfulness of attention as an explanation remains debated. [13] [14] [15] Other work has mined sequential patterns before applying SHAP to explain Transformer predictions. 24 AToP reverses this process by using attribution to guide pattern discovery and then scoring the identified patterns among patients whose records contain them.

Many highly attributed patterns included conditions already known to be associated with readmission. Patterns that increased predicted risk were mainly psychiatric sequences involving major depressive disorder and suicidal ideation, and oncologic sequences involving cancer anemia, bone metastases, and ascites. Both have established links to readmission: depressive symptoms are associated with higher readmission rates after medical hospitalization, 34 and patients with advanced cancer are frequently rehospitalized. 35 Patterns that decreased predicted risk were mainly obstetric patterns and chest pain evaluations. For obstetric patterns, this is consistent with national data showing lower readmission rates after childbirth than after admissions for mental health conditions or cancer. 36 AToP thus recovered recognizable clinical risk profiles rather than new patient groups. Its added value lies in showing how combinations and sequences of these conditions contribute to the model's predictions.

Unspecified diagnosis codes appeared frequently among highly attributed tokens and patterns, including psychosis NOS, mood disorder NOS, and recurrent sequences involving depression and suicidal ideation. This may reflect diagnostic uncertainty, documentation practices, or coding conventions. Other patterns combined symptoms with diagnostic procedures, such as chest pain with chest radiography and respiratory measurements. AToP therefore surfaces care processes as well as clinical conditions, and distinguishing the two requires pattern-specific studies to replicate the findings and assess their plausibility.

Limitations

Our evaluation assessed whether AToP explanations reflected model behavior, not whether they improved clinical decisions. 37 Pattern attribution sums constituent token attributions and does not isolate the independent effects of their combination or temporal order. The explanations also exclude attribution to [CLS] and [VISIT] tokens and do not separately quantify the contribution of admission count.

Masking may create inputs that differ from those encountered during training. 38 Because the masked patterns were selected using IG, this analysis does not establish whether they are the most influential patterns among all candidates. Between-patient reversal may reflect differences in patient characteristics, while within-patient reversal changes entire visits, including their other events and recency.

IG and KernelSHAP differed in reference distribution, output scale, sparsity, and pattern scoring. KernelSHAP also reused representatives selected through IG-based Jaccard deduplication, so the limited cross-visit agreement cannot be attributed solely to the attribution methods. The 80% saliency threshold was heuristic. Risk-increasing pattern rankings were relatively stable across evaluated thresholds with the mined dictionary fixed, but protective rankings were less stable (Figure S12). Patterns with fewer than 20 carriers should be considered exploratory.

Evaluation was limited to one dataset, task, and model architecture. Applying AToP to larger pretrained models, including EHR foundation models, is a natural next step. Single-admission patients were labeled as not readmitted by construction, readmissions outside MIMIC-IV may have been missed, and death was not modeled as a competing outcome. The performance difference from the baselines reflects both feature availability and model architecture.

Conclusion

This work introduced AToP, an explainability framework that organizes token attributions from a Transformer into temporal patterns in longitudinal EHR data. By identifying combinations of clinical events within visits and sequences across visits, AToP provides explanations of model predictions that go beyond conventional rankings of individual features. KernelSHAP treated each patient's non-special tokens as features and replaced masked tokens with [PAD]. The background consisted of ten randomly generated masks per patient, retaining each token with probability 0.5. Approximately 50 coalition samples were used per patient across the analytic test cohort (n = 43,602), and 200 were used for the per-patient comparison in Figure S9.

Integrated Gradients reference baseline

We computed Integrated Gradients (IG) on the model's pre-sigmoid logit using an all-[PAD] embedding reference and 30 integration steps. The reference retained each patient's padding mask and position embeddings, so its output varied across patients. Completeness was assessed by comparing the sum of attributions with the difference between the patient's logit and the reference logit. The completeness residual had a median absolute value of 0.012 logits (90th percentile 0.032), or 1.8% of the logit difference. Across 300 sampled test patients, the [CLS] and [VISIT] positions carried a median 46% of total absolute attribution.

Patient-level explanations and pattern analyses used attribution to clinical tokens only, excluding [CLS] and [VISIT] positions. The full attribution accounting, including the reference logit and special-token contributions, is provided for the examples in Figures 2 and S2A-C.

Pattern scoring and position allocation

For global rankings, patterns were matched to patients' complete records in chronological order. Each pattern step was assigned to the earliest eligible visit after the preceding step, with all tokens in a step required to occur in the same visit. For each carrier, we summed the signed IG values of the matched tokens, using their first occurrence within each assigned visit. Scores were averaged across carriers to obtain carrier-averaged ΣIG. Patterns were scored independently, including when they shared token positions.

For patient-level explanations (Figure 2), patterns were matched to salient visit blocks. Matches were prioritized by earliest matched visit, then decreasing number of visit blocks, and finally decreasing |ΣIG|. Each salient token position was assigned to at most one pattern, with remaining attribution displayed separately.
