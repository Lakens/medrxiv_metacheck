Reporting p values imprecisely (e.g., p < .05) reduces transparency, reproducibility, and re-use (e.g., in p value meta-analyses). Best practice is to report exact p-values with three decimal places (e.g., p = .032) unless p values are smaller than 0.001, in which case you can use p < .001.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (e.g., “decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

P-Value Text
P<0.05 Findings were considered replicated when associations in the discovery and replication cohorts were in the same direction and were statistically significant using two-sided tests (P<0.05).
P>0.05 Four of eight species were associated with IHD in the same direction as observed in the discovery cohorts; however, none were statistically significantly associated with incident IHD in the meta-analyzed estimates from the replication cohorts (P>0.05).

The APA manual states: Report exact p values (e.g., p = .031) to two or three decimal places. However, report p values less than .001 as p < .001. However, 2 decimals is too imprecise for many use-cases (e.g., a p value meta-analysis), so report p values with three digits.

To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 1 non-significant p value that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Metacheck does not yet analyze automatically whether sentences which include non-significant p-values are correct, but we recommend manually checking the sentences below for possible misinterpreted non-significant p values.

Text Sentence
P>0.05 Four of eight species were associated with IHD in the same direction as observed in the discovery cohorts; however, none were statistically significantly associated with incident IHD in the meta-analyzed estimates from the replication cohorts (P>0.05).

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download files from additional data repositories reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

ABSTRACT

Background: Variation in the gut microbiome composition has been associated with atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) in case-control designs. We aimed to investigate associations with incident ASCVD events applying a prospective design to reduce the impact of reverse causality.

Methods:

We assessed associations between gut microbiome features measured by shotgun metagenomic sequencing and incident total ASCVD, ischemic heart disease (IHD) and ischemic stroke, including fatal and non-fatal events as defined by registry follow-up in 17,216 participants from three Swedish population-based cohorts (SCAPIS, SIMPLER, and MOS). Results from Cox proportional hazards regression models adjusted for demographic, and lifestyle factors, medication, and prevalent diabetes were combined across cohorts using a fixed-effect meta-analysis accounting for multiple testing. Associations were tested for replication in HUNT and FINRISK.

Results: During a median of 6-8 y follow-up, 769 incident ASCVD events occurred. Species diversity was inversely associated with ASCVD and IHD, with a hazard ratio for IHD of 0.86 per SD increase in species richness (95% CI: 0.79-0.95). Sixty-eight common species (i.e., present in ≥30% individuals) were associated with incident IHD after adjusting for sex and birth country, but only eight remained in fully adjusted models, including Ruminococcus_B gnavus. Among others, inverse associations were observed for SFEL01 sp004557245 and Fimadaptatus sp900553645 from Christensenellales, an order in which butyrate production has been reported. Streptococcus mutans and Limosilactobacillus fermentum, oral-associated species previously associated with coronary atherosclerosis, were associated with a higher risk of incident IHD, which remained after adjustment for dental health. Replication analysis in the HUNT study supported the finding for S. mutans. No associations were observed with ischemic stroke.

Conclusions: Gut species diversity and specific species abundance were associated with incident IHD, providing evidence supporting a protective role of two Christensenellales species and an adverse role of oral species and R. gnavus in the development of IHD.

INTRODUCTION

The gut microbiome, a collection of microbes living in the gastrointestinal tract that plays important roles in human physiological processes, has been implicated in the pathogenesis of atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD). [1] [2] [3] [4] [5] Proposed mechanisms for the role of the gut microbiome in ASCVD pathophysiology include the production of microbial-derived metabolites, such as short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) and secondary bile acids, which are involved in lipid metabolism and immune signaling. 1, 2 Detection of bacterial DNA and biofilms of the oral pathogen Streptococcus in coronary plaques suggests an additional mechanism specific to oral bacteria. It has been postulated that oral bacteria and/or their components can translocate into the bloodstream as well as through the gastrointestinal tract, inducing local and systemic inflammatory responses, and modulating atherosclerosis. [3] [4] [5] However, available human studies to date offer limited evidence of causality. Most published studies employed a case-control design and lacked control for confounding factors such as lifestyle and medications, 6, 7 known to affect the gut microbiome and modify ASCVD risk. [8] [9] [10] [11] Such designs cannot rule out the disease or other unaccounted factors causing the observed changes in the gut microbiome. Using data from 8,973 individuals without a history of CVD, we have previously reported gut enrichment of Streptococcus spp. associated with worse subclinical atherosclerosis in a cross-sectional analysis, 12 providing support for the link between oral taxa and CVD risk. Longitudinal designs can help establish temporality; however, few studies have published follow-up data on ASCVD events, with largely null associations. [13] [14] [15] These studies, which were mostly based on low-resolution 16S sequencing data that cannot reliably resolve bacterial species, included at most 254 events and thus were likely underpowered.

Microbial species associated with the development of ASCVD, therefore, remain incompletely characterized. We therefore set out to investigate the association between gut microbiome, measured by deep metagenomic sequencing, and the development of ASCVD in three large longitudinal Swedish cohorts including a total of 17,216 individuals free from ASCVD at baseline.

METHODS

Ethical considerations

The present study included data from three longitudinal Swedish cohorts (Fig. 1; discovery cohorts). The Swedish Ethical Review Authority has approved the conduct of the present study (DNR 2022-06137-01 with amendments 2023-04785-02, 2024-05416-02, and 2025-03082-02) and all included participants have provided informed consent. Approval was also obtained from the Regional Committee for Medical and Health Research Ethics Midt-Norge for the HUNT study (REK-656785) and the Coordinating Ethical Committee of the Helsinki and Uusimaa University Hospital District for the FINRISK cohort (558/E3/2001). FINRISK data were obtained with permission from THL Biobank and Findata. All studies were conducted according to principles laid out in the Declaration of Helsinki.

Discovery cohorts

SCAPIS

The Swedish CArdioPulmonary BioImage Study (SCAPIS) aimed to improve understanding and prediction of cardiopulmonary diseases, and its design has been previously described. 16 The participants were invited based on a random selection from the population register in six areas adjacent to six university hospitals across Sweden 2014-2018 (50% participation rate).

A subset of 9,829 participants from Uppsala and Malmö sites collected fecal samples at home, stored at -20°C until the visit to the study site, then stored at -80°C until further processing. 17

SIMPLER

The Swedish Infrastructure for Medical Population-Based Life-Course and Environmental Research (SIMPLER) cohorts comprise the Cohort of Swedish Men (COSM) and the Swedish Mammography Cohort (SMC). 18 The two large ongoing population-based cohorts enrolled almost 110,000 individuals residing in Uppsala, Västmanland, and Örebro counties in 1997 (COSM) and 1987-1990 (SMC), respectively. During 2010-2011, a subsample of the participants from Uppsala and Västmanland was invited to a health examination and was asked to collect fecal samples at home and store the samples in home freezers (-20°C) and bring them to the study visit, except for a few participants in Uppsala who received home visits (n=196). The fecal samples were then stored at -80°C until further processing.

MOS

The Malmö Offspring Study (MOS) was established to study the influence of family-related factors on the development of chronic diseases. 19 Adult children and grandchildren (≥18 y) of the participants of the cardiovascular arm of the Malmö Diet and Cancer study were invited to participate in the MOS. Consequently, MOS includes related participants. Between 2013 and 2017, 2,644 participants were recruited. The participants were asked to collect fecal samples after the first visit and store them at home (-20°C) until the second visit to the study site, after which the samples were stored at -80°C. The present study included participants who were not part of the SCAPIS cohort.

Population for analysis -discovery cohorts

For the present analyses, only participants with fecal metagenomic data passing quality control and who consented to registry linkage were eligible (SCAPIS: 9,791; SIMPLER: 6,150; MOS: 2,215). Because we aimed to investigate new events of ASCVD, data from participants with a history of ischemic heart disease (IHD) or ischemic stroke at fecal sampling were excluded. This resulted in a total of 17,216 individuals free from ASCVD for analysis (SCAPIS: 9,546; SIMPLER: 5,485; MOS; 2,185 (Supplementary Fig. 1)).

Metagenomic DNA extraction and shotgun metagenomic sequencing

DNA extraction from fecal samples and subsequent metagenomic sequencing in SCAPIS, SIMPLER, and MOS cohorts have been previously described in detail. 12, 17 For SCAPIS and MOS samples, DNA extraction, quality control, and sequencing were performed by Cmbio (Copenhagen, Denmark). In short, at least one negative control and positive control Stored fecal samples from SIMPLER participants were thawed and a pea-sized aliquot was prepared to which DNA/RNA Shield (Zymo Research) was added, and the samples were then refrozen in the biobank before delivery, with each batch containing one negative and one positive control. DNA extraction from samples was performed using MagPure Stool kit (Magen Biotechnology), and the DNA was subsequently fragmented and used to generate the library using the MGIEasy FS DNA Library Prep Set kit. The sequencing was performed using 2x150bp paired-end reads on the DNBSEQ G400 or T7 sequencing instrument (MGI), resulting in an average yield of 50.8 million read pairs/sample.

Taxonomic profiling

Identified host read pairs based on the mapping to the human reference genome were removed using Bowtie2 v.2.4.2 (SCAPIS and MOS) or Kraken 2 (SIMPLER). Subsequent bioinformatic processing, including calculation of relative abundances and annotation to taxonomic ranks, was performed by Cmbio (Copenhagen, Denmark) using the CHAMP profiler, 20 based on the Human Microbiome Reference HMR05 catalog 20 and the Genome Taxonomy Database (GTDB) release 214 (date: 28 April 2023). 21, 22 In non-rarefied data, 4,741 species were detected in SCAPIS, 3,927 in MOS and 4,676 in SIMPLER. Species richness, Shannon index, and inverse Simpson index were calculated as measures of alpha diversity on rarefied data, with the latter two indices calculated using the vegan R package (R v4.3.1).

The main exposures in the present study were alpha diversity, and relative abundances of metagenomic species present in at least 30% of individuals in each discovery cohort, resulting in 427 species, hereafter referred to as "common gut species." Additionally, we examined an additional 114 rare species (<30% prevalence) hereafter referred to as "rare candidate species," that were associated with coronary artery calcium score (CACS) in SCAPIS (Supplementary Methods) using the same model specification as in the previous SCAPIS publication. 12

Ascertainment of ASCVD events

The three primary outcomes were 1) incident total ASCVD, which included IHD and ischemic stroke events, and separately, 2) incident IHD, or 3) incident ischemic stroke.

Information about ASCVD events was obtained through linkage to the Swedish National Patient Register and the Cause of Death Register. Individuals with prevalent ASCVD at baseline were identified and excluded from all association analyses. IHD included non-fatal and fatal IHD. Non-fatal IHD included ICD-10 codes I21-I22 or ICD-9 410 as main diagnoses, or coronary procedures such as percutaneous coronary intervention and coronary artery bypass graft surgery (Nordic surgery codes FNG02, FNG05, FNC, FND, and FNE).

Fatal IHD included deaths coded as I20-I25 as the underlying cause of death. Ischemic stroke was defined as events with ICD-10 I63 or ICD-9 433-434 as main diagnosis or underlying cause of death. For analysis with total ASCVD as the outcome, only the first occurrence of either IHD or ischemic stroke was considered. Follow-up time was calculated for each individual from the date of fecal sampling (for SIMPLER) or screening date (for SCAPIS and MOS) until the date of incident event, death, emigration date, or the end of follow-up, whichever came first. The end of follow-up was 31 December 2022 for SIMPLER, 31 December 2023 for MOS, and 30 September 2024 for SCAPIS.

Covariates

Covariates are harmonized across discovery cohorts when possible; expanded information is provided in the Supplementary Methods. Information on age and sex was derived from the Swedish Population Register. Education and physical activity level were self-reported by using questionnaires. Education level was categorized into ≤9 years, 10-12 years, and >12 years. Physical activity was limited to leisure time, with cohort-specific categories. Birth country, based on questionnaire responses or linkage to the Swedish Tax Register, was categorized in SIMPLER and SCAPIS into Sweden, Europe other than Sweden, and others, and in MOS as Sweden or others. Smoking status was categorized into current, former, or never smokers. Data on usual dietary intakes were collected at the same time as fecal sampling, using validated food frequency questionnaires in SCAPIS 23 and SIMPLER, 24 or a 4-d web-based food record in MOS, 25 and nutrient intakes were obtained by linkage to the Swedish food composition database. Information on medication use across all cohorts was obtained primarily through linkage to the National Prescribed Drug Register and was additionally confirmed by questionnaire or detected drug metabolites in blood when available.

Cardiovascular risk factors

During visits to study centers, participants underwent anthropometric measurements including height (in cm) and weight (in kg), from which body mass index (BMI, kg/m 2 ) was calculated, and waist circumference (in cm). Systolic and diastolic blood pressure were measured twice, and the average of the two measurements was used. Venous blood samples were collected (non-fasted samples <1% in SCAPIS). Fresh plasma samples were used to measure glucose (mmol/L) with the enzymatic method with hexokinase, lipids (total cholesterol and HDL cholesterol (HDL-C), mmol/L), high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hsCRP, mg/L) using the immunoturbidimetric method, and creatinine (µmol/L) with the enzymatic colorimetric method. LDL cholesterol (LDL-C, mmol/L) was directly measured or calculated using Friedewald's formula. EDTA-plasma samples were used to measure apolipoprotein B (ApoB), lipoprotein(a) (Lp(a)), cystatin C (mg/L), and liver enzymes aspartate aminotransferase (ASAT, µkat/L), alanine aminotransferase (ALAT, µkat/L), gamma-glutamyltransferase (GGT, µkat/L). Estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) was calculated using the CKD-EPI 2021 formula using both creatinine and cystatin C. 26

Plasma metabolites

The procedure has been described in detail previously in Dekkers et al. 27 In short, the measurement and identification of plasma metabolomes in SCAPIS was performed by Metabolon (Durham, NC, USA). The EDTA plasma samples were analyzed in three different batches: Malmö (n=3,720), Uppsala-1 (n=2,825), Uppsala-2, (n=1,855). Untargeted ultrahigh-performance liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (UPLC-MS/MS) across four different platforms was used to measure the metabolites, which were subsequently identified by comparison with a reference standard library. When the measured value was below the detection threshold, the next higher value was used to impute the measured value.

Dental health measures

Data on dental procedures, diagnoses, and the number of teeth were obtained for 96% SCAPIS participants through linkage to data from the Swedish National Dental Health Register (during 2010-2020) and were processed to derive measures of periodontitis status, caries status, and the number of missing teeth. The derivation and validation of these measures have been described in detail previously. 28, 29 Severe periodontitis status (present vs. absent) was defined by using records indicating a history of tooth extraction or surgical treatment (procedural codes starting with "4") due to periodontitis (condition "periodontitis") and two extensive sessions of nonsurgical periodontal treatment (procedural codes 342/343, along with the condition "periodontitis"). Caries status was defined as no entries (no caries) or any entries (caries) due to primary or secondary caries (any procedure paired with condition code 4001, 4002, 4011, 4012, or 4022). For descriptive purposes, the number of missing teeth was also categorized as ≥5 or <5.

Replication cohorts

HUNT

The Trøndelag Health Study (the HUNT study) is an ongoing population-based cohort comprising adults ≥20 y residing in Trøndelag county, Norway, and has recruited >200,000 participants since its start in 1984. Between 2017 and 2019, the participants were invited to the HUNT4 survey, and 56,042 individuals were included. 30 Of these participants, 13,268 participants provided fecal samples for gut microbiome measurement. For the replication analysis, microbiome data were available from 12,887 participants. After excluding 2,736 participants with missing covariate data and 373 prevalent IHD cases, data from 9,778 participants were analyzed. Median follow-up was 5.4 y. Methods for gut microbiome measurement, outcome and covariate definition are further described in the Supplementary Methods.

FINRISK

The FINRISK cohort is a national cohort initiated in 1972 whose data collection was conducted every 5 years by the Finnish Institute for Health and Welfare up to 2012. 31 The data used in this study were from the FINRISK 2002 cohort, which included 8,738 participants aged 25-74. At the 2002 baseline, the participants completed questionnaires on sociodemographic and lifestyle factors, underwent health examinations, and donated fecal samples. For the replication analysis, participants without fecal samples, with missing covariates or with prevalent IHD were excluded (total n excluded=1,722), resulting in data from 5,321 individuals, with a median follow-up of 17.8 y. Methods for gut microbiome measurement and definition for the outcome and covariates are further described in the Supplementary Methods.

Statistical analysis

Association of gut microbial features and incident ASCVD

A base-2 logarithmic function was used to transform non-rarefied species relative abundance data after replacing zero values with half of the species-specific minimum value. Cox proportional hazards models with age as the underlying timescale were used to estimate hazard ratios and 95% confidence intervals (HR [95% CI]) for associations between gut microbial features and incident total ASCVD, IHD, and ischemic stroke, implemented using the survival R package (v.3.5-5).

Two analytical models were used. The minimally adjusted model included sex, birth country, and a technical covariate (metagenomic extraction plate in SCAPIS and MOS, aliquoting plate in SIMPLER). The fully adjusted model additionally included education level, smoking status, leisure time physical activity, energy-adjusted intakes of carbohydrate, polyunsaturated fatty acid, saturated fatty acid, and fiber, total energy intake, alcohol intake (not energy-adjusted), cardiovascular medication use, proton-pump inhibitor use, and prevalent diabetes. To account for the relatedness among MOS participants, we added a frailty term to the Cox models for MOS data. All covariates were specified a priori, and a directed acyclic graph was used to inform the adjustment sets (Supplementary Fig. 2). To handle missing data in covariates, multiple imputation with ten imputed datasets was performed in each discovery cohort, and estimates from the imputed datasets were pooled using Rubin's rules; all implemented using the mice R package (v3.16.0). Details for missing data handling were further expanded in Supplementary Methods. All analyses were performed using RStudio v2023.12.1.402 (R v4.3.1), unless specified otherwise.

Meta-analysis of discovery cohorts

Cohort-specific estimates were meta-analyzed using a fixed-effects model with inversevariance weighting to estimate outcome-specific hazard ratios and 95% CIs for each microbiome feature (alpha diversity and metagenomic species). Microbiome features were considered to be associated with the outcome in the meta-analysis when passing multiple testing correction using the Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate set at 5% (q<0.05).

For the outcome total ASCVD and IHD, cohort estimates from 80 rare candidate species with prevalence >1% in SIMPLER and SCAPIS, and >10% in MOS (for total ASCVD only) were meta-analyzed due to unstable estimates for species below these prevalence cut-offs. For the outcome ischemic stroke which had much fewer events, we used the cut-off prevalence of 1.5%, and estimates from 75 species were meta-analyzed. Meta-analyses were performed using the metafor R package (v4.8.0) in RStudio v2025.05.0+496 with R v4.5.2.

Sensitivity analysis

Sensitivity analyses were performed in each cohort, and results from each cohort were subsequently meta-analyzed. We 1) further adjusted the results from the primary analyses (i.e., the fully adjusted model) for BMI, 2) excluded individuals receiving antibiotic treatment six months prior to fecal sampling, 3) excluded those with prevalent inflammatory bowel diseases, and 4) excluded the first two years of follow-up by starting the follow-up time two years after the fecal sampling to assess potential reverse causality. For analysis of microbial species, we also 5) further adjusted the results for richness, 6) performed censoring after three years of follow-up, and 7) applied different types of transformations to relative abundance data (details in Supplementary Methods). In SIMPLER, collection of fecal samples occurred over a longer time span relative to SCAPIS or MOS; we therefore also performed an additional sensitivity analysis to examine this effect by 8) including age-at-entry as a stratifying variable when fitting Cox regression on SIMPLER data.

Correlation of microbiome features with risk factors

We performed partial Spearman correlations for microbial features identified in the primary analyses with age, sex, birth country, adiposity (BMI, waist circumference), and clinical ASCVD risk factors when available in the cohort, including plasma glucose, lipids, hsCRP, and measures of kidney and liver function. For age, sex, and birth country, correlations were adjusted for technical variation (plate). For all other risk factors, Spearman's partial correlations were adjusted for age, sex, and technical variation. Multiple testing adjustment was performed using a 5% false discovery rate per microbiome feature identified in the primary analyses.

Correlation of microbiome features with plasma metabolites

We performed partial Spearman correlations between microbial features identified in the primary analyses and 1,326 plasma metabolites in the SCAPIS cohort (max n=8,400), adjusting for metabolomics delivery batch, metagenomic extraction plate, age, sex, and birth country. We analyzed all metabolites detected in at least 100 samples. For metabolites that were detected in <2% of the individuals or classified as drug metabolites (n=105), these were analyzed as binary variables (detected/not detected), with the exception of 2,6dihydroxybenzoic acid and salicylate (detected in >99% of the individuals). Multiple testing adjustment was performed using the 5% false discovery rate per microbiome feature. To investigate potential shared metabolic pathways among microbial features identified in the primary analyses, we further selected metabolites with moderate correlations (absolute correlation≥0.3) with richness or the IHD-associated species. Metabolites with the 20 strongest correlations were selected instead when the strongest correlation with a species was <0.3.

Post-hoc analysis for associations with dental health

We additionally adjusted the Cox models from the primary analysis for measures of dental health, one at a time.

Replication in HUNT and FINRISK

Microbiome features passing the multiple testing correction threshold in the fully adjusted model (q<0.05) were analyzed in the replication cohorts (HUNT and FINRISK) using the same model specification as in the discovery cohorts. Details on replication cohorts are provided in the Supplementary Methods. Findings were considered replicated when associations in the discovery and replication cohorts were in the same direction and were statistically significant using two-sided tests (P<0.05).

RESULTS

We analyzed fecal metagenomic data from 17,216 individuals without a history of ASCVD across three large longitudinal Swedish cohorts (Figure 1, Supplementary Table 1). The SIMPLER cohort comprised older individuals than those in SCAPIS and MOS; it also had fewer current smokers, and a higher proportion of individuals using cardiovascular medication and proton-pump inhibitors at the time of fecal sampling. During up to 10 y follow-up, we identified 769 ASCVD events, largely due to IHD (514 events; Supplementary Table 2). For IHD and ischemic stroke, estimates were available only from the two larger cohorts, as only 22 IHD and 9 ischemic stroke events occurred in MOS.

Individuals who developed IHD events during the follow-up were more often males, and at baseline, less likely to be never smokers or be physically active, had higher saturated fatty acid and lower fiber intakes, as compared to those who remained free from IHD. Those who later experienced IHD events were also more likely to have diabetes and had a worse cardiovascular risk profile at baseline, despite also being more likely to be using cardiovascular medications (Supplementary Table 3). Individuals with higher microbial alpha diversity, measured by species richness at baseline, were more likely to be female, highly educated, more physically active, never smokers, and to have a healthier diet (i.e., higher fiber and lower saturated fatty acid intakes), but less likely to have diabetes or used antibiotics recently (Supplementary Table 4).

Associations of alpha diversity with ASCVD

Higher microbial diversity was associated with a reduced hazard of incident ASCVD events, with estimates attenuated slightly after accounting for lifestyle, diet, medication use and presence of diabetes in the fully adjusted model (e.g., for Shannon, HRfull=0.92 [0.85-0.99]) (Fig. 2, Supplementary Table 5) and with incident IHD (e.g. a 13% reduction of IHD hazard per 100-species increase in richness (HRfull=0.87 [0.79-0.95]). Results were similar when excluding those with recent antibiotic use, or prevalent inflammatory bowel disease, or when excluding the first two years of follow-up (Supplementary Fig. 3), or when adjusting for BMI (Supplementary Fig. 3, Supplementary Table 13). No associations were observed for ischemic stroke (Fig. 2).

Associations of common species with ASCVD

We found 74 and 68 common species associated with risk of ASCVD and IHD, respectively, in the minimally adjusted model (Supplementary Tables 6 and 7, respectively), while none of the species were associated with ischemic stroke after multiple testing correction (Supplementary Table 8). After adjustment for lifestyle, diet, medication use and prevalent diabetes, only eight common species remained significantly associated with IHD (q<0.05, Fig. 3, Supplementary Table 7). Direction of associations was consistent across cohorts, with low to moderate heterogeneity (0-65%) in the meta-analyzed estimates. Higher abundances of S. mutans, R. gnavus, and Anaerotruncus colihominis (order Oscillospirales) were associated with increased hazard of incident IHD events (Figure 3, Supplementary Table 7). Each doubling of SFEL01 sp004557245 abundance was associated with a 3% lower hazard of IHD events (HRfull=0.97 [0.95-0.98]). Other species associated with lower IHD hazard included Blautia_A wexlerae_B and three uncultured species from the order Oscillospirales (Angelakisella sp900547385), Christensenellales (Fimadaptatus sp900553645), and Lachnospirales (Choladousia sp902363135). Heterogeneity in the estimates was greatly reduced in the fully adjusted model versus the minimal model, except for Blautia_A wexlerae_B.

Associations of rare candidate species with ASCVD

We additionally examined candidate species previously associated with CACS in SCAPIS, based on reanalysis of metagenomics data processed using the same pipeline as in the present study (Supplementary Table 9). A total of 155 candidate species with prevalence ranging from 0.01% to 92.1% were associated with CACS (q<0.05). For associations with incident events, we meta-analyzed cohort estimates from 80 rare candidate species with prevalence >1% to <30% in SIMPLER and SCAPIS, and >10% to <30% in MOS in relation to total ASCVD and IHD, and from 75 species in relation to ischemic stroke.

Of the 80 rare candidate species, 18 and 19 species, including Limosilactobacillus fermentum, L. oris, Ligilactobacillus salivarius, and Thomasclavelia ramosa, were associated with a higher incidence of both total ASCVD and IHD in the minimal model (q<0.05) (Supplementary Tables 10 and 11, respectively). However, only one species, L. fermentum, remained associated with IHD in the fully adjusted model after multiple testing correction (HR=1.07 [1.03-1.11], q=6.1x10 -3 ) (Supplementary Table 11). Significant heterogeneity was observed in the meta-analyzed estimates in the fully adjusted model (I² = 83%), with stronger associations observed in SCAPIS than in SIMPLER. No associations were observed in relation to ischemic stroke (Supplementary Table 12).

Post-hoc associations of S. mutans and L. fermentum accounting for dental health Two species identified in the primary analyses have been previously associated with dental caries. 32, 33 A higher proportion of individuals with severe periodontitis or ≥5 missing teeth was observed among individuals experiencing IHD events compared to those who remained IHD free, but the proportion for any caries was similar (Supplementary Table 3). In a sensitivity analysis, we adjusted the estimates from the fully adjusted model with either severe periodontitis status, caries status, or the number of missing teeth, and observed similar estimates to the primary analysis (i.e., fully adjusted model) in SCAPIS (Fig. 4).

Sensitivity analysis of microbial species associations

Similar estimates for IHD associations were observed for all species in the BMI-adjusted model, and when excluding individuals with recent antibiotic treatment or those with inflammatory bowel disease (Supplementary Fig. 4, Supplementary Table 13). Including ageat-entry as a stratifying variable in SIMPLER analysis yielded similar associations (Supplementary Fig. 5). Excluding the first two years of follow-up yielded similar estimates, except for S. mutans, A. colihominis, and L. fermentum, which were somewhat attenuated (Supplementary Fig. 4). Further analyses restricting to the first three years of follow-up showed stronger associations for these same species. Supplementary Fig. 6 showed that results for IHD associations from the primary analyses were strongly correlated to results from other types of transformations applied to the relative abundance data (Spearman's r≥0.96), with slightly lower correlation with results from rankinverse-normal transformation (for common species, rs=0.87) or binary presence/absence analysis (for rare candidate species, rs=0.77).

Species richness as well as other alpha diversity measures were positively correlated with protective IHD species, and negatively with adverse species (Supplementary Table 14).

Therefore, to examine whether richness is driving the species-IHD associations, we further adjusted the species associations for richness. Results showed that the estimate was virtually unchanged for SFEL01 sp004557245 after adjustment, while attenuated associations were observed for other protective species such as Fimadaptatus sp900553645, as well as the adversely associated A. colihominis (Supplementary Fig. 4). Abundances of S. mutans and L. fermentum were only weakly correlated (absolute rs≤0.11) with the abundance of protective species or other adverse IHD species or any measures of alpha diversity (Supplementary Table 14).

Replication in HUNT and FINRISK cohorts

Prevalence of the IHD-associated species was relatively similar in discovery cohorts compared to the replication cohorts, except for S. mutans, which was very low (2%) in FINRISK but was 33-43% in other cohorts (Supplementary Table 15), which might indicate a limitation of the measurement in FINRISK, which used shallow-sequencing. A simulation of the effect of low sequencing depth used in FINRISK did, however, not fully explain the low prevalence (Supplementary Methods). Supplementary Table 16 showed results from replication using HUNT (161 events) and FINRISK cohorts (403 events). Analysis in HUNT showed that higher abundance of S. mutans was also associated with higher incidence of IHD events (HR 1.06 [1.02-1.11], P=0.010). Four of eight species were associated with IHD in the same direction as observed in the discovery cohorts; however, none were statistically significantly associated with incident IHD in the meta-analyzed estimates from the replication cohorts (P>0.05).

Cardiovascular risk factors correlated with richness and IHD-associated species

To investigate the potential causal pathways linked to richness and the IHD-associated species, we examined whether richness and IHD-associated species are correlated with established cardiovascular risk factors in SCAPIS and SIMPLER (Fig. 5). Species richness and most IHD-associated species correlated most strongly with adiposity, hsCRP, GGT, and most lipids, in particular with triglycerides and HDL-C, but not with LDL-C, with comparatively stronger correlations for richness than for individual species. The directions of effect were as expected, given the respective associations with IHD. The strongest correlations (absolute Spearman's r) were 0.22 for richness with triglycerides and adiposity measures in SCAPIS (Fig. 5a; Supplementary Table 17). Of note, Blautia_A wexlerae_B and Choladousia sp902363135, both from family Lachnospiraceae, were only weakly correlated with triglycerides, HDL-C, GGT and hsCRP (absolute rs<0.10) (Fig. 5, Supplementary Table 17). Correlations with similar direction albeit of lower magnitude were observed for most risk factors in SIMPLER (absolute rs ≤0.14) (Fig. 5b).

Metabolites correlated with richness and IHD-associated species

Gut microbial metabolites have been hypothesized to link gut microbiome and ASCVD; 2 therefore we further examined the correlations of species richness and the IHD-associated species with mass-spectrometry based plasma metabolites in the present study (Supplementary Table 18), with 83 strongest associations ordered by their strength of correlation with species richness summarized in Supplementary Fig. 7. The strongest positive correlation was observed for species richness with 5alpha-androstan-3beta,17alpha-diol disulfate, an androgenic steroid (rs=0.54), which also correlated with the species in a varying magnitude (absolute rs=0.08 to 0.45) (Supplementary Table 18). Many metabolites were similarly correlated with richness and IHD-associated species, such as 3-phenylpropionate (hydrocinnamate) and cinnamoylglycine, as well as a few uncharacterized metabolites, were among those with the strongest positive correlations with richness (Supplementary Fig. 7, Supplementary Table 18). Several metabolites previously associated with prevalent cardiometabolic disease were correlated with richness and IHD species. Imidazole propionate correlated negatively with richness (rs=-0.24) and protective IHD species (r= -0.06 to -0.18), and positively with adverse IHD species (rs=0.09-0.22). Low to moderate correlations were observed with multiple secondary bile acids (absolute rs≤0.38); the strongest positive correlation was for isoursodeoxycholate with R. gnavus (rs=0.38), and the same bile acid was negatively correlated with species richness (rs=-0.37). In comparison, abundances of S. mutans and L. fermentum were not correlated or only weakly correlated with secondary bile acid metabolites (rs<0.1). Additionally, S. mutans abundance correlated negatively with vitamin A metabolites and ergothioneine, and positively with the omeprazole metabolite and several tobacco metabolites (Supplementary Table 18, Supplementary Fig. 7).

DISCUSSION

We present a large prospective study assessing the association between the gut microbiome and incident ASCVD, including 17,216 community-dwelling participants with 769 ASCVD events over a median follow-up of 6-8 years. We identified four bacterial species that were adversely associated with IHD after extensive adjustment for confounders, including the oral bacteria S. mutans and L. fermentum. Furthermore, gut microbial diversity and five species were inversely associated with risk of IHD.

We found that the gut abundance of the oral species S. mutans and L. fermentum, known to be involved in dental caries when present in the mouth, was positively associated with IHD in the Swedish cohorts, with similar effect estimates in the HUNT study. These findings corroborate our earlier cross-sectional analysis of the microbiome composition in relation to image-based measures of coronary atherosclerosis in SCAPIS. 12 Gut abundances of Streptococcus and other oral taxa have been repeatedly reported to be associated with prevalent cardiovascular disease, [34] [35] [36] [37] [38] [39] and in a recent prospective study with new-onset hypertension. 14 Oral disease has been associated with increased ASCVD risk, 28, 33 with proposed mechanisms including increased translocation of oral bacteria and/or bacterial components from the oral cavity to the gut and/or to circulation due to poor oral health, as well as modulation of local and systemic inflammation by the oral bacteria. 33, 40 It has also been suggested that an increased abundance of oral species in the gut is a marker for microbial imbalance ("dysbiosis") and depletion of commensal bacteria. 41 Our analysis strategy accounted for medications related to such depletion, and models adjusted for diversity showed virtually unchanged estimates, making this explanation less likely. We also explicitly accounted for several measures of oral health and observed similar estimates, indicating that adverse associations for these species cannot be fully explained by poor oral health. A recent study showed that the same strain of S. mutans was found in the oral cavity and the gut, but exhibited different gene expression and predicted functions, suggesting that S. mutans was able to adapt to the gut environment. 42 We observed somewhat attenuated associations for S. mutans and L. fermentum in the analysis excluding the first two years of follow-up, which could indicate reverse causation, temporal instability of the exposure, or a chance finding. However, we consider reverse causation unlikely as we have excluded individuals with prior ASCVD events in our analysis and adjusted for cardiovascular medications. Temporal instability might explain the diminished effects of an exposure as the follow-up period lengthens. 43 Members of Lactobacillales, such as the genus Streptococcus, have been previously reported to largely fluctuate within individuals over time. 44 Enrichment of R. gnavus, a prevalent Gram-positive bacterium (assigned Mediterraneibacter gnavus in GTDB R232) was associated with increased IHD risk in the present study, consistent with several previous studies. Nooij and colleagues showed that R. gnavus is particularly enriched in ASCVD patients from 86 studies. 45 In the HELIUS cohort (9.5 y follow-up, 129 events) R. gnavus group spp. was associated with an increased risk of major adverse cardiovascular events in the age-adjusted model, although it was no longer associated in multivariable analysis. 45 R. gnavus is further reported to correlate with many traditional CVD risk factors, such as excess adiposity 46, 47 and prevalent, 45 as well as incident type 2 diabetes. 48, 49 R. gnavus might affect IHD risk by modulating the levels of microbial-derived metabolites such as secondary bile acids 50 and imidazole propionate, 51, 52 which were also observed in the present study. Another species associated with IHD risk, A. colihominis, showed a similar pattern of associated metabolites to R gnavus and has been shown to be enriched in patients with acute coronary syndrome. 39 While A. colihominis and R. gnavus have been reported to produce SCFAs, in SCAPIS data, their abundances were correlated negatively with dietary fiber intakes and positively with inflammatory marker CRP. A. colihominis and some strains of R. gnavus have been reported to utilize mucin as a nutrient source. 53, 54 A habitually low fiber diet was shown to promote expansion of mucin-degraders, and lead to increased inflammation, 55 which potentially explains the adverse association for these species in the present study. Abundance of both species was also enriched in those with recent antibiotic use. 56 We also observed that further adjustment for microbial diversity or exclusion of individuals with recent antibiotic use attenuated the associations of both species. This suggests that the increased abundance of R. gnavus and A. colihominis might also simply reflect a microbial community with a reduced diversity. Gut enrichment of inflammatoryassociated taxa, such as R. gnavus, has been suggested to characterize a microbial community with low diversity, 57 although the directionality of causality is unknown.

We observed an inverse association between gut microbial diversity and risk of IHD.

Previous evidence for an association of microbial diversity and ASCVD is conflicting; most case-control studies 6, 37, 38, 58 reported no associations, while a limited number of crosssectional analyses in individuals without prior CVD showed that microbial diversity was inversely associated with CVD risk score 59, 60 and with subclinical atherosclerosis in SCAPIS. 12 The few available prospective analyses also reported no associations, but they were likely insufficiently powered (≤254 events). [13] [14] [15] High microbial diversity is generally regarded as beneficial for health, given the low microbial diversity observed in diseased states, such as inflammatory bowel disease. 61 Westernization, often characterized by low fiber and high saturated fat content in the diet, has been associated with lower microbial diversity, with concomitantly reduced microbial capacity to degrade complex carbohydrates, resulting in lower production of short-chain fatty acids, [62] [63] [64] involved in host physiological processes also related to the pathogenesis of ASCVD, such as lipid metabolism and immune system modulation. 2, 65 Antibiotic use, which severely reduces microbial diversity, has also been linked to increased CVD events in a few studies. 66, 67 In the present study, individuals with higher microbial diversity at baseline tend to have healthier lifestyles and better cardiometabolic health compared to those with less diverse gut microbes, consistent with previous cross-sectional studies. 57, 59, 68, 69 The association of gut microbial diversity with the risk of IHD in the present study remained after extensive adjustment of demographic and lifestyle factors, medication use, and prevalent diabetes, known to affect both gut microbiome and ASCVD risk, which suggests that microbial diversity may also affect ASCVD risk through another pathway that is independent of these factors.

A highly diverse ecosystem is thought to be stable and resilient; a diverse microbial community is more resistant to perturbations and would recover faster from perturbation than a community with lower diversity, due to high functional redundancy. [70] [71] [72] Impaired resilience against perturbations therefore may have detrimental consequences for host health. Additional research is needed to elucidate the pathways through which microbial diversity confers protection against ASCVD.

In this study, several taxa are associated with a lower risk of IHD. SFEL01 sp004557245 and Fimadaptatus sp900553645 belong to the order Christensenellales, whose members have been linked to cardiometabolic health and butyrate-producing capacity. In a recent study, SFEL01 sp004557245 was identified as having buk, one of the terminal genes for butyrate production from complex polysaccharides, encoding butyrate kinase. 73 Depleted abundance of members of Christensenellales has previously been reported to be associated with higher cardiovascular risk (estimated by CVD risk scores) in individuals without CVD. 13, 60, 74 We further investigated their associations with plasma metabolites, revealing many shared, correlated metabolite associations with species richness. We also recently found the same genetic variant to be associated with both species richness and SFEL01 sp004557245, 17 leading us to suspect that the abundance of this species might be a marker of microbial diversity. However, adjusting for species richness and excluding antibiotic users showed virtually unchanged estimates for SFEL01 sp004557245, suggesting that its abundance is not merely a predictor of high diversity. Associations for other protective species, Blautia_A wexlerae_B, and uncultured species Fimadaptatus sp900553645, Choladousia sp902363135 and Angelakisella sp900547385, were slightly attenuated after adjusting for species richness or antibiotic use. These uncultured species have not been previously described in relation to ASCVD or its risk factors, except for Angelakisella sp900547385 whose abundance was inversely associated with visceral fat 75 and 24-hour blood pressure. 76 Although still limited, there is growing evidence linking Blautia wexlerae (NCBI taxonomy for Blautia_A wexlerae_B) to obesity and diabetes. 77-79 B. wexlerae abundance was inversely associated with prevalent obesity and type 2 diabetes in Japanese individuals, 78 and in two different experiments, interventions with B. wexlerae seemed to protect against obesity and diabetes in murine model. 78, 79 Although B. wexlerae lacks the ability to produce propionate and butyrate, it can produce succinate, lactate, and acetate, 78 which can be used by other bacteria to produce propionate or butyrate. Such cross-feeding interactions may also be important for cardiovascular health and warrant further investigation.

Strengths of our present analyses include a prospective design, a large sample size, registerbased ascertainment of outcomes, harmonized high-resolution microbiome characterization, and extensive adjustment for confounders, with prioritized species that are robust to variation in data transformation methods. Firstly, the prospective design substantially reduced the risk of reverse causation compared with previous studies that largely relied on case-control designs. Secondly, the number of ASCVD events included in this study is by far the largest owing to linkage to national patient and death registers, enabling us to obtain detailed information about diagnoses, hospitalizations, and causes of death, and to reduce the risk of outcome misclassification 80 . Third, shotgun metagenomic sequencing and a harmonized bioinformatic pipeline across cohorts enabled species-level resolution. Previous studies have used low-resolution 16S rRNA data 6, 13, 14 or shallow metagenomic sequencing 15 and thus were only able to report findings at genus level or higher. Fourth, the extensive inclusion of confounding factors is important because environmental factors such as diet and medications shape the composition of an individual's gut microbiome and are also linked to cardiovascular risk. [8] [9] [10] [11] However, previous studies have not fully accounted for these factors, as well as other lifestyle factors such as smoking, physical activity, and presence of diabetes.

When we adjusted only for demographic and technical variation, as in previous studies, 68 common and 19 rare species were associated with IHD; after further controlling for differences in lifestyle, medication use, and prevalent diabetes, only eight common and one rare species remained. This underscores the importance of rigorous control of confounding in causal investigations of the microbiome and disease risk, and helps prioritize species for further functional characterization and as potential targets for intervention. Additionally, the harmonization of confounders appears important when including data from multiple cohorts.

Heterogeneity in associations among most species was substantially reduced in the fully adjusted model, suggesting that after accounting for differences attributable to lifestyle and medications, the associations are more consistent across cohorts. To note, our model specification assumes that associations of microbial species with ASCVD have a similar confounding structure, which may not hold across all tested microbial species; however, this modeling approach also allows us to test a large number of species and provides greater flexibility in testing our assumptions through multiple sensitivity analyses. Some studies controlled for differences in BMI statistically or by matching, instead of adjustment for differences in diet or physical activity. 38, 60, 81 However, BMI might also lie in the causal pathway between the gut microbiome and ASCVD, 82, 83 and importantly, adjusting for BMI but not diet or physical activity assumes that the influence of these factors on the gut microbiome and ASCVD risk is solely through BMI modification. Nevertheless, our results showed that further adjustments for BMI showed virtually unchanged estimates, likely because diet and physical activity, already adjusted for in the analysis, are strongly correlated with BMI.

Several limitations also need to be considered. Owing to the observational nature of the study, we cannot offer definitive conclusions regarding the causal relationship between the gut microbiome and ASCVD. Despite our efforts to control for confounding and the availability of extensive linkage with registers, residual confounding due to measurement errors cannot be ruled out. We tried to limit this as much as possible, for example, by capturing proton-pump inhibitors use based on their detected metabolites in plasma, as these drugs can be both prescribed and available over the counter. We also attempted to replicate our findings in external cohorts; however, meta-analyzed estimates from HUNT and FINRISK cohorts showed largely non-replicated associations, except for S. mutans, which was adversely associated with IHD in HUNT participants and was not reliably measured in the shallow-sequenced FINRISK. While these results suggest that these species may not be truly associated with ASCVD, we cannot rule out methodological differences and the much longer follow-up duration in FINRISK contributing to the non-replication. The low prevalence of some species might have further limited the statistical power to detect associations. Inconsistency in published findings remains a concern in microbiome research, 84 partly due to a lack of standardized protocols for sample collection, DNA extraction and sequencing, and bioinformatics pipeline, which can substantially influence the observed microbial composition. [85] [86] [87] Despite these limitations, our study could offer insights on the importance of ensuring sufficient power (i.e., having many events) by including more cohorts, ideally with gut microbiome data processed using a standardized bioinformatic pipeline, and controlling for important covariates such as diet, lifestyle and medication use, in microbiome-disease studies aiming for causal inference. In this study, the fecal metagenomic data was only available at baseline, assuming that all microbial species remained stable throughout the follow-up period. Investigations using repeated measurements of the gut microbiome and atherosclerosis progression would provide stronger evidence of causality between the gut microbiome and ASCVD.

Collectively, our data support the role of the gut microbiome in the pathophysiology of ASCVD. Our present analysis provides evidence for the protective effect of higher microbial diversity on the long-term risk of ASCVD and shows that low microbial diversity can be present years before the disease occurs, supporting the temporality of the association. Our data also add to the evidence that gut enrichment of oral taxa S. mutans and L. fermentum, and inflammatory-associated R. gnavus, is likely harmful, while enrichment of two Christensenellales species seems to be protective. Furthermore, our study identifies several microbial features as targets to prioritize in future studies to confirm their causal roles.
