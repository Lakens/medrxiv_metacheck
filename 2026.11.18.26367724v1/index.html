What we found

We found 1 non-significant p value that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Metacheck does not yet analyze automatically whether sentences which include non-significant p-values are correct, but we recommend manually checking the sentences below for possible misinterpreted non-significant p values.

p=0.29 In multivariable models, global Wald tests indicated evidence of interaction between pandemic period and cancer type (p<0.001), health region type (p=0.001), and quintile of material deprivation (p<0.001), but not by sex (p=0.29) or age at admission (p=0.05).

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). Statcheck might still miss one-sided tests that are described in another sentence, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

The COVID-19 pandemic disrupted healthcare delivery globally. In Québec, Canada, measures were introduced to prioritize pediatric oncology care. Using administrative data, we estimated pandemic-related changes in cancer diagnoses, treatments, and length of hospital stay among individuals aged 0-19 in Québec (2015-2023). Interrupted time series analyses using autoregressive integrated moving average (ARIMA) models assessed immediate and sustained changes in monthly agestandardized rates of diagnoses, treatments (chemotherapy, surgery, radiotherapyrelated encounters), positron emission tomography scans, and hospital admissions. Separate ARIMA models fitted to pre-pandemic monthly counts generated counterfactual forecasts to estimate the cumulative differences between observed and expected diagnoses and treatments over 3, 12, and 46 months of the pandemic. Generalized linear mixed models assessed changes in length of stay between the preand post-pandemic periods and whether these changes differed by patient characteristics. We identified 2,570 patients: 1,475 diagnosed through February 2020 and 1,095 from March 2020 onward. We found no evidence of immediate or sustained changes in monthly incidence rates. Cumulative diagnoses and treatments remained within expected levels across periods, although estimates were imprecise. For diagnoses, the 3-, 12-, and 46-month cumulative differences were -1 (95% uncertainty interval [UI] -17 to 15), 24 (95% UI, -9 to 57), and 2 (95% UI, -94 to 96), respectively. Changes in length of hospital stay varied across subgroups, although their clinical significance remains uncertain. Pediatric cancer diagnosis and treatment activity was largely maintained across Québec throughout the pandemic, consistent with the objectives of provincial and institutional measures to preserve pediatric oncology services.

Significance: (43/50) This population-based study provides a long-term assessment of pandemic-related changes in pediatric oncology care in Québec, Canada, extending earlier evidence focused on the initial pandemic period. The findings provide reassurance regarding the continuity of cancer diagnosis and treatment activity despite widespread healthcare disruptions.

INTRODUCTION

Cancer remains a leading cause of disease-related death among children and adolescents worldwide. 1, 2 Although survival has improved substantially in recent decades, particularly in high-income settings, these gains depend on timely recognition of disease and access to specialized treatment. 3 Disruptions in care pathways and healthcare-seeking behaviour, such as those introduced by the COVID-19 pandemic, raise concerns about the continuity of pediatric cancer care. The pandemic's immediate impacts were heterogeneous by region and far-reaching. 4 Due in part to overburdened healthcare systems and risk mitigation strategies, reductions in cancer diagnoses among adult populations were documented across North America, [5] [6] [7] [8] Europe, 9, 10 and the United Kingdom, 11 particularly for screendetectable cancers. 5 The impact of the pandemic on adult cancer diagnosis and care cannot be assumed to be equivalent for childhood cancer. Unlike adult cancers, population-based screening is not routinely implemented for childhood and adolescent cancers; diagnoses are driven by clinical presentation. 12, 13 Fewer healthcare encounters during the pandemic may have limited opportunities for signs and symptoms to be recognized and investigated. Notably, in the first year of the pandemic, family physician services among 0-17-year-olds decreased by 24% to 36% across four Canadian provinces. 14 Even when care was sought, nonspecific symptoms may have been attributed to COVID-19 rather than to an underlying cancer, potentially delaying cancer diagnosis. 15 Canadian evidence on the impact of the pandemic on childhood cancer care remains mixed. While no changes in the number of newly diagnosed childhood cancers were observed during the early pandemic period, 16 analyses extending through 2022 reported fewer childhood cancers diagnosed overall, 17 suggesting patterns may have differed as the pandemic evolved. Importantly, evidence regarding the delivery and continuity of cancer treatment among children and adolescents remains limited. Although a small survey of families receiving cancer care in Canada and the United States indicated that most treatments were unaffected, a minority reported changes to their type, timing, or delivery. 18 A broader Canadian analysis also identified a 20% reduction in all cancerrelated services during the first year of the pandemic, but did not provide pediatricspecific estimates. 19 In Québec, governmental and institutional measures were implemented to protect pediatric oncology services. 20, 21 A central component of this response was preserving the specialized workforce delivering pediatric cancer care. Institutions were required to establish contingency plans for replacing absent medical and nursing staff with appropriately qualified personnel, while redeployment of oncology staff to acute-care settings was discouraged to limit transmission. 21 The delivery of care during this period has not been well characterized at the population level in Québec. It therefore remains unclear whether childhood cancer diagnosis, treatment delivery, hospital admission, and length of stay changed during the pandemic, and whether any changes persisted beyond the early pandemic period.

Our objective was to estimate changes in the rates of childhood and adolescent cancer diagnosis and treatment delivery in Québec, Canada, attributable to the COVID-19 pandemic, and to assess differences in length of hospital stay before and during the pandemic.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study design and data sources

We conducted a retrospective, population-based cohort study using administrative health data from Québec, Canada, a province where a publicly funded healthcare system covers over 95% of the population and delivers medically necessary physician and hospital services. 22 We linked records from the Maintenance et exploitation des données pour l'étude de la clientèle hospitalière (MED-ECHO) database, which captures all acute-care hospitalizations and day surgeries, with data from the Régie de l'assurance maladie du Québec (RAMQ), including fee-for-service physician claims, diagnostic and intervention codes, and patients' demographic information. We identified children and adolescents aged 0-19 years with at least one diagnostic code for a malignant tumour between January 2015 and December 2023 recorded in MED-ECHO. We categorized cancer diagnoses into 12 diagnostic groups as defined by the International Classification of Childhood Cancer, third edition (ICCC-3). 23 International Classification of Diseases for Oncology, third edition (ICD-O-3) codes were mapped to ICCC-3 categories using the published conversion algorithm. 23 We applied a five-year washout period (2010-2014) to exclude prevalent cancer cases and their associated treatments. This secondary data analysis was approved by the McGill Institutional Review Board, in accordance with the Canadian Tri-Council Policy Statement of Ethical Conduct for Research Involving Humans.

Outcomes

We assessed the monthly age-standardized rates (ASRs) per 1,000,000 population for newly diagnosed cancers, administered chemotherapy, radiotherapy-related services, treatment-related surgeries, positron emission tomography (PET) scans, cancer-related hospital admissions, and all treatments combined. All treatment and healthcare utilization outcomes were assessed among the identified cancer cases only. PET scan utilization was examined as a potential indicator of disruptions to cancer care given its role in disease staging and treatment response assessment. 24, 25 We assigned the date of cancer diagnosis using a previously described hierarchical algorithm that prioritized biopsy as the most definitive diagnostic procedure. 26 Chemotherapy administration, radiotherapy-related services, treatment-related surgeries, and PET scans were identified using Canadian Classification of Health Interventions (CCI) and physician billing codes. As the setting of treatment delivery could not be reliably determined from physician billing codes, inpatient and outpatient services were not differentiated. Chemotherapy administration was restricted to services provided by specialists in hematology, medical oncology, or pediatric hematology-oncology. Radiotherapy-related services included treatment planning or new radiation services. We compiled billing codes for administered chemotherapy, radiation-related services, and surgery to create a composite measure of all treatments combined, allowing patients to contribute to more than one treatment type within the same month. We assessed all treatments combined overall and stratified by age group for children aged 0-14 years and adolescents aged 15-19 years. Codes used to define each outcome are reported in Supplementary Table 1.

We calculated hospital length of stay as the number of days between admission and discharge. When admission and discharge occurred on the same day, such as in the case of ambulatory surgery, we assigned a length of stay of 0.5 days to reflect partialday hospital admission.

Exposures and covariates

We defined the COVID-19 post-pandemic period as March 2020 through end of followup to December 2023, beginning with the declaration of the public health emergency in Québec and the implementation of public health restrictions. 27 January 2015 through February 2020 constituted the pre-pandemic baseline period.

Clinical and demographic characteristics relevant to healthcare access and patterns of care were summarized descriptively, including age at diagnosis (<1, 1-4, 5-9, 10-14, 15-19), cancer type, sex, and quintile of material and social deprivation index, assigned at the census dissemination area level using postal code of residence at diagnosis. 28, 29 We assessed two geography-based variables. Residential rurality was classified based on the patient's place of residence at diagnosis using Statistics Canada Census Metropolitan Area classification. Health region type, reflecting the geographical location of the facility providing care, was assessed at diagnosis and for each subsequent hospital admission. The health region recorded for each encounter was then categorized into types according to the presence of and proximity to academic and specialized care facilities. 30

Statistical analysis

We used an interrupted time series approach to evaluate immediate and sustained changes in childhood and adolescent cancer diagnosis and cancer-related service utilization attributable to the COVID-19 pandemic. We fitted autoregressive integrated moving average (ARIMA) models to monthly ASRs, calculated using direct standardization to the 2021 Canadian standard population aged 0-19 years to account for changes in population size and age structure over time. 31, 32 ARIMA models accounted for autocorrelation, underlying temporal trends, and seasonality in monthly rates. We divided the time series into a pre-pandemic baseline period (January 2015 to February 2020) and a post-pandemic period (March 2020 to December 2023), with March 2020 representing the interruption point. The impact model included a level shift to estimate the immediate change in monthly ASR at the onset of the pandemic, and slope change to estimate any subsequent change in trend relative to the pre-pandemic period. 33 We assessed variance stability and stationarity using visual inspection of the time series, autocorrelation plots, and the augmented Dickey-Fuller and Kwiatkowski-Phillips-Schmidt-Shin tests. 34, 35 When necessary, we applied nonseasonal or seasonal differencing to achieve stationarity. Candidate models were selected based on autocorrelation and partial autocorrelation function plots and the minimum Akaike information criterion. Residual autocorrelation was assessed using the Ljung-Box test. 32, 36, 37 We used each outcome-specific ARIMA model fitted to pre-pandemic data to forecast and plot counterfactual monthly ASRs and corresponding 95% prediction intervals (PI) for the post-pandemic period.

To quantify cumulative differences in the number of cancer diagnoses, chemotherapy administrations, surgeries, radiation services, and all treatments combined among children in Québec, we repeated ARIMA analyses fitted to pre-pandemic monthly event counts rather than ASRs. We generated 10,000 simulated counterfactual trajectories from the fitted models, incorporating both parameter uncertainty through the variancecovariance matrix and forecast uncertainty from future ARIMA innovations. For each simulated trajectory, we summed the observed and expected monthly counts over the early pandemic period (3 months, March 2020-May 2020), mid-pandemic period (12 months, March 2020-February 2021), and total pandemic period (46 months, March 2020-December 2023). We then calculated the cumulative difference by subtracting the expected total from the observed total, and the cumulative ratio by dividing the observed total by the expected total. We used the 2.5 th and 97.5 th percentiles of the simulated differences and ratios to construct 95% uncertainty intervals (UI). These differences represent the estimated excess or deficit in diagnoses and services relative to counterfactual expectations over specified periods of the pandemic.

We analyzed length of hospital stay at the individual admission level. We restricted to cancer-related hospital admissions occurring after diagnosis among individuals aged 0-19 years identified in the MED-ECHO tumour file between January 2015 and December 2023. We summarized mean length of hospital stay by patient characteristics and stratified by pandemic period. Because length of stay was right-skewed, we fitted univariate and multivariable generalized linear mixed models with a gamma distribution and log link function to estimate associations with patient characteristics. 38 All models included a random intercept for patient identifier to account for multiple admissions within patients. We adjusted for sex, cancer type, age at hospital admission, pandemic period at admission, material deprivation quintile at admission, health region type of the admitting facility, day of the week, month of admission, and calendar time. For covariates with missing data, we included a missing-indicator category to retain all individuals in the analysis. Model-based marginal mean length of stay was estimated using least-squares means. For selected covariates, we used pairwise least-squares mean contrasts to estimate differences in mean length of stay on the absolute scale (days) and the multiplicative scale (ratios). We calculated confidence intervals for absolute differences using the delta method. To assess whether the association between pandemic period and length of hospital stay differed across patient and clinical characteristics, we fitted separate models that included interaction terms between pandemic period and each of the covariates of interest: sex, cancer type, health region type of the admitting facility, age at admission, and material deprivation quintile. For each multivariable model, we assessed the global interaction using a Wald test. We calculated marginal mean length of stay and estimated differences on the absolute and multiplicative scales for the post-pandemic period compared with the pre-pandemic period within each level of the interacting variable.

RESULTS

We identified 2570 children and adolescents aged 0-19 years diagnosed with cancer in Québec, Canada, between 2015 and 2023, including 1475 cases diagnosed during the pre-pandemic period (January 2015-February 2020) and 1095 during the postpandemic period (March 2020-December 2023). The demographic, clinical, and geographic characteristics of cancer cases were similar in both periods (Table 1). Overall, leukemias, lymphomas, and CNS tumours predominated, accounting for over half of all diagnoses. Most patients resided in Montréal and were diagnosed in facilities located in university-affiliated health regions. The distribution of dissemination area-level material and social deprivation was similar across time periods, with the largest proportion of pre-pandemic cases occurring in the least socially deprived quintile.

Missingness was limited for both material and social deprivation before (6.46%) and during (6.85%) the pandemic.

There was no evidence of an immediate level change at the onset of the pandemic or a subsequent change in monthly trend for newly diagnosed cancers, chemotherapy administration, treatment-related surgeries, radiotherapy-related services, all treatments combined, PET scans, or hospital admissions. Estimates were generally imprecise across outcomes, as reflected by wide confidence intervals. For newly diagnosed cancers, the estimated level change in monthly ASR at pandemic onset was 0.80 cases per 1,000,000 population (95% CI: -0.90 to 2.44), and the estimated slope change was -0.05 cases per 1,000,000 population per month (95% CI: -0.10 to 0.01). For all treatments combined, the estimated level change in monthly ASR at pandemic onset was -5.84 treatments per 1,000,000 population (95% CI: -61.6 to 49.9), and the estimated slope change was -1.16 treatments per 1,000,000 per month (95% CI: -3.34 to 1.02). Outcome-specific ARIMA orders and model estimates are reported in Table 2, and observed and counterfactual ASR trends are shown in Figure 1.

Table 3 presents the cumulative observed-minus-expected differences and ratios for cancer diagnoses, chemotherapy administration, treatment-related surgeries, radiotherapy-related services, and all treatments combined over the first three months (March-May 2020), 12 months (March 2020-February 2021), and 46 months (March 2020-December 2023) of the pandemic. Across outcomes and time periods, cumulative differences were generally small and compatible with expected variation. For cancer diagnoses, the difference between observed and expected cases was -1 (95% UI: -17 to 15) in the first three months, 24 (95% UI: -9 to 57) during the first 12 months, and 2 (95% UI: -94 to 96) over the full 46-month pandemic period.

Length of cancer-related hospital stay was right-skewed overall and within each pandemic period, with most admissions ending in same-day discharge (Figure 2). Among the 220 COVID-19-related hospital admissions, 85 listed COVID-19 as the primary reason for admission and 135 as a secondary reason. Mean length-of-stay ratios from univariate and multivariable models are reported in Supplementary Table 2, with associations varying across covariates and their levels. Unadjusted models indicated shorter mean length of stay during the post-pandemic period [ratio, 0.83 (0.79 to 0.88)] compared to the pre-pandemic period, although no difference was observed after adjustment [ratio, 1.01 (0.94 to 1.10)]. Table 4 presents observed mean length of stay before and during the pandemic, together with pandemic-period contrasts from interaction models on the absolute and multiplicative scales. In multivariable models, global Wald tests indicated evidence of interaction between pandemic period and cancer type (p<0.001), health region type (p=0.001), and quintile of material deprivation (p<0.001), but not by sex (p=0.29) or age at admission (p=0.05). Among individuals with retinoblastoma, mean length of stay was shorter in the post-pandemic period, with an adjusted marginal mean difference of -0.43 days (95% CI: -0.70 to -0.16), corresponding to a 35% reduction in mean length of stay [mean length-of-stay ratio 0.65 (95% CI: 0.51 to 0.83)]. Mean length of stay was also shorter among individuals in the fourth quintile of material deprivation [-1.18 days (95% CI: -1.78 to -0.58)], representing a 23% reduction in mean length of stay [mean length-of-stay ratio 0.77 (95% CI: 0.68 to 0.88)]. No differences were observed among individuals in other deprivation quintiles. In contrast, mean length of stay was 42% longer in the post-pandemic period among individuals with bone tumours [+1.62 days (95% CI: 0.77 to 2.46); ratio, 1.42 (95% CI: 1.20 to 1.67)] and 74% longer among admissions to facilities in peripheral healthcare regions [+1.23 days (95% CI: 0.36 to 2.09); ratio, 1.74 (95% CI: 1.23 to 2.46)].

DISCUSSION

In this population-based study of children and adolescents with cancer in Québec, Canada, we found no evidence of immediate or sustained changes in cancer diagnosis or cancer-related service utilization following the onset of the COVID-19 pandemic. Monthly variations in diagnoses and treatments were largely within expected ranges given pre-pandemic trends. We also found no evidence of cumulative differences between observed and expected numbers of diagnoses or treatments over the first 3, 12, or full 46 months following pandemic onset. However, we did observe changes in length of hospital stay from March 2020 onward for select cancer types, health region types, and quintiles of material deprivation.

Our findings are consistent with earlier pan-Canadian evidence suggesting no disruption in the diagnosis of childhood cancer during the early pandemic period. 16 Our cumulative analyses further showed no long-term evidence of excess or fewer diagnoses over the full pandemic period, despite reports of differences during later pandemic years. 7 However, the extent to which these later-period differences were attributable to the pandemic remains unclear. 17, 32, 33 The absence of detectable change in childhood cancer diagnoses contrasts with the 15% reduction in reporting of new adult cancers in Québec during the first year of the pandemic, 8 and may reflect differences in how cancers come to clinical attention in children. Although encounters with family physicians and emergency departments declined among children across Canada, 14, 40, 41 the pediatric emergency department visits that did occur tended to involve higher-acuity presentations. 40 Because childhood cancer diagnosis is largely symptom-driven rather than reliant on asymptomatic screening, families may have continued to seek care for acute, persistent, or progressively concerning presentations, preserving opportunities for cancer recognition despite lower overall healthcare utilization.

Evidence of pandemic-related disruption was also limited for treatment-related activity, PET scans, and hospital admissions, despite reports of disruptions to cancer services in other settings. In Ontario, early reductions in cancer-related surgery and radiotherapy services occurred alongside increases in systemic treatment and PET scans, 19 suggesting that pandemic effects on cancer services varied across service types. In contrast, we observed no evidence of immediate or sustained changes in monthly rates of treatment-related activity. While treatment backlogs have been reported, particularly for cancer-related surgeries, 4, 19, 42 cumulative treatment activity in our study remained within expected levels over the entire 46-month pandemic period, suggesting that any short-term disruptions did not develop into sustained deficits at the population level. The absence of observed changes in core pediatric oncology services in Québec may partially reflect provincial guidance and institutional measures intended to prioritize care for children and adolescents with cancer. 43, 44 This prioritization was also reflected in treatment-specific guidance. Although reductions in radiotherapy activity were recommended for the broader patient population, treatment for pediatric patients was to continue according to clinical need despite the risk of COVID-19 infection. 20, 21 Guidance similarly prioritized clinical trial enrolment and participation, reinforcing efforts to minimize pandemic-related departures from planned treatment. 21 Importantly, provincial guidance was not universally restrictive, with children receiving or recently completing treatment encouraged to return to school in September 2020 when clinically appropriate, and siblings encouraged to attend even when the child with cancer was advised to remain home. 45 While the relative contribution of individual protective measures was not investigated, the absence of detectable changes in diagnosis, treatment, and hospital admission rates is compatible with a role for provincial guidance in maintaining pediatric oncology services.

While length of hospital stay has important implications for patient morbidity, family wellbeing, and healthcare utilization, its clinical relevance and interpretation are context dependent. 46 Interactions between pandemic period and cancer type, health region type, and material deprivation indicated that changes in length of stay were non-uniform in both direction and magnitude. Indeed, pandemic-related changes in care could plausibly have contributed to longer or shorter stays: COVID-19 testing requirements, preoperative admissions, and isolation protocols may have prolonged some hospitalizations, 4, [47] [48] [49] [50] while efforts to reduce in-hospital transmission may have favoured earlier discharge when clinically feasible. 18, 44 For patients with bone tumours, whose care frequently involves complex surgical intervention, observed increases in length of stay may have been influenced by pandemic-related changes in perioperative care, 44, 49 including prolonged or earlier hospitalization to facilitate access to constrained operating room capacity. 51 Conversely, shorter stays among patients with retinoblastoma may reflect earlier discharge following enucleation, a procedure with predictable postoperative recovery. 52, 53 Variation in length of stay by health region type of the admitting facility may also reflect differences in admission practices. 40, 41 In peripheral health regions, characterized by greater geographic separation from major health centres, 30 brief or precautionary admissions may have been reduced with increased use of virtual care. 54 In contrast, admissions in university-affiliated health regions, where protocol-driven cancer treatment and complex care are concentrated, 55 may have been less susceptible to such changes given the prioritization of essential pediatric oncology services. While greater material deprivation has been previously associated with longer hospital admission among children with medical complexity, 56 we observed no consistent gradient. The isolated reduction in length of stay among those in the fourth deprivation quintile should therefore be interpreted cautiously and may reflect chance variation. More broadly, changes in admission practices and the use of alternative care pathways may have altered the case mix of hospitalized patients, 20, 21 such that observed differences in length of stay partially reflect who was admitted rather than changes in inpatient care itself. Accordingly, the clinical significance of changes in length of stay during the pandemic remains unclear.

Strengths and limitations

We used administrative data to capture cancer-related healthcare encounters among children and adolescents in Québec, Canada, from 2015 to 2023. These data provide near-complete population coverage and include care delivered at both pediatric and adult facilities, reducing the potential for missed encounters across care settings. However, the available data only permitted identification of malignant diagnoses. Because ICCC-3 includes benign and borderline tumours within the CNS and germ cell tumour diagnostic groups, 23 some of these tumours were not captured. This is particularly relevant for CNS tumours, for which non-malignant neoplasms comprise approximately 13-18% of all diagnoses in children. 57 Further, bundled billing codes prevented identification of the specific care delivered during some encounters, while billing frequency restrictions and initiation-only billing codes limited the capture of ongoing treatment encounters, potentially underestimating monthly rates. 58 Information on treatment route and scheduling was also insufficient to characterize treatment phase, delivery, and intensity, including potential adaptations to treatment delivery during the pandemic; we were unable to reliably differentiate treatments delivered in inpatient versus outpatient settings. The rarity of childhood cancer precluded stratified interrupted time series analyses, potentially obscuring heterogeneity across diagnostic groups. Small monthly counts and variability in rates also limited the precision of our estimates, warranting cautious interpretation of the findings. Monthly aggregation may also have obscured brief delays in diagnosis or treatment occurring within the same calendar month. While length-of-stay analyses accounted for repeated admissions and estimated subgroup differences on both absolute and relative scales, we could not distinguish planned from unplanned admissions, complicating attribution of observed differences to care practices versus clinical circumstances.

CONCLUSION

Our findings suggest that cancer diagnosis and treatment services for children in Québec, Canada, remained largely stable during the pandemic despite widespread healthcare disruption. While we observed changes in length of hospital stay across select subgroups, the observed pattern of care is consistent with the objectives of governmental and institutional measures to preserve pediatric oncology services during the pandemic.
