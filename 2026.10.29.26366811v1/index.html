Power Analysis Check

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. Some differences come from the ground truth: it lacks p-values with a correction name (“p Bonf = .061”), and counts partial eta squared written with its subscript after it (“η 2 p = .05”), “p = .05”, and some table notes and figure legends (“† p < .10, *p < .05”), which this module leaves out, as non-significant p-values. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

Abstract

This study explored Mental Performance Consultants' (MPCs') perspectives on the prospective use of the Self-Regulation in Sport Practice (SRSP) survey when consulting adolescent athletes. Twelve Canadian MPCs completed mock SRSP surveys and interviews about the survey's content and what they anticipated they could gain by using it with clients. Most of the MPCs claimed they could use the SRSP as an applied consulting resource. They believed that the SRSP could benefit the self-awareness of athletes and serve their information-gathering during case conceptualization. Most MPCs foresaw framing and enlarging SRSP statements/scores in joint discussion with a client, as part of triangulation, with some valuing the survey metrics for athletes and oneself. However, MPCs were philosophically divided on survey use, exposing uncertainty and negative preconceptions for some around quantitative assessment.

Understanding How the Self-Regulation in Sport Practice Survey is Received by Mental Performance Consultants for Prospective Use in Applied Practice

Mental Performance Consultants (MPCs) in applied sport psychology must gather information about a client-including their current difficulties, a profile of strengths and weaknesses, as well as their history and context, to guide an intervention. Information gathering is essential in case conceptualization [1], the process whereby a consultant formulates an intervention plan for a client. During this process, an MPC is tasked with connecting assessment to evidence-based strategies. Information can be captured through different assessment methods including client-report surveys, interviews, behavioral observations, functional analysis, performance profiles, biofeedback, and performance data [2]. The methods an MPC chooses to glean client-related information are related to their professional philosophy, their training, and whether the consulting focus is on diagnosing (evaluation) or raising awareness (coaching) [2, 3].

Athlete self-report surveys have long been considered as a form of assessment in sport psychology [4]. Asking clients to report using an established questionnaire dedicated to a topic of interest has been advanced as an effective approach to gather information for effective service delivery [e.g., 2, 5]. This view, however, is not shared unequivocally by today's practitioners. For example, Vealey et al. [3] reported trends showing that MPCs may have less preference for questionnaires as part of their practices.

They compared the attitudes of MPCs certified by the Association for Applied Sport Psychology (AASP)

in 2003 with a similar cohort 14 years later. In 2003, 53% of MPCs said that they used self-report surveys with clients and found them 'useful or extremely useful'; only 23% evaluated their use to be 'useless or marginally useful'. In 2017, only 28% of respondents indicated that surveys were useful or extremely useful, with 48% evaluating surveys to be useless or marginally useful. Such trends may signal that we are witnessing changing philosophies whereby practitioners are seeing less value in using surveys as part of their interventions. For instance, Nideffer and Sagal noted in 2001 that trainees were getting little to no preparation in psychological assessment using surveys, adding that this was a consequence of applied literature with an anti-testing bias. Whereas Vealey et al. indicated that consultants in 2003 were more likely to couch surveys as a mode of promoting self-awareness and self-assessment in clients, in 2017, Survey Use for Consulting on Sport Practice 5 they were more likely to view surveys as diagnostic instruments. Distancing from survey use may also relate to practitioners' conceptions of a lack of "practical validity of such measures" [3, p. 521] in applied settings, such that research-created instruments feel out of step with applied procedures. As we entered the current decade, Vealey et al. noted that many consultants were uncertain how to use surveys as part of assessment practices due to a lack of training and mentorship, and that there was little innovation for applying new questionnaires.

Little published research has probed the elements of practical utility that are important to MPCs in using client-report surveys for case conceptualization. In 2001, Nideffer and Sagal lamented the disappearing literacy for quantitative survey use among the new generations of consultants, which they claimed was especially evident in needs analysis, interviewing with surveys, and delivering survey feedback to clients. Recently, Teschuk et al. [6] conducted a scoping review of four popular athlete selfreport questionnaires for assessing mental skills relating to the practice setting. They concluded that there was a remarkable lack of guidance in the published literature for how to use the surveys to enhance interventions. Following a comprehensive search that returned 369 published citations pertaining to the four quantitative surveys, only nine articles detailed aspects for how practitioners/intervenors used survey scores to inform/influence ongoing approaches within an intervention (i.e., not just pre-to-post monitoring) [see 7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15]; only one published case study explicitly described an MPC's use of a survey to engage a client in dialogue [16].

Altogether, there appears to be surprisingly little published literature informing how to use quantitative surveys in strategic ways within an intervention, and specifically in case conceptualization.

Yet, there continue to be recommendations for more practically informed approaches to surveys and assessment [e.g., 17]. More specifically, Young et al. [18] called for fulsome consideration of how the Self-Regulation of Sport Practice (SRSP) [19] survey could support practitioners' consulting approaches.

They called for more applied research to understand consultants' use of the SRSP for "the psychology of practice" [18, p.1], i.e., supporting athletes' psychological proficiencies that facilitate skill acquisition and learning in practice settings. Despite the SRSP having more of an empirical and less of an applied origin Survey Use for Consulting on Sport Practice 6 than other surveys such as Durand-Bush et al.'s [20] OMSAT-3 and Hardy et al.'s [21] TOPS-2 [6], this call for greater examination of the SRSP through the lens of a practitioner was predicated on the notion that there is metacognitive assessment in the SRSP that differs from mental skills assessment on other surveys, and that such content might lend itself well to consultation around practice-enhancement [22].

Siekańska et al. [16] also noted there was little known about how such the SRSP survey could be used in applied practice and invited more innovation in embedding the survey in consulting dialogue. As such, this study aimed to explore MPCs' views on the SRSP survey and its prospective use in applied practice.

The current researchers chose to explore the prospective practical validity of this survey because of their scholarly interest in the psychology of practice, because some of them had previously worked on the psychometric development of the SRSP survey, and because they wished to interrogate how the SRSP might be received by applied practitioners who were apart from them.

The SRSP survey [19] is grounded in self-regulated learning (SRL) theory [23], which emphasizes the self-agency and reflexivity of learners, and outlines psychological characteristics that support how learners prioritize and direct energies towards tasks that are more likely to benefit their development. The survey features a sport practice-enhancement orientation [24]. It asks athletes to consider practice contexts in which they engage in taxing/difficult practice tasks, that are not always necessarily enjoyable, and that they consider pertinent to their talent development. Statements assess athletes' report of various psychological processes they can enact when preparing for, engaging in, or reflecting upon their practice activities (either singular practice trials, or session as a whole) [18]. Thirtyone items assess five psychological processes related to the self-management of goal-oriented practice (see Supplementary Appendix A); items for 'planning', 'checking', and 'evaluation-reflection' scales are metacognitive, while scales for 'effort' and 'self-efficacy for challenge' are motivational in nature [19].

Wilson et al. [19] tendered this survey as a highly reliable and valid assessment instrument, which benefitted from about a decade of mostly psychometric endeavours in which measures that were imported from educational psychology became progressively attuned to the field of sport talent development, particularly to the sport practice setting [see 24, 25, 26, 27]. Methods

Research Paradigm

The overarching philosophical orientation guiding this study was pragmatism. Pragmatism is a practical consequence-driven view [29] that attempts to understand real world phenomena by selecting whatever combination of philosophies, methods, and procedures provides the best explanation to the research questions [30]. As such, the research aims, methods (i.e., having each MPC complete an SRSP survey for a prospective client), and recruitment criteria were all oriented towards applied findings.

Pragmatism also adopts a value-oriented approach [30], which was reflected in the researchers' and the participating MPCs' desire to support young athletes as learners in the practice context. Since our purpose was to understand how MPCs interpreted and made sense of prospective uses of the SRSP in their applied practice, we pragmatically adopted a constructivist epistemological lens when interpreting the interview data. Constructivism was selected because it is well suited to examining the multiple, context-dependent meanings that MPCs attribute to this topic, and for understanding how such meanings are co-constructed through interview dialogue [31]. With constructivism, knowledge is generated through interactive dialogue between participants and researchers, with the goal of interpreting how participants make sense of their experiences of a topic. Constructivists recognize that realities are socially constructed and subjective, meaning individuals actively build their understanding of the world through experiences and particularly social exchanges/processes. Consequently, knowledge and understanding of the topic are co-constructed between a participant and an interviewer.

In this paradigm, the interviewer's knowledge/experience is not separated from the process.

Rather, it can be an asset for eliciting interpretations from an interviewee, especially if the interviewer is highly cognizant of the topic, and as long as they effectively prompt the interviewee to share insights on the topic freely and extensively, and support the interviewee's dialogue in a responsive fashion (i.e., supporting the conversation in the direction it is being led by the interviewee, while keeping it within the purview of the research aims). Notably, the first and second author are professional members of the CSPA and AASP who hold the Certified Mental Performance Consultant credential. Their professional experiences informed reflexive interpretation of the data, while their pragmatic orientation ensured that the practical utility of the findings for practitioners remained at the forefront of that interpretive process.

Although pragmatism and constructivism are philosophically distinct [29], they are compatible for the purposes of applied qualitative inquiry because both prioritize understanding experiences in context and reject the notion that knowledge can be completely detached from human experience [31]. As such, we refrained from claims of singular reality and embraced context-dependent explanations. That said, pragmatism asks researchers to consider statements on degree of agreement "as a way to approach" what Survey Use for Consulting on Sport Practice 9 might be considered practical, though not universal, truths [29, p.22]. Thus, in presenting the findings, we highlighted areas of convergence and divergence among MPCs by noting, where appropriate, the number of participants who aligned with or differed in their interpretations and preferred approaches. In sum, our pragmatic orientation justified the use of a constructivist lens because understanding participants' contextdependent meaning-making was deemed the most useful way to address the research questions. When interpreting the results we refrained from claims of universal or objective truth (constructivism), but signalled areas of convergence and divergence in practical interpretations and approaches among MPCs (pragmatism).

Participants

A University Ethics Board approved all procedures. All participants gave written informed consent. Using the professional roster of the Canadian Sport Psychology Association [32], MPCs were emailed invitations to complete an online questionnaire beginning November 20, 2021. Procedures

Mock SRSP Survey

Participants completed a mock version of the SRSP survey [19] ahead of an interview. The survey typically asks athletes about how they manage aspects of their training. Items cover metacognitive scales for planning (9 items), checking (3), and evaluating-reflecting (4), and motivational scales for self-efficacy Survey Use for Consulting on Sport Practice 10 for challenge (5) and effort (5). Likert responses range from 1 (strongly disagree) to 7 (strongly agree). In this study, we were not interested in gaining respondents' self-reports. Instead, when presented with the mock version, each MPC was asked to "have a particular client in mind, responding as if you were said client" (see Supplementary Appendix A). They first entered demographic information for this client (see Table 2), then did the mock SRSP. This procedure familiarized the MPCs with items relevant to the assessment of psychological processes enacted in the practices of athletes. It also readied them to talk about how they might guide a client in consulting. Each MPC was emailed the mock SRSP within 48 hours of their upcoming interview and all returned it, completed, prior to meeting.

Each MPC engaged in a semi-structured ZOOM interview. The first part of the interview began by asking about their views on SRL, how they described it conceptually, and how they described features of effective SRL in adolescent athletes. The first author (FA) and third author independently piloted the interview guide with two MPCs.

They conducted the first interview together, then debriefed to ensure coherency and to reflect on the quality of their questioning. The FA conducted seven of the remaining interviews, with the third author doing three. Interviews were recorded and transcribed.

Data Analysis

In keeping with reflexive thematic analysis [36], the FA began by reading all transcripts and making notes to become familiar with the data [34]. Data matching the purpose and our key questions in this article amounted to 68 pages of single-spaced transcribed text, which were coded apart from data for Young et al. [33]. The FA initially coded passages at a lower-order, semantic level, populating lowerorder themes into separate coding sheets corresponding to the sequence of the interview. Within each sheet, the FA inductively analyzed the data to create middle-order themes of shared meaning. The FA next met the second author to present the sheets. They elected to collapse sheets from the early interview segments (i.e., 'big picture thoughts', 'global sense of what is being captured', and 'what psychological content is being assessed') into a higher-order theme for 'initial reception of the survey and its content'. Second, they noted a latent, cross-cutting theme involving tensions between different MPCs around the use of a quantitative survey, which warranted its own higher-order theme. Third, we made the latent observation of distinctions in the data for the information that MPCs could acquire by giving the SRSP survey to a client. Specifically, substantial data represented what an MPC could learn using the SRSP to initially gather information when figuring out a client's case, whereas other data represented how an MPC Survey Use for Consulting on Sport Practice 12 believed they could begin gainfully acting on the SRSP information in joint work with a client. Each of these interpretations warranted their own higher-order theme. After interpreting the higher-order themes, the FA sequenced them in a story-telling fashion, beginning with how the MPCs initially received the survey and its content, then ordering them according to phases of case conceptualization (i.e., an MPC initially gathers information to understand a client's case before acting on this information), while limiting duplicity among subthemes (see Supplementary Appendix C for a table of themes and subthemes.) Results

Findings begin with the MPCs' perspectives upon initial receipt of the SRSP. We then present philosophical divisions among the MPCs around using the quantitative survey, which emerged early and influenced their subsequent responses about the SRSP. The MPCs' Initial Reception of the Survey and Its Content

The MPCs first shared 'big picture' perceptions on the survey and their perspectives on the type of content being captured. All of the MPCs, except one, were naïve to the survey prior to being emailed.

They disclosed several notable subthemes, including: the SRSP can elicit information benefiting the MPC; the survey can elicit greater self-awareness and help structure self-feedback for athletes; its content captures the extent to which an adolescent gives themselves a role in their own learning; it captures SRL, metacognitive and motivational content; it neglects to assess some content.

Many of the MPCs first remarked that the SRSP could elicit valuable information. Lucia saw it as useful "to get to the nitty gritty in what [athletes] are thinking and feeling during practice-this survey kind of helps me to see whether they're actually doing that [psychological processing]". She believed that information generated from the SRSP would allow her to move past unreliable assumptions she might have about what athletes understand in terms of cognitive skills around practice. In the absence of a Survey Use for Consulting on Sport Practice 13 similarly pertinent survey, Maria believed the SRSP could meet an applied need, commenting, "This is one of the places where we have to grow as MPCs. We need to be able to generate some sort of evaluation or assessment of growth around learning and practice."

The MPCs initially responded that the survey could raise self-awareness benefiting athletes. Allan explained that when an athlete responds to the SRSP, "they're getting an understanding of how well they process information in their practice and ultimately providing themselves feedback with how well they're doing." He said, "part of this is having an athlete understand the perspectives they can have in their own evaluation." For Sofia, the SRSP "actually asks an athlete, 'are you thinking about what you're doing?' and invites them to engage in "a process focus" when appraising their practice habits. Ayesha contributed, "having a client do the SRSP would be a great self-awareness exercise around practice. The questions challenge them to think about themselves, like an entire reflective process." She explained that a process supporting self-awareness and self-feedback could be approached in a more structured fashion using the survey. Ayesha reflected, "the SRSP would be perfect for working with a client who's like, 'I'm not getting better, and I don't know what's going on'. So, really creating some sort of structure for them to approach practice in a way that allows them to be mindful and deliberate." Several MPCs noted that a more structured approach to enhancing awareness on psychological functioning around practice would meet a need, because this topic is not prioritized for adolescents. For example, Maria explained: Coaches don't spend any time explaining the things exemplified in this survey to kids about how they can manage the practice situation more effectively, how they can prepare for it, how they can manage their emotions, the distractions… That's what gives the SRSP some real value.

Allan opined, "young athletes obviously understand, like, how a practice is important. But they won't apply as many mental skills in practice, or as frequently, as they do in competition." The MPCs appreciated that information from the SRSP would signify an important focus and provide new insights on practice. Whereas Sofia thought such focused self-awareness could benefit adolescents mostly in Survey Use for Consulting on Sport Practice 14 preparing ahead of practice, many of the MPCs saw "more self-awareness and self-regulation around these pieces [in the SRSP] as an opportunity to increase the quality of practice" (Allan).

Some of the MPCs remarked that the survey content would capture the extent to which an athlete gives themself a role in their learning process. For Oliver, the SRSP: … surveys an individual's ability to take learning in their own hands and understand themselves and their performance enough to be able to make adjustments wherever necessary. [The SRSP] is capturing, quite well, self-regulated learning as a concept, and as a cycle of reflecting, preparing, and performing … But it pushes a little bit further. There are certain concepts of motivation, and some autonomy surveyed for sure, and being open to applying yourself to that learning, and the athlete actually having a role in that. Which, unfortunately, athletes don't really have a say all that often. So being able to foster that is important.

Use of the survey could support adolescents' development of competencies whereby they can more confidently insert themselves into their own learning. For example, Ronda said, "athletes [knowing their responses on the survey] could really create some self-awareness that they too have the capacity to engage in their own learning", which feeds the notion that "they can plan their effort, they can control how much effort they put in, they can evaluate themselves. Knowing that I can evaluate myself, that I don't have to always rely on feedback from my coaches, that's a big [insight]". The MPCs commonly highlighted three categories of content reflected in the SRSP. All commented that "the SRSP invites athletes to look at like how well they apply self-regulated learning processes in completing tasks or [working on] technique." They all recognized that the SRSP comprehensively captures metacognitive content important to practice and many acknowledged that the survey effectively represented motivational content. A few of the MPCs discerned what the SRSP did not capture. Two MPCs felt emotions were not portrayed directly and could only be addressed if they were to impress their own narrative on emotional responsiveness onto the items. For example, Charlotte pointed to "[item #12] 'something unexpected' or a coach changing the planning [on the athlete]", adding, "this item definitely relates to the reality of training. And so how does the athlete react to that? And there's going to Survey Use for Consulting on Sport Practice 15 be emotional responses first that need to be considered, before I consider the athlete's ability to adapt and re-plan." Ronda commented, "The SRSP is reporting how much the athletes use self-regulated learning strategies or do those strategies. If people are using these strategies more, they get a higher score, though it might not capture if they can do the strategies".

Divisions Among MPCs Around Using a Quantitative Survey in Consulting

How the MPCs viewed what they could learn by giving out the SRSP depended on their philosophy. Several MPCs preferred to figure out and set the guiding questions in client cases based predominantly (if not exclusively) on interviews or narrative approaches; these MPCs were more likely to problematize the survey or mention limitations to its use early in the interview. Notably, their issues with survey use were not exclusive to the SRSP but extended to any survey. Their responses portrayed tension between survey use for diagnostic assessment and narrative intake. Several MPCs were concerned that surveys could be construed as a way of "testing" a client and as a form of "assessment" for diagnosing weaknesses. Myriam, Jose, Allan, Emily and Ayesha, who were in this camp, cautiously viewed any survey as an impersonal diagnostic instrument. They revealed they rarely used surveys; they shared philosophical reasons for alternative methods, and worried, for example (Ayesha), about "taking a survey and using it as an independent source to define a person". Ironically, when these MPCs were asked later in the interview whether the SRSP scales addressed what they were supposed to address, their responses fixated on validity and reliability of scores (i.e., psychometric terms) despite professing their disinterest in psychometrics. Instead of talking from a narrative perspective, they talked about "accuracy" and how they were uninterested in "reliability and validity" of instruments, in some cases sharing doubt about the accuracy/skewness of self-report scores. Thus, they fixated on a view of surveys as diagnostic tests, disjointed from humanistic approaches to understanding a client, with a risk of scores casting respondents in a critical/unconstructive light.

Other MPCs were in a different camp. Maria, Hugo and Ronda were devoted to surveys and saw them in a constructive light. They often used surveys in strategic, reasoned ways to enhance their client interactions. These MPCs were quick to say that they find surveys helpful in furthering dialogue and find that adolescents like surveys and putting numbers to things. Curiously, in later segments of the interview, when they were asked whether the SRSP scales and items addressed what they were supposed to, their responses focused on what they thought of the content of the items. They were less likely to stray into conversations on score accuracy, validity and reliability. A couple of these MPCs acknowledged that they knew about all the work that goes into establishing the psychometrics of a survey but added that this was not really a concern for them when using surveys in applied practice. The three other MPCs-Lucia, Oliver, and Charlotte, did not appear to fit in either camp and liked to move between survey use and narrative case examination, depending on context, client, preferences and needs.

Altogether, there was an ironic tension impressed on many responses for how the MPCs would receive the SRSP, depending on the camp in which they presented themselves. Those who embraced surveys did so because they intertwined assessment with dialogue. Those who were dismissive or highly Survey Use for Consulting on Sport Practice 17 reserved saw survey assessment as impersonal and curtailing dialogue. As these MPCs worked through the interview segments dedicated to the SRSP survey, this tension somewhat diminished, with all the MPCs eventually acknowledging ways they could see prospective reasons for using aspects of the SRSP.

Using the SRSP to Initially Gather Information to Understand the Client's Case

The MPCs portrayed different ways of using the SRSP to initially gather information about a client. A prevalent subtheme was that the scores mattered as a source of information they could analyze and interpret to understand a case. Although they varied in their convictions about the absolute importance of numeric scores, most of the MPCs saw lower scores (or unusually lower ones) as flagging an area to interrogate to determine whether there was a need for improvement. They expected to spend more time in dialogue in these areas. Maria said, "If I see a 4 when it's typically a 6, we'd have a conversation about that." She would "be honest that we're low, ask them why they felt that way" because this score tells me that something's happening, they're not leaning in, not adapting, or not managing the challenges." Ronda Myriam had a distinct perspective on gathering information by exploring low and high scores. She conjectured that attending to lower scores might tell her that a client lacks knowledge/support for developing SRL skills. She explained, "I need to figure out if they're low because they've never been exposed to this content, like, maybe they don't know that this is gonna help them", adding, "maybe their coach never gets them to do anything like this, like, they just go to practice, do as their told, and don't think about it." Alternatively, she anticipated that she might be able to verify whether high scores represented initiative by an athlete: "I would try to figure out, is this something you work on with your coach, or something you've done yourself? And how did you come to do that on your own?". In doing this, she felt that she could determine whether high scores might be a consequence of obligation because a parent, coach, or MPC had asked the athlete to engage in SRL content.

How the MPCs Could Begin Acting Upon the SRSP Survey to Formulate an Intervention

In addition to describing the useful types of information that the SRSP survey might permit them to gather about an athlete, the MPCs also relayed how they would begin to use the SRSP to formulate an Survey Use for Consulting on Sport Practice 20 intervention. Specifically, they discussed psychoeducation and moving the dialogue towards the development of psychological proficiencies according to four subthemes: i) starting a discussion by framing SRSP statements/scores for a client; ii) using the survey as a resource at intake, then corroborating; iii) enlarging the survey items in joint discussion; and iii) using survey scores as metrics for the client and for oneself.

Starting a Discussion by Framing SRSP Statements or Scores for a Client.

All of the MPCs explained that it would be critical to tie the SRSP to discussion. For the initial skeptics of surveys, like Jose, "the SRSP is foremost a discussion tool"-they demanded this to justify using the survey. Emily Myriam noted that surveys can be tricky with adolescents because of short attention spans, poor comprehension, and teens feeling they are being tested. By framing the SRSP survey content within a discussion, Myriam could build rapport and confidence in connecting her interview/narrative intake approach to subsequent facets of intervention.

Charlotte commented that MPCs generally need more methods to lead young athletes to find their voice during consulting sessions. She explained:

The individual is most important, not the numbers on that survey. In observing how they're responding, can we create spaces [as MPCs] where the athlete answering the SRSP question asks questions? Can we create a space where they feel comfortable in elaborating and asking, 'well, I think this is fine…but…'. Like, creating a dialogue that opens that up?

The MPCs felt that they could frame discussion to engage the athlete's voice by leading the client to 'question the questions'. Jose said, "I'm less interested in actual scores and more interested in discussing afterwards why these questions are asked and what they mean for the athlete." These same MPCs who liked numbers also described how they could use an athlete's repeated SRSP scores to monitor the direction of their intervention and to gather evidence for whether the client is improving on certain proficiencies. Charlotte said, "The SRSP scores would let me see the impact of my work, to monitor if I made a difference." For Lucia, the scores could help her "get to the bottom of whether these skills are being learned and applied by the athlete." Emily and Ronda noted that trending toward higher scores over time could provide evidence to validate their work as MPCs. Picking out improvements on scores could be important for validating an intervention, however, Ronda made a final comment so as not to overstate this point---"qualitative learning happens even when an athlete gives the same score over time", which is something to which MPCs should be attuned.

Discussion

Our overriding purpose was to explore MPCs' initial perspectives about prospective use of the SRSP survey among competitive adolescent athletes. In our sample of Canadian MPCs who work on the topic of psychology of practice, most of the responses were favorable. Most interviewees were open to considering the survey as a resource or, at least, adopting facets of the survey that they could tailor to their personal approaches. There was, however, an initial tension where five MPCs were reticent to integrate the survey into quantitative assessment practices, which appeared to stem from broader perspectives on survey administration, and which initially constrained what they thought they could gain from the SRSP.

Views on Assessment with Quantitative Surveys in Applied Practice

The term 'assessment' pertaining to quantitative surveys appeared to be attached to negative preconceptions for these five MPCs. For this group, assessment could be narrative and dialogue-based, Survey Use for Consulting on Sport Practice 27 where they wished to rely on their own generation of questions and personal checklists of queries to engage a client in qualitative description during case conceptualization. For them, assessment with quantitative surveys was associated with testing, with clinical insinuations involving diagnostics that seemed laborious, or with a psychometric world that was somewhat foreign or not applicable to them. The impertinence of quantitative surveys, including the SRSP, appeared related to their professional consulting philosophy, which is a critical factor guiding practitioners' selection of assessment methods [37]. This camp of MPCs never did explicitly claim that they would use the SRSP. However, we observed that they did, throughout the course of the interview, describe conditions under which this survey could be used. They articulated circumstances in which the SRSP could be employed, for example, when they had already used dialogue to ascertain that there are trying situations compromising an athlete's ability to train independently. They also described instances where the SRSP could complement their narratives, for example, by lifting specific statements from the survey (not the whole survey or not even entire scales) and plugging them into ongoing dialogue with a client. These applications/derivatives of use contrasted with their initial comments, which seemed oriented broadly against quantitative surveys. Moreover, using surveys as derivatives-in essence, 'plugging and playing' items into a consulting narrative represented a personalized and non-traditional way of using quantitative surveys.

Considering there were other MPCs who wished to use quantitative surveys as they were designed, as multifactorial assessment tools, one of our notable interpretations is that assessment is not a monolithic practice or term. Our results showed that it can be complex, situated within conversational narratives, leaning on interpretive extensions by the MPC in concert with an athlete. We interpreted that survey use is not necessarily contrary to narrative approaches. The idea that surveys can facilitate dialogue is not novel; however, the notion that many MPCs wished to embed items and scales within a conversation, as part of a think-aloud to ascertain metacognitive/motivational information from a client, is different than a more sequenced 'assess first', then 'analyze and discuss second' approach. This approach blends dialogue and survey items in exchanges between an MPC and a client with the goal of 'assessment as learning' rather than 'assessment of learning'. Overall, we suggest survey use could be beneficial and Survey Use for Consulting on Sport Practice 28 innovative, perhaps for many more consultants, were a less stringent psychometric approach ascribed to assessment. Such an approach implicates MPCs using surveys in personalized, integral, and reasoned ways. Notably, several of our seasoned MPCs expressed that they believed they could work through the SRSP content by relying on their prerogatives and own expertise to extend into dialogue. This is an important observation because expert MPCs like to rely on their accumulated experience in their own work to guide aspects of service delivery [38]. Based on many of our MPCs' responses, the SRSP appears to provide a foundation which is not constraining, from which experienced MPCs believe they can adequately enlarge dialogue based on their personal expertise.

When quantitative surveys are construed by MPCs in monolithic terms (e.g., exclusively psychometric diagnosis), the prospective uses of survey content may be significantly diminished or dismissed. Moreover, survey use cannot be based on anecdotal intuitions in the absence of evidence-based methods. Our results are important because they offer a preliminary, prospective evidence base for different ways of using surveys, apart from preconceived/stereotyped notions related to testing and clinical diagnosis. This evidence permits MPCs to think of client-centered ways to integrate a survey into consulting, strategically, to prompt qualitative needs analysis. The evidence invites a person-centric approach in which survey use is personalized by the MPC and tailored to the client's needs. These findings may therefore be insightful for MPCs who have been trained in recent decades to develop more client-led, flexible, values-oriented consulting accorded to humanistic philosophies [e.g., 38,39,40], for whom a client's needs analysis is predicated upon "a range of methods" [41, p. 506), featuring "a qualitative type of description" [p. 507], but commonly omitting surveys.

For those in our camp of Canadian MPCs who preferred quantitative survey use, the scores mattered. They mattered for potentially providing metrics that could support tracking within an unfolding trajectory of consulting. Scores also mattered for prompting dialogue during needs analyses and gathering information for case conceptualization. With respect to this latter facet, the prospective goal of these MPCs was not to use surveys to simply obtain scores/metrics, rather to ensure rich dialogue based on scores/metrics. Thus, our evidence shows the use of scores within applied consulting is very different than Survey Use for Consulting on Sport Practice 29 positivist conceptions of scores, which are concerned with minimizing threats to the objective reality of data [e.g., 42].

Initial Findings Contributing to the Prospective Practical Validity of The SRSP Survey

Sport psychology scholars have lamented a research-to-practice gap [43], reflecting the disconnect between what evidenced-based empirical work provides and how practitioners can actually use such evidence in real-world practice [44]. This disconnect is attributed to many factors, including the inability to reconcile the needs/observations of practitioners with the theoretical/measurement concerns of academics, the lack of input from practitioners into academic researchers' designs, a predominant emphasis in academic research on internal validity, failure of researchers to produce works that are intended for the practitioner, and the under-reporting or over-complicating of implementation recommendations [43, 44]. Winter et al. [41] interviewed British sport psychologists about the processes of evidence-informed decision making around methods in applied sport psychology. The practitioners expressed that the sport psychology literature commonly lacks the types of research that are most useful in aiding their applied decision-making processes and therefore, they are not inclined to consider methods arising from this work. Because published quantitative surveys typically originate in an academic setting with goals for testing, practitioners have concerns that these instruments neglect how the tools may be used in real-world practice where goals are different.

The SRSP was designed from academic work, for empirical purposes, that did not originate with practitioners [19]. Much of the early research leading to the SRSP survey [e.g., 24,26,27] was concerned with measurement/psychometrics and/or testing whether self-report scores could reliably discriminate more-elite groups of athletes from less-elite groups. This academic research was successful in determining an elite-advantage around SRSP scores, leading to the conclusion that better training habits and enhanced development of elite athletes could be attributed to their strategic engagement in constituent SRSP proficiencies [18]. However, it remains uncertain whether the SRSP survey could be of utility to MPCs in supporting the development of individual athletes, and specifically in a dyadic consulting context.

Survey Use for Consulting on Sport Practice 30

The findings show initial evidence supporting the prospective use of the SRSP among professional MPCs. Our results show that Canadian MPCs who have an interest in working on psychological proficiencies to enhance practice are open to using the SRSP survey and see potential value in its use. The exception is consultants who have philosophical reservations of using any type of quantitative survey. Furthermore, we found that most of the Canadian MPCs believed that the survey could benefit them in early phases of case conceptualization with a client, which involves assessment and analysis, interpretation of strengths and weaknesses, and enlargement to understand an athlete's needs around training, and various scenarios and contextual features. The MPCs' responses suggested that use of the SRSP could vary from traditional quantitative assessment with psychometric underpinnings to more innovative, exploratory narrative inquiry, all personalized to an MPC's consulting philosophy. Overall, the findings are an initial step that establishes the prospective practical validity of the SRSP survey.

The current findings are an extension of the perspectives of Canadian MPCs offered by Young et al. [33]. In that article, researchers noted that consultants were aware of the topic of SRL and could identity with it in their practice (i.e., the 'what' of psychology of practice). The MPCs also described the merits of adolescent athletes being self-regulated learners in terms of quality practice (the 'why' of psychology of practice). That article tendered self-regulated sport practice as a worthwhile and attractive area of consultancy that MPCs were interested in targeting with adolescents -a form of practical validity.

The current findings add to this evidence by locating the SRSP survey as a prospective instrumental asset for some MPCs.

Our findings align with a case study in which a Polish MPC used a SRSP survey to engage a client in a reflective exercise on the development of practice habits [16]. By asking an international-level athlete to complete the survey while reflecting on their habits from five years ago, then redoing the same exercise asking them to reflect on their habits today, the MPC opened up a narrative on growth whereby she could reinforce metacognitive strengths for the athlete. To our knowledge, this is the lone study that animates a specific exercise leveraging the SRSP survey in consulting. The current findings further contribute to dialogue supporting the SRSP among practitioners. One of our key research questions was 'what information do MPCs believe they can gain by giving the SRSP to clients?'.

We interpret a two-fold answer. First, the MPCs who were inclined to use the SRSP said they could gain information benefitting their role in case conceptualization. Secondly, they could describe ways in which they might begin to act on information drawn from survey-related discussion as they move towards analyzing and developing an athlete's proficiencies. On the latter point, however, more detail on specific actionable steps, prescription of psychological skills, and structured aspects of intervention that follow from SRSP-narratives is needed to elaborate on practical utility. Moreover, moving beyond prospective to authentic practical validity requires research involving actual use of an athlete's SRSP responses, exploration of whether these responses reflect the athlete's own understandings, and whether and how an MPC can interpret, analyze and act on responses effectively with that athlete. It is possible that the mock exercise may have encouraged participants to imagine idealized responses rather than the difficulties that arise when working with real clients; as a result, the current accounts may be more optimistic than practitioners' actual behavior when employing the survey in an authentic applied setting. Still, we believe that the current findings are a first step in starting a conversation to 'narrow the gap' between the theoretical/measurement works on the SRSP and its implementation in the field.

Discerning Autonomy Support, Perceived Autonomy and Self-Regulated Sport Practice Proficiencies

Another of our key questions inquired about MPCs' perceptions of the psychological content being assessed by the SRSP. In their descriptions, they recognized metacognitive, motivational and SRL content, which is faithful with the conceptual background of the survey [19]. More broadly, the MPCs' interpretations of content suggest that the SRSP could be useful for discussion of triadic interactions between: environmental constraints on self-directedness; an athlete's qualities of ownership, feelings of independence and self-leadership; and an athlete's capability to proficiently self-manage aspects of their training.

Autonomy is a ubiquitous term within sport psychology literature. Certainly, autonomy is an integral psychological need for adolescent athletes supported by vast research within self-determination theory [see 45]. However, the sport psychology literature sometimes espouses autonomy with little Survey Use for Consulting on Sport Practice 32 discernment for the environment, the person, and the psychological properties that the athlete needs to 'step into' autonomy. Our finding that many MPCs wish to use the SRSP to figure out whether a client has opportunities to self-direct within their training environment is connected to the notion that different sport cultures (e.g., gymnastics versus canoe/kayak) and different coaching styles (a very coach-directed/coachcontrolled style versus an "autonomy-supportive" style) [46] may constrain/open opportunities for selfregulated sport practice learning and application. For example, Radowits and Young [47] found variability in how adolescent sport coaches afforded "time and space" for prompting and reinforcing adolescent athletes' experimentation with SRL around training. These affordances are important and intersect with an athlete's perceptions of autonomy in various times or spaces where they might exercise self-regulated sport practice. The finding that some of our MPCs would use the SRSP to figure out the extent to which an athlete felt a sense of ownership and cognitive engagement in their own learning can be inserted in this facet. Finally, even when an environment affords opportunities to exercise autonomy, and even when an athlete perceives autonomy, it is not guaranteed that they will act skillfully. Thus, we submit that selfregulated sport practice proficiencies may be the requisite psychological properties that equip young athletes to act skillfully in an autonomy-supported environment when they feel they can exercise autonomy. These properties are necessary because affordances and perceptions are not sufficient to optimize skill acquisition for sport talent development. Young people are expected to vary in how skillfully they apply such properties and, as clients, they may benefit by gaining these proficiencies as outcomes of future applied interventions. The current study is limited in that it neither operationalizes the nature of these proficiencies nor the specific actionable strategies that support their development. These should be aims of future work in SRSP-related research.

Limitations and Future Research

In line with pragmatism, we present this article as an overture to inquiry on the psychology of practice, involving relevant practitioners, and tender the findings as provisional knowledge that should be subject to future revision by other researchers [30]. Findings are limited to a Canadian context, to a selfselected sample of MPCs that was restricted to practitioners already interested in consulting on practice Survey Use for Consulting on Sport Practice 33 enhancement. This was appropriate for eliciting informed views, but it also means that we cannot provide an account of practitioner receptivity across the wider profession. These MPCs participated in Young et al. [33], which meant they had considered the concept of SRL in an earlier part of their lengthy interview before being asked about the SRSP, which could have resulted in more coherent responses. Whereas we prompted the consideration of clients via a mock SRSP, future research should include authentic, inperson interactions with clients. Research is also needed to examine what real athlete clients think about the survey and what they believe they need from MPCs in SRSP-related protocol.

The current study looked at initial and global perspectives on the SRSP survey. Future studies are required to understand how MPCs plan to support and engage a client in meaning-making for each of the five constituent scales of the SRSP, how these meanings relate to experiences/scenarios in sport training, and how the MPCs would specifically intervene to support psychological skill development. The current article presented findings limited to early phases of case conceptualization and client analysis (hence the importance of enlargement) and therefore did not fully consider other aspects of dyadic consulting. For instance, case conceptualization ultimately directs subsequent selection stages of sport psychology consulting, including prescriptions/actions supporting psychological skill training [1]. Moreover, case conceptualization is an iterative process whereby the profile of a client and initial working hypotheses for intervention are constantly updated as an intervention unfolds [1, 48]. How the SRSP survey and SRSPrelated narratives interact with such considerations will be important to address in future research. Finally, some of our MPCs mentioned surveys they already use in response to issues around practice, such as the OMSAT-3 [20], TOPS-2 [21], and PCDEQ [49]. It would be beneficial to explore what Wilson et al.'s [19] SRSP survey offers uniquely and complementarily, to practitioners, relative to these established resources.
