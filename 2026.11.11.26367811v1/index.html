An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). Statcheck might still miss one-sided tests that are described in another sentence, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. Abstract

Accurate segmentation of skin layers in histopathological whole-slide images (WSIs) is important for identifying disease-associated structural changes and supporting computer-aided dermatopathological analysis. However, existing approaches are often restricted to single magnification levels and may inadequately capture the heterogeneous morphological characteristics of individual skin layers. We propose DermNet, a multi-resolution class-incremental learning framework designed to achieve accurate skin layer segmentation while mitigating catastrophic forgetting.

DermNet employs a SegFormer backbone within a teacher-student architecture and progressively learns class-specific features from WSIs acquired at 10×, 5×, and 2× magnifications. The framework incorporates exemplar replay, mutual knowledge distillation, classification and Kullback-Leibler divergence losses, boundary loss, and Elastic Weight Consolidation to preserve previously learned representations while incorporating new tissue classes. The model was trained and evaluated using a publicly available dataset of 290 WSIs, with segmentation focused on the epidermis, papillary dermis, reticular dermis, hypodermis, keratin, and background.

DermNet consistently outperformed U-Net and alternative SegFormer training strategies across all three magnification levels. At 10× magnification, DermNet achieved Dice scores of 0.95 for epidermis, 0.92 for papillary dermis, 0.94 for reticular dermis, 0.97 for keratin, and 0.98 for hypodermis, outperforming UNet++, DeepLabv3+, TransUNet, SwinUNet, and nnU-Net under the same experimental conditions. Evaluation on an independent publicly available dataset further demonstrated cross-dataset generalizability, with Dice scores ranging from 0.89 to 0.97 across classes.

DermNet enables accurate and robust multi-resolution skin layer segmentation by combining classincremental learning with knowledge distillation and mechanisms for preserving previously learned features. Its performance across magnification levels and an independent dataset supports its potential for scalable and generalizable automated dermatopathological image analysis.

Materials and Methods

Dataset

The dataset utilized in this study is a publicly available repository comprising 290 WSI samples, including 100 shave biopsies, 58 punch biopsies, and 132 excisional biopsies. The histological slides were obtained from patients aged 34-96 years, with a median age of 70 years. Females constituted approximately twothirds of the cohort, while males comprised approximately one-third. Images were captured using a brightfield microscope at three magnification levels: 10×, 5×, and 2×. Each sample was associated with a ground-truth image labeled for 12 classes, including background (BKG); skin layers comprising epidermis (EPI), hypodermis (HYP), papillary dermis (PAP), and reticular dermis (RET); other tissue and structural features including follicle (FOL), keratin (KER), and inflammation (INF); and carcinomas comprising BCC, intraepidermal carcinoma (IEC), and SCC. For the present study, the ground-truth annotations were further modified to retain only the classes relevant to skin layer segmentation: epidermis, papillary dermis, reticular dermis, hypodermis, and keratin, while the remaining regions were assigned to the background class. In incremental learning, to avoid catastrophic forgetting, it is essential to replay some exemplar instances of classes learned in the previous stage S i -1 to be replayed in the current stage S i . As illustrated in Figure 3, for the first stage we trained the model on 50% of instances of epidermis from the dataset. In the second stage, to train the student model, we utilized 50% samples of the hypodermis (papillary dermis, reticular dermis) along with 25% instances of epidermis to prevent catastrophic forgetting. Finally, in the third stage, 100% samples of keratin and hypodermis, along with 50% samples of papillary and reticular dermis and 25% samples of epidermis were utilized in model training. This exemplar replay strategy reinforces previously learned class representations while progressively incorporating new classes, thereby mitigating catastrophic forgetting. Data from all three magnification levels (10×, 5×, and 2×) were incorporated across the incremental training stages to facilitate multiresolution feature learning from coarse to fine scales. To address the challenge of the high-resolution dataset, where each image is comprised of approximately 100,000 pixels, we have adopted a patch-based approach. Before model training, the dataset was pre-processed into non-overlapping 256x256 patches. This method has helped us reduce memory consumption and computation time. Furthermore, the patch-based technique has also allowed the model to focus on specific regions, allowing it to gather contextual features more precisely thereby improving overall segmentation performance. Methodology

Instead of training the entire model in a single stage, we propose a multi-staged training process via a student-teacher model. This technique enables concentrated and systematic learning of the various skin layers marked in the ground truth, allowing the model to adequately represent the intricacies of each layer. Note that the dataset consists of WSIs at different magnification levels and hence during training, all magnifications should also be incorporated into the model. This multi-resolution technique allows the model to use both the wider contextual information from lower magnifications and the finer details from higher magnifications, resulting in thorough and accurate segmentation across all skin layers.

Experimentation and Results

Experimental Setup

The proposed framework was implemented using Python 3.11.4 and PyTorch and trained on an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 2070 GPU. For Stage I, the teacher model employed a SegFormer backbone pretrained on the ADE20K dataset. The models were trained using the Adam optimizer with a batch size of 9 for 6 X 10 -5 learning rate. The teacher model was trained for 100 epochs, whereas the student models were trained for 50 epochs at each subsequent incremental stage. Data augmentation involving variations in image scale and orientation was applied to reduce overfitting and improve robustness. The dataset was divided into 70% training and 30% testing subsets.

Ablation Study

To investigate the contribution of the proposed incremental-learning strategy, three training configurations were evaluated. Model-I consisted of a SegFormer model trained using conventional transfer learning and fine-tuning. Model-II (FL) incorporated incremental learning with selective freezing and fine-tuning of the SegFormer backbone. Model-III (DermNet) represented the complete proposed incremental-learning framework. For Model-II (FL), layer freezing was progressively applied to balance retention of previously learned features with adaptation to newly introduced classes. During Stage I, the complete SegFormer encoder and decoder were trained on the initial dataset. During Stage II, the early encoder layers were frozen to retain lower-level representations, while later encoder layers responsible for higher-level semantic information were fine-tuned and the decoder remained fully trainable. During Stage III, both early and intermediate encoder layers were frozen, whereas the later encoder layers and decoder continued to be trained. In contrast, DermNet retained all model layers as trainable throughout the incremental stages, while the proposed distillation and regularization strategy was used to preserve previously acquired information. This design allowed both lower-and higher-level feature representations to continue adapting as new tissue classes were introduced.

Comparison with the Baseline Model

The first set of experiments compared DermNet with a U-Net-based skin segmentation model, the conventionally fine-tuned SegFormer (Model-I), and the incrementally trained SegFormer with selective layer freezing (Model-II [FL]). Class-wise segmentation performance was evaluated at 10×, 5×, and 2× magnifications.

As presented in Table 2, DermNet achieved the strongest overall class-wise segmentation performance across the three magnification levels. Model-I showed comparatively lower performance for classes with smaller pixel representation, particularly EPI and PAP. Model-II (FL) improved upon Model-I across the evaluated classes and magnifications, indicating better preservation of previously acquired representations during sequential training. However, DermNet further improved segmentation of challenging tissue classes, including EPI, PAP, keratin (KER), and reticular dermis (RET), while maintaining performance across different magnification levels.

DermNet was subsequently compared with several established segmentation architectures, including UNet++, DeepLabv3+, TransUNet, SwinUNet, and nnU-Net, with all models trained and evaluated under the same experimental conditions on the Queensland dataset at 10× magnification [13] [14] [15] [16] [17] . As shown in Table 3, DermNet achieved the highest Dice score across all six segmentation classes: 0.98 for BKG, 0.95 for EPI, 0.92 for PAP, 0.94 for RET, 0.97 for KER, and 0.98 for HYP. Among the comparator models, TransUNet achieved the next-highest scores for EPI and PAP at 0.88 and 0.85, respectively, whereas SwinUNet achieved 0.87 and 0.84 and nnU-Net achieved 0.86 and 0.83 for these classes. These results demonstrate consistently higher segmentation performance for DermNet across both larger and more structurally challenging tissue compartments.

To determine whether the observed differences were statistically significant, pairwise Wilcoxon signedrank tests were performed between DermNet and the competing segmentation models. As illustrated in Figure 6, all comparisons yielded p values below the predefined significance threshold of 0.05, indicating statistically significant differences in performance between DermNet and the evaluated comparator models. Comparison with different loss functions

We conducted an empirical study by employing various combinations of the loss functions to evaluate the model's performance. Table 4 shows how well Model-III works with various sets of loss functions, and it is proof of how such combinations can increase the segmentation ability of skin tissue layers.

From the results, it is found that the inclusion of complementary loss functions significantly boosts the model's ability to learn complex patterns and, therefore, enhances the performance in terms of accuracy.

The classification loss ( L C ) alone already establishes a baseline performance with 0.85 accuracy and a mean Intersection over Union of 0.76. While ( L C ) works well for general classification, it does not tackle the intricacies associated with tissue structure segmentation, such as the keratin layer, for which more accurate boundary delineation is required. The mutual distillation loss L md significantly improves the performance with an mIoU of 0.85 and a recall of 0.89. This loss function L md promotes the sharing of knowledge among different branches of the model, thereby enhancing the learning of more robust and complete feature representations required for discrimination between subtle differences in tissue classes. Adding the Kullback-Leibler divergence loss, ( L K L ) , fine-tunes the class probability distributions of the predictions, thus improving class discrimination and making more accurate predictions. The recall values achieve 0.92. The boundary loss ( L bound ar y ) is crucial in the definition of fine details in tissue boundaries, especially for classes that have limited pixel representation, such as the keratin layer and epidermis. The addition of ¿ increases recall to 0.93 by reducing false negatives and allowing better segmentation of neighboring or overlapping classes. By adding up ( L C + L md + L K L + L bound ar y ) , the best performance is obtained at an accuracy of 0.94, mean IoU of 0.90, and F1-score of 0.93. Altogether, these loss functions allow the model to perceive general as well as specific characteristics of the skin tissue layers. These handle problems such as class imbalance, misalignment of probabilities, and ambiguity at boundaries.

We conducted further experiments about the performance evaluation of the proposed framework with other leading incrementally learning frameworks. Specifically, the comparison was performed between two frameworks: the first evaluation with the proposed model running with L w M loss that replaces the general L K L (KL divergence) loss with the L A D (adversarial divergence) loss; and the second entry adds with the L EM (embedding distillation) loss in addition to the losses of L CE (cross-entropy) and L K D (knowledge distillation).

Table 5 shows the outcome efficiency of the models, indicating that the inclusion of L K L makes the model able to converge better than other loss functions such as L A D and L EM in the alternative frameworks. The loss L K L centers on the relative entropy or change in the probability distribution of the extracted features and enables the model to concentrate on the importance of each feature in the new learning process. This results in a stronger learning trajectory for this model as retaining important features while discarding irrelevant ones is essentially encouraged. As evident from the results of Model-III, which adopted L K L , yielded maximum accuracy (95.55%), mIoU (93.72%), and F1-score (95.42%) are obtained using all loss function combinations; thus, indicating L K L is one of the contributing factors to the model's superior performance. As evident from Table 5, L A D loss in L w M framework does provide good improvements, however, does not achieve the highest result. This is because L A D focuses on a very simple minimization of the adversarial divergence while the real task is rather about relative entropy, where the actual measure of difference comes in the definition between two feature distributions, thus making it relatively less accurate in guiding the model through the learning. Additionally, the L EM loss when profused with Model-III according to its workability fails to provide the same amount of improvement as does L K L for the present study. Although Model-II (FL), with L EM as the underlying loss function, gives stellar results in mIoU (90.50), performance metrics for Model-III using L K L leave all others behind, producing both higher mIoU and F1 score (94.83 and 96.74, respectively). Ultimately, results in Table 5 speak volumes about the importance of the proposed framework utilizing L K L loss in combination with the rest of the loss functions to achieve better performance than when including either L A D or L w M.

Evaluating DermNet on other Publicly Available Dataset

To evaluate generalizability beyond the development dataset, DermNet was tested on the independent Histo-Seg: H&E Whole Slide Image Segmentation Dataset, comprising 38 H&E-stained WSIs with corresponding histopathologist-generated ground-truth annotations.

As presented in Table 6, DermNet maintained high segmentation performance on the independent dataset, with class-wise Dice scores ranging from 0.89 for PAP to 0.97 for BKG. EPI and RET, which represent relatively thin and morphologically complex tissue regions, achieved Dice scores of 0.92 and 0.93, respectively. Corresponding IoU and recall values were also consistently high across the evaluated classes.

These results demonstrate that the segmentation performance observed on the development dataset was largely maintained when DermNet was evaluated on an independent histopathological dataset.

Discussion

In this study, we developed DermNet, a multi-resolution class-incremental learning framework for automated segmentation of skin layers in histopathological WSIs. DermNet progressively learns epidermis, papillary dermis, reticular dermis, keratin, and hypodermis while incorporating images acquired at 10×, 5×, and 2× magnifications. The proposed framework demonstrated consistently strong segmentation performance across tissue classes, outperformed conventional and state-of-the-art segmentation architectures, and maintained high performance on an independent public dataset. These findings support the utility of combining multi-resolution representation learning with incremental knowledge transfer for complex dermatopathological segmentation. Accurate delineation of skin structures is clinically relevant because the localization and distribution of pathological features across the epidermis, dermis, and deeper tissues contribute to histopathological diagnosis. Previous studies have demonstrated that segmentation of clinically meaningful structures in skin biopsy WSIs can facilitate automated tissue analysis and downstream diagnostic classification 7, 18, 19 . The publicly available dataset used in the present study provides densely annotated WSIs encompassing multiple skin compartments and non-melanoma skin cancers, creating an important resource for developing automated dermatopathology algorithms 8 . Unlike approaches primarily designed to identify tumors or individual structures, DermNet was developed to distinguish multiple anatomically related skin layers and tissue compartments within a unified segmentation framework.

A key feature of DermNet is its incorporation of multiple magnification levels. Histopathological interpretation inherently requires assessment of both global tissue architecture and fine morphological detail. Multi-resolution computational pathology approaches have similarly demonstrated that combining contextual information obtained at lower magnification with fine-grained information at higher magnification can improve semantic segmentation. For example, HookNet integrates information from different resolutions and has demonstrated advantages over single-resolution approaches in histopathological tissue segmentation 20 . More recent work has further supported multi-resolution deep learning for extracting biologically meaningful structures from H&E-stained WSIs 21 . DermNet extends this concept to skin layer segmentation by incorporating 10×, 5×, and 2× WSIs throughout incremental training, allowing the model to learn complementary tissue representations across spatial scales. Another important contribution is the application of class-incremental learning to skin layer segmentation. Conventional deep learning models are generally trained simultaneously on all available classes and may require substantial retraining when new classes or datasets are introduced. Continual learning instead enables models to acquire information sequentially, although catastrophic forgetting of previously learned representations remains a central challenge 22, 23 . DermNet addresses this problem through exemplar replay, knowledge distillation, and EWC, enabling previously learned class representations to be retained while new tissue classes are progressively introduced. The favorable FWT, BWT, and FS observed with DermNet further support the effectiveness of this strategy in balancing acquisition of new information with retention of previously learned representations.

The ablation experiments further demonstrated the contribution of the proposed optimization strategy. Classification loss alone established baseline segmentation performance, whereas sequential incorporation of mutual distillation, KL divergence, and boundary loss improved the evaluated performance metrics. These components provide complementary objectives: mutual distillation facilitates knowledge transfer between successive models, KL divergence promotes consistency between probability distributions, and boundary loss emphasizes spatial agreement along tissue interfaces. Boundary-aware optimization is particularly relevant in skin histology, where thin or morphologically adjacent structures may have complex and irregular interfaces. The superior performance of the complete loss formulation therefore suggests that combining class-level knowledge transfer with boundary-sensitive optimization contributes to accurate skin layer segmentation.

The findings are further supported by the model's external validation. DermNet maintained strong segmentation performance on the independent Histo-Seg dataset, with class-wise Dice scores ranging from 0.89 to 0.97, suggesting that the learned representations were not restricted to the development dataset. This is important because differences in tissue preparation, staining, scanning, and institutional workflows can introduce domain variability in computational pathology. Continual-learning research increasingly recognizes such distributional changes as an important challenge for the clinical translation of medical imaging models [22] [23] [24] . Although evaluation on a single independent dataset does not establish broad clinical generalizability, the preservation of performance across datasets provides preliminary evidence of the robustness of DermNet to variations in histopathological images. The explainability analyses provide additional insight into model behavior. Class-specific activation maps demonstrated spatial correspondence between areas of greater model attention and the relevant tissue compartments, while ROC and precision-recall analyses demonstrated strong class discrimination. The t-SNE visualization further demonstrated class-associated clustering of learned representations across the six segmentation classes. Although these analyses cannot independently establish clinical interpretability or reliability, they provide complementary evidence that DermNet learns class-specific histological representations rather than relying exclusively on nonspecific image characteristics. Beyond segmentation performance, DermNet may have potential applications within digital dermatopathology workflows. Automated delineation of tissue compartments could facilitate downstream quantitative analysis, disease localization, and computer-assisted diagnosis while potentially reducing the time required for manual tissue annotation. The use of multiple magnification levels also more closely reflects the multiscale assessment performed during conventional histopathological examination. However, the present findings should be considered a demonstration of technical performance rather than evidence of improved clinical workflow or diagnostic accuracy; these potential benefits require prospective evaluation. Despite these promising findings, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, DermNet was developed using a relatively limited public dataset of 290 WSIs, although the dataset provides detailed annotations across multiple tissue types and biopsy formats 8 . Second, class imbalance remains inherent to histopathological segmentation because tissue compartments occupy substantially different proportions of WSIs. Third, although external validation supports generalizability, broader evaluation across independent institutions, scanners, staining protocols, biopsy types, and disease populations is necessary before clinical implementation. Fourth, the present study evaluates segmentation performance rather than improvement in prospective diagnostic decision-making or pathologist efficiency. Last, the exemplar-replay strategy requires retention of previously encountered samples, which may introduce storage and data-governance considerations on larger clinical datasets. Future studies should therefore evaluate DermNet using larger, multicenter datasets and investigate strategies for addressing class imbalance and domain variability. Continual WSI analysis has demonstrated the feasibility of sequentially adapting models to new datasets and tasks, supporting further investigation of this approach in computational pathology [23] [24] [25] . Incorporating additional histopathological classes and imaging modalities may further improve the framework's applicability and generalizability. Prospective studies should also determine whether automated skin layer segmentation can improve downstream disease classification, quantitative biomarker assessment, diagnostic accuracy, or pathologist efficiency.

DermNet for Skin Layer Segmentation Conclusion and Future Work

The proposed DermNet framework demonstrates that class-incremental learning can enable accurate and robust segmentation of skin layers from whole slide images (WSIs). By combining mutual knowledge distillation with multi-resolution learning at 10×, 5×, and 2× magnifications, DermNet progressively learns class-specific features while preserving previously acquired knowledge and mitigating catastrophic forgetting. The combination of complementary loss functions further enhances feature representation and segmentation performance, while the ablation studies, class-wise performance metrics, activation maps, and t-SNE analysis support the robustness and reliability of the framework. DermNet also has potential applications in digital dermatopathology by facilitating automated tissue characterization, reducing diagnostic workload, and supporting more consistent analysis of WSIs. Its multi-resolution approach reflects the evaluation of tissue at different magnifications routinely performed by pathologists and may therefore facilitate integration into digital pathology workflows. Future studies should focus on addressing class imbalance, validating the framework across larger and more diverse multicenter datasets, and incorporating additional imaging modalities to further improve its generalizability and clinical applicability.
