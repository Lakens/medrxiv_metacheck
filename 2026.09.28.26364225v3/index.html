Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. Abstract

Accurate prediction of individual medical outcomes is essential for optimizing treatment allocation amid rising costs, coverage denials, and limited clinical resources. Traditional predictive models, including linear regression and neural networks, rely on average effects and cannot tailor predictions to the specific circumstances of individual cases. We present relevancebased prediction (RBP), a model-free and task-specific method that predicts outcomes as weighted averages of observed cases, with weights determined by a rigorously defined and theoretically justified measure of relevance. Unlike model-based methods that apply an estimated relationship to patient-specific characteristics to capture conditional effects, RBP revisits the original data for each prediction and measures the alignment of the relevance weights and outcomes for many combinations of cases and variables. In this way RBP explicitly considers which cases and variables contribute to each specific prediction as well as the strength of the patterns in the data that support that prediction. We illustrate RBP by applying it to predict opioid treatment outcomes and demonstrate how it provides case-specific insights that build on the insights given by conventional models, including how each historical case contributes to a specific prediction, how each variable affects each predicted outcome and the reliability of that prediction, and how reliably each outcome can be predicted before it is known. These additional insights may help to prevent misleading average-based decisions and reduce harmful or suboptimal treatment.

Introduction:

Health care expenditures are escalating worldwide. Medical costs are projected to increase globally by ~10.3% 1, 2 In the USA, healthcare spending totaled over US$5.3 trillion in 2024, averaging to US$15,474 per person, accounting for ~18.0% of GDP and rising over 7.2% from the previous year, surpassing GDP growth. 3, 4 Resources are continuing to be overstrained: hospital care, physician and clinical services, and prescription drugs together consume a significant share of that expenditure, and demand for treatments is rising. [5] [6] [7] Alongside the financial pressures, prediction of medical outcomes, such as response to treatment, risk of complications, or long-term morbidity, remains limited. Predictive performance can vary substantially across patient populations and clinical settings, and established tools may perform less well in patients with multimorbidity. 8, 9 Models that inadequately represent interactions between treatment and patient characteristics may also give misleading estimates of treatment benefit for individuals. 10 The inability to forecast which patients are most likely to benefit (or to be harmed) leads to inefficient use of interventions, over-treatment, and waste, as well as undertreatment in high-risk groups. There is a pressing need for more accurate, validated prediction models in treatment response and outcome trajectories, which would allow health systems to better direct scarce resources (e.g., costly therapies, specialist visits, hospital beds, etc.) toward patients most likely to derive benefit, improving both effectiveness and equity while restraining costs.

Despite the promise of predictive modeling in medicine, especially with machine learning, its real-world performance has often fallen short, illustrating the risks and opportunity costs of imprecise prediction. In migraine management, a recent systematic review and meta-analysis found that prediction models had a pooled area under the curve of 0.86 (95% CI, 0.67-0.95), yet most studies carried high risk of bias and substantial heterogeneity, limiting confidence in clinical deployment. 11 More broadly, clinical risk models often suffer from instability and miscalibration when applied to new populations. One analysis demonstrated that many models' predictions vary greatly when retrained on bootstrapped subsets, thus undermining robust performance in external settings. 12 Furthermore, embedding predictive models into scarceresource allocation frameworks (e.g., for organ transplantation) has in some cases exacerbated inequities, because biases in the training data propagate into unfair prioritization across demographic groups. 13, 14 On the other hand, novel predictive strategies applied to resource planning (e.g., dynamic model predictive control tools to allocate interventions iteratively) demonstrate potential in simulation and early implementation, enabling responsive and optimized deployment of limited capacity. 15, 16 Better predictive models could enable health systems to direct high-cost therapies, advanced diagnostics, and vigilant monitoring toward patients with the greatest likelihood of benefit, thereby maximizing outcomes under constrained budgets.

A critical obstacle to effective prediction of medical outcomes is the limited transparency of model-based approaches to prediction. Models are mathematical prediction rules that contain parameters whose values must be estimated from a training sample. After the parameter values are computed the model makes predictions based on these values and no longer requires the data.

The data is typically discarded and is not available to a practitioner who relies on the model's predictions for guidance. Moreover, a model's parameter values are chosen to minimize the average prediction error across the training sample. It is difficult, if not impossible, for a practitioner who can only observe parameter values and associated statistical tests to ascertain the prevalence and characteristics of prior cases that inform a specific prediction. For example, a prediction might be influenced heavily by a prior case for which there is a confounding condition that is not captured by the model. If one is made aware of this case-specific dependence, he or she may be inclined to investigate this case, decide whether to remove it or retain it in the case sample, and judge confidence in the prediction accordingly.

Additionally, most model interpretability methods focus on the average influence of predictive variables on the reliability and magnitude of a prediction. A regression model's p-value estimates how a predictive variable affects the average reliability across all predictions, and its β coefficients reflect average impact on the prediction value across a wide range of predictions.

Moreover, these metrics measure marginal effects, which could be distorted by collinearity. The SHAP method, which is commonly used to measure variable importance in machine learning models, does measure total effects rather than marginal effects, but its assessment of a variable's contribution to the reliability of a prediction is based on the model's parameters. Therefore, it cannot evaluate how the variables impact the reliability of specific predictions in the context of how many observations informed that prediction from the training sample. 17 Model-based prediction may fail to detect that a variable, though having a positive influence on average, could be harmful to specific patients. Finally, model-based summary statistics reveal the average reliability of predictions. For example, a linear regression's R-squared measures the average predictive efficacy of the model. It is based on some good predictions, some mediocre predictions, and some bad predictions. It does not necessarily reveal much about the trustworthiness of a specific prediction. Likewise, the mean squared errors (MSE) of a machine learning model's predictions for a full or partial sample of training data reflects an average over many circumstances rather than an assessment tailored to one specific prediction. In these cases, the reliability of a specific prediction is most directly revealed after one observes the outcome. We describe for the first time within a medical context an alternative prediction method called relevance-based prediction (RBP), which has previously been introduced to finance and sports analytics. [18] [19] [20] RBP gives full visibility into the formation of each prediction, and it gives advance guidance of each prediction's reliability. We show in the appendix that fit is an exact decomposition of R-Squared in linear regression. The enhanced visibility that RBP gives into the formation of individual patient predictions may guide a caregiver to choose a different treatment plan for a patient whose predicted outcome is known in advance to be untrustworthy compared to a patient whose outcome can be confidently predicted.

Relevance

Relevance measures the importance of a previously observed case to the current prediction task based on a set of variables that describe both circumstances. Relevance is composed of similarity and informativeness, which are both measured using a statistic called the Mahalanobis distance (MD). 26 The MD is an elegant measure of the distance between values of a set of variables that accounts not only for how the corresponding values compare independently but how they interact with each other. Similarity measures the multivariate nearness of cases to each other.

Informativeness measures the multivariate distance of cases from average and therefore the unusualness of cases. An important tenet of information theory is that unusual cases warrant close attention because they contain more information than typical cases. 27 The bottom line is that cases that are like the current case but different from the average of all cases are more relevant than those that are not.

If there were no relevance values, we would be limited to forming a prediction as an equally weighted average of the outcomes of prior cases which yields a static prediction that does not explain any variation in outcomes. With relevance, however, we can increase or decrease the weight of each prior outcome according to its relevance. These predictions account for the unique circumstances of each prediction task. It is important to note that RBP converges to linear regression under special circumstances. When relevance weights are applied to every case in a sample using the full set of predictive variables, the weighted average of outcomes gives exactly the same prediction as linear regression. 18 From this equivalence, it is clear to see that the commonly used approach of linear regression exhibits both desirable and undesirable behaviors.

It places large positive weights on relevant cases, implying that similar outcomes may occur, which makes sense. However, it places large negative weights on the least relevant cases, reasoning that the opposite of the most opposite experiences will occur. Predicting the oppositeof-opposites only works for relationships that are stable and symmetric, which does not always hold. RBP is based on the premise that we can form a better prediction by focusing on the most informative and reliable subsamples of relevant cases. Fit

Before the subsequent outcome is known, fit measures the extent to which there are useful patterns in the dataset to support a given prediction task. To the extent there is stronger alignment between the prediction weights and the outcomes, we should expect a more reliable prediction. Although other methods may provide advance guidance about the quality of individual predictions, they often rely on model-based estimates of uncertainty. RBP assesses reliability based on the empirical relationships that are relevant to each specific prediction. The classical R-squared statistic provides information about a model's quality, but it is a grand average. By contrast, fit gives advance notice of the quality of each individual prediction, distinct from the next. It is analogous to an R-squared for an individual prediction. In fact, we have proven that fit is a mathematically exact decomposition of R-squared in the case of linear regression. We give a proof of this result in the appendix.

To see how fit is computed, consider a pair of cases that is used to form a prediction. Each case has a relevance weight and an outcome. We are interested in the alignment of the weights of the two cases with their outcomes. We first standardize them by subtracting the average value and dividing this difference by standard deviation -in essence, converting them to z-scores. We then measure their alignment by taking the product of these standardized values. If the product is positive, their relevance is aligned with their outcomes, and the larger the product, the stronger the alignment. We perform this calculation for every pair of cases in our sample. Grid Prediction

Thus far, we have implicitly assumed that we always use the same set of predictive variables and only consider different subsamples of relevant cases. But it is unwise to assume that a fixed set of predictive variables will be equally effective across all subsamples of cases. And it is equally unwise to assume a fixed subsample of cases will yield the best results irrespective of the chosen predictive variables. Instead, we must recognize that the choice of cases and the choice of predictive variables are codependent on the specific circumstances of each prediction task.

This codependence is best illustrated by a grid in which the columns represent different combinations of predictive variables, and the rows represent subsamples of cases determined by different relevance thresholds. Each cell contains a prediction and its reliability weight. Grid prediction forms a composite prediction as a reliability-weighted average of the predictions from all possible calibrations. Equation 14 defines reliability weights, 3 , as the adjusted fit for a parameter calibration, 4, divided by the sum of all adjusted fits across all parameter calibrations.

We form a composite prediction by taking a weighted average of a representative set of cells in which the weights are based on the adjusted fits of the predictions, as shown by equation 15. By forming a composite prediction, we guard against data errors or overreliance on potentially tenuous information.

An exhaustive search of the grid could be computationally infeasible, which is why we sample the grid to form a composite prediction that is both robust and efficient to compute.

Alternatively, we could search the grid and use the cell with the highest fit to form our prediction. This approach, however, concentrates the prediction on a single calibration which may include an important data error, or which may give a very different prediction from another cell whose fit is nearly as high. In any event, by diversifying the composite prediction across a reasonable number of cells, we are less vulnerable to data errors or spurious relationships because many specific problems will be confined to a limited number of grid cells.

It is important to note that the prediction grid allows us to filter observations based on both relevance, which contains similarity and informativeness, as wells as similarity by itself.

Although information theory shows that unusual observations contain more information than common observations, it is sometimes the case that extrapolation from unusual observations is not warranted. In such instances it may be preferable to filter by similarity. Fortunately, we need not worry which filter to apply. We rely on fit to make this determination. That said, we always form the prediction weights based on relevance.

We also wish to note that the relevance thresholds, variable combinations, and reliability weights are determined independently of prediction outcomes. RBP is principle based as opposed to a model whose calibration requires empirical validation.

Before proceeding to our illustration of RBP, we would like to further motivate its application by reviewing its theoretical justification and by comparing its transparency to several alternative prediction methods. 19 As we have shown, RBP forms a prediction as a weighted average of observed outcomes with weights determined by a precisely defined statistic called relevance. The theoretical motivation of relevance draws on information theory, the Central Limit Theorem, and the Mahalanobis distance. Information theory holds that the information contained in an observation is the negative logarithm of its probability, implying that unusual observations contain more information than common observations. The Central Limit Theorem provides motivation for using a multivariate normal distribution to characterize data for the purpose of initially determining relevance. The normal distribution provides a natural prior because it is the maximum entropy distribution for a given mean and covariance matrix. It therefore represents the broadest distribution of uncertainty consistent with these constraints without imposing additional assumptions. And setting aside the constants, the negative logarithm of the multivariate normal density equals one-half the squared Mahalanobis distance. This relationship provides the theoretical justification for incorporating informativeness into relevance. That relevance gives a principled way to form prediction weights is further justified by the following mathematical equivalences. When RBP is implemented using the full sample of observations, it produces the same prediction as linear regression. And under the Gauss-Markov assumptions, ordinary least squares provides the best unbiased estimator. And as we have mentioned, fit, which depends on relevance and guides the selection of observations and variables, is an exact decomposition of R-squared in linear regression. Having reviewed the theoretical foundations of RBP, we now compare it to several alternative prediction methods to further clarify RBP's distinctive features.

RBP versus Neural Networks

A neural network uses large numbers of parameters to model nonlinear relationships through sequences of linear combinations and nonlinear transformations. They are highly flexible and capable of identifying complex relationships in large datasets. However, their specification and structure are determined empirically rather than deduced from theory or first principles. As a result, they are susceptible both to overfitting spurious relationships and to underfitting important but rare relationships.

RBP is a principle-based prediction routine as opposed to an empirically determined model.

RBP forms new prediction weights for each task whereas a neural network uses the same parameters to address all prediction tasks. Therefore, RBP only needs to address the conditionalities relevant to a single prediction circumstance. This feature gives RBP the power to capture the same nonlinear and conditional relationships as a neural network in most cases.

Transparency is RBP's most distinctive feature compared to neural networks. Neural networks are often characterized as black boxes because they provide little insight into how observations or variables influence predictions. RBP, by contrast, explicitly reveals how each observation contributes to each prediction and how each variable contributes to the value and reliability of each prediction.

Also, neural networks do not give advance notice of a specific prediction's reliability. This information is known only after we observe the outcomes. By comparison, RBP uses fit to assess each prediction's reliability beforehand, and it also reveals in advance the distribution of solo predictions which shows whether the underlying observations provide mutually reinforcing or contradictory information.

RBP versus Gaussian Kernels and k-Nearest Neighbors

A Gaussian kernel forms predictions as weighted averages of nearby observations, in which the weights decline smoothly according to a Gaussian distribution the further they are from the prediction circumstances. The weights that are used to form a prediction depend on the Euclidean distance and the width of the Gaussian distribution.

Although RBP shares the intuition that similar observations should matter more than dissimilar observations, it differs in several important respects. First, RBP measures similarity using the Mahalanobis distance rather than the Euclidean distance, allowing it to account for covariation among variables. Second, RBP incorporates informativeness in addition to similarity. Third, RBP adapts the relevance thresholds to each prediction task, using fit and the prediction grid for each specific prediction task, rather than applying one fixed weighting scheme to all prediction circumstances.

Gaussian kernels provide some transparency because they reveal the relative influence of observations. However, they do not evaluate reliability or variable importance, and they rely on a single calibration for all predictions, such as a fixed width of the Gaussian distribution. RBP's grid prediction framework allows both observation weights and variable combinations to vary across prediction tasks, which improve flexibility and interpretability.

The related algorithm k-nearest neighbors predicts outcomes by averaging a fixed number of nearby observations and weighting them equally. Relative to both Gaussian kernels and k-nearest neighbors, RBP is both more rigorous and transparent.

RBP versus XGBoost

XGBoost is a gradient boosting algorithm that builds an ensemble of decision trees sequentially.

Each new tree attempts to correct the residual errors made by previous trees, and the final prediction is the sum of the trees' outputs. XGBoost is highly flexible and effective at capturing nonlinear relationships. XGBoost relies primarily on empirical calibration rather than theoretical structure. Its parameter values are optimized according to average performance across training data, thereby rendering it vulnerable to spurious relationships when future conditions differ from those represented in the training sample.

RBP addresses complex conditional relationships differently. Instead of fitting a global model to all tasks, it evaluates a vast number of combinations of observations and variables for each specific prediction. This task-specific approach allows RBP to adapt to varying prediction circumstances while remaining transparent. XGBoost can quantify how predictive variables influence individual predictions using methods such as SHAP, but it does not inherently reveal how each training observation contributes to a prediction or how each variable affects its reliability. Moreover, conventional XGBoost predictions do not inherently provide a predictionspecific measure of reliability. RBP, by contrast, explicitly reveals how the observations and variables influence each prediction, and it gives an estimate of each prediction's reliability based on the fit associated with the predictions from a vast number of combinations of observations and predictive variables.

Data Source and Availability

Data were provided by Celéri Health, Inc. to Cambridge Prediction Analytics under a data use agreement (DUA). The analytic extract was limited to opioid initiation episodes and comprised 119,966 follow-up assessments across 16,920 episodes in 13,641 patients from 36 states. We selected the 180-260 day window to obtain a reasonably consistent measure of treatment outcomes approximately six to eight months after treatment. Assessments conducted earlier may not fully capture the treatment effect, while assessments conducted substantially later may be more susceptible to intervening changes in patient health, additional treatments, or other confounding factors. Lastly, we removed any observations that represent a second or subsequent treatment for each patient. The final analytic cohort included 5,851 episodes in which each patient is included only once. Episodes were excluded if they did not meet the above criteria. The Distribution of Information

We illustrated that RBP gives an indication of each prediction's unique reliability as measured by fit in contrast to a regression model, for example, whose R-squared implicitly treats all predictions as equally reliable (Figure 1). As discussed previously, fit quantifies the strength of the patterns in the data used to form each prediction. Fit can be thought of as the strength of the signal that supports each prediction. Moreover, compelling evidence was provided of fit's ability to distinguish more reliable predictions from less reliable predictions by reporting the gap in realized outcomes between cases predicted to have high impact scores versus those predicted to have low impact scores as a function of fit (Table 3). We now present a complementary measure of a prediction's reliability by showing the distribution of the information that supports the predictions for case A and case B (Figure 3). These distributions represent deviations relative to the prediction value. The difference in the width of the bars is to enable comparison of the two distributions. They give a visualization of the noise that surrounds the signals given by fit for these two predictions. Notice that the solo predictions given by the observations for case B are more broadly dispersed than those for case A, indicating that there is less agreement among the observations for case B than there is for case A. This information reinforces the assessment that the prediction for case B is less trustworthy than the prediction for case A. Not only is the signal for case B weaker. It is also surrounded by more noise compared to case A as indicated by relatively higher probability bars for high and low outcomes.

Discussion

We introduced RBP which forms a prediction as a weighted average of observed outcomes in which the weights are based on a precise and theoretically justified statistic called relevance. We described the three key features of RBP: relevance, fit, and grid prediction. Relevance measures the importance of a case to a prediction and is composed of similarity and informativeness, which are both measured as MD. Cases that are like the current case but different from the average of all cases are more important to a prediction than those that are not. Fit quantifies the prevalence of useful patterns in a dataset and gives advance guidance about a prediction's reliability. As such, fit determines the optimal blend of cases and predictive variables for each individual prediction task based on the unique circumstances of the prediction task. Grid prediction forms a composite prediction from many combinations of cases and predictive variables in a way that places more weight on combinations that are more reliable. Additionally, the prediction grid naturally yields comprehensive measures of variable importance. We also described how to measure the solo predictions given by individual cases, which enables us to view the distribution of information that supports each prediction which is the aggregation of the solo predictions. It is intuitive to think of fit as a measure of the strength of the signal that supports a prediction and the distribution of information as a visualization of the noise that surrounds the signal given by fit.

In an illustrative application, we demonstrated that RBP generates case-specific information about the reliability and formation of predictions of the impact score for patients treated with opioids that is unobservable from linear regression models. Specifically, RBP reveals the precise effect of each case on each prediction and the importance of each predictive variable to each prediction. Our analysis demonstrated that the influence of cases and predictive variables is highly prediction-specific, thereby highlighting that RBP offers valuable incremental insights to the information typically given by models.

The efficacy of medical treatment is often limited by reliance on model-driven summary information that ignores important case-specific information. For example, a regression model yields no information about the impact of specific cases on a prediction's value beyond revealing that, on average, all the cases contribute equally to the value of the prediction, which is fruitless.

Additionally, a regression model's β coefficients assume that each variable's impact on the value of a prediction is the same across all cases, and its t-statistics assume that each variable's contribution to a prediction's reliability also is the same across all cases. Finally, a regression model's R-squared implicitly assumes that all predictions rendered by the model are equally reliable.

Since RBP is model-free and prediction-specific, it gives distinctive transparency into the formation and reliability of each prediction. For example, it reveals explicitly how each case informs each specific prediction. It shows how each predictive variable influences the reliability and value of each specific prediction. Most importantly, it assesses the reliability of each specific prediction before the prediction is rendered, thereby enabling clinicians to interpret and act on uncertain predictions with appropriate caution. Moreover, RBP has several additional advantages compared to model-based approaches to prediction. RBP is a theoretically grounded framework supported by information theory, the Central Limit Theorem, the MD, and important mathematical convergences. RBP is less prone to overfitting small samples in three ways. Fit optimally balances the stronger patterns that come from using a focused sample of more relevant cases with the additional noise that comes from shrinking the sample. Because RBP proceeds one task at a time, it needs only enough data to address the complexities associated with a specific task. And because RBP explicitly reveals how each case contributes to each prediction, it reveals how reliant a prediction is on one or just a few cases. Finally, even though it was not needed in this analysis because the data did not contain any missing values, when RBP is confronted with missing data it preserves information that a model must exclude or attempt to replicate. This occurs because data that is missing in one cell in the prediction grid will most likely be recovered in some of the other cells of the grid. Moreover, RBP explicitly accounts for the relative importance of missing data in forming a prediction and assessing its reliability.

As has been well documented in the finance and data science literature, RBP extracts as much information from complex datasets as machine learning models but more efficiently and with full transparency. 22, 24 Our paper highlights RBP's extraordinary transparency based on a dataset of self-reported impact scores for patients who have been treated with opioids.

RBP represents a novel contribution to the medical literature, grounded in rigorous concepts long used in finance, and offers a transparent alternative to conventional model-based prediction.

Since it is model-free, it can be deployed without large training volumes and, importantly, can accommodate incomplete datasets by explicitly accounting for missing information rather than excluding affected cases. RBP also preserves informative extremes by avoiding routine outlier removal, thereby maximizing the utility of real-world clinical data while keeping the logic of each prediction auditable. Together, these features may yield more accurate and equitable decision support-helping clinicians and health systems better target interventions that are most likely to succeed, reduce avoidable harm, and ultimately conserve scarce resources and cost while minimizing deleterious effects.
