Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). Statcheck might still miss one-sided tests that are described in another sentence, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. Abstract

The Canadian province of Newfoundland and Labrador (NL) had high vaccination coverage, and few infections were reported prior to the establishment of the Omicron variant. We investigated whether changes in the timing and rate of booster dose administration could have reduced severe disease outcome and reliance on non-pharmaceutical interventions (NPIs) during the Omicron wave in NL. We used a vaccination-stratified epidemiological model calibrated to serology data to investigate the impact of vaccination relative to counterfactual vaccination strategies under actual, strict and no NPI scenarios. Compared with a Canadian vaccination rate scenario, the actual vaccination rollout in NL was estimated to have prevented approximately 519 hospitalizations and 122 deaths from December 15, 2021 to December 15, 2022, corresponding to reductions of 25.9% and 30.9%, respectively. These reductions reflected contributions from NL's higher initial primary series coverage (81.3%), higher final booster dose coverage (57.3%), and the implementation of various NPIs for 89 days after the Omicron BA.1 variant became established. Earlier and faster administration of booster doses reduced the duration of strict NPIs required to achieve hospitalization targets. Our findings show how the timing and rate of vaccination rollout can complement NPIs to reduce severe disease and the duration for which strict NPIs are required.

1 Introduction

Early in the COVID-19 pandemic, non-pharmaceutical interventions (NPIs) were implemented to control the spread of the SARS-CoV-2 virus and consisted of social distancing, gathering limits, and business and school closures (Brauner et al, 2021; Flaxman et al, 2020; Sonabend et al, 2021). By mid-2021, a SARS-CoV-2 vaccine had been developed and distributed, and the global adult population was rapidly becoming vaccinated. Globally, COVID-19 vaccination was estimated to have prevented about 19.8 million deaths during the first year of vaccine deployment (Watson et al, 2022). In Canada, the implementation of NPIs and vaccination programs during the first two years of the pandemic reduced the number of pandemic deaths (Ogden et al, 2022). Despite their effectiveness, NPIs were economically and socially disruptive (Clemente-Suárez et al, 2021; Koren and Pető, 2020; Mofijur et al, 2021) and were 'rolled back' when local populations became highly vaccinated (Giordano et al, 2021; Moore et al, 2021). By mid to late 2021, many jurisdictions were in the process of 're-opening', which involved implementing fewer and less restrictive NPIs (Brankston et al, 2022; Hale et al, 2021; Levitt et al, 2022), and the public was fatigued from prolonged periods of intermittent NPIs (World Health Organization, 2020).

After nearly two years of the pandemic, on November 24, 2021, the Omicron variant (B.1.1.529) was detected (Karim and Karim, 2021; Viana et al, 2022; World Health Organization, 2021), began spreading throughout the world, and was determined to have substantial immune evasion capabilities against both vaccine-derived and natural immunity (Shrestha et al, 2022). The Omicron variant was highly transmissible and likely to cause many severe infections (Nealon and Cowling, 2022; Shrestha et al, 2022; World Health Organization, 2021). An additional dose of vaccine, referred to as a 'booster dose', could decrease the chance of infection and the risk of severe disease due to infection with the newly emerged Omicron SARS-CoV-2 variant (Abu-Raddad et al, 2022; Andrews et al, 2022).

Prior to the establishment of the Omicron variant, cumulative SARS-CoV-2 incidence and vaccination coverage varied across provinces and territories in Canada (Maquiling et al, 2023; Murphy et al, 2023). The situation in Newfoundland and Labrador (NL) was distinctive due to the province's low cumulative incidence and high vaccination coverage. Serosurveillance estimates show that around 2% of the NL population, as compared to 9% of the Canadian population overall, had experienced a SARS-CoV-2 infection by November 2021 (Murphy et al, 2023). This low cumulative incidence was due to several factors, including NL's implementation of a containment strategy, such as travel measures (Mohammadi et al, 2025), and its enactment of a precautionary approach that protected vulnerable populations in the province (Government of Newfoundland and Labrador, 2022). By January 2022, NL was one of the most highly vaccinated jurisdictions in the world, with 93.7% of the NL population having received at least one dose of vaccine (Treble, 2022). This vaccination coverage included 74.7% of 5-11-year-olds, despite vaccinations becoming available to this age group only in mid-November 2021.

Whether alternative vaccination rollout strategies could have reduced the need for NPIs while mitigating the impact of the Omicron variant in NL is not known. We investigate the effect of different vaccination rollout and NPI implementation strategies specifically for Omicron variant spread in the Canadian province of NL. We focus on NL because the epidemiology and public health response to COVID-19 are regionally specific, vaccination and infection data are available for the NL population, and because we have estimated epidemiological quantities and reported key information for the COVID-19 pandemic in NL in our previous publications (Anokye et al, 2026; Doig et al, 2026; Hurford et al, 2023; Martignoni et al, 2023). Studying this population helps generalize recommendations for future pandemic preparedness.

We develop an epidemiological model to implement counterfactual scenarios where booster dose administration: began earlier; occurred at a slower or faster rate; or occurred as it did in the Canadian population. The Canadian rate scenario also assumes that the initial vaccination coverage is at either the NL or average Canadian levels. The effects of no NPIs, strict NPIs and the actual NPIs are assessed in combination with the different vaccination scenarios. We evaluate how vaccination affected the need for NPIs at the start of the Omicron wave in NL and determine the number of days that strict NPIs would need to be in place to achieve particular mitigation goals. We calculate the number of hospitalizations and deaths that would have occurred in NL under each of these counterfactual scenarios.

2 Methods

2.1 Model

We numerically solve a mechanistic Susceptible-Exposed-Asymptomatic-Infected-Recovered (SEAIR) model, previously developed by Anokye et al (2026), to quantify the impact of different combinations of vaccine administration and NPI implementation. Within this SEAIR structure (see Figure 1), susceptible individuals (S) become exposed (E) after contracting SARS-CoV-2 through contact with an infectious individual. The exposed individuals transition to either the asymptomatic (A) or symptomatic (I) compartments after a latent period. These individuals later recover and move on into the recovered compartment (R) where they remain immune to reinfection. Our model stratifies the population of NL into three cohorts based on vaccination status after receiving a Health Canada-approved COVID-19 vaccine, without distinguishing between vaccine types. The cohorts include unvaccinated individuals or those who have received one dose from a two-dose primary series (referred to as 'no primary series'), individuals who have completed the primary series (referred to as 'primary series'), and those who have one or more additional doses after receiving the primary series (referred to as 'booster doses'). Collectively, the 'no primary series' and 'primary series' cohorts are referred to as 'no booster doses'. Individuals can transition from the no primary series cohort to the primary series cohort, and then from the primary series cohort to the booster dose cohort. This assumption matches the definition of these vaccination statuses, for example that individuals must complete the primary vaccination series before receiving a booster dose.

The protection conferred by vaccination is modelled as a reduction in susceptibility to infection, and this is occurs through cohort-specific parameters (κ 1 , κ 2 , and κ 3 ) that contribute to the force of infection. The model assumes vaccine-induced protection is immediate. The original model diagram and parameterization are provided in Anokye et al (2026). We assume only susceptible individuals in each vaccination cohort can receive vaccine doses and transition to another vaccination cohort. We do not model vaccination for exposed, asymptomatic, or symptomatic individuals since few individuals with these states are likely to get vaccinated. We do not model vaccination of recovered individuals because recovered individuals are assumed to be immune from infection, such that vaccination does not afford additional protection, and modelling the vaccination of recovered individuals is not likely to change our results.

Hospital admissions and deaths were calculated directly from infection counts using vaccination-specific perinfection severity risks. We let I 1,t , I²,t and I 3,t denote the number of new infections on day t among individuals having received no primary series, a primary series, and a booster dose. The daily number of hospital admissions, H t , and deaths, D t , were calculated as: where the probabilities of hospitalization given infection were 0.0158, 0.0060 and 0.0025 for individuals with no primary series, a primary series, and a booster dose, respectively.

Given the lack of an infection-hospitalization risk estimates specific to NL, these probabilities were estimated assuming that 0.58% of infections resulted in hospitalization, which corresponds to the lower bound of an estimate reported during the Omicron period in England (0.62%, 95% CI: 0.58-0.65%; Ward et al 2024), and due to the relative risks for each vaccination group (Danza, 2022; Havers et al, 2022). The probability of death given infection is 0.005 (Danza, 2022), 0.001 (Buchan et al, 2022; Danza, 2022) and 0.0007 (Buchan et al, 2022; Danza, 2022; Grewal et al, 2023) for the respective cohorts.

The initial conditions for our simulations are the model variable values on December 15, 2021 and were determined by implementing a burn-in period of 150 days to enable the number of individuals in each state to approach equilibrium proportions. The results we report consider the value of model variables from December 15, 2021 to December 15, 2022. Our model is parameterized for the BA.1 Omicron variant. During the COVID-19 pandemic, the BA.2 variant replaced the BA.1 variant after a few months in NL. Our simulations aim to understand the longterm impact of vaccination and NPI implementation on BA.1 spread under the hypothetical situation whereby BA.1 was not replaced by any other variants. We assume no waning of immunity and the simulation period we have chosen is sufficiently long such that herd immunity is surpassed and any further infection that occurs contributes negligibly to the total number of infections. The full system of equations and parameter estimates for our model are provided in the Supplementary Material.

NPI scenarios

NPI implementation in NL during the spread of the Omicron variant consisted of an alert level system and delayed re-opening of kindergarten to grade 12 schools as described in (Anokye et al, 2026). The public health emergency was repealed in NL on March 14, 2022 (Government of Newfoundland and Labrador, 2022) and at this time NPI implementation to mitigate the spread of COVID-19 returned to pre-pandemic levels. To estimate the actual timevarying transmission rate of SARS-CoV-2 from December 15, 2021 to March 14, 2022, we refitted our model to serology data following the approach described in Anokye et al (2026). The transmission rate, β(t), was modelled as a smooth exponential spline using natural cubic spline basis functions, with the spline coefficients estimated by fitting the model to seroincidence data. Further details on the formulation and fitting procedure are provided in Anokye et al (2026). The model fit to the seroincidence data (Fig. S1) and estimated trajectory of β(t) (Fig. S2) are shown in the Supplementary Material.

For all NPI scenarios, for March 15, 2022-December 15, 2022, we set the transmission rate equal to the maximum value estimated during the period December 15, 2021-March 14, 2022 (β(t) = 0.3675; R t = 2.43). This allows us to compare NPI scenarios. For December 15, 2021-March 14, 2022, the scenarios are: (1) Actual NPIs: the time-varying transmission rate as estimated from the NL serology data; (2) No NPIs: the maximum of time-varying transmission rate as estimated for the actual NPIs scenario (β(t) = 0.3630; R t = 2.40); (3) Strict NPIs: the minimum of the timevarying transmission rate as estimated for the actual NPIs scenario (β(t) = 0.2467; R t = 1.63).

These scenarios are shown in Figure 2.

We also consider how long strict NPIs (defined as the minimum transmission rate estimated during the serology data fitting; β(t) = 0.2467; R t = 1.63) would need to be implemented to achieve 1254 total hospitalizations and 28 peak daily hospital admissions for the period December 15, 2021-December 15, 2022. For these simulations, we consider the actual booster dose scenario and strict NPI implementation beginning on December 15, 2021 and lasting for a given number of days.

Vaccination scenarios

The 'actual' vaccination scenario

In NL, booster doses were first offered on November 8, 2021 to people for whom at least six months had passed since their second dose (Government of Newfoundland and Labrador, 2022). Most of the NL population was ineligible at this time as only 9,673 people (≈ 1.9% of the NL population) met the necessary condition of at least 6 months since their second dose (Public Health Agency of Canada, 2026). The administration of booster doses in NL accelerated substantially 35 days later (beginning December 13, 2021) when the required interval between receiving two vaccine doses was decreased to 22 weeks. This change in eligibility meant that 154,100 people (≈ 30% of the NL population) were now eligible for booster doses.

We obtained weekly cumulative vaccination counts for NL and Canada from the Public Health Agency of Canada website (Public Health Agency of Canada, 2022, 2026). These records include individuals who completed the primary (one or two dose) series and those who received additional booster doses. First and second additional doses administered after the primary series were combined into a single booster category.

The vaccination data for NL had missing records for booster doses from the start date, 8 November 2021 to 7 February, 2022. We interpolate these missing values by considering a report from the Newfoundland and Labrador Department of Health and Community Services (Government of Newfoundland and Labrador, 2022), which stated that approximately 25% of eligible individuals in the province had received a booster dose by January 11, 2022 (Government of Newfoundland and Labrador, 2022). Therefore, 20.9% of the total NL population had received a booster doses by January 11 (see Figure 3B; marked as a red star) because the eligible population was individuals aged 12 years and older (426,198 individuals) and the total population of NL is 510,550 individuals. We used this information and applied a monotonic power-law function to interpolate and fill the missing booster dose administration records (Figure 3B; black triangles). We required that the interpolated booster coverage rose gradually to reach 20.9% of the total population on January 11, 2022 and then continued to increase until February 7, 2022. Vaccination count data were reported as weekly aggregates and had constant values at some periods followed by sudden increases, which may be due to delayed reporting. We smoothed the counts by redistributing later increases backward across the constant periods to correct the gaps and delayed reporting. We converted these corrected weekly cumulative counts into a daily cumulative vaccination series using a monotone cubic-spline interpolation with an Hyman filter, and calculated daily rates as first differences of the interpolated cumulative series. The resulting interpolated data is used for the 'actual' scenario for vaccination in NL and is shown in Figure 3 (black line) alongside the PHAC vaccination data (black dots).

Counterfactual vaccination scenarios

The booster vaccination scenarios for NL were constructed by varying the timing and rate of booster administration, as well as a 'Canadian rate' scenario (Figure 3B; green line). Under the 'slower rate' booster scenario (Figure 3B; purple line), booster doses were administered at 50% of the actual NL booster dose administration rate. Under the 'faster rate' booster scenario (Figure 3B; orange line), booster doses are administered at 150% of the actual NL rate. In the 'earlier' booster scenario (Figure 3B; blue line), the actual booster administration curve is shifted to occur 35 days earlier. Thirty-five days was selected since this is the number of days between when booster dose administration first occurred in NL (but with restricted eligibility) and when eligibility requirements were relaxed (see Section 2.1). For all of the 'slower rate', 'faster rate', and 'earlier' booster dose scenarios, the asymptotic proportion of the population that receives booster doses is 57.3 % and is equal to that of the 'actual' scenario, which reflects the PHAC vaccination data. In addition, for all of these scenarios, the proportion of the population in each vaccination cohort when the booster dose rollout begins is equal to that of the 'actual' scenario, although we note that this start date is 35 days earlier for the 'earlier' scenario.

To construct the 'Canadian rate' scenario, we assumed that booster vaccination in NL followed the Canadian national average rather than the observed NL rollout that occurred. Using the PHAC vaccination data for November 6, 2021, we calculated the proportion of individuals in Canada who had one or fewer vaccine doses, had completed the primary vaccination series, and had received booster doses, and applied this proportion to the NL population to estimate the initial number of individuals in the no primary series, primary series, and booster dose vaccination cohorts. These calculations were used to determine the rates of transition between the cohorts and the Michaelis-Menten method (Bolker, 2008) was applied to preserve the non-negativity of model variables. For the Canadian rate scenario, booster dose coverage in NL asymptotes to 53.1 % of the total population and the initial number of individuals in the vaccine cohorts is set to the Canadian values. 3 Results

Across all vaccination scenarios, strict NPI implementation led to delayed and reduced epidemic peaks and prolonged outbreaks (Figure 4 right column), while the absence of NPIs resulted in earlier and larger epidemic peaks with shorter outbreaks (Figure 4 left column). Across all NPI scenarios, a faster rate (Figure 4A-C) and earlier start (Figure 4D-F) of booster dose vaccination (as compared to the NL rate), and the actual NL vaccination rollout (as compared to the Canadian average rate; Figure 4G-I) delayed and reduced epidemic peaks and prolonged outbreaks. In Figure 4, the incidence in individuals who had not received booster doses is shown with light shading. The corresponding cumulative number of infections occurring in individuals without booster doses across these vaccination and NPI scenarios is shown in Fig. S3 in the Supplementary Material. When infections occur only after individuals have received booster doses, the probability of hospitalization (equation 1) and death (equation 2) decreases, which we explore in the following paragraphs. When infections were delayed, fewer people were infected before receiving a booster dose (Fig. S3; Supplementary Material), which corresponded to fewer hospitalizations and deaths (Figure 5). For all vaccination scenarios, strict NPIs resulted in fewer hospitalizations and deaths than actual NPIs and no NPIs (Figure 5C compared with A and B). For the no NPI scenario, the ranking of the vaccination strategies (from highest hospitalizations and deaths to lowest) was slower rate, Canadian rate, then actual NL rate, with the earlier and faster rate scenarios performing similarly and best (Figure 5A). For the actual and strict NPI scenarios (Figure 5B), these relative rankings of the vaccination strategies remain the same, except that the actual NL scenario performs more similarly to the earlier and faster scenarios, which are still the best strategies (Figure 5B-C). Figure 5 suggests that the combination of the NPIs and booster dose vaccination rollout that actually occurred in NL during the early spread of the Omicron variant was effective in reducing hospitalizations and deaths, but this efficacy was contingent on the implementation of NPIs, as otherwise the NL vaccination rollout would have been outperformed by an earlier or faster vaccination rollout. To understand the reduced number of hospitalizations and deaths (Figure 5D-F) for the actual NL (black bars) as compared to the Canadian average (green bars) scenarios, we consider scenarios with the initial NL vaccination coverage where booster doses were implemented at the Canadian rate (Figure 6A-C, yellow lines) and the initial Canadian vaccination coverage where booster doses were implemented at the NL rate (Figure 6A-C, dark green line). Both of these scenarios (yellow bars and dark green bars), which contained one element of the actual NL and Canadian average vaccination scenarios, performed intermediate to the actual NL (black bars) and Canadian average scenario (Figure 6D-F, which shows reductions relative to the Canadian average scenario, i.e., see Figure 5D-F green bars).

Both the higher initial primary series coverage in NL prior to the administration of booster doses (yellow bars) and the higher final booster dose proportion (dark green bars) contributed to reducing the number of hospitalizations (by 10.1% and 15.1%, respectively) and deaths (by 17.2% and 12.9%, respectively) that occurred in NL (Figure 6E). The effect of NL's higher initial primary series coverage and higher final booster dose coverage is nearly additive. Figures 5E and 6E show that our modelling predicts that NL's high vaccination coverage reduced the number of hospitalizations by 519 (25.9%) and the number of deaths by 122 people (30.9%) that occurred in NL from December 15, 2021-December 15, 2022, relative to vaccination if it had occurred at the Canadian rate under the actual NPI scenario. Therefore, both NL's higher initial vaccination coverage and higher percentage of people that received booster doses were important components of reducing hospitalizations and deaths. For our three best-performing booster dose administration strategies, 43-57 days of strict NPI implementation are sufficient to meet the target number of total (A) and peak (B) hospitalizations (Figure 7 blue, orange, and black lines). For a less stringent target, i.e., 1,700 total hospitalizations or a peak number of 25 daily hospital admissions, for the early (blue) and faster (orange) booster dose administration strategies, these targets could be met without any strict NPIs. In NL, some form of NPIs were implemented from December 15, 2021-March 14, 2022 (89 days), although these NPIs were often weaker than our definition of strict, and so we find that 56-57 days of strict NPIs would be sufficient to meet the hospitalization targets for the actual NL vaccination scenario (Figure 7A,B; black line). The number of days that strict NPIs needed to be implemented in NL to achieve these targets could be reduced by 7-14 days if booster doses had been implemented earlier or more quickly, where the exact number of days reduction depends on whether it is necessary to meet both hospitalization targets. Conversely, if vaccination coverage in NL was instead that of the Canadian average (Figure 7A,B; green line) the number of days that strict NPIs needed to be implemented to achieve the hospitalization targets increases from 56-57 days to 101-147 days. We found that even with 150 days of strict NPIs it was not possible to achieve the target number of peak hospitalizations if booster doses were administered at the slow rate (7B pink line).

Figure 7C shows the interrelatedness of the hospitalization targets we selected (dashed lines) for the different vaccination scenarios. The number of days to achieve both targets is similar for the early (blue), faster (orange), and actual NL (black) booster dose scenarios, but for the Canadian average booster dose administration scenario (green) the target total number of hospitalizations is met with many fewer days of strict NPIs (below the horizontal dashed line) than it would take to meet the target peak number of hospitalizations (to the left of the vertical dashed line; Figure 7C). During the COVID-19 pandemic, Newfoundland and Labrador (NL) had the highest vaccination coverage in Canada (Treble, 2022) and was likely amongst the most vaccinated jurisdictions in the world (see Section 2.1). We used our previously developed and parameterized compartmental model ( (Anokye et al, 2026)) to explore vaccination and NPI implementation scenarios. We found that relative to the average vaccination coverage in Canada, the higher vaccination coverage in NL reduced the number of hospitalizations by 519 and the number of deaths by 122 from December 15, 2021 to December 15, 2022 (Figure 6E).

We found that a booster dose rollout that began 35 days earlier (blue bars) or occurred at 150% the rate of the NL booster dose rollout (orange bars), would have reduced the number of hospitalizations by a further 8-14 people and the number of deaths by 3-4 people (Figure 5B). The effectiveness of different vaccination scenarios depends on whether NPIs were implemented. NPIs delay infections, which allows more time to administer vaccines, and can offset the negative impacts of slower vaccination strategies. If no NPIs had been implemented in NL from December 15, 2021 to March 14, 2022 we find that the actual NL booster dose rollout would have performed worse than earlier and faster vaccination scenarios (Figure 5A).

Characteristics of NL were: (i) implementation of some form of NPIs for the first 89 days of Omicron's spread (Figure 3A; see also (Anokye et al, 2026)); (ii) high primary series coverage when Omicron established (81.3%); and (iii) booster dose administration to around 50% of the population by early February and to 57.3% of the population in the subsequent weeks (Figure 3B). Our results suggest that all of these characteristics were important and acted together to reduce the number of hospitalizations and deaths that occurred in the year following the establishment of the Omicron variant in NL (Figure 6E) for a hypothetical scenario were the BA.1 variant continued to spread (without the emergence of the BA.2 variant) and assuming no waning of immunity.

NPIs are socially and economically disruptive (Clemente-Suárez et al, 2021; Koren and Pető, 2020; Mofijur et al, 2021) and when the Omicron variant established, residents were already fatigued from public health interventions that were implemented since the start of the pandemic two years earlier. A reason why it would have been advantageous to start the booster dose vaccination rollout earlier in NL (blue line) or to administer vaccines at a 1.5 times faster rate (orange line) is that less stringent hospitalization targets, such as no more than 1,700 total hospitalizations or 25 peak daily hospital admissions, might have been met without any implementation of strict NPIs (Figure 7). The actual booster dose administration strategy that occurred in NL was slower and later than our fast and early scenarios, but was still effective, in part, because NPIs were also implemented to delay infections and allow time for more people to receive booster doses before they were infected with the Omicron variant.

A limitation of our work is that we have not stratified infection risk, vaccination coverage, or the probability of hospitalization and death by age, gender, location, ethnicity, socio-economic status, or type of employment. NL's population has the highest proportion of elderly people in Canada ( (Statistics Canada, 2021)), and so we expect that the projected number of hospitalizations and deaths would increase for all scenarios had a realistic age-structure been considered. Immunocompromised people may be particularly vulnerable to infections, even when vaccinated, and are exposed to disproportionate risk when NPIs are suspended, while household members that experience domestic violence are exposed to disproportionate risk when 'stay-at-home' requirements are enacted.

An economic analysis might link the cost of NPIs and the cost of slow or late vaccination campaigns to identify an optimal combination of public health measures. Our results contribute to this possible future direction as we use an epidemiologically-based method to quantify the hospitalizations and deaths for different vaccination and NPI scenarios to which the economic considerations might be added. Additionally, we note that investment in vaccination campaigns, particularly ones that occur urgently, can divert resources from other branches of the healthcare system and a holistic analysis would also consider these factors.

Our simulations predicted that NL's high vaccination coverage prior to the establishment of the Omicron SARS-CoV-2 variant (81.3%) and booster dose vaccination campaign (resulting in 57.3% of NL's population receiving booster doses) reduced the number of hospitalizations occurring in Newfoundlanders and Labradorians in the next year by 519, and the number of deaths by 122 relative to a counterfactual scenario that implemented vaccination at the average Canadian rate (Figure 6E). These reductions occurred, in part, because NL implemented some form of NPIs during the first 89 days after the Omicron variant established to offset the time that it took to administer booster doses. We find that had the booster dose campaign in NL been implemented at half the actual rate (pink line), NPIs would need to have been enacted for a much longer period to achieve the same hospitalization targets (Figure 7). Our results support the value of vaccinations in protecting populations from severe outcomes of infectious diseases.
