Cluster-wide scan of imputed structural variants 11p15.5 contains repeat and structural variation that haplogroup tagging cannot reach, including the MUC2 VNTR domain. We therefore asked whether any common, well-imputed VNTR or structural-variant allele across the cluster (109 variants) is associated with IPF independently of rs35705950, recognising that this sample can only detect large effects. Twenty-one reached the cluster-wide threshold marginally, but all were attenuated by adjustment for rs35705950 and none reached it after adjustment (Supplementary Table S5). This is a power-limited negative. Taking the effect sizes and allele frequencies Kousathanas et al. report for the eight mucin-cluster 11p15.5 signals, this sample has 74% power to detect their most strongly associated MUC5B signal (OR 1.71 at a frequency of 0.20) at the cluster-wide threshold but 1% to 2% power for each of the four MUC2 and MUC6 signals, whose odds ratios are 1.15 to 2.10 at allele frequencies of 0.007 to 0.69 (Methods). The absence of those signals here is therefore uninformative.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

How Preregistration Check works

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Take-home message: In a dedicated IPF case-control study, the MUC5AC H3 and MUC5B H1 associations were not separable from the MUC5B promoter variant rs35705950. A MUC5AC H2 association that did survive adjustment here was not replicated in a larger independent sample.

Introduction

Idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) is a progressive and frequently fatal fibrotic lung disease (Raghu et al., 2022). The strongest common genetic risk factor is a gainof-function promoter variant in the mucin gene MUC5B (rs35705950; minor allele frequency ~9% in Europeans), whose minor (T) allele confers an odds ratio of approximately 4 per allele (Seibold et al., 2011; Allen et al., 2017) and explains an estimated 2-11% of variation in IPF risk against an overall heritability of 28% (Moll et al., 2023). It increases MUC5B expression in the bronchiolar epithelium and is thought to predispose to fibrosis through mucociliary dysfunction (Seibold et al., 2011; Hancock et al., 2018) or epithelial cell stress (Dobrinskikh et al., 2023; Bridges et al., 2025).

MUC5B and MUC5AC lie within the 11p15.5 mucin cluster and encode gel-forming mucins with large central variable number tandem repeat (VNTR) regions. Plender et al. used long-read sequencing to define three MUC5AC haplogroups (H1 short, H2 medium, H3 long) and two MUC5B haplogroups (H1 less common, H2 common), with tagging SNPs (tSNPs) allowing assignment in short-read data (Plender et al., 2024). Both are phylogenetic clades from putatively neutral non-coding sequence, but the MUC5AC clades also differ in repeat length (H3 is ~670 amino acids longer than H1), whereas the MUC5B protein clades do not.

There are biological grounds to ask whether haplogroup matters beyond rs35705950. The VNTR encodes the serine-and threonine-rich backbone carrying the O-glycans that set mucus viscoelasticity, and MUC5AC and MUC5B are the principal determinants of mucociliary clearance (Hill et al., 2022; Abrami et al., 2024). VNTR structure could therefore act on that mechanism through protein architecture rather than expression. Length is not the only route: clades differ in other architectural features and may carry regulatory or other coding variation. At MUC5B, where length is essentially invariant in Europeans (Plender et al. report expanded VNTRs in seven haplotypes, all of African ancestry), any effect must be of that kind, which is the reason to test the MUC5B haplogroups despite their shared repeat length.

This has not been tested directly. In the All of Us phenome-wide scan no MUC5AC haplogroup tag survived multiple-testing correction, and the MUC5B locus was represented by rs35705950 rather than a haplogroup tag (Plender et al., 2024). A genomewide tandem-repeat study in IPF reported an associated repeat at 11p15.5 that lost significance once rs35705950 was included, but its catalogue was restricted to repeat units of ≤20 bp, so it indexed a shorter intergenic repeat rather than the MUC5AC (24 bp) or MUC5B (87 bp) coding VNTRs, and it did not test the haplogroup classification (Oketch et al., 2025). We therefore ask, in IPFJES (the IPF Job Exposures Study), a dedicated IPF case-control study (Reynolds et al., 2023), how much haplogroup association, if any, is independent of rs35705950. Tagging SNPs are the usual way to index these clades in array data, and such a test shows whether clade membership adds to the promoter variant, not which feature of a clade is responsible.

Methods

Study population

IPFJES is a multicentre UK case-control study of men, recruiting from 21 hospitals in England, Scotland, and Wales (Reynolds et al., 2023). IPF cases were diagnosed at multidisciplinary team meetings by 2011 ATS/ERS/JRS/ALAT guidelines; recruitment ran from February 2017 to October 2019. Controls were participants without IPF from the same centres, frequency-matched for age. All participants provided written informed consent. Ethical approval was granted by the East Midlands -Nottingham 1 Research Ethics Committee (REC 17/EM/0021; IRAS 203355). Of 902 IPFJES participants with clinical data, 829 also had typed-array and imputed chromosome 11 genotypes with complete covariates. One centre (n=31) enrolled cases only, so its centre indicator predicted case status perfectly and the corresponding coefficient could not be estimated (complete separation); that centre was excluded, leaving 798. Because genetic ancestry is the principal confounder here and adjustment alone may not remove it, analyses were then restricted to participants of self-reported white ethnicity, who form a single genetic cluster: 25 of the 27 excluded lie beyond 3 SD of the whiteethnicity mean on PC1. This gave an analytical sample of 771 participants (385 cases, 386 controls) used throughout, with principal components recomputed within it.

Genotyping and imputation

Participants were genotyped on the Biobank Affymetrix Axiom array (GRCh37). As previously described (Chin et al., 2026), sample-level quality control removed individuals with low genotyping call rate (<95%), high heterozygosity, sex mismatches, duplicates, and high relatedness. Imputation used the TOPMed reference panel (GRCh38); imputed variants with r²<0.5 or minor allele count ≤3 were excluded.

Haplogroup assignment

We used a two-step procedure. First, 17 MUC5AC tSNPs were extracted from our GRCh38 imputed data using PLINK v2: 16 of the 92 tags listed in Plender et al. Supplementary Table S4, plus rs35779873 from their Table S6. Second, one concordance SNP was chosen per haplogroup as the primary tag, the H3 and H2 tags entered as allele-dosage counts and H1 serving as the reference: H3 by rs28542750 (r²=1.0) and H2 by rs879201833 (r²=0.888, the best available H2 tag), with H1 as reference (the most common haplogroup, r²=0.899 for rs769768817). The remaining 14 tSNPs were retained as a cross-check on those three (Supplementary Table S1). Tagging r² values are from Plender et al. Supplementary Table S4, except rs35779873, whose r² is from their Table S6. Allele orientation was resolved separately for each tSNP and recorded with its evidence: from the risk allele given in Plender et al. Table S6 where listed, and otherwise by matching the counted-allele frequency in our controls to the published haplogroup frequency. Plender et al. name the two concordance tSNPs in their main text but do not give their alleles, so those were oriented by frequency; rs35705950 was oriented by its established risk allele. The direct H2 tag is primary because it tags H2 directly rather than by subtraction. The joint model is therefore an approximate rather than exact H1-reference parameterisation. Tags were cross-checked against 14 further tagging SNPs (Supplementary Table S1). Plender et al.'s main text transposes the haplogroup labels for rs28542750 and rs769768817 relative to Plender et al. Table S4; we followed the latter (Supplementary methods).

The MUC5B H1/H2 discriminator (chr11:1,244,757; H2 r²=1.0) was taken from the Plender main text; being strand-ambiguous, its orientation was resolved by allele frequency (Supplementary methods). All tSNPs passed Hardy-Weinberg testing in controls (minimum p=0.39) and were hard-called with no missingness; their alleles, frequencies and tagging r² are in Supplementary Table S1.

Covariates and exposure

Five genetic principal components, computed with PLINK v2 from LD-pruned typedarray data (309,453 variants) within the 771 analytical participants themselves, were used in all models. Smoking history and lifetime occupational history were collected at the same structured telephone interview, by an interviewer unaware of case status (Reynolds et al., 2023); ever-smoking and age were covariates in all models. Occupational asbestos exposure was ascertained by Peto job-exposure matrix applied to the occupational history (binary classification); 502 of 771 participants (65.1%) were classified as exposed.

TOPMed sample

The replication sample comprised 1,961 IPF cases and 3,816 controls of European ancestry from the National Institutes of Health Trans-Omics for Precision Medicine (TOPMed) programme, after restriction to participants with complete covariate data. Cases were diagnosed according to American Thoracic Society/European Respiratory Society criteria at centres in the United States, Europe and Australia and underwent whole-genome sequencing through the TOPMed Whole Genome Sequencing in Familial and Sporadic Idiopathic Pulmonary Fibrosis Study. Controls were unaffected individuals aged 50 years or older selected from the TOPMed Genetic Epidemiology of COPD Study (COPDGene), TOPMed Framingham Heart Study (FHS) and TOPMed Multi-Ethnic Study of Atherosclerosis (MESA). All participants gave written informed consent under IRB-approved protocols for their parent studies. No two participants were related at third degree or closer, and European ancestry was assigned using ancestry-informative principal components (Peljto et al., 2023). MUC5AC haplogroups were assigned from the same tagging SNPs, alleles and coding used here; the MUC5B H1/H2 discriminator failed genotyping quality control and was not analysed. Models were adjusted for sex, age, smoking status and the first four principal components. Unlike IPFJES, the sample includes women, and all models are therefore adjusted for sex.

Statistical analysis

Logistic regression (Python 3.12; statsmodels) was performed with IPF case/control status as outcome. Haplogroup counts were entered additively (0/1/2 copies); all models adjusted for age and ever-smoking as established IPF risk factors, PC1-5 (genetic ancestry), and recruitment centre (regression N=771).

The MUC5AC models, with H1 as reference in the joint model, were (1a) H3 main effect; (1b) H3 + imputed rs35705950 T count; (1c) joint model with H2 and H3 entered simultaneously; (1d) additionally adjusted for rs35705950. The MUC5B models were (2a) H1 main effect and (2b) H1 + rs35705950 T count. rs35705950 was also modelled alone.

Sensitivity analyses, detailed in the Supplementary methods and tabulated there where indicated, comprised rs35705950-stratified models (Table S2), alternative H2 tags (Table S3), genotypic rather than additive fits for H2 and H3, imputed MUC5AC exon-31 repeat length (Table S4), an agnostic conditional scan of 109 common well-imputed structural variants across the mucin cluster, imputed from the Bai et al. (2026) long-read assembly panel rather than from the TOPMed panel used for the SNP imputation (Table S5), and conditioning of the H2 tag on the mucin-cluster subset of the conditionally-independent 11p15.5 IPF signals reported by Kousathanas et al. (2025). Nested models were compared by likelihood-ratio test.

The same models were fitted in an independent TOPMed IPF case-control sample (described above); between-sample heterogeneity was assessed by a two-sided z test on the difference in log odds ratios, equivalently Cochran's Q on one degree of freedom, with standard errors recovered from the reported confidence intervals; where the two samples are pooled, this is a fixed-effect inverse-variance meta-analysis (code/15_orientation_vs_topmed.py).

Power for the cluster-wide scan was computed for each of the eight mucin-cluster 11p15.5 signals reported by Kousathanas et al. (their Supplementary Table 5), using the European allele frequency and odds ratio reported there. For each signal, genotypes were simulated under Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium at the reported frequency, the scan model was fitted in the 771 analytical participants to obtain the standard error of the log odds ratio, and power was evaluated analytically at the cluster-wide threshold (α = 0.05/54, the effective number of independent tests; code/17_cluster_scan_power.py).

Four primary haplogroup coefficients were tested; p-values are two-sided and uncorrected, and nominal results described as such.

Results

Participant characteristics

Table 1 shows the participant characteristics. Cases were slightly older (median 77 vs 76 years, p=0.010); ever-smoking (75.8% vs 69.7%, p=0.066) and occupational asbestos exposure (67.5% vs 62.7%, p=0.182) did not differ significantly. The rs35705950 T allele frequency was 0.33 in cases and 0.12 in controls; 59.7% of cases and 23.1% of controls carried at least one copy, and 6.8% and 1.0% respectively were homozygous (p=9.9×10⁻²⁵ for carriage).

Haplogroup frequencies

In the IPFJES controls (n=386), which approximate a population reference since cases are enriched for risk haplotypes, haplogroup frequencies were MUC5AC H1 ~57%, H2 ~23% and H3 ~20%, and MUC5B H2 ~85%. The MUC5AC H3 figure matches the ~21% reported for Europeans; the MUC5B comparator of ~82% is a panel-wide estimate across all ancestries rather than a European one (Plender et al., 2024). All four haplogroup tags showed no departure from Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium in controls (p=0.45-0.74), as did every variant in the panel (minimum p=0.39; Methods).

Linkage disequilibrium between MUC5AC H3 and rs35705950

Correlations with rs35705950 are reported as the signed r, because the direction of tagging differs between haplogroups; r² is given alongside in Supplementary Table S6. MUC5AC H3 count was correlated with rs35705950 T count (r=0.363, p=1.7×10⁻²⁵), sharing ~13% of its variance; among H3 carriers, 61.4% also carried the T allele. MUC5B H1, MUC5AC H1 and MUC5AC H2 were inversely correlated with T count (r=-0.113, -0.140 and -0.235), so the risk allele co-occurs with the MUC5B H2 and MUC5AC H3 backgrounds. Carrier status for H3 and for the T allele was nonetheless discordant in 241 of 771 participants (31%). Only H3 has appreciable LD with rs35705950, and its estimate in the IPFJES controls (r²=0.10, slightly lower than the full-sample r² of 0.13 because cases are enriched for risk haplotypes) is consistent with the 1000 Genomes European reference (0.10; Supplementary Table S6), so the LD is representative rather than a feature of this cohort.

Haplogroup associations with IPF

Full estimates are in Table 2. MUC5AC H3 was positively associated with IPF (OR 1.63, 95% CI 1.26-2.09; p=1.8×10⁻⁴), but not independently of rs35705950: adjusting for the promoter variant attenuated H3 to the null (OR 1.10, 95% CI 0.83-1.45; p=0.52), while rs35705950 itself retained a strong effect (OR 3.90, 95% CI 2.83-5.38; p=1.1×10⁻¹⁶). Each additional MUC5B H1 copy, in place of H2, was associated with lower odds of IPF (OR 0.71, 95% CI 0.52-0.98; p=0.038), and this likewise attenuated after adjustment (OR 0.82, 95% CI 0.58-1.15; p=0.25).

The three-haplotype joint model (H1 reference) showed the same pattern for H3, but a MUC5AC H2 association persisted after additional adjustment for rs35705950, each additional H2 copy in place of H1 being associated with lower odds of IPF (OR 0.69, 95% CI 0.51-0.94; p=0.017, which does not survive correction for the four primary coefficients). This estimate was stable across tag choices, agreeing with the three panel H2 tSNPs and with the diplotype complement, that is, H2 count inferred by subtraction from the other two haplogroups (2 -H1 -H3) rather than from a tag of its own (Supplementary Table S3).

Among participants carrying no copy of the rs35705950 T allele, the promoter variant has no variance and so cannot account for any association. In that stratum H3 was directionally consistent but imprecise (151 cases, 297 controls; OR 1.15, 95% CI 0.79-1.69; Supplementary Table S2). Genotypic fits for H3 and H2 did not improve on the additive model (Supplementary methods).

Comparison with an independent TOPMed sample

The same models were fitted in the TOPMed IPF case-control sample (1,961 cases, 3,816 controls; adjusted for sex, age, smoking and PC1-4), with haplogroups assigned from the same tags and coding.

The MUC5AC H3 findings replicated: OR 1.62 (95% CI 1.47-1.78) unadjusted in TOPMed against 1.63 (1.26-2.09) here (p=0.96 for heterogeneity). In both samples the association was abolished by adjustment for rs35705950, falling to 0.94 (0.84-1.05) in TOPMed and 1.10 (0.83-1.45) here (p=0.31 for heterogeneity); neither adjusted interval excludes 1, so this is attenuation to the null rather than a reversal of direction. H2 is estimable only alongside H3, with H1 as reference, so the estimate before adjustment for rs35705950 is the H3-adjusted one: 0.83 (0.74-0.93) in TOPMed against 0.58 (0.43-0.78) here (p=0.028 for heterogeneity). The rs35705950-adjusted H2 residual, however, did not replicate: it was null in TOPMed (OR 0.99, 0.88-1.11), an interval that excludes the 0.69 (0.51-0.94) estimated here, in a sample with five times as many cases. Because IPFJES recruited men only, the TOPMed models were refitted separately by sex (adjusted for age, smoking and PC1-4). The pattern was the same in both: H3 was associated with IPF before adjustment for rs35705950 (men 1.68, 1.48-1.90; women 1.40, 1.19-1.65) and null after it (1.01, 0.88-1.17; 0.85, 0.70-1.02), and the H2 residual was null in both (1.00, 0.87-1.16; 0.97, 0.79-1.19). The failure of the H2 association to replicate is therefore not explained by the maleonly composition of IPFJES. The MUC5B H1/H2 discriminator failed quality control in TOPMed, so the MUC5B association could not be tested there. The samples differ in composition. A higher proportion of TOPMed controls than TOPMed cases had ever smoked (73.9% versus 68.7%), the reverse of the pattern in IPFJES (69.7% of controls versus 75.8% of cases). TOPMed controls came from the COPDGene, Framingham and MESA cohorts. IPFJES recruited men only, and its controls were patients attending outpatient clinics, so neither control group represents the general population.

Cluster-wide scan of imputed structural variants 11p15.5 contains repeat and structural variation that haplogroup tagging cannot reach, including the MUC2 VNTR domain. We therefore asked whether any common, well-imputed VNTR or structural-variant allele across the cluster (109 variants) is associated with IPF independently of rs35705950, recognising that this sample can only detect large effects. Twenty-one reached the cluster-wide threshold marginally, but all were attenuated by adjustment for rs35705950 and none reached it after adjustment (Supplementary Table S5). This is a power-limited negative. Taking the effect sizes and allele frequencies Kousathanas et al. report for the eight mucin-cluster 11p15.5 signals, this sample has 74% power to detect their most strongly associated MUC5B signal (OR 1.71 at a frequency of 0.20) at the cluster-wide threshold but 1% to 2% power for each of the four MUC2 and MUC6 signals, whose odds ratios are 1.15 to 2.10 at allele frequencies of 0.007 to 0.69 (Methods). The absence of those signals here is therefore uninformative.

Discussion

We tested whether MUC5AC or MUC5B haplogroup is associated with IPF independently of the MUC5B promoter variant rs35705950 in a dedicated IPF case-control study. MUC5AC H3 was associated with IPF, but the association is largely explained by its linkage disequilibrium with rs35705950, as was that of MUC5B H1. A residual MUC5AC H2 association survived adjustment in this cohort but did not replicate in a larger independent sample. Adjusting rs35705950 for H3 barely changes the rs35705950 estimate (OR 4.03 to 3.90), whereas adjusting H3 for rs35705950 abolishes the H3 estimate (1.63 to 1.10); adding H3 to a model already containing rs35705950 does not improve fit (χ²₁=0.41, p=0.52), whereas the reverse does (χ²₁=77.8, p=1.2×10⁻¹⁸). This pattern is consistent with the H3 association being explained by rs35705950, or by variation more closely tagged by rs35705950 than by the H3 proxy. The LD between them (r=0.36) is the key interpretive constraint: because H3 and rs35705950 are co-inherited, no sample of this size can say whether an H3 association reflects VNTR length, rs35705950mediated expression, or another co-inherited variant. That LD was also addressed directly rather than only by adjustment: nearly a third of participants are discordant for H3 and T carrier status, and the non-carrier stratum removes the promoter variant's variance entirely, where H3 remained directionally consistent but imprecise (OR 1.15, 95% CI 0.79-1.69, on 151 cases). The limit there is power, not LD. The tag itself is not the limitation, since it indexes H3 at r²=1.0 in Plender's panel and tracks independently imputed repeat length at r=0.98 here. The joint-model ordering (H2 depleted, H1 intermediate, H3 enriched for the T allele) is what would be seen if risk tracks rs35705950 frequency by haplogroup rather than repeat length. MUC5AC H2 behaved differently within this cohort: adjustment for rs35705950 did not abolish it (0.58 to 0.69), the estimate was stable across alternative tags and agreed closely with the diplotype complement (r=0.97; Supplementary Table S3), and conditioning on the eight mucin-cluster 11p15.5 signals reported by Kousathanas et al. left the point estimate essentially unchanged, though no longer nominally significant (0.71, p=0.051). However, that residual did not replicate. In the independent TOPMed sample the rs35705950-adjusted estimate was null (OR 0.99, 95% CI 0.88-1.11), an interval that excludes our estimate, in a sample with five times as many cases, so we do not claim an H2 effect independent of the promoter variant. The samples differ in control ascertainment and in the men-only composition of IPFJES, but the parsimonious reading is that our estimate was inflated by chance in a small sample.

Whether repeat number itself carries risk can be addressed more directly. Imputing MUC5AC repeat length into IPFJES from the Bai et al. long-read panel recovered the exon-31 length variants at high fidelity (imputation r²=0.99 for the 751 bp insertion), but imputed length proved equivalent to the clade tag, correlating with H3 count at r=0.98 and carrying the same weak linkage with rs35705950 (r=0.37). It behaved identically to the H3 tag (unadjusted OR 1.60, 95% CI 1.24-2.07; adjusted 1.06, 0.79-1.41), and a composite of total exon-31 length gave the same result (Supplementary Table S4). The H3 tagging r² of 1.0 is Plender's panel estimate and need not transfer to a European cohort, but the tag and the independently imputed length agree at r=0.98 in our own data, so little remains for imputation to add. In Bai et al.'s UK Biobank statistics the MUC5AC exon-31 repeat is likewise not associated with the pulmonary-fibrosis phecode, an independent and far larger test, though one using a heterogeneous phecode and without conditioning on rs35705950 (Bai et al., 2026).

Cysteine-rich domain copy number and glycosylation density are among the features invisible to a length-based model, as are the predicted protein-truncating haplotypes Plender et al. report at MUC5B, whose status is unresolved (Plender et al., 2024). The MUC5B H1 inverse association (0.71) is explained as H3's is: H2, the predominant MUC5B haplogroup, carries rs35705950 T, making H1 appear protective by contrast, and the estimate attenuates on adjustment (0.82, p=0.25).

Strengths and limitations

Strengths include validated tSNPs from long-read sequencing and a cohort recruited specifically for IPF, with MDT-confirmed diagnoses. The principal limitation is that array-based tSNPs index a phylogenetic clade within an LD block, so even a surviving association identifies the clade, not the feature driving it. The second concerns the variants themselves: per-variant imputation quality for the tagging SNPs could not be assessed beyond the r²≥0.5 QC threshold, so imputation error in a tag propagates into haplogroup assignment, and Plender's tagging r² values are averaged across a 206-haplotype panel assembled from 104 individuals, only 10 of them European, so they carry no guarantee of transferring to a European cohort. The third concerns rs35705950. It lies within the same locus as the haplogroups and in partial linkage disequilibrium with them, so it is not an external covariate: adjusting for it may remove part of the haplogroup signal itself, and the adjusted estimate asks whether haplogroup matters among chromosomes already matched on promoter genotype, which is a narrower question than whether haplogroup matters at all. The fourth is power: where an association attenuates, this sample cannot exclude a modest residual (the adjusted MUC5AC H3 interval, 0.83-1.45, remains compatible with a per-allele OR of about 1.45), though pooled with TOPMed it narrows to 0.96 (0.87-1.07). IPFJES recruited men only, so applicability to women is unknown.

Conclusions

MUC5AC H3 and MUC5B H1 are associated with IPF risk in directions consistent with their LD with rs35705950, and neither association is separable from it. A MUC5AC H2 association did survive adjustment for rs35705950 here, but was not replicated in an independent sample with five times as many cases, where the rs35705950-adjusted es-timate was null (OR 0.99, 95% CI 0.88-1.11). We therefore do not claim a haplogroup effect independent of the promoter variant.

Sensitivity analyses

A fifth Plender H2 tag, rs1201597847 (H2 r²=0.859), is present in the imputed data but adds nothing, being in complete LD with the panel tags. rs35705950 was additionally estimated within H3-carrier strata, where it was comparable in H3 non-carriers (OR 3.35, 95% CI 2.11-5.32; N=429) and H3 carriers (4.40, 2.71-7.14; N=342), with no interaction (p=0.42). Because Plender et al. infer heterozygote advantage at H3 from balancing-selection signatures in 1000 Genomes Europeans, MUC5AC H3 was also fitted as a two-degree-of-freedom genotypic term, as was MUC5AC H2 in the joint model. MUC5AC VNTR repeat length was imputed into the 771 from the Bai et al. long-read reference panel using the ImputeSV server (SHAPEIT5 phasing, Minimac4), and both the 751 bp exon-31 length allele and a composite of total exon-31 repeat length were tested against IPF with and without adjustment for rs35705950 (Supplementary Table S4; code/10_imputed_vntr_length.py).

The cluster-wide scan covered every common (MAF≥0.01) well-imputed (imputation r²≥0.8) VNTR and structural-variant allele across MUC6-MUC5B (chr11:1.01-1.263 Mb; 109 variants after de-duplicating the VNTR and SV panels), with the cluster-wide threshold set from the effective number of independent tests (Li and Ji, 2005; Supplementary Table S5; code/11_mucin_cluster_scan.py). Kousathanas et al. report nine conditionally-independent 11p15.5 signals. We conditioned on the eight lying within the mucin cluster (chr11:1.01-1.263 Mb; by their nearest-transcription-start-site annotation, one nearest MUC6, three MUC2 and four MUC5B), retaining rs35705950 in the model; the ninth (rs34787245, chr11:1,289,602) lies in TOLLIP, outside the cluster, and was not included (code/12_h2_conditional_on_locus_signals.py). Supplementary methods: software and reproducibility

Analyses used PLINK v2.00a3.7 for LD-pruning, PCA, dosage export and Hardy-Weinberg testing, and PLINK v1.90b6.26 for single-variant text extractions, with Python 3.12 (numpy, scipy, pandas, statsmodels, pandas-plink) for statistical analysis. Haplogroup label assignment

The haplogroup labels for rs28542750 and rs769768817 in Plender et al.'s main text appear transposed relative to Plender et al. Supplementary Table S4. That table resolves it, giving per-haplogroup r² of 0.111 (H1), 0.055 (H2) and 1.000 (H3) for rs28542750, and 0.899 (H1), 0.012 (H2) and 0.001 (H3) for rs769768817. We therefore assigned rs28542750 to H3 and rs769768817 to H1. Our own control frequencies corroborate this: rs28542750 (0.203) falls within the H3 tag set and matches the ~21% European H3 frequency, and rs769768817 (0.571) within the H1 tag set; this also fixes the orientation of both palindromes. The primary inference is in any case invariant to the labelling; only which clade is described as the long one would change.
