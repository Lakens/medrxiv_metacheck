Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. Some differences come from the ground truth: it lacks p-values with a correction name (“p Bonf = .061”), and counts partial eta squared written with its subscript after it (“η 2 p = .05”), “p = .05”, and some table notes and figure legends (“† p < .10, *p < .05”), which this module leaves out, as non-significant p-values. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Background. The Canadian Association of Pathologists recommends keeping glass slides for 20 years, a recommendation made before digital pathology; United States regulation requires 10 years and United Kingdom guidance permits 8 years when the block is kept. Glass is inexpensive to store, whereas digital whole slide images (WSI) incur recurring storage charges, and how often old material is requested has rarely been measured.

A deterministic cost model (2026 Canadian dollars) compared 10-and 20-year rules in illustrative laboratories accessioning 100,000, 500,000, and 750,000 slides/year under three strategies: glass only, glass plus WSI of every slide, and glass plus a hypothetical selective WSI archive. Requests for discarded glass were met by recuts. Because no published data describe second-decade requests, their share was set at an illustrative 0.5%, varied from 0% to 2%, and break-even thresholds were calculated. Off-site glass storage was a secondary analysis.

Results. For glass only, the 20-year rule cost less than the 10-year rule only if more than 0.32% of slides were requested at ages 10 to 19 years (or, at 0.5%, if recuts cost more than CAD 14.54 per slide); at 0.5% it was 6.6% to 6.9% cheaper, and at 0.01% 14.6% to 14.9% more expensive. With WSI of every slide, 20year retention increased annual costs by 35.3% to 36.8% (CAD 153,210/year, academic laboratory). Keeping glass 20 years and WSI 10 years removed this increase. Selective WSI was cheapest only under favourable selection assumptions. Off-site glass storage after the first year added 43% to 62%.

Whether a 20-year glass rule costs more than a 10-year rule depends on how often old glass is requested, which is largely unknown; applied to digital images, a 20-year rule added substantial cost unless more than about 1.7% of slides were requested in their second decade. Flexibility in storage rules can allow laboratories to optimize storage for cost and access to diagnostic material.

Introduction

Surgical pathology laboratories maintain archives of stained glass slides and formalin-fixed, paraffinembedded tissue blocks to support subsequent clinical care, secondary consultations, quality assurance audits, and medicolegal inquiries. Regulatory and accreditation retention minimums were established when physical glass slides and tissue blocks constituted the sole diagnostic record, and mandated retention durations vary substantially across international jurisdictions. In the United States, the Clinical Laboratory Improvement Amendments of 1988 (CLIA) regulations require laboratories to retain histopathology slides for at least 10 years from the date of examination, a standard mirrored by the College of American Pathologists (CAP). 1, 2 In the United Kingdom, guidance from the Royal College of Pathologists and the Institute of Biomedical Science (RCPath/IBMS) permits disposal of histopathology glass slides after 8 years, provided that the tissue block is retained for 30 years and comparable sections can be recut. 3 In Canada, the Canadian Association of Pathologists (CAP-ACP) recommends a 20-year retention period for both surgical pathology glass slides and paraffin blocks, a position formulated in 2005 prior to the clinical introduction of whole slide imaging (WSI) and reaffirmed in the 2024 British Columbia provincial guideline. 4, 5 Certain provincial statutes and community laboratory guidelines specify a 10-year minimum for adult glass slides. 6, 7 We are not aware of survey data on which period Canadian laboratories follow; in our experience, many follow the 20-year CAP-ACP recommendation. That recommendation is twice the 10-year minimum in the United States and Australia and 2.5 times the United Kingdom minimum for slides that can be replaced from the block, and requirements for retaining the corresponding digital images also differ between jurisdictions. [8] [9] [10] [11] During the era of conventional light microscopy, the operational cost difference between a 10-year and a 20-year slide retention policy was considered modest. Physical glass slides are relatively inexpensive to store once filed; personnel labour for initial filing, case retrieval, refiling, and end-of-retention culling accounts for much of glass archive expenditure, 12 and few cases are recalled more than a year after diagnosis. 13 Published data on how often glass is requested 10 or more years after diagnosis are, however, lacking. The transition to primary digital diagnosis alters this cost structure. A surgical pathology slide digitized at 40× magnification generates a compressed file of the order of 1 to 2 GB, depending on tissue area, scanner and compression, and commercial cloud archival storage incurs recurring capacity charges for every additional year of retention. As laboratories digitize high-volume surgical pathology workflows, they must determine whether legacy 20-year glass retention rules should be extended to WSI. Regulatory frameworks have begun to link digital image retention to physical slide requirements. In October 2026, the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services and the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention published a proposed rule under CLIA that would amend 42 CFR §493.1105(a)(7)(i)(B) to require retention of both the histopathology glass slide and, if applicable, the corresponding digital slide image for 10 years; the rule had not been finalized at the time of writing. 8 In Canada, statutory retention schedules do not explicitly address diagnostic WSI, and the CAP-ACP telepathology guideline defers image retention policy to regional jurisdictions. 11 Across Europe and North America, clinical practice remains heterogeneous: some fully digital laboratories retain all diagnostic WSI indefinitely or for the full statutory period, whereas others purge WSI after several months or selectively archive cases of high clinical or educational value. [13] [14] [15] Implementation reports of digital pathology frequently identify digital storage as a major expense, 14, 15 but in the archive and implementation literature we reviewed we did not identify a study that isolates the cost of the retention period itself. We therefore developed an activity-based cost model to evaluate the annual and longitudinal costs of 10-year versus 20-year retention policies in illustrative small community, academic, and large academic pathology laboratories operating glass-only, universal WSI, and selective WSI archives, and compared on-site with commercial off-site storage of glass slides. Because several key inputs have not been measured, we present the results as scenario and threshold analyses rather than as estimates for any particular laboratory.

Methods

Study Design and Reporting Standards

We constructed a deterministic activity-based cost model from the perspective of the anatomic pathology laboratory, capturing the capital, space, vendor, and personnel costs required to store, retrieve, recut, and purge archived diagnostic material. Costs are economic costs: they include allocated staff time, annualized cabinet capital, and the opportunity cost of floor space, which are not all cash expenditures that change immediately with a retention policy. The study was designed and reported in accordance with the Consolidated Health Economic Evaluation Reporting Standards 2022 (CHEERS 2022) statement 16 (Supplementary File 2). All monetary values are expressed in 2026 Canadian dollars (CAD); prices quoted in United States dollars (USD) were converted at the Bank of Canada daily exchange rate of 1.4257 CAD per USD 17 without further inflation adjustment. The spreadsheet model with live formulas is provided in Supplementary File 1, and all input parameters, data sources, and sensitivity ranges are detailed in Supplementary File 3 (Supplementary Tables S1 to S11). Each input is classified in Supplementary Table S1 as a published value, a published value used as a proxy, or an assumption; the request rates, the second-decade request share, the selection rule, labour times, and several cabinet inputs are assumptions because published data are lacking.

Laboratory Tiers, Archival Scenarios, and Retention Policies

To reflect a range of Canadian practice, we modelled three illustrative laboratory volume tiers: a small community hospital laboratory accessioning 100,000 slides per year, an academic medical centre laboratory accessioning 500,000 slides per year, and a large academic health network accessioning 750,000 slides per year, assuming a mean of 3 slides per surgical pathology case. The same value was used for slides per requested case. Each laboratory tier was evaluated across three archival scenarios under both a 10-year and a 20-year retention rule: 1.

Glass-only archive. Physical glass slides are retained on site for 10 or 20 years. Paraffin tissue blocks are retained for 20 years under both rules; consequently, any clinical request for a case aged 10 to 19 years under the 10-year slide rule is fulfilled by pulling the retained tissue block, recutting serial sections, and performing hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining. 2. Glass plus universal WSI archive. Every glass slide is digitized at 40× magnification for primary diagnosis, and both the physical glass slide and its corresponding WSI are retained for the same mandated duration (10 or 20 years).

3.

Glass plus selective WSI archive. Physical glass slides are retained for 10 or 20 years as in Scenario 1, but long-term cloud archival of WSI is restricted to a curated subset of 10% of slides flagged at sign-out (such as complex oncology, consultation, teaching, or medicolegal cases), which is assumed in the base case to capture 90% of digitally servable retrieval requests. This selection performance is hypothetical; no validated method has been shown to achieve it, and the effort of selection was not costed.

Because slide scanning, image management system (IMS) licensing, local area network infrastructure, and short-term hot-tier caching during active primary sign-out are incurred irrespective of long-term archival duration, these upstream operational costs were excluded. In secondary analyses, we evaluated the United Kingdom 8-year retention rule and hybrid medium-specific policies in which physical glass slides and WSI are retained for different durations (10 versus 20 years).

Clinical Retrieval Demand and Block Recutting

Retrieval demand is the least well documented input. Published institutional series report roughly 2.7 to 12 glass requests per 100 accessioned cases per year, 18, 19 and digital access logs show that most first accesses of an image occur within months of scanning, 20 but we found no study reporting requests by specimen age over two decades. We therefore assumed that each year's cases generate 5.0 retrieval requests per 100 accessioned cases while aged 0 to 9 years, where a request is one case (3 slides) and the rate is expressed per 100 cases accessioned per year rather than as an annual probability for every stored case. Of these requests, 85% were assumed to occur within the first 12 months after sign-out and the remaining 15% were spread evenly across years 1 through 9 (0.083% of cases per year at each age). For cases aged 10 to 19 years, a small fraction of slides are requested for late clinical review, recurrence comparison, or medicolegal inquiry. Under a 20-year glass retention policy, these late requests are satisfied by retrieving the archived glass slides. Under a 10-year glass retention policy, the original glass slides have been culled, requiring retrieval of the paraffin block, microtomy recutting, and H&E staining of 3 slides per case (total cost, CAD 79.97 per case request). The share of slides requested at ages 10 to 19 years is unknown. Ali et al. reported that 390 of 162,761 cases (0.24%) reported in a UK department between July 2011 and December 2015 were recalled from off-site storage more than 12 months after diagnosis; 13 this proportion is not an age-specific rate and does not describe the second decade. Radiology data suggest that old images are opened more often when they are readily available. 22 We therefore used 0.5% of an annual slide cohort as an illustrative base value, tested values from 0% to 2%, and report the share at which the 20-year and 10-year rules cost the same. The model assumes that a recut substitutes for the original slide; it does not capture exhausted tissue or recuts that fail to reproduce the original diagnostic material. No retrieval requests were modelled at 20 years or beyond, when paraffin blocks are also eligible for disposal. Whenever a retained WSI was available in the digital archive, retrieval requests were fulfilled electronically, except for a 10% residual fraction of requests requiring physical glass slides for specialized microscopy (such as polarization), recuts, or external slide loan. 18, 23 Resource Valuation and Unit Costs Laboratory technical labour was valued at CAD 0.74 per productive minute, derived from the median hourly wage for a medical laboratory assistant (NOC 33101) in an Ontario economic region (CAD 28.14) 24 inflated to 2026 with the Statistics Canada consumer price index, with an assumed 27% benefits load and an assumed 85% productive time fraction. On-site glass slide filing (0.25 minutes/slide) and endof-retention culling (0.05 minutes/slide) were modelled as one-time per-slide activities invariant to retention duration at steady state. Each physical glass retrieval event was assumed to require 15 minutes of staff labour (locating, pulling, logging, and refiling 3 slides) plus a 0.02% expected slide-loss replacement allowance, yielding CAD 11.11 per glass request (Supplementary Table S1); these labour times are assumptions, as we found no time-motion data. Unstained recutting and H&E staining were valued at USD 16.10 (CAD 22.95) per slide from a US academic core laboratory's internal fee schedule, 25 used as a proxy because we did not identify a published Canadian cost; the marginal in-house cost may be lower or higher. On-site glass archives were housed in modular 12-unit steel slide cabinet stacks (51,000 slides per stack at an assumed 85% usable capacity), 26 with capital costs annualized over an assumed 20year cabinet lifespan; the cabinet price is the list price shown on the cited product page on November 25, 2026 (USD 199 per cabinet), less an assumed 10% volume discount; the price of the cabinet base and the discount are assumptions. Floor space was valued at commercial gross office rent, as a proxy for hospital space, including aisle and circulation allowances. 27 Digital WSI files (1.5 GB per slide at 40× magnification, an assumed value within published ranges 20, 23 ) were archived in Microsoft Azure Archive Blob Storage (Locally Redundant Storage, Canada Central region) under a 3-year reserved capacity commitment (100 TiB and 1 PiB reservation blocks, with file sizes treated as GiB when sizing reservations), the lowest-priced Canadian-region archive option among those evaluated at published list prices. 28 On a continuous per-slide-year basis, cloud archival storage of a 1.5 GB WSI cost CAD 0.0360 per year, or 5.0 times the on-site physical storage cost of a glass slide (CAD 0.0073 per slide-year). Each digitally fulfilled retrieval request triggered asynchronous rehydration of the 3-slide case (4.5 GB) from the archive tier, which takes up to 15 hours at standard priority, 29 retention of the restored copy in hot-tier storage for 6 months, and one internet egress download. Images in active use for primary diagnosis are held in a separate diagnostic cache whose cost does not depend on the retention rule and was excluded. Alternative Canadian cloud archive tiers (Amazon Web Services S3 Glacier Deep Archive, Azure Archive pay-as-you-go, and Google Cloud Archive) were evaluated as scenarios. 30, 31 The model keeps images in Canadian cloud regions; we did not assess contractual, availability, recovery, privacy, or record-preservation requirements beyond data location.

Outcome Measures and Sensitivity Analyses

The primary outcome measure was the modelled steady-state annual cost (CAD/year) of operating a fully populated archive under each retention rule. The difference between the 20-year and 10-year rules represents the recurring annual premium (or saving) of maintaining the second decade of archived material. As a secondary outcome, we calculated the 30-year net present cost (NPC) starting from an empty archive, discounted at the 1.5% real annual reference-case rate recommended by Canada's Drug Agency (CDA-AMC; sensitivity range, 0% to 3%); 32 in this calculation cabinets were charged as an equivalent annual cost while in use, consistent with the steady-state model, and reserved cloud capacity was charged at one third of each 3-year commitment per year. Deterministic one-way sensitivity analyses were performed for the second-decade request share (0% to 2%), selective WSI capture, overall retrieval request rates, residual glass pull rates, retrieval labour time, hot-tier retention duration (3 to 12 months), network egress pathway, and discount rate (Supplementary Tables S5 and S6). Cloud provider tier, 20× scanning (0.5 GB/slide), and off-site glass storage change several linked costs and are reported as scenarios. We calculated two-way break-even thresholds for the second-decade request share against recut cost, and for selective WSI coverage against the selection effort that would offset its saving (Supplementary Figure S3 and Supplementary Table S11). We did not undertake a probabilistic analysis, because the inputs that matter most lack data from which to specify distributions.

Secondary Analysis: Off-Site Glass Storage

Because hospital space is constrained, we compared on-site storage of glass slides with commercial offsite storage. We did not identify a public Canadian tariff for slide storage, so we used the national price list that Iron Mountain submitted to a United States public-sector purchasing cooperative in 2025, converted at the same exchange rate without inflation adjustment. 33 Two contracting models were priced. Under the pathology lines, each case is received, listed, retrieved, and refiled individually (temperaturecontrolled storage USD 2.55 per cubic foot per month; receiving and listing USD 7.27 per case; retrieval and refiling USD 7.02 per case), giving an intake cost of CAD 3.67 per slide. Under the climatecontrolled records lines, slides are sent in cartons indexed by the laboratory (storage USD 2.15 per cubic foot per month; receiving USD 1.97 per cubic foot, the general records line, which differs immaterially from the climate-controlled line of USD 1.90; retrieval and refiling of a 1.2 cubic foot carton USD 8.45), giving an intake cost of CAD 0.21 per slide and a storage cost of CAD 0.0130 per slide-year. Whether these terms apply to pathology material in Canada, and the packing density and courier batching assumed, would need confirmation with a vendor. In the hybrid policy, slides were filed on site for their first year, when most first-decade requests (85%) occur, then packed (0.25 minutes per slide) and transferred to the vendor; requests for older cases were delivered the next day (an assumed 20 requests per courier trip and 6 minutes of in-house labour per request; CAD 24.30 per request under the records lines), and the vendor destroyed slides at the end of retention. We also evaluated transfer to the vendor at accession and valued on-site floor space at new hospital construction cost (CAD 610 per m² per year). Paraffin blocks remained on site in all policies, so recuts always incurred the on-site block retrieval cost.

Results

Scenario 1: Glass-Only Archive

Under a 10-year retention policy, the modelled steady-state annual cost of a glass-only archive was CAD 61,454 for the small community laboratory, CAD 306,897 for the academic laboratory, and CAD 460,160 for the large academic laboratory, corresponding to CAD 0.61 per accessioned slide (Table 1). At the illustrative base share of 0.5%, extending glass slide retention from 10 to 20 years reduced steady-state annual costs by 6.6% to 6.9% across the three laboratory tiers (Figure 1), saving CAD 4,060 per year in the small laboratory, CAD 21,042 per year in the academic laboratory, and CAD 31,562 per year in the large laboratory (CAD 0.57 versus 0.61 per accessioned slide). Under the 10-year rule, the assumed second-decade requests for discarded glass generated 167, 833, and 1,250 case recut requests per year, costing CAD 13,329, CAD 66,643, and CAD 99,964 annually, whereas the cabinets and floor space for a second decade of glass cost CAD 7,417, CAD 36,343, and CAD 54,514 per year. The direction of this result depends on the assumed request share (see Sensitivity Analyses). Under the 10-year rule, personnel labour for initial slide filing and end-of-retention culling accounted for 36% of total annual cost, retrieval labour for 30%, block recuts for 22%, and physical storage (cabinet amortization and floor space) for only 12% (Figure 2).

Scenario 2: Glass Plus Universal WSI Archive

Retaining a 40× WSI of every slide alongside the physical glass archive reduced manual glass pulls by 90% but increased total archive expenditure at every laboratory scale (Table 1). Under a 10-year retention rule, universal WSI plus glass cost CAD 85,114, CAD 416,098, and CAD 624,298 per year across the small, academic, and large laboratories (CAD 0.83 to 0.85 per accessioned slide), representing a 35.6% to 38.5% increase over glass-only retention. Under a 20-year retention rule, total annual expenditure rose to CAD 115,138, CAD 569,308, and CAD 851,191 per year (CAD 1.13 to 1.15 per slide). Thus, whereas at the base request share a 20-year rule reduced the cost of a glass-only archive, extending retention from 10 to 20 years when universal WSI were kept increased annual archive costs by 35.3% to 36.8% (CAD +30,024, +153,210, and +226,893 per year) (Figure 1). Under the 10-year rule, cloud storage capacity charges accounted for 94.3% to 94.5% of total digital cost (96.7% to 96.8% under the 20-year rule), whereas digital retrieval (archive rehydration fees, 6-month hot-tier caching, and data egress) represented 5.5% to 5.7% (Figure 2). Per slide, glass incurred filing at accession (CAD 0.18) and destruction at disposal (CAD 0.04) and then CAD 0.0073 per slide-year for cabinets and space, whereas a 40× WSI incurred CAD 0.036 per slide-year, so the cumulative storage cost of an image exceeded that of its glass slide after 7.7 years (Figure 3). Maintaining both glass and universal WSI for 20 years cost 1.85 to 1.87 times as much annually as maintaining a 10-year glass-only archive.

Scenario 3: Glass Plus Selective WSI Archive

Archiving WSI for a curated 10% subset of slides assumed to capture 90% of digitally servable requests was the least expensive strategy in the base case across all laboratory sizes and retention durations (Table 1), under the hypothetical selection assumption. Under a 10-year rule, selective WSI plus glass cost CAD 53,614, CAD 262,526, and CAD 390,348 per year (CAD 0.52 to 0.54 per slide), yielding net annual savings of 12.8% to 15.2% (CAD 7,840 to 69,813 per year) compared with a 10-year glass-only archive. These savings occurred because a compact cloud footprint (150 to 1,125 TB at 10 years) reduced physical glass pulls within the image retention period by 81.0%. Under a 20-year rule, selective WSI cost 11.4% to 12.1% less than a 20-year glass-only archive and 3.5% to 5.2% less than selective WSI under a 10-year rule, because retained glass served second-decade requests without recuts (CAD 252,409 versus CAD 262,526 per year in the academic laboratory).

Decoupled Media Retention Policies and Jurisdiction Comparisons

When every slide is digitized for primary diagnosis, decoupling the WSI retention period from the physical glass retention period eliminated the cost penalty of a 20-year glass rule (Table 2). In the academic laboratory, retaining physical glass slides for 20 years to satisfy CAP-ACP guidance while purging WSI after 10 years reduced annual expenditure from CAD 569,308 (both media kept 20 years) to CAD 395,057 per year, saving CAD 174,251 annually, and cost CAD 21,042 less than keeping both media for 10 years, because the retained glass served second-decade requests without recuts. Conversely, retaining glass slides for 10 years and selective WSI for 20 years cost CAD 226,969 per year in the academic laboratory, below the cost of either glass-only archive. Applying an 8-year glass rule within this Canadian model (blocks kept 20 years; the United Kingdom provisions for blocks and irreplaceable slides were not modelled) showed that shortening glass retention below 10 years did not reduce costs for a glass-only archive (+3.8% versus 10 years; Supplementary Table S7 and Supplementary Figure S1), because cases aged 8 and 9 years still generate sufficient retrieval demand that block recutting expenses outweigh the 2-year reduction in cabinet storage. The 8-year rule was less expensive than a 10-year rule only when universal WSI were retained (-5.0% to -5.8%).

Sensitivity Analyses

The economic advantage of selective WSI archival was highly sensitive to how well cases are selected at sign-out (Supplementary Table S5 and Supplementary Figure S2). When the 10% archived subset followed an illustrative imperfect capture curve (exponent g = 3, capturing 27.1% of requests), selective WSI approximately broke even with glass-only retention (CAD +1,254, +1,097, and -1,611 per year across the three laboratories). Under random 10% selection, selective WSI was more expensive than glass-only retention by 3.7% to 6.1%. A 10% selective archive must capture at least 26% to 36% of digitally servable requests under a 10-year rule (and 47% to 49% under a 20-year rule) to offset its cloud storage and retrieval costs. Selection effort was not costed: the base-case saving would be cancelled by selection effort costing CAD 0.24 to 0.28 per case accessioned, about 19 to 23 seconds of laboratory assistant time (Supplementary Figure S3B).

The direction of the glass-only result depended on the second-decade request share and on the cost of a recut (Supplementary Table S6, Supplementary Table S11 and Supplementary Figure S3A). The 20-year and 10-year glass rules cost the same when the share of slides requested at ages 10 to 19 years multiplied by the recut and staining cost per slide equalled the cost of storing a slide for a second decade (CAD 0.073 per slide in the academic laboratory). At CAD 22.95 per recut slide, a 20-year glass rule cost more than a 10-year rule when fewer than about 0.32% of slides were requested at ages 10 to 19 years (0.032% of cases per year); at a 0.5% share it cost more if recut and staining cost less than CAD 14.54 per slide. At the previously assumed share of 0.01%, a 20-year rule increased glass-only costs by 14.6% to 14.9%, and at 0.1% by 9.8% to 10.1%, whereas at 1% and 2% it reduced them by 20.8% to 21.0% and 37.9% to 38.1%. For universal WSI, a 20-year rule increased costs at every request share up to approximately 1.7% (by 17.4% to 62.3% across the 0% to 1% range) and reduced them only at 2% (-6.6% to -7.0%).

Across the other one-way analyses and scenarios, the glass-only 20-year rule remained within -8.1% to +1.3% of the 10-year rule at the base request share, whereas the universal WSI increment ranged from +8.6% to +52.5% (Supplementary Table S6). Substituting alternative Canadian cloud archive tiers increased the 20-year universal WSI increment in the academic laboratory from +36.8% (Azure reserved) to +43.5% (AWS Glacier Deep Archive), +43.5% (Azure pay-as-you-go), and +52.4% (Google Cloud Archive); these tiers differ in retrieval times and service terms, so the comparison is of list prices only. A 20× scanning scenario (0.5 GB/slide) reduced the 20-year universal WSI cost increment to 10.9% to 11.3% and made a 10-year universal WSI archive less expensive than a 10-year glass-only archive in the academic laboratory (CAD -18,908/year); 20× images are not interchangeable with 40× images for every diagnostic purpose. Over a 30-year horizon from an empty archive (discounted at 1.5%/year; Supplementary Table S9), the net present cost of a 20-year over a 10-year rule in the academic laboratory changed by -6.2% (CAD -387,870) for glass only versus +19.4% (CAD +1,578,199) for universal WSI.

Off-Site Glass Storage (Secondary Analysis)

Commercial off-site storage increased costs in every laboratory, scenario, and retention rule (Table 3). Keeping glass on site for its first year and then transferring it to the vendor under the records lines increased glass-only steady-state costs by 43% to 45% under a 10-year rule and by 60% to 62% under a 20-year rule (CAD 458,234 versus CAD 285,856 per year in the academic laboratory). Vendor storage cost 1.8 times as much per slide-year as on-site cabinets, each slide was handled twice (filing, then packing for transfer), and each off-site request cost CAD 24.30 versus CAD 11.11 on site. Under the pathology lines, per-case receiving and listing fees raised glass-only costs 7.1-to 7.7-fold. Sending glass to the vendor at accession rather than after 1 year changed glass-only costs by less than 1%. Valuing onsite floor space at new hospital construction cost raised on-site glass-only costs by only 5% to 11%; onsite storage would cost as much as the hybrid off-site policy only if floor space cost approximately CAD 2,027 per m² per year (glass only, 20-year rule, academic laboratory), 7.2 times the office rent used in the base case. For universal WSI, in which 90% of requests are served digitally, vendor storage from accession added 9% to 13% and the hybrid policy 26% to 29%. With glass off site, a 20-year glass-only rule cost 4.2% more than a 10-year rule at the base request share, because off-site storage of the second decade costs more than on-site cabinets.

Discussion

This analysis shows a divergence between the cost dynamics of physical glass archives and digital WSI archives. In a glass-only workflow, whether extending slide retention from 10 to 20 years adds or saves money depends on two inputs that have not been measured: how often glass is requested in its second decade and what a recut costs. A second decade of cabinet storage cost about CAD 0.073 per slide, so a 20-year rule saved money when the request share multiplied by the recut cost per slide exceeded that amount; at CAD 22.95 per recut slide the break-even share was about 0.32%, and no published data establish which side of this threshold Canadian laboratories are on. Physical glass slides are inexpensive to house once accessioned: high-density steel cabinets occupy a modest footprint, representing only 12% of annual glass archive costs at 10 years, while the larger labour costs of initial filing, culling, and early post-diagnostic retrieval do not depend on whether slides are discarded at year 10 or year 20. Digital pathology reverses this relationship. At 40× magnification, storing a 1.5 GB WSI in the lowest-priced Canadian archive tier evaluated costs 5.0 times as much per slide-year as on-site cabinet storage of the physical slide. Because the cost of glass is incurred mostly at filing and disposal, whereas the cost of a WSI accrues every year it is kept, the cumulative storage cost of a 40× image overtakes that of its glass slide at 7.7 years (Figure 3). Consequently, when laboratories archive a WSI of every slide, extending retention from 10 to 20 years increased total annual archive cost by 35% to 37% (CAD 153,210 per year in a 500,000-slide academic laboratory) at the base request share, and the increase persisted at request shares up to about 1.7%.

These findings are relevant to Canadian anatomic pathology laboratories as they transition to digital signout. In Canada, the CAP-ACP published national guidance in 2005, prior to the clinical adoption of WSI, recommending that surgical pathology glass slides and tissue blocks be retained for 20 years, a timeframe reaffirmed in 2024 British Columbia guidance. 4, 5 Provincial hospital regulations and community laboratory guidelines specify a 10-year minimum for glass slides, 6, 7 and we are not aware of data on which period laboratories follow. The CAP-ACP recommendation is twice the 10-year minimum in the United States and Australia and 2.5 times the 8-year minimum for replaceable slides in the United Kingdom. [1] [2] [3] 9 A 20-year standard was financially benign when archives consisted solely of glass, and in our model its net cost for glass depended on late requests; by contrast, automatically extending a pre-digital 20-year retention rule to WSI imposes a substantial and recurring cost on laboratories adopting digital pathology.

The regulatory treatment of diagnostic digital images is now actively evolving.

In October 2026, the United States Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services and Centers for Disease Control and Prevention published a proposed rule under CLIA, not yet finalized, that would amend 42 CFR §493.1105(a)(7)(i)(B) to require clinical laboratories to retain both the histopathology glass slide and, if applicable, the digital slide image for 10 years, and §493.1101(e) to require that digital images be stored under conditions that ensure their preservation, noting that both media may be required to investigate complaints or quality issues. 8 Similarly, the 2025 United Kingdom RCPath/IBMS guidance recommends retaining diagnostic WSI for 8 years, matching the retention period of replaceable glass slides, and explicitly states that digital images are not accepted as a substitute permitting early disposal of physical glass. 3 By contrast, Australia's National Pathology Accreditation Advisory Council (NPAAC) permits glass slides to be discarded after 4 years if the primary diagnosis was rendered on a WSI retained for 10 years, 9 the CAP checklist position ties 10-year WSI retention to the unavailability of the original glass slides, 10 and the 2014 CAP-ACP telepathology guideline defers WSI retention policy to regional requirements. 11 If Canadian accrediting bodies or hospital policies adopt the dual-retention principle of the proposed CLIA rule and the RCPath/IBMS guidance by requiring WSI to be kept for the full duration of the glass slide requirement, a 20-year Canadian baseline would roughly double the image archive, and its storage cost, relative to a 10-year rule.

Our model indicates that Canadian laboratories and guideline bodies could avoid this cost escalation by decoupling digital image retention from physical glass and block retention, where clinical, accreditation, and legal requirements permit. If physical glass slides and paraffin blocks remain available for recutting or repeat scanning should a late clinical review, quality audit, or medicolegal inquiry arise, the economic case for maintaining petabyte-scale cloud WSI archives for two decades is weak; whether glass or a recut is an adequate substitute for the original digital record, including annotations, was not assessed. Empirical studies of digital pathology and radiology archives show that clinical image access decays steeply after the first year following diagnosis, [19] [20] [21] although one radiology archive audit found that 5% of studies older than 10 years were opened over a 2-year period when they remained readily available. 22 In our academic laboratory model, maintaining glass slides and blocks for 20 years while retaining WSI for 10 years reduced annual cost from CAD 569,308 to CAD 395,057, removing the second-decade cost increment at the base request share, while preserving 10 years of digital access for clinical care and 20 years of glass for late requests. Digital access from an archive tier is not immediate: restoring an image takes up to 15 hours at standard priority, 29 so urgent requests would require faster restoration or a cached copy at additional cost. Placing post-diagnostic WSI in deep-archive cloud storage rather than hot storage kept digital retrieval costs (rehydration, 6-month hot caching, and egress) below 6% of the digital budget.

Selective WSI retention, archiving digital images for only a curated subset of cases, was the cheapest strategy in our base case only because we assumed that 10% of slides selected at sign-out would capture 90% of subsequent retrieval requests, a performance that no validated selection method has demonstrated. When evaluated against an illustrative imperfect capture curve (g = 3), 13, 18, 20 selective WSI approximately broke even with glass-only retention, random selection increased net costs, and selection effort costing more than about CAD 0.28 per case would cancel the base-case saving. Selection by case and selection by slide also differ, particularly for large oncology cases. Laboratories contemplating selective digital archival should therefore audit their institutional requisition logs by specimen type, diagnosis, and case age before relying on selective retention to offset storage budgets.

Our results are consistent with prior operational and economic studies of pathology archives. Previous reviews have highlighted that personnel time represents a primary direct cost of physical slide archives, 12 and institutional implementation series from Memorial Sloan Kettering Cancer Center and European centres have documented that digital WSI availability reduces physical slide room requests by 85% to 93%. 18, 19, 23 While comprehensive business-case frameworks for digital pathology have identified longterm image storage as a major capital and operational expense, 14, 15, 34 our study isolates the specific marginal cost of the retention rule itself and explains why the United Kingdom 8-year rule fails to reduce costs in a glass-only workflow: slides aged 8 and 9 years are requested frequently enough that the labour and histology costs of block recutting exceed the marginal savings in cabinet space. The off-site analysis points the same way. At published tariff rates, moving glass to a commercial vendor after the first year added roughly 43% to 62% to glass archive costs, because handling and retrieval, rather than storage space, dominate those costs; off-site storage is therefore justified by a shortage of hospital space rather than by savings.

Several limitations warrant consideration, most of which reflect the paucity of published data on pathology archive use. First, the second-decade request share is an illustrative assumption: we found no published study of how often glass aged 10 to 19 years is requested, and the recall data we cite describe cases recalled more than a year after diagnosis within a 4.5-year calendar window, not the second decade. 13, 22 Because the glass-only result changes direction near a 0.3% share, we report the full range and the break-even share. Second, recut and staining were costed from a US academic fee schedule used as a proxy; the glass-only result also reverses if this cost falls below about CAD 15 per slide at a 0.5% share, and a recut may not reproduce the original diagnostic material. Third, baseline retrieval demand (5 requests per 100 cases accessioned per year, 85% in the first year) was synthesized from tertiary-centre and digital access series rather than local logs, and the same slide count was used for accessioned and requested cases. Fourth, the selective WSI rule is hypothetical, the capture curves are illustrative, and the effort of selection was not costed. Fifth, labour times, the benefits load, productive time, cabinet prices, and cabinet life are assumptions, and floor space was valued at office rent as a proxy for hospital space. Sixth, our model excludes scanner acquisition, image management software, network upgrades, and active diagnostic caching, and therefore must not be interpreted as a total cost-of-ownership model for digital pathology. Seventh, cloud and vendor costs were calculated from public list prices on a single reference date; the off-site prices are a United States public-sector tariff, archive tiers differ in retrieval time and service terms, and enterprise discounts or future price changes would alter absolute digital costs, although the 2-fold volume ratio between a 20-year and a 10-year steady-state digital archive will persist. Eighth, storage of paraffin blocks was not modelled. Finally, this economic evaluation does not adjudicate the clinical, educational, or medicolegal adequacy of any specific retention duration. For these reasons the results should be read as scenario and threshold analyses that laboratories can update with local data, not as estimates of costs in any particular laboratory.

Conclusion

Under the modelled assumptions, whether extending glass retention from 10 to 20 years adds or saves money depends on how often glass is requested in its second decade and on the cost of recutting discarded material; at the illustrative base values, avoided recuts offset the additional cabinet and space costs, and the result reverses below a second-decade request share of about 0.32%. Extending universal 40× WSI retention from 10 to 20 years increased archive costs by 35% to 37% in the base case, predominantly through storage charges. Separate retention periods for glass and images, and selective WSI retention, warrant further evaluation, with local measurement of retrieval patterns by specimen age and of selection performance, and with clinical, accreditation, and legal requirements considered separately.
