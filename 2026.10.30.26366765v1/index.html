Power Analysis Check

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). Statcheck might still miss one-sided tests that are described in another sentence, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Abstract

Objective: 22q11.2 deletion syndrome (22q11DS) is the leading genetic cause of palatal anomalies, presenting a heterogeneous spectrum of clinical manifestations that complicates early diagnosis. No study has systematically investigated the co-occurrence of symptoms spanning cardiovascular, neuropsychiatric, craniofacial, immune, endocrine, renal, and familial domains within a single cohort using unsupervised learning methods for 22q11DS stratification. This study proposes a pipeline to identify subgroups and associated symptom patterns from binary clinical data, aiming to improve screening and referral for genetic testing.

Design: Cross-sectional, retrospective study comparing three unsupervised clustering frameworks-K-medoids, DBSCAN, and Latent Class Analysis (LCA)-followed by association rule mining applied to individuals with confirmed deletion.

Participants: A cohort of 297 individuals with suspected 22q11DS.

Main Outcome Measures: Cluster separability (silhouette coefficient), characterization of phenotypic subgroups, and sensitivity/specificity of the current screening protocol stratified by subgroup.

Results: LCA achieved the highest cluster separability (silhouette = 0.165), yielding four distinct subgroups: Cardiac-predominant (C0), Craniofacial-palatal (C1), Neurodevelopmental-psychiatric (C2), and Mild-craniofacial (C3). Cluster coherence was confirmed by medical specialists. Quantitative protocol assessment revealed adequate overall sensitivity, with markedly lower performance for C2 and C3, identifying profiles at higher risk of being missed by current screening criteria.

Conclusions: These findings provide a data-driven basis for recalibrating symptom weights and referral thresholds, improving sensitivity for underperforming subgroups while reducing unnecessary referrals, optimizing the balance between diagnostic coverage and the financial burden of genetic testing.

1 Introduction

Anomalies are present in approximately 3% to 5% of all live births and include craniofacial malformations such as orofacial clefts, craniosynostosis, mandibulofacial dysostosis, and macrosomia 1 among the most common alterations. 1, 2 The 22q11.2 deletion is the leading genetic cause of palatal anomalies and the second most frequent cause of developmental delay associated with congenital heart defects, in addition to being the most common microdeletion syndrome. [3] [4] [5] It is a rare disease, with an estimated prevalence ranging from 1:3,000 to 1:6,000 live births, whose diagnosis often depends on costly genetic testing. 6, 7 Therefore, assembling a large cohort of cases for clinical data analysis becomes challenging.

The 22q11.2 deletion syndrome is highly heterogeneous, with manifestations ranging from subtle to complex, even within members of the same family. This clinical variability complicates diagnosis, particularly when signs are not evaluated collectively. 6, 8 The challenge is further amplified by phenotypic overlap with other conditions such as CHARGE, Noonan and Wiedemann-Steiner syndromes, [9] [10] [11] and by the recognition that not all observed manifestations are necessarily attributable to the deletion itself -additional genetic or environmental influences, including epigenetic modifications and mosaicism, 8, 10 may independently affect gene expression and contribute to the observed phenotypic variability. Given this complexity, final diagnosis relies on genetic testing, including FISH, MLPA, CMA, and Whole Exome Sequencing, each offering different levels of genomic resolution. Previous research indicates that, although CMA is roughly ten times more expensive than other approaches in the Brazilian public healthcare system, it still offers the most extensive genome-wide assessment. 5, 12 In this context, Brazil's Craniofacial Project (BCFP) was established in 2003 as a voluntary multicenter network aimed at generating scientific evidence on craniofacial anomalies, 13 including the creation of the Brazilian Database on Craniofacial Anomalies (BBAC) for standardised populationbased clinical data collection. 14 Based on longitudinally collected data through the BBAC, a clinical guideline was developed to optimise decision-making in suspected 22q11.2 deletion cases, incorporating a hierarchical diagnostic approach in which individuals are first filtered based on clinical criteria, followed by FISH and MLPA as primary genetic screening tests, with CMA reserved for cases that remain under clinical suspicion following negative initial results. 12 Individuals fulfilling the proposed screening protocol criteria were 3.82 times 12 more likely to receive a confirmed 22q11.2 deletion diagnosis, with the complete hierarchical strategy estimated to generate savings of $175,135.13 in the Brazilian public health system (SUS) for each group of one thousand individuals. 12 Nevertheless, diagnostic accuracy reached up to 34.7% depending on the combination of manifestations analysed, indicating that a substantial proportion of cases remains undetected, suggesting that data-driven approaches may help to capture phenotypic patterns that the current guideline has yet to formalise.

From a machine learning standpoint, previous studies have applied unsupervised methods to characterise heterogeneity in 22q11.2 deletion syndrome. Using behavioural, morphological, and neuroimaging data from 50 paediatric individuals, k-means clustering identified distinct neurobehavioral subgroups. 15 Hierarchical clustering applied to DNA methylation data revealed three epigenetically distinct subgroups within the deletion spectrum. 16 Latent class analysis of cognitive profiles further demonstrated gradients of overall functioning rather than discrete phenotypic subtypes. 17 Nevertheless, although these studies highlight the clinical heterogeneity of the syndrome and demonstrate the utility of clustering for subgroup identification, no previous study has conducted a comprehensive multivariate analysis combining cardiovascular, neuropsychiatric, craniofacial, immune, endocrine, renal, and familial features simultaneously. Furthermore, no systematic comparison of clustering frameworks has been conducted to identify the most suitable approach for binary clinical data in this population, nor have co-occurring symptom patterns been examined across these domains in conjunction with a quantitative assessment of the clinical screening protocol currently used. 18 Hence, this study aims to investigate the clinical profiles of individuals using BBAC collected data 14 in a cohort of 297 individuals evaluated for 22q11.2 deletion syndrome, including 94 confirmed positive cases. Clustering analysis was performed to identify subgroups, comparing three different unsupervised methods: K-Medoids (distance-based), 19 DBSCAN (density-based), 20 and Latent Class Analysis (probabilistic model-based). 21 Subsequently, the best-performing technique was selected, and association rule mining was applied within each generated cluster to further characterise the clinical patterns of the individuals with confirmed deletion in each subgroup. To validate the clinical relevance of the resulting subgroups, the identified clusters and association rules were reviewed by medical specialists in clinical genetics and craniofacial anomalies, who assessed their coherence with established syndromic knowledge and current diagnostic practice.

The present study investigates whether clinically meaningful subgroups exist within the 22q11.2 deletion syndrome population based on co-occurring symptom patterns, and whether an unsupervised pipeline can elucidate these structures to support improved screening and genetic referral in a real clinical setting. To this end, this paper is organised as follows: Section 2 describes the dataset, preprocessing pipeline, clustering framework comparison, and association rule mining; Section 3 reports the identified latent classes, MCA visualisations, and their clinical feature profiles; Section 4 presents the medical specialist validation of the identified subgroups, their implications for the current craniofacial screening protocol, limitations, and future directions; Section 5 concludes the paper.

2.2 Feature Selection and Filtering

Feature selection was performed by the responsible medical team based on the representative characteristics defined in the 22q11.2 screening protocol, 18 resulting in 57 clinical features, which limited the inclusion of attributes associated with other craniofacial anomalies present in the dataset. 2.3 Feature Engineering and Data Preprocessing

The distribution of each clinical attribute was analysed to assess missing data prevalence. Nine attributes exhibited a high proportion of missing values, however their exclusion was not feasible given their clinical relevance, and removing affected samples would have disproportionately reduced the number of individuals with a confirmed deletion. Attributes with more than two missing ob-servations were therefore one-hot encoded, expanding each categorical variable into multiple binary support columns, whereby missing values are represented as a distinct binary indicator rather than treated as absence of the feature. 23 Individuals with confirmed, typical, or atypical deletions were classified as positive, following the literature consensus. 24, 25 Individuals with missing values for the primary outcome variable (Deletion Positive) were excluded from the analysis (n=5), as deletion status is required for cluster validation and screening protocol assessment, and its absence could introduce noise into the clustering procedure. To reduce redundancy and dimensionality, intellectual disability was defined as positive if any of the following sources reported a positive finding: formal neuropsychological evaluation, medical staff clinical assessment, or the general intellectual disability. Tetralogy of Fallot was binarized such that all positive presentations (classic, stenotic, or unspecified) were coded as positive, while absence of the condition was coded as negative.

The two validation attributes, Deletion Positive and Meets Screening Criteria, were excluded from the clustering procedure, resulting in a final feature space of 61 binary dimensions used for analysis. Given the binary nature of the data, conventional outlier detection methods are not directly applicable. Outlier removal, which consists of excluding observations that deviate substantially from the overall data distribution, was performed by computing the Euclidean distance of each observation from the global centroid, excluding observations beyond the 90th percentile. This threshold was chosen empirically by observing the effect of different thresholds on the Multiple Correspondence Analysis (MCA) projections (described in Section 2.5), selecting the value that removed only observations visibly distant from the main data distribution while minimising sample loss, given the inherent scarcity of clinical data in rare disease cohorts. Following removal, 29 samples were excluded -including 12 confirmed positive cases -reducing the positive subset from 94 to 82 cases.

2.4 Clustering paradigms

Clustering method selection was guided by the binary nature of the data and the features with potential outliers. 22 Hence, three algorithmic frameworks were evaluated -distance-based, densitybased, and probabilistic.

K-medoids is a distance-based clustering algorithm that partitions data into k clusters, using actual samples as cluster representatives (medoids) rather than computed centroids, an appropriate property for binary clinical data, where a mean point may not correspond to any real individual profile. 19 The alternate optimisation method was adopted for cluster. 19 Hamming distance (Equation 1) was chosen as the dissimilarity metric, as it is naturally suited to binary feature spaces: it does not assume continuous data and assigns equal weight to the presence and absence of each clinical feature. The indicator function 1[x il ̸ = x jl ] equals 1 when the values differ between the two samples. The silhouette coefficient (Equation 2) was used to select the optimal number of clusters, where a(i) represents the average intra-cluster distance and b(i) the minimum average inter-cluster distance. The term max{a(i), b(i)} is used for normalization, constraining the silhouette coefficient to the interval [-1, 1].

The algorithm requires two parameters: ε, defining the neighbourhood radius, and MinPts, the minimum number of samples required to form a dense region. The k-NN distance plot, 20 computed for k ∈ {3, 5, 7}, was used to guide the selection of ε, informing a search range of ε ∈ {0.09, 0.10, 0.11, 0.12}. ε and MinPts∈ {3, 5, 7, 10, 15} were then jointly selected via a systematic grid search, choosing the configuration that maximised the number of clusters while keeping the noise proportion low, as the goal was to separate individuals into distinct phenotypic subgroups.

Finally, Latent Class Analysis (LCA) is a probabilistic clustering method widely used in medical research for identifying subphenotypes and uncovering latent group structures within clinical datasets. 21, 26 Rather than assigning each individual to a single cluster, LCA estimates the posterior probability of membership across all latent classes.

LCA requires the number of classes k as an input parameter. The optimal value of k was determined using model selection criteria, specifically the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC)(Equation 3) and the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC)(Equation 4). In these formulations, L denotes the maximized log-likelihood of the model, p represents the number of free parameters, n is the sample size, and K corresponds to the number of latent classes. Lower AIC and BIC values indicate better model fit, to balance model interpretability and cluster granularity while avoiding overfitting, both criteria were jointly considered in the selection of the optimal number of classes since BIC tends to avoid complexity and AIC to maximise it. Relative entropy was additionally examined as a measure of classification certainty, with values closer to 1 indicating clearer separation of individuals into latent classes (Equation 5), where N is the total number of individuals, K the number of latent classes, and p ik the posterior probability of individual i belonging to class k.

Based on these metrics, the best parameter configuration was selected by considering both statistical performance and the interpretability of the resulting clusters. To compare clustering approaches, we used the silhouette score (Equation 2) with Hamming distance (Equation 1) and Multiple Correspondence Analysis (MCA) for cluster visualization. The goal was to identify the configuration with the highest silhouette score and the most visually separated and interpretable clusters. Multiple Correspondence Analysis (MCA) is a dimensionality reduction technique designed for categorical and binary data, 27 projecting observations into a lower-dimensional space while preserving the main variance structure. In this study, MCA was applied to visualise cluster separability by projecting individuals into a two-dimensional space, where similar clinical profiles appear closer together and overlapping groups become visually apparent.

2.6 Association Rules

Based on the best clustering technique, each cluster was treated as an independent dataset for association rule extraction. Association rules identify combinations of clinical features that frequently co-occur, expressing the likelihood that the presence of a set of symptoms is associated with a given outcome. The Apriori algorithm 28 was applied to all individuals within each cluster, including both deletion-positive and deletion-negative cases, with the consequent fixed as confirmed deletion status and the number of antecedents limited to four to ensure interpretability. A minimum confidence of 0.8 and minimum support of 0.04 were defined, where confidence measures the conditional probability of the consequent given the antecedent, and support reflects the frequency with which a rule appears in the dataset. Given the heterogeneity of individuals, support values were empirically determined to balance the number of generated rules while avoiding extremely rare patterns. The top 10 rules per cluster were selected based on lift (Equation 6), defined as the ratio of rule confidence to the marginal support of the consequent -where A denotes the set of antecedent symptoms, C the consequent (deletion-positive status), and lift > 1 indicates that the co-occurrence is more frequent than expected by chance. For instance, a rule may state that the co-occurrence of Long face, absence of Tetralogy of Fallot, Intellectual disability, and Velopharyngeal insufficiency is associated with a confirmed deletion.

3 Results

The proposed pipeline identified four distinct groups within the 22q11.2 deletion syndrome cohort via LCA, which achieved the highest cluster separability among the three evaluated frameworks (silhouette = 0.165). MCA projections (Fig. 3) corroborate this finding, showing clearer group separation under LCA, although similar results for k-medoids. K-medoids resulted in relatively low silhouette scores, ranging from 0.070 to 0.126. Although k=4 achieved the highest silhouette value, indicating the best separation among the evaluated configurations, the overall scores remained low. This suggests limited cluster compactness, indicating a possible overlap between clusters.

DBSCAN yielded the weakest performance among the three evaluated frameworks. Comparison across parameter combinations showed that DBSCAN frequently collapsed observations into a single dominant cluster under lower-noise configurations, limiting its utility for detailed phenotypic profiling. The best identified configuration, ε=0.09 and min_samples=3, produced three clusters but resulted in 79 noise samples (30% of the cohort), with two markedly small clusters and the majority of observations concentrated in a single group, as illustrated in Fig. 3. LCA results indicated that k=4 provided the optimal balance between statistical fit and clinical interpretability, assessed through the degree of cluster separation and the coherence of the resulting subgroup profiles with established medical knowledge. While the 3-cluster solution achieved the lowest BIC (AIC = 10492.30, BIC = 11151.74), the difference relative to the 4-cluster solution (AIC = 10334.11, BIC = 11214.54) was small (∆BIC = 62.8). Although BIC applies a stronger penalty for model complexity and marginally favoured the simpler solution, the 4-cluster model was preferred as it produced more granular and distinct combinations of features. AIC continued to decrease with increasing k, suggesting overfitting beyond k=4 when considered alongside BIC. The 4-class solution yielded a relative entropy of 0.987, comparable to the highest value observed (E rel =0.989 at K=6), indicating high classification certainty despite lower model complexity.

3.1 Cluster Characterisation

Symptom prevalence was computed as the proportion of individuals presenting each clinical sign within each cluster, as illustrated in Fig. 4.

Cluster 0 (C0) comprised 11 cases with a confirmed deletion, representing 14% of the cluster. It was almost exclusively defined by congenital heart defects (98%), with approximately 80% of individuals also presenting facial signs, including Tetralogy of Fallot (46%), bulbous nose (44%), and hooded eyelids (41%). Notably, orofacial cleft was completely absent in this cluster despite being present in all others, as was velopharyngeal insufficiency, and schizophrenia, features that emerge in other subgroups.

Cluster 1 (C1) represented the largest positive subgroup, comprising 32 cases with a confirmed deletion (49% of the cluster). While sharing the cardiac involvement observed in Cluster 0 as congenital heart defects were present in 86% of cases, this cluster was distinctly characterised by the co-occurrence of structural orofacial anomalies -most notably orofacial cleft (72%), which was completely absent in Cluster 0. Additional predominant features included facial signs (60%) and alar hypoplasia (40%), suggesting a craniofacial-palatal phenotype combined the cardiac profile.

Cluster 2 (C2) diverged markedly from the preceding groups, representing the most neurodevelopmentally distinct profile in the cohort with 19 cases with a confirmed deletion. Its defining feature was intellectual disability, present in 89% of cases with a confirmed deletion, a prevalence observed in no other cluster at comparable levels. Congenital heart defects were substantially lower (32%) and Tetralogy of Fallot nearly absent (3%), in contrast to Clusters 0 and 1. This cluster also recorded the highest prevalence across all groups for orofacial cleft, hypernasal speech, and learning disability, consolidating a craniofacial-neurodevelopmental phenotype largely separated from cardiac involvement.

Cluster 3 (C3) comprised 20 individuals with the deletion, representing 32% of the subgroup, and was distinguished by a markedly lower prevalence of congenital heart defects (3%) and Intellectual disability (6%), both common in Cluster 2, alongside a more heterogeneous overall profile, with no single feature exceeding 80%. Its predominant characteristics were other facial signs (63%), learning disabilities (44%), orofacial cleft (47%), and other psychiatric abnormalities (44%).

3.2 Association Rule Mining per Cluster

Following the identification of distinct subgroups via unsupervised clustering, association rule mining was applied to characterise the clinical profiles of individuals with a confirmed deletion within each cluster, restricting the consequent to confirmed deletion status. For instance: Hooded eyelids (Yes), Velopharyngeal insufficiency (Yes), Global developmental delay (Yes) ⇒ Deletion positive (Yes), indicating that the co-occurrence of these three features is associated with a confirmed 22q11.2 deletion within that subgroup. In Cluster 0, the identified rules reached a lift of 5.8, reflecting a markedly non-random cooccurrence of these clinical features within this cluster. The predominant associations involved ventricular septal defect, congenital heart defect, and other facial signs. This subgroup yielded only four association rules meeting the defined thresholds, despite the selection criteria allowing up to ten rules per cluster.

In Cluster 1, association rules reached lifts approaching 2.0, indicating that the associated clinical features co-occurred at approximately twice the frequency expected under statistical independence within the cluster. The rules show that the presence of isolated atrial septal defect already represented a strong diagnostic suspicion. Furthermore, combinations involving ventricular septal defect associated with craniofacial and neurodevelopmental manifestations also demonstrated high discriminative power. Overall, the rules suggest the existence of two main phenotypic profiles within this cluster. The first corresponds to a classic cardiofacial phenotype, characterised by ventricular septal defect, orofacial cleft, learning disability, and congenital heart defect. The second represents a predominantly facial phenotype, marked by bulbous nose, prominent nasal bridge, high nasal bridge, and the presence of hooded eyelids.

In Cluster 2, association rules achieved lifts approaching 2.0, indicating that the associated clinical features co-occurred at nearly twice the frequency expected under statistical independence within the cluster. This subgroup was characterised by a predominantly craniofacial and velopharyngeal phenotype, marked by long face, hypernasal speech, velopharyngeal insufficiency, orofacial cleft, and global developmental delay. Notably, the combination of long face, velopharyngeal insufficiency, and absence of Tetralogy of Fallot was sufficient to exclusively identify individuals with a confirmed deletion, highlighting palatal and speech features as strong phenotypic markers. Additional patterns involving hypernasal speech, orofacial cleft, and global developmental delay further reinforced the relevance of velopharyngeal and craniofacial manifestations in this subgroup. Cluster-specific facial features, including tubular nose and hooded eyelids, were also associated with positive diagnosis in the absence of severe congenital heart defects, consolidating a predominantly craniofacial and neurodevelopmental profile.

In Cluster 3, association rules yielded lifts up to 3.9, indicating that the associated clinical features co-occurred at nearly four times the frequency expected under statistical independence. This subgroup represents a predominantly craniofacial and neurodevelopmental phenotype, characterised by learning disability, velopharyngeal insufficiency, long face, and orofacial cleft. Notably, the combination of velopharyngeal insufficiency and learning disability demonstrated strong discriminative power for positive diagnosis even in the absence of congenital heart defects. Additional rules suggested an association between orofacial cleft and global developmental delay.

4 Discussion

The diagnostic journey of 22q11.2 deletion syndrome is frequently marked by prolonged referral across multiple specialists before a correct diagnosis is established. To optimise cost and referral decisions, a hierarchical approach has been adopted within Brazil's Craniofacial Project (BCFP) partner institutions: clinical screening filters individuals first, followed by low-cost genetic tests, with CMA reserved for cases remaining under suspicion. 18 However, the screening protocol evaluates clinical signs independently, without accounting for symptom co-occurrence -which, as this study demonstrates, differs meaningfully across subgroups, leaving individuals with mild or atypical presentations at risk of being missed.

This study employed Latent Class Analysis to identify subgroups based on the clinical signs of 22q11.2 deletion syndrome, further characterised through symptom prevalence analysis and association rule mining to uncover co-occurring patterns among individuals with a confirmed deletion. Four phenotypically distinct clusters were identified: Cardiac-predominant (C0), Craniofacial-palatal (C1), Neurodevelopmental-psychiatric (C2), and Mild-craniofacial (C3). A certain degree of overlap between subgroups was anticipated given the well-documented clinical heterogeneity of the condition, 8 and was evidenced by the low overall silhouette scores observed for both LCA and K-medoids, as well as the limited cluster separation visible in the MCA projections. Previous studies applying clustering to cognitive profiles in 22q11.2 have reported continuous gradations rather than discrete groups; 17 however, by examining a broader set of clinical features spanning cardiac, craniofacial, immunological, and neurodevelopmental domains, this study captures a wider spectrum of phenotypic variation. While some overlap between subgroups is expected, the concentration of cardiac features in C0 and neurodevelopmental features in C2 potentially suggests that incorporating a broader spectrum of clinical characteristics across multiple domains may yield more distinct subgroup separation when applying clustering methods to individuals with suspected deletion. The following clinical interpretation reflects the integration of data-driven findings with the assessment of medical specialists in clinical genetics and craniofacial anomalies.

Cluster 0, characterised by a predominance of congenital heart defects, highlights the importance of considering 22q11.2 deletion as a differential diagnosis in cardiac presentations; however, the relatively low deletion-positive rate within this subgroup corroborates existing evidence that isolated congenital heart disease alone is an insufficient predictor of deletion status. 29 Notably, the higher positivity observed in cases of ventricular septal defect within this cluster complements prior descriptions in the literature, 18, 30 while also revealing that this association is not yet adequately reflected in the current screening protocol 18 -suggesting a concrete opportunity for targeted refinement.

In the profile drawn by Cluster 1, cardiac malformations showed their relevance in the investigation of suspected cases of 22q11.2 deletion when associated with characteristic craniofacial anomalies and dysmorphisms (bulbous nose, prominent nasal bridge, high nasal bridge and presence of hooded eyelids). This result is of great relevance in pointing to a high positivity rate when associating cardiac defects with common craniofacial alterations, such as orofacial cleft and dysmorphisms, which may not be analyzed together during the diagnostic odyssey. Thus, this cluster outlines an important clinical parameter in characterising suspected cases of 22q11.2 deletion, defining a relevant phenotypic profile to be disseminated across various health care settings. In addition, although the cluster reinforces the importance of the presence of several signs in cases of positive suspicion for the syndrome, the pattern found reveals a positive trend in cases of isolated atrial septal defect and in the absence of conotruncal defects, a type of alteration already associated with a higher prevalence of 22q11.2 deletion. 31 Cluster 2 highlighted the relevance of suspecting 22q11.2 deletion in cases of craniofacial anomalies, such as orofacial cleft and velopharyngeal insufficiency, especially when associated with global developmental delay. The importance of diagnostic investigation in cases of orofacial cleft was already evident in the literature, since 22q11.2 deletion is the most common genetic cause for these alterations. 7 However, the present analysis shows an important association of positive cases with the concomitant presence of global developmental delay and learning disability. Notably, these findings reinforce that the absence of congenital heart disease should not reduce clinical suspicion, as the craniofacial and neurodevelopmental profiles identified across clusters represent strong, literaturesupported diagnostic indicators of the 22q11.2 deletion in their own right. 32 In addition, in cases of hypernasal speech, velopharyngeal insufficiency, long face associated with developmental deficit, the absence of Tetralogy of Fallot proved to be a good differential for positive cases, corroborating the distinct phenotypic profile already presented in the literature and compatible with another cluster. 31 Cluster 3 was the most heterogeneous among the groups, with a low prevalence of specific characteristics and focusing the analysis on facial signs and anomaly.

When comparing individuals who meet the screening criteria proposed by Monteiro et al., 18 across cluster profiles, the protocol showed varying levels of sensitivity and specificity, as indicated in Table 2. Sensitivity reflects the protocol's ability to correctly identify true individuals with a confirmed deletion, minimising missed diagnoses, while specificity reflects its capacity to avoid unnecessary referrals of individuals who do not carry the deletion. C0 demonstrated the most balanced performance (sensitivity 72.7%, specificity 72.9%), indicating that the protocol is best suited for cardiac-predominant presentations. C1 and C2 presented low specificity (25.0% and 27.8%) but relatively higher sensitivity (75.0% and 57.9%), meaning that while most positive cases are captured, a substantial proportion of unaffected individuals are unnecessarily referred for genetic testing -contributing disproportionately to testing costs. In contrast, C3 showed higher specificity (58.3%) but the lowest sensitivity (50.0%), suggesting that half of the individuals with a confirmed deletion who present with this phenotypic profile may be missed by current screening criteria. Clusters C1 and C2, which presented the lowest specificity, are likely the primary drivers of unnecessary genetic referrals, directing a substantial proportion of unaffected individuals toward costly confirmatory testing. Conversely, individuals in C2 and C3, with sensitivities of 57.9% and 50.0%, face a higher risk of remaining undiagnosed -circulating across multiple specialists without receiving a confirmed 22q11.2 diagnosis, perpetuating the diagnostic odyssey described in the literature. 33 The Neurodevelopmental-psychiatric subgroup (C2) presents the most critical challenge, combining low sensitivity and low specificity -meaning that individuals with a confirmed deletion who present with this profile are simultaneously at risk of being missed by current screening criteria and of triggering unnecessary referrals among individuals without the deletion who exhibit overlapping neurodevelopmental presentations. This may occur since these manifestations are also common across a broad range of neurodevelopmental and psychiatric conditions, limiting their specificity for syndrome recognition. 34 In future work, these findings may support the integration of symptom co-occurrence patterns as an additional scoring criterion within the existing protocol, building upon established approaches to phenotype-driven clinical decision support for rare disease diagnosis. 18 Individuals whose clinical profiles match the co-occurrence patterns identified in deletion-positive subgroups could receive greater diagnostic weight even when individual symptoms do not meet referral thresholds -effectively transforming the current rule-based scoring system into a pattern-aware referral mechanism. This may improve early identification of 22q11.2 deletion syndrome, particularly for phenotypic profiles currently underserved by uniform screening criteria, and represents a tractable direction for extending the pipeline toward a clinically deployable decision support tool.

Several data-related limitations should be acknowledged. The unbalanced dataset may bias the results, and focusing only on positive symptoms limited the identification of negative patterns. One-hot encoding increased data dimensionality, potentially overweighting some clinical variables during clustering. Additionally, keeping features with overlapping definitions may have introduced collinearity and redundancy, a trade-off accepted to preserve clinical relevance and avoid further sample loss due to incomplete data in other variables.

These results come from a single Brazilian reference database, and the specific subgroups and symptom patterns may differ in other populations and healthcare settings. The pipeline itself, however, is not tied to this cohort and can be applied to other databases, although the phenotypic profiles it identifies may vary. Replication in independent cohorts is needed.

5 Conclusion

The present study proposed a five-stage unsupervised pipeline for the phenotypic stratification of 22q11.2 deletion syndrome, a rare condition presenting marked clinical heterogeneity. LCA achieved the highest cluster separability (silhouette = 0.165), identifying four phenotypically distinct subgroups: Cardiac-predominant (n=11), Craniofacial-palatal (n=32), Neurodevelopmental-psychiatric (n=19), and Mild-craniofacial (n=20). Assessment of the current screening protocol revealed ad-equate overall sensitivity; however, sensitivity was markedly reduced in the Neurodevelopmentalpsychiatric and Mild-craniofacial subgroups (57.9% and 50.0%, respectively), indicating that a substantial proportion of individuals with a confirmed deletion within these profiles may be missed by current referral criteria. Validated by medical specialists, these findings suggest that phenotypic stratification may support more targeted referral pathways, reducing unnecessary genetic examinations and improving the cost-effectiveness of diagnostic screening for 22q11.2 deletion syndrome.
