No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

Abstract

Background Genomic surveillance of respiratory viruses typically relies on sequences from individuals seeking health care, and whether these sequences represent the viruses circulating in the community is not well-characterized. We aimed to compare the diversity, proportion, and dynamics of influenza, respiratory syncytial virus (RSV), and SARS-CoV-2 between clinical and community samples.

In this retrospective comparative genomic analysis, we used whole-genome sequences from community-based surveillance studies in the Seattle area, and clinical sequences from the Washington State Department of Health. We compared clade proportions and dynamics, sequence-cluster diversity, non-synonymous mutations, and Ct values between clinical and community samples.

INTRODUCTION

Viral whole genome sequencing (WGS) has transformed infectious disease surveillance and outbreak response over the past decade 1, 2 . The ability to rapidly characterize pathogen genetic diversity provides critical insights for public health decision-making, such as tracking transmission chains during outbreaks, identifying emerging variants and monitoring antimicrobial resistance 3 . Genomic data now routinely complement traditional epidemiological approaches, enabling higher-resolution understanding of disease dynamics than was previously possible with phenotypic or partial gene sequencing methods alone 4.

The COVID-19 pandemic catalyzed an unprecedented expansion of pathogen genomic surveillance infrastructure globally 5 . In the United States, initiatives like the CDC's Baseline and National SARS-CoV-2 Strain Surveillance programs, combined with substantial investments in state and local public health laboratory infrastructure created and expanded sequencing capabilities across the country allowing genomes to be rapidly shared through public repositories like NCBI GenBank 6 . This infrastructure has enabled extension of routine genomic surveillance to other respiratory pathogens of public health importance, particularly respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) and influenza (flu) virus 7 . These three pathogens represent the major viral causes of acute respiratory illness and collectively impose substantial morbidity, mortality, and healthcare burden, especially in the elderly and immunocompromised 8, 9 . In 2019, an estimated 33 million children younger than 5 years contracted RSV and over 101,000 children died due to RSV-associated causes 10 . Influenza causes infections in 5-10% of adults and 20-30% of children 11 . RSV genomic surveillance has gained urgency following the licensure of RSV vaccines and monoclonal antibody prophylaxis, creating a need to monitor for immune escape variants 12.

Viral epidemiology requires population-level surveillance of viral strains, and multiple strategies for measuring the presence of different strains at scale have been utilized for respiratory viruses. This includes sampling specific populations, such as hospitalized patients 13 , random sequencing of positive samples 14 , and sequencing of wastewater samples 15 . Genomic epidemiology of respiratory viruses has identified developments that are crucial for public health responses, such as inter-continental transmission of new pathogenic SARS-CoV-2 variants, nearly complete replacement of RSV clades between seasons, and prediction of emerging variants of influenza virus [16] [17] [18].

The COVID-19 pandemic was a unique period in which broad, community-level testing was widely available and samples from community members were routinely sent to laboratories for testing and sequencing. As this community testing infrastructure has waned, laboratories generally no longer have access to community samples, and genomic surveillance now relies predominantly on specimens from individuals who present for clinical care. Whether this clinical sampling frame is representative of the viruses circulating in the broader population remains unclear, mainly due to the lack of studies that track viral dynamics in both hospital and community settings 19 . Past studies have utilized localized wastewater sampling followed by targeted clinical screening to contain SARS-CoV-2 outbreaks 20 , demonstrating that these data can be highly impactful. Given the high cost of viral genomic surveillance and the reduced funding for the sequencing of respiratory viral isolates post-pandemic, public health agencies need data to decide how to judiciously apply limited resources for the measurement of respiratory virus transmission.

In this study, we analyzed the viral diversity of seasonal RSV and influenza virus strains and pandemic SARS-CoV-2 strains isolated from both community members and from individuals seeking healthcare in the same time frame and geographic location. The overall diversity of SARS-CoV-2 clades differed between sampling modalities but remained similar for RSV and influenza virus, highlighting that pandemic-scale infections in hospitalized populations do not completely reflect the pattern in the community at large. Measurement of the viral diversity over time revealed that intra-clade temporal dynamics also differ between clinical and community infections for SARS-CoV-2. Finally, we found that for SARS-CoV-2, sampling from clinical specimens led to the identification of more diverse strains than sampling from the community, whereas RSV and influenza showed no significant difference. Together, these results suggest that clinical sampling may adequately capture the diversity of seasonal respiratory viruses, but that during pandemic-level transmission, viral populations sampled in hospital settings can diverge from those circulating in the community. Future surveillance programs should prioritize a diverse sampling strategy.

METHODS

Study design and setting

We conducted a retrospective comparative genomic analysis of three respiratory pathogens (SARS-CoV-2, RSV, and influenza) using whole genome sequencing data from clinical and community surveillance sources in Washington state. The study period spanned 2019 to 2022, encompassing multiple respiratory virus seasons and capturing periods of varying transmission intensity for each pathogen. Sequences were classified into two surveillance categories based on the context of specimen collection: clinical surveillance, comprising specimens collected from individuals seeking medical care at healthcare facilities; and community surveillance, comprising specimens collected through population-based sampling programs that did not require participants to be seeking medical attention.

Data sources

Sequences and metadata were derived from four main sources: the Seattle Flu Study, Seattle Coronavirus Assessment Network, Husky Coronavirus Testing, and publicly available sequences from Genbank.

The Seattle Flu Study (SFS) was a multi-arm surveillance study established in 2018 to characterize the genomic epidemiology of respiratory pathogens in the Puget Sound region 19 . SFS enrolled participants through multiple community-based channels including kiosks at public venues (libraries, transit stations, community centers), home-based self-swab collection, and outreach at schools, workplaces, daycare centers, and homeless shelters. In addition, the clinical arms enrolled individuals seeking care at four regional hospitals and multiple clinics. For this analysis, SFS specimens collected during hospital-based care visits were classified as clinical, whereas specimens collected at outpatient clinics or kiosks set up within these locations (Sea Mar Community Health Centers, UW Hall Health, and Seattle Children's Hospital outpatient clinic) were classified as community samples. Participants with symptoms associated with acute respiratory illness (ARI) provided nasal swabs specimens and linked information including demographics, symptoms/illness characteristics, and other metadata. Specimens underwent multiplex RT-PCR testing for a panel of respiratory pathogens, with positive samples reflexed to whole genome sequencing for influenza, RSV, and SARS-CoV-2.

The Seattle Coronavirus Assessment Network (SCAN) was launched in March 2020 as an extension of SFS infrastructure to enable population-based SARS-CoV-2 surveillance 21.

SCAN utilized home-based self-collection of nasal swabs, with test kits distributed through mail, drive-through sites, and community partner locations. The program was designed to capture SARS-CoV-2 infections across the community regardless of symptoms, testing access, or healthcare utilization, with enrollment open to all King County residents during the study period.

SCAN contributed RSV and influenza sequences from specimens tested on multiplex respiratory panels.

The Husky Coronavirus Testing (HCT) program 22 provided SARS-CoV-2 screening to students, staff, and faculty at the University of Washington from September 2020 to July 2023.

Participants completed daily attestations via email or text message, and those reporting symptoms, recent travel, or exposure to a known SARS-CoV-2 case were offered SARS-CoV-2 testing. Participants could also request testing for any reason in the absence of exposure or symptoms. Participants self-collected nasal swabs at campus testing sites or returned them via campus drop-boxes or courier. Samples were tested by PCR and SARS-CoV-2-positive specimens with Ct values under 30 underwent sequencing using a whole genome amplicon sequencing approach (Illumina COVIDSeq) as described previously 22 . HCT contributed SARS-CoV-2 sequences to the community surveillance arm of this study.

Together, these studies sampled infections across a spectrum of severity, including asymptomatic and mildly symptomatic cases that would not typically present to healthcare settings. While the programs were concentrated in the Seattle metropolitan area, this geographic focus provides comparability with clinical surveillance data, which was also predominantly from this region.

Clinical Surveillance

Clinical surveillance sequences for SARS-CoV-2 only were obtained from the Washington State Department of Health (WA DOH) genomic surveillance system, which aggregates sequences from diagnostic testing performed at clinical laboratories throughout the state 14 . The primary sources of clinical sequences included hospital-based diagnostic laboratories, commercial reference laboratories (e.g., LabCorp, Quest Diagnostics), and the University of Washington Clinical Virology Laboratory, which serves as a major reference laboratory for healthcare facilities throughout the Pacific Northwest.

Although the WA DOH surveillance system receives specimens from patients presenting to a range of healthcare settings, including emergency departments, inpatient hospital units, outpatient clinics, and urgent care facilities, the sequences provided by WA DOH for this analysis were specifically from individuals hospitalized as a result of SARS-CoV-2 infection, rather than from all individuals presenting for care. Testing was performed for diagnostic purposes as part of routine clinical care, and specimens were subsequently sequenced through WA DOH's genomic surveillance program or sequencing efforts at submitting laboratories. The selection of specimens for sequencing varied across the study period and across laboratories, and ranged from sequencing of all positive specimens, random sampling of positives, to targeted sequencing of specimens from hospitalized patients or suspected vaccine breakthrough cases.

Classification of sequences as clinical was performed using the Washington Disease Reporting System (WDRS), WA DOH's integrated disease surveillance platform. Sequences were classified as clinical if they were linked to a diagnostic test ordered in the context of a healthcare encounter, as documented in WDRS. No additional metadata on these samples was available.

Study Period and Temporal Considerations

Temporal matching between surveillance sources is critical for valid comparison, as pathogen diversity changes rapidly over time. The study period was selected to maximize temporal overlap between community and clinical surveillance data sources while capturing sufficient genomic diversity for meaningful comparison. For time-stratified analyses, we selected periods with at least 3 months of continuous detection in both surveillance sources. For SARS-CoV-2, the study period of 2020 to 2022 encompasses multiple variant waves, including the emergence and spread of Delta (21J) and the Omicron sub-lineages BA.1 (21K) and BA.5 (22B). The HCT program operated from September 2020 to September 2022, defining the window for community surveillance data availability. For RSV and influenza, the study period of 2019 to 2021 encompasses two respiratory seasons (2019/2020 and 2020/2021), with influenza sequences derived from the 2019/2020 season only, with SFS/SCAN enrollment occurring throughout this period.

Sequence data and phylogenetic analysis

Specimens from community studies (SFS, SCAN, HCT) were processed and sequenced using standardized protocols within each program (see above). Generally, respiratory specimens were tested by multiplex RT-PCR, and pathogen-positive specimens with cycle threshold (Ct) values below thresholds defined for the study or surveillance program were selected for whole genome sequencing. Sequencing and genome assembly approaches depended on the study, timeperiod and submitting laboratory, and assembled genomes were deposited to NCBI Genbank.

Accessions for sequences used in this analysis are listed in Table S1. Downloaded sequences were assigned lineages/clades using Nextclade v3.20.0 23 using the "sars-cov-2/wuhan-hu-1/orfs" dataset for SARS-CoV-2, "rsv/a/EPI_ISL_412866" and "rsv/b/EPI_ISL_1653999" datasets for RSV-A and RSV-B respectively, and "flu/h1n1pdm/ha/MW626062", "flu/h3n2/ha/EPI1857216", "flu/b/ha/KX058884" for influenza H1N1, H3N2, and B respectively.

Genomes with a quality score of less than 100 (corresponding to 'good' and 'mediocre' quality classes in Nextclade) were included in the final dataset.

Phylogenetic trees were constructed using the Augur pipeline with default settings 24.

Trees were pruned with treeshrink v1.4.0 25 and visualized using ggtree 26 . SNP distances were calculated using snp-dists v1.2.0.

Statistical analysis and data visualization

All statistical analyses were performed in R v4.6.1 and figures were generated using ggplot2 v4.0.3. Differences in the proportional abundance of viral clades between community and clinical samples were assessed using a χ² test of independence, with Cramér's V reported as a measure of effect size. Differences in clade dynamics over time between the two sampling sources were assessed using a Kruskal-Wallis test, and differences in Ct values between community and clinical samples were assessed using a Wilcoxon rank-sum test. All p-values were adjusted for multiple comparisons using the false discovery rate method. Significance was defined as q < 0.05. RESULTS

An aggregate dataset of clinical and community samples across multiple studies and data sources was assembled for this analysis.

The Seattle Flu Study (SFS), Seattle Coronavirus Assessment Network (SCAN), and Husky

Coronavirus Testing (HCT) studies were conducted from 2018-2022. In these community-based surveillance studies, we identified and sequenced thousands of respiratory viruses before, during, and after the COVID-19 pandemic. For each specimen, metadata recorded whether the virus was collected from a community participant or from a patient presenting to clinical care.

Community sampling occurred through mail-in swab kits, in-person kiosks across the Seattle metropolitan area, and enrollment at schools, workplaces, childcare centers, and shelters (Figure S1). Additionally, the Washington State Department of Health (DOH) provided 1002 SARS-CoV-2 sequences known to be from individuals hospitalized due to SARS-CoV-2 infection (see methods).

For this analysis, we assembled an aggregate dataset containing sequences from these four sources over selected timeframes where there was adequate representation of both community and clinical samples and circulation of the viruses of interest. All sequences were downloaded from NCBI Genbank (Table S1). Our initial dataset included 538 RSV sequences (278 community and 260 clinical sequences, Figure 1A), 1506 flu sequences (260 community and 1246 clinical sequences, Figure 1C) and 8660 SARS-CoV-2 sequences (5923 community and 2737 clinical sequences, Figure 1E. All RSV sequences (100%) and 8624 SARS-CoV-2 (99.6%) sequences passed our filtering criteria (see Methods). RSV sequences were collected during two seasonal spreading events in the winter months of 2019/2020 and 2020/2021 (Figure 1B) and the maximum number of samples collected in a single day was 14 specimens. Flu sequences were collected over a single transmission season in 2019/2020 and the maximum number of samples collected in a single day was 37 (Figure 1D). Conversely, the SARS-CoV-2 isolates were collected during the 2020-2022 SARS-CoV-2 pandemic and the maximum number of samples collected in a single day reached 182 specimens (Figure 1F). RSV sequences demonstrated seasonal variation with both RSV-A and RSV-B circulating in the 2019/2020 season, while only RSV-B was detected in the 2020/2021 season (Figure 2A-B).

Due to the seasonality of RSV, we first analyzed the overall RSV clade composition in each season. We found no significant difference in the proportion of clades between community and clinical samples (Figure 2A; χ² test with Cramér's V = 0.027-0.10, all p adj > 0.05). A similar analysis for flu clades also showed no significant differences in clade proportions between community and clinical samples (Figure 3A; χ² test with Cramér's V = 0.023 -0.067, p adj = 0.029 -0.36). These results demonstrate that, at the level of viral clades, RSV and flu viral diversity in hospitalized patients frequently resembles that in community members. In contrast, the proportion of viral clades in SARS-CoV-2 isolates differed significantly between community and clinical samples (Figure 4A; χ² test with Cramér's V = 0.0069 -0.22, p adj = 1.25 x 10 -96 -0.52). Clades 21J (Delta) and 21K (Omicron BA.1) were isolated more frequently in community samples compared to clinical samples, while clade 22B (Omicron BA.5) was more abundant in clinical samples.

Next, we compared the temporal dynamics of each clade between clinical and community specimens. For RSV, the dynamics of isolates in the A.D.3 clade were significantly different in clinical and community members (Figure 2B; Kruskal-Wallis test, test statistic = 0.097-0.55 and all p adj > 0.05). No significant differences in dynamics were observed within flu clades (Figure 3B We next examined diversity at finer genetic scales by examining clustering of community and clinical sequences on phylogenetic trees. In maximum likelihood phylogenies of RSV-A and RSV-B (Figure 2C), and similarly in flu phylogenies for H1N1, H3N2, and B (Figure 3C), and SARS-CoV-2 (Figure 4C), we observed high level of intermixing between community and clinical isolates suggesting that these sequences derived from common ancestors.

Next, we hypothesized that clinical infections presented a bottleneck to diversity because only some infections progress to a severity warranting hospitalization and the viruses causing these infections are selected from a bigger, more diverse pool of viruses in the community. To test this, we compared the diversity of sequence clusters (genomes within <5 SNPs of one another) between clinical and community sequences through a rarefaction analysis, which accounts for differences in sample sizes through repeated sampling 27.

Rarefaction curves of RSV and FLU clusters revealed no strong difference between sources (Figure 5A-B). However, SARS-CoV-2 clinical sequence clusters were more diverse than community sequences (Figure 5C). This suggests that sampling hospitalized patients reveals greater genetic diversity than sampling pandemic viruses in the community, possibly because participants in community screening were more homogeneous in their exposures, and thus sampled fewer transmission chains, than individuals hospitalized for SARS-CoV-2 infection.

Non-synonymous substitutions, including antibody-resistance mutations, differ in frequency between clinical and community samples for RSV and SARS-CoV-2 but not flu.

We then compared the abundance of non-synonymous amino acid mutations in flu, RSV, and SARS-CoV-2 between clinical and community populations, as single substitutions can alter drug resistance or pathogenicity. For RSV (Table S2), 37 non-synonymous mutations differed significantly in frequency between the two sources (n = 373 clinical and 165 community sequences; χ² test with Cramér's V = 0.13-0.32, all p adj < 0.05). All 37 were in RSV-A and all were enriched in clinical samples, concentrated in the attachment glycoprotein G (15 mutations) and the polymerase L (14 mutations); the largest differences were at G:I265L (66.2% of clinical versus 31.5% of community sequences) and L:L1438Q (66.5% versus 32.7%; p adj = 4.7 × 10¹²). For SARS-CoV-2 (Table S3), 80 mutations differed significantly between sources (n = 2,717 clinical and 5,907 community sequences; Cramér's V = 0.09-0.24, all p adj ≤ 2.0 × 10¹), but enrichment was bidirectional with 51 mutations more frequent in clinical samples and 29 in community samples. The strongest clinical enrichments were S:L452R (43.6% versus 20.1%; Cramér's V = 0.24, p adj = 4.6 × 10¹¹¹), S:F486V (21.1% versus 6.2%) and M:D3N (18.4% versus 4.7%), whereas spike changes characteristic of Omicron BA.1, including S:Q493R (44.2% versus 66.6%), S:L212I and S:ins214EPE (both ~26% versus ~47%), were more frequent in community samples. The bidirectional pattern for SARS-CoV-2 is consistent with the clade-composition differences described above, whereas the uniformly clinical enrichment in RSV-A was not accompanied by a detectable difference in clade proportions. For flu, no mutations differed significantly in frequency between clinical and community sequences.

We extended this analysis by comparing the enrichment of SARS-CoV-2 spike protein mutations associated with monoclonal antibody resistance (Figure 5D; χ² test with Cramér's V = 0.11 -0.24, p = 4.55 x 10 -111 -2.22 x 10 -27 ). We hypothesized that antiviral resistance mutations would be preferentially enriched in clinical isolates due to the higher usage of antivirals in this population. Surprisingly, certain mutations (R346K, S371L, Q493R, G496S, N856K) were more prevalent in community samples. These results demonstrate that clinically relevant mutations can be widespread in the community.

Clinical samples have higher viral loads than community samples

Given that clinically attended infections are more severe compared to infections in community members, we hypothesized that the C t value (inversely proportional to number of viral genome copies) in clinical samples would be lower than in community samples. We compared the range of C t values per clade for the subset of sequenced samples where both the C t values and lineage assignments were available for flu (1462, 97%), RSV, (538, 100%), and SARS-CoV-2 We found that high-level viral diversity at the level of clades was significantly different between these two sources for SARS-CoV-2 isolates but not for RSV or flu. This could be due to number of factors. First, the transmission and spread of SARS-CoV-2 during our sampling period was a pandemic-level mass infection of a susceptible population while RSV transmission occurred in a seasonal, periodic manner in a population with some level of pre-existing immunity. RSV transmission also occurred after suppression from the COVID-19 pandemic and before anti-RSV monoclonal antibodies were available. Basic transmission differences such as the number of people being infected and those who are more likely to be severely infected, such as infants with RSV 28 , may factor into this difference. RSV, influenza, and SARS-CoV-2 are also genetically distinct viruses that replicate through different mechanisms. Another contributing factor to this finding could be the unequal sampling between clinical and community sites, especially for the SARS-CoV-2 isolates 29 . Oversampling certain groups, for example on college campuses, would yield more closely related viruses, underestimating community diversity and skewing community clade frequencies toward locally circulating strains.

Differences in sample size may also increase power to detect clade differences for SARS-CoV-2, and lower-viral-load community infections may be underrepresented among sequenced samples because sequencing success depends on viral load.

We hypothesized that severe infections would result in a bottleneck and therefore expected within-clade diversity to be lower in clinical samples. However, rarefaction analysis of genomic clusters showed the opposite pattern for SARS-CoV-2, with clinical sequences more diverse than community sequences, and no clear difference between sources for RSV or flu.

One explanation for this may be that individuals hospitalized for SARS-CoV-2 infection were drawn from a broader cross-section of the population than participants in community testing programs, as noted above, and therefore captured a wider range of transmission chains.

Another is that clinical infections are associated with a higher viral load, which likely increases the intrapersonal diversity of viral strains in an individual and a greater composite divergence from the original infecting strain. Nosocomial spread of respiratory viruses is well-documented, and multiple studies have found direct lines of transmission of RSV and SARS-CoV-2 between patients using genomic analyses; however, such transmission would be expected to enrich a few outbreak strains and lower diversity, and WA DOH sequences came from individuals hospitalized because of SARS-CoV-2 infection rather than infected after admission. [30] [31] [32] The number of mutations between viral clades can be large and accumulation of these mutations can occur slowly, while single substitutions within a viral clade can rapidly lead to drug resistance or increased pathogenicity 33, 34 . We therefore compared the frequency of individual non-synonymous substitutions, including mutations associated with monoclonal antibody resistance, between clinical and community sequences for each of the three viruses.

At this resolution the two sources also diverged, but not in the direction expected. RSV showed non-synonymous mutations enriched in clinical sequences despite indistinguishable clade proportions, while SARS-CoV-2 showed differentially abundant mutations with enrichment in both directions, and no flu mutations differed significantly. Several spike substitutions associated with monoclonal antibody resistance were in fact more prevalent in community samples, indicating that clinically relevant mutations circulate widely outside of care settings and may be detected late by clinical sampling alone.

This study has a number of limitations. First, sampling differed markedly across programs in geography, demographics, symptom status, and specimen-selection criteria; although rarefaction addresses unequal depth through repeated sampling at sequential levels of observations, it cannot fully remove these other biases. Second, the comparison rests on three viruses sampled in one region over a limited window, including the atypical pandemic period, which perturbed RSV seasonality and may limit generalizability. Third, assembly and specimenselection methods varied among the laboratories contributing clinical sequences, which we mitigated with uniform quality filtering but could not fully standardize. Fourth, Ct values were available for only a subset of specimens and are sensitive to sampling timing and swab type.

Fifth, the level of sequences available for each virus differed greatly. This can inflate the power of analyses used here and confounds direct comparisons between each virus. Sixth, clade definitions differ in granularity across the three viruses: the Nextclade nomenclature for SARS-CoV-2 partitions diversity into more, finer-scale clades than those currently defined for RSV and influenza, so more within-clade diversity is collapsed for the latter. Finer partitioning increases the opportunity to detect differences in clade composition between sampling sources, which may partly contribute to the larger differences observed for SARS-CoV-2. Our cluster-based rarefaction analysis, which uses a uniform SNP threshold independent of clade nomenclature, partially mitigates this concern.

DISCUSSION Respiratory virus surveillance often aims to identify new variants which may have increased drug resistance or pathogenicity. Most sequences in public repositories, however, come from specimens collected when people seek medical care, as opposed to community-based programs that sample individuals irrespective of care-seeking. It is unclear how well either source of sampling represents the true diversity of viruses circulating in a population. In this work, we compared multiple levels of viral diversity between samples acquired from community and clinical sampling programs for RSV, flu, and SARS-CoV-2.
