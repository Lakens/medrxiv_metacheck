Patterns are designed to capture:

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Background: Researchers increasingly use large language models (LLMs) to assess risk of bias in randomized trials, but how task-specific fine-tuned models compare with frontier models remains uncertain. Objective: To develop RoB-BERT, a fine-tuned RoB 2 assessment system, and to compare its agreement with human assessments against that of three frontier LLMs. Methods: RoB 2 rates five bias domains and the trial overall as low risk, some concerns or high risk, based on answers to 22 signalling questions. RoB-BERT answers each question, quotes the supporting sentences from the trial report, and applies the official RoB 2 algorithm. We trained RoB-BERT on 5,304 reviewer-checked assessments, none from a test trial, and compared it with Claude Fable 5.1, GPT-6 Astra and Gemini 4 Argon, each given identical prompts and text, against 419 published human assessments (ROBoto2, LogiMed-RoB and Cochrane reviews) made without AI assistance. Each reference was the single published judgement for that trial, made by one assessor or agreed by two, rather than an expert reference standard, so we also report agreement between two human assessors of the same trial. Results: In ROBoto2, pairs of human assessors who rated the same trial agreed on 61.2% to 64.7% of domain judgements (κ 0.15 to 0.35) and 46.2% to 50.0% of overall judgements (κ 0.15 to 0.17); across the 185 ROBoto2 trials, RoB-BERT agreed with the human judgement on 67.4% (κ 0.35) and 53.5% (κ 0.20). Across all 419 assessments, domain agreement was 67.9% (κ 0.29) for RoB-BERT, 65.0% (κ 0.24) for Claude Fable 5.1, 52.7% (κ 0.21) for GPT-6 Astra and 66.7% (κ 0.26) for Gemini 4 Argon; overall agreement was 46.1% (κ 0.16), 47.3% (κ 0.17), 31.0% (κ 0.03) and 46.5% (κ 0.14), respectively. For domains the reference rated high risk, sensitivity was 22.2% for RoB-BERT, 23.1% for Claude Fable 5.1, 41.9% for GPT-6 Astra and 24.8% for Gemini 4 Argon, with specificity of 92.5%, 92.8%, 80.0% and 95.0%. On the 52 ROBoto2 trials rated by two assessors, the second assessor's sensitivity for the reference assessor's high-risk domains was 23.1% (specificity 90.3%) and RoB-BERT's was 30.8% (specificity 92.7%). RoB-BERT located 95.7% of its quotations verbatim in the report and cost US$0.005 per trial, 2% to 6% of the frontier models' cost. Conclusions: RoB-BERT agreed with single human assessors at least as closely as two human assessors agreed with each other, and matched or exceeded frontier LLMs at a fraction of their cost. The results show the poor reliability of human assessors using RoB 2 and the need for consensus ratings, with RoB-BERT potentially serving as one rater.

Introduction

The Cochrane risk-of-bias tool for randomized trials (RoB 2), a popular option for rating risk of bias in randomized trials, requires experienced reviewers to answer 22 signalling questions across five bias domains for every outcome of interest (1, 2). In one systematic review, assessment took a mean of almost six hours of staff time per study (3), and reliability is limited even between trained assessors: in one study, agreement between assessors on the overall judgement was κ -0.15 before they used detailed implementation guidance and κ 0.42 once they had (4). As trial volume grows and living reviews shorten update cycles, the assessment step represents a bottleneck in evidence synthesis.

Large language models (LLMs) offer one strategy to address that bottleneck, and several groups have evaluated general-purpose chatbots against human RoB 2 assessments (5) (6) (7) (8). Generalpurpose LLMs read long reports and write readable rationales, but their answers vary between runs, sometimes describe procedures the report does not contain, change with prompt wording, and add a non-trivial cost per trial (5, 6, 8). Smaller classification models of the BERT family (9-11) are fast and, because they choose among fixed answers rather than write text, do not invent content, but they cannot cite the sentence behind an answer and have shown limited agreement with experts (12) (13) (14) (15).

Whether supervised fine-tuning on reviewer-audited assessments can match or surpass frontier models on a shared benchmark remains untested. A small, local, deterministic system performing comparably to hosted frontier models could reduce the cost and improve the privacy and reproducibility of large-scale evidence synthesis.

We developed RoB-BERT, a hybrid of a fine-tuned generative reader and a biomedical encoder feeding the published RoB 2 decision algorithm, and compared it with Claude Fable 5.1, GPT-6 Astra and Gemini 4 Argon on a fixed external benchmark of 570 primary human assessments under a pre-registered protocol.

Methods

Study design and reporting

We report this developer-led model development and external evaluation study according to TRIPOD-LLM (16). The evaluation followed the RoB-BERT Fine-Tuned Models Protocol, version 1.3, which we registered on the Open Science Framework before running any comparator (https://doi.org/10.17605/OSF.IO/47ZYX). We did not amend the protocol. We fixed reference assessments, prompts, settings, and model weights before external runs began and verified them by checksum (Supplementary Table S1).

Development corpus

The development corpus consisted of 5,304 RoB 2 worksheets, each an outcome-level assessment of an individually randomized parallel-group trial, from four sources (Table 1): a reviewer-audited trial collection, audited assessments from 18 systematic review projects, a reviewed selection from a larger pool of draft assessments, and 16 assessments completed independently by one investigator and reserved for evaluation. We grouped worksheets by trial so that reports or outcome rows of the same trial could not fall on both sides of a split, giving 4,343 trial groups partitioned into training (4,237 worksheets), validation (519), internal test (532) and the independent investigator set (16). Before training we screened the corpus against every benchmark trial by identifier, DOI and title; two training rows matched and were removed, so no benchmark trial contributed to training or validation.

System architecture

RoB-BERT v6.1 has three parts (Figure 1). The reader is Qwen3.8-27B with a QLoRA adapter (17), trained in two supervised stages on the training worksheets using FlashAttention and gradient checkpointing to fit long inputs on a single accelerator (18, 19). Given the trial text, outcome and effect of interest, it emits a structured record for each of the 22 signalling questions: the answer on the five-category RoB 2 scale (yes, probably yes, probably no, no, no information), one or more verbatim quotations, and a short rationale.

Decoding follows a grammar that enforces the RoB 2 question flow, including branches that depend on earlier answers, at temperature 0 with extended thinking off. Quotations are checked against the source text after generation; unlocatable quotations stay in the output but are counted as unlocated. The system normalizes the input, splits it into sections and truncates it by section priority to a 16,384-token context when the article is longer, allowing up to 3,072 new tokens for the answer.

The encoder is BioClinical ModernBERT-large, a long-context biomedical encoder (14), with a classification head that predicts the answer category for each signalling question from the first 8,192 tokens of the same input. We trained three seeds on a fixed schedule and selected the seed with the highest validation macro-F1 (W-s32) before external evaluation; its outputs are unscaled softmax probabilities (15). In the combined system the encoder acts as a gate: wherever its probability for an answer reaches 0.85, that answer replaces the reader's.

We set the threshold on the validation split and did not change it afterwards.

Signalling-question answers from every AI system, including the frontier models, pass through the same implementation of the published RoB 2 decision tables to produce domain judgements and the overall judgement (2). Domain-level and overall differences between systems therefore reflect only differences in signalling-question answers, not differences in how the algorithm was applied.

External benchmark and reference assessments

Our external evaluation used the RoB 2 AI Benchmark, version 1.0.0, a fixed, publicly available collection of RoB 2 assessments with corresponding trial texts, unchanged since its release (https://github.com/CoreSR/rob2-ai-benchmark). We assembled it from three published sources: the ROBoto2 dataset, which contains both unassisted assessments and assessments made with an LLM's suggestions on screen (assisted) (5); LogiMed-RoB Track A (13); and

Cochrane review assessments used in an earlier evaluation of GPT-4, restricted to the 41 trials with open-access full text (6). Unless otherwise specified, human assessments in this report are the unassisted assessments. The reference for each row is the single judgement recorded by the source project, made by one assessor or agreed by two; no reference was adjudicated by an independent expert panel. We verified labels and texts against published checksums before any run.

The primary population comprised the 570 primary assessments with available trial text: 185 ROBoto2 unassisted, 192 LogiMed, and 42 Cochrane rows (419 pooled unassisted rows, 418 trials), plus the 151 ROBoto2 assisted rows, which the protocol specified for separate analysis because an LLM assisted in producing their reference (Table 2). ROBoto2 rows assess the primary result at the study level; LogiMed and Cochrane rows name a specific outcome. A sensitivity population included every scored outcome row with text (1,008 rows in all: LogiMed 600, Cochrane 72, ROBoto2 336).

Frontier comparators

We accessed Claude Fable 5.1, GPT-6 Astra and Gemini 4 Argon through the OpenRouter API (20) with identical settings: high reasoning effort, a 16,000-token output limit and provider defaults otherwise. Each request carried a fixed system prompt, identical for every model (checksummed and printed in the protocol) and a user message containing the RoB 2 tool block with the 22 signalling questions, the outcome, the effect of interest and the frozen benchmark text, so every system saw exactly the text given to RoB-BERT. Rows with byte-identical prompts shared one request (961 unique requests for 1,008 rows); all primary rows were unique. An output with fewer than 22 parsed answers was requested once more; a second failure remained invalid. We did not edit any output, and we recorded the time, settings, returned model and provider, token counts, billed cost, finish reason and every attempt. Preliminary runs of Claude Fable 5.1 and GPT-6 Astra on the 336 ROBoto2 rows on 12 October 2026, made with different output limits and reasoning settings while the pipeline was being built, are excluded from all comparisons; we report their agreement with the protocol runs only as a measure of run-to-run variation (Supplementary Table S2).

Human inter-assessor agreement

ROBoto2 includes trials assessed by more than one person: 52 trials with both an unassisted and an LLM-assisted assessment, and 10 trials with several LLM-assisted assessments (60 assessor pairs). For the 52 trials, the unassisted assessment is the benchmark reference. We used agreement within these pairs as the reference point for each system's agreement; these values are descriptive and carry no confidence interval. In a post hoc analysis not specified in the protocol, we dichotomized domain and overall judgements as high versus not high. On the 52 trials with both an unassisted and an LLM-assisted assessment, we calculated the sensitivity and specificity of the assisted assessor's judgements, taking the unassisted assessor's judgements as the reference, and then the same measures for each AI system against that unassisted assessor. We also calculated sensitivity and specificity for a high-risk overall judgement for every AI system against the reference on the 419 pooled rows. Confidence intervals used the bootstrap described under Statistical analysis.

Outcomes

The primary outcomes were percentage agreement and Cohen's κ (21, 24, 25) between each system and the reference at three levels: signalling question (five categories, ROBoto2 trials, unanswered questions scored as no information per benchmark convention), domain (low, some concerns, high) and overall judgement (low, some concerns, high). Secondary outcomes were error direction (overall judgements below the reference category, sensitivity and specificity of high-risk domain judgements against the reference, and domains called high that the reference did not), output validity, the proportion of reader quotations located verbatim in the article (22, 23), and cost and time per trial.

Statistical analysis

We scored a row without a valid set of signalling answers (due to output failure, parsing failure, or incompleteness) as disagreement at every level. Its predicted class was an additional category, invalid, which never occurs in the reference, reducing agreement and adding a column to the κ confusion matrix with zero chance agreement. A frontier output was valid when all 22 answer lines parsed; a reader output was valid when it completed a parsable record under the grammar.

We derived confidence intervals from 2,000 cluster-bootstrap resamples using the trial as the unit (26, 27), seed 20261103, and percentile 95% intervals. Within each population and cohort, we scored every AI system on the same resampled trials, so paired differences (RoB-BERT minus comparator) use identical resamples. We excluded replicates with undefined κ from that interval and reported their count. Intervals are pointwise and unadjusted for multiplicity; we performed no hypothesis tests, pre-specified no non-inferiority margin, and interpreted intervals directly (28). Component analyses compared the combined system with the reader alone and with the encoder alone on the same resamples.

We pre-specified four sensitivity analyses: SA1, rows where every AI system returned a valid output; SA2, all 1,008 outcome rows; SA3, articles where reader preprocessing truncated input versus those it did not; and SA4, the Cochrane cohort excluding the RECOVERY platform trial comparisons (NCT04381936), which affected one primary row. For descriptive context only, we report the agreement of GPT-4 (2023) on the same Cochrane rows, noting that GPT-4 received the PDF, registration and protocol rather than text alone (6). Hosted-model costs are OpenRouter billings and times are per-request wall time at eight concurrent requests; reader time and cost are batch generation on one H200 accelerator at US$5.00 per hour, excluding model loading; the encoder runs on a workstation CPU at no accelerator cost. The analysis code reproduced the benchmark scorer's agreement, κ and error-direction counts exactly, and all code, prompts, request records and prediction files are released with the benchmark.

Post hoc adjudication of disagreements

To determine whether disagreements reflected model error, reference error or defensible differences in judgement, two reviewers (TP and DZ) adjudicated a sample of the 672 domainlevel disagreements (341 rows) in the 419 pooled rows: all 26 Cochrane rows (34 domains) and random samples of 40 ROBoto2 rows (73 domains) and 40 LogiMed rows (81 domains), 106 rows and 188 domains in all. Working from written RoB 2 guidance and blinded to provenance, the reviewers received case packets containing the trial text, outcome and both structured assessments (judgements, signalling answers, quotations and rationales) with origin-disclosing phrases removed, and treated registries and protocols as unavailable. The reviewers divided the sample between them and worked independently; each recorded their own judgement before unblinding, then classified the disagreement as RoB-BERT correct, reference correct, both defensible, both wrong or indeterminate, coding error type and severity. Counts pool both reviewers' judgements; there was no consensus step and no overlap from which to measure inter-reviewer agreement. For benchmark Cochrane trials also assessed in other Cochrane reviews, we verified trial identity by DOI, PMID or title and compared judgements across reviews.

Benchmark runs and output validity

Every AI system covered all 570 primary and all 1,008 outcome rows. RoB-BERT returned a valid assessment for every primary row and for 1,007 of 1,008 outcome rows (one reader output reached the generation limit). Claude Fable 5.1 returned no answers for 3 of 419 pooled rows (3 of 570 primary rows) and 7 of 1,008 outcome rows, in every case because the provider's content filter stopped the completion. Gemini 4 Argon returned no or incomplete answers for 6 of 419 pooled rows (8 of 570 primary rows) and 12 of 1,008 outcome rows, in all but one case because it reached the 16,000-token output limit during reasoning. GPT-6 Astra returned a valid output for every row (Supplementary Table S3). Invalid outputs therefore had little effect on agreement: no AI system had more than 6 invalid outputs among the 419 pooled rows, and restricting the analysis to rows with a valid output from every AI system changed estimates by about one point or less (SA1). On the 336 ROBoto2 rows, the protocol runs gave the same signalling answer as the preliminary October runs for 88.2% (Claude Fable 5.1) and 91.8% (GPT-6 Astra) of answers and the same overall judgement for 85.1% and 94.6% of rows (Supplementary Table S2). The reader's preprocessing truncated input for 250 of 961 requests, almost all LogiMed and Cochrane articles, because the sectioning rules, fixed before the runs, failed to recognize most LogiMed headings; the protocol prohibited changing preprocessing, so we analyzed these rows as run and examined them in SA3.

Internal evaluation

On the 532 internal test worksheets, the final reader adapter answered signalling questions with κ 0.67 against the audited answers, with domain κ 0.64 and overall κ 0.48, parsed every record, and located 79% of its quotations verbatim in the source text. The selected encoder reached signalling-question κ 0.67, domain κ 0.47 and overall κ 0.31; at the 0.85 threshold it received 48% of answers with 93% accuracy on those answers. On the 16 worksheets assessed independently by one investigator, signalling-question κ was 0.43 for the reader and 0.41 for the encoder.

Component analyses

The reader alone and the combined system performed almost identically at the domain and overall levels; the encoder gate added +1.0 point (0.1 to 1.9) of signalling-question agreement in the pooled population and nothing beyond it, having replaced 550 of 14,752 reader answers (3.7%). The encoder alone matched the reader at the signalling-question (42.6% versus 40.8%) and domain (67.0% versus 68.2%) levels but carried almost no information about the overall judgement (overall κ 0.05, -0.02 to 0.13, in the pooled population and -0.03 on the ROBoto2 trials) because it defaulted to the majority category where the reader did not; the combined system's overall κ exceeded the encoder's by 0.11 (0.02 to 0.20). The discrimination of the overall judgement therefore comes from the generative reader (Supplementary Table S7).

Sensitivity analyses

Restricting to the 559 primary rows with a valid output from every AI system (SA1) changed estimates by about one point or less and reversed no paired difference (Supplementary Table S8). Extending to all 1,008 outcome rows (SA2) lowered overall agreement for every AI system by up to three points; on the 857 pooled rows, RoB-BERT's domain agreement was +1.6 points (-0.4 to 3.8) against Claude Fable 5.1, +16.9 (14.0 to 19.8) against GPT-6 Astra and -0.8 (-2.8 to 1.1) against Gemini 4 Argon (Supplementary Table S9). The truncation analysis (SA3) explained the LogiMed pattern: on the 87 pooled rows where preprocessing truncated input, RoB-BERT's overall agreement was 24.1% (κ -0.14) against 43.7% for Claude Fable 5.1, whereas on the 332 untruncated rows it was 51.8% (κ 0.23) against 48.2% for Claude Fable 5.1 and 48.5% for Gemini 4 Argon, with the highest domain agreement of any AI system (68.4%; +4.1 points, 1.9 to 6.4, against Claude Fable 5.1) (Supplementary Table S10). Removing the RECOVERY comparison (SA4) changed pooled estimates by at most 0.2 points (Supplementary Table S11).

Post hoc adjudication of disagreements

Of the 188 adjudicated domain-level disagreements, the reviewers judged RoB-BERT correct in 98 (52%), the reference correct in 35 (19%), both defensible in 32 (17%), both wrong in 15 (8%) and 8 (4%) indeterminate (Supplementary Table S13). RoB-BERT was most often judged correct in domain 1 (32 of 46) and domain 5 (31 of 46); in 55 of the 98 domains where the reference was wrong and RoB-BERT correct, the reference had rated domain 1 low risk without information on allocation concealment or domain 5 low risk without an accessible analysis plan.

The reference's errors were mostly misapplied RoB 2 guidance (44, 32 of them major) or leniency on a borderline call (25), and 13 relied on information outside the report, which may reflect reviewer access to a registry or protocol; RoB-BERT's errors concentrated in domain 3, where 14 of 16 were high-risk calls built on miscounted denominators (Supplementary Table S14). Twenty-three benchmark Cochrane trials had also been assessed by a second, independent Cochrane review team, which agreed with the benchmark reference in 27% to 68% of judgements depending on the domain (Supplementary Table S15).

Discussion

Principal findings

Agreement of humans using Cochrane RoB 2 with one another was very poor, with κ uniformly less than 0.4 and often appreciably lower. A 27-billion-parameter reader fine-tuned on audited RoB 2 worksheets, paired with a biomedical encoder and the published decision algorithm, showed agreement similar to that between humans, matched or exceeded three frontier LLMs on identical trial text, and did so at 2% to 6% of their cost. RoB-BERT agreed with the reference more often than Claude Fable 5.1 and Gemini 4 Argon at the signalling-question level, more often than Claude Fable 5.1 and GPT-6 Astra at the domain level, and much more often than GPT-6 Astra at the overall level; otherwise it performed similarly to the frontier models. It returned a valid assessment for every primary row, and almost all of its quotations could be found verbatim in the source text. On articles the reader saw in full, it had the highest domainlevel agreement of any AI system. Like Claude Fable 5.1 and Gemini 4 Argon, RoB-BERT identified fewer than a quarter of the domains the reference rated high risk, about as many as a second human assessor identified on the same trials.

Interpretation

The skewed distribution of judgements, with low risk predominating in most domains, may have contributed to the low κ values (24, 25). Even absolute agreement was limited, however: human assessors agreed with one another on less than 65% of domain judgements and on 50% or less of overall judgements.

Agreement between human assessors is the appropriate yardstick for judging the performance of the AI systems, and the comparison is therefore with a flawed instrument. Low reliability is not inevitable in risk-of-bias assessment: junior researchers using the more recently developed ROBUST-RCT tool (29) achieved at least moderate agreement (30).

In our study, RoB-BERT's agreement with the reference at the domain and overall levels was similar to the agreement between humans, and the best frontier models achieved this at the domain level and for overall percentage agreement but not for overall κ.

Adjudication suggests that RoB-BERT's disagreements with the reference often reflected strict application of the RoB 2 rules rather than error, although the adjudicators included the system's developer. The commonest pattern was a reference rating of low risk for randomization or for selection of the reported result where the report gave no information on allocation concealment or no accessible analysis plan, which may reflect pragmatic leniency or unrecorded access to a registry or protocol; RoB-BERT, which saw only the report, applied the written rules. Its own characteristic error, a high-risk call on a miscounted denominator in domain 3, is visible in the quoted evidence and can be targeted in training. One in six disagreements was judged defensible on both sides, a component of RoB 2 agreement that no system can remove.

The component analyses separate the roles of the two parts. The encoder is a high-confidence gate: where it is certain it overrides the reader and slightly improves signalling-question agreement, but where it is uncertain it defaults to the majority answer, low risk. Because the overall RoB 2 judgement is set by the worst domain, a model that defaults to low risk when uncertain has an overall κ near zero. The generative reader must commit to an answer for every question and support it with quoted text, and it supplies nearly all of the signal for the overall judgement.

The truncation finding is a limitation of this version of the system, not of the comparison, and is fixable. The frontier models received the whole article; the reader saw a shortened, sectionprioritized window because the fixed sectioning rules failed on the heading conventions of LogiMed's source journals, losing the outcome-reporting and analysis-plan material that drives the domain 3 to 5 judgements. Correcting the sectioning rules or extending the context would be the first change in a subsequent version, and SA3 shows the expected gain.

Comparison with previous work

Earlier evaluations of general-purpose chatbots for RoB 2 reported agreement in the range of the frontier models here. GPT-4 on Cochrane assessments reached domain κ near zero despite access to protocols and registrations (6); ChatGPT-4o gave moderate domain agreement with substantial variation across domains (7); and an LLM-assisted workflow improved speed more than agreement (8). ROBoto2 found that showing assessors an LLM's suggestions changed their answers, which is why the assisted cohort is reported separately (5). LogiMed-RoB showed that LLMs often give domain judgements inconsistent with their own signalling answers (13); passing every AI system's answers through the same algorithm removes that failure mode here.

Encoder-only models for evidence quality assessment have been limited by the lack of grounding (12); the present system gives the quotation task to the reader and the confidence task to the encoder. No previous study evaluated a fine-tuned RoB 2 system and current frontier models on identical text with recorded settings and a pre-registered analysis.

Strengths and limitations

The evaluation was registered prospectively under an unamended protocol, with benchmark texts, reference assessments, model weights and prompts frozen and checksummed. The development corpus was screened against the benchmark by trial identifier, DOI and title so that no benchmark trial was used in training. Every AI system received identical text and invalid outputs were scored as disagreement rather than excluded. Intervals were clustered by trial on paired resamples and all prediction files, prompts, request records and analysis code are public.

The study has limitations. First, each reference is a single published judgement, made by one assessor or agreed by two, rather than an expert reference standard, so it carries the limited inter-rater reliability of RoB 2. Second, preprocessing truncated the text of a subset of articles, mostly in the LogiMed cohort, removing context for domain judgements in those trials. Third, frontier-model performance is sensitive to prompt and decoding settings, and we tested one standardized configuration. Fourth, the adjudication was post hoc, by two reviewers who are authors of this study, on a sample of disagreements divided between them, with no consensus step and no measure of inter-reviewer agreement, and its findings are descriptive. Fifth, we assembled the benchmark as well as the system under test; the reference assessments come from three independently published sources and were frozen before any run, but the choice of sources and scoring conventions was ours. Sixth, every pair of human assessors used as the inter-assessor reference included at least one assessor who had seen LLM suggestions, which may have increased their agreement with each other. Given how poor that agreement was, and because any such inflation would make the human yardstick harder rather than easier to meet, this is unlikely to be a major problem. Seventh, the comparison of high-risk judgements was post hoc and rests on 52 trials with 13 high-risk reference domains, so its confidence intervals are wide. Finally, we measured offline benchmark performance on report text, not reviewer workflow efficiency or usability in live reviews.

Implications

For evidence synthesis teams, these findings support RoB-BERT as a first-pass assessor whose output a reviewer -or given the poor agreement among human reviewers, probably two reviewers -must then check. At half a cent per trial with deterministic, complete output, it makes assessing every trial at every update of a living review affordable, which hosted models at many times the cost do not. Because almost all of its quotations can be located in the report, a reviewer can audit each answer against the quoted sentence rather than re-read the trial.

Conclusion

Human agreement on RoB 2 was poor, with κ values uniformly less than 0.4. A task-specific fine-tuned system matched or exceeded three frontier LLMs in agreement with human RoB 2 assessments at the signalling-question and domain levels, matched the best of them at the overall level, and did so at a small fraction of the cost with complete output in which each answer cites the supporting text. Its domain and overall agreement with single human assessors was within or above the agreement between two human assessors rating the same trial, it identified the reference's high-risk domains as often as a second assessor did, and every system tended to err in a direction that matters for certainty ratings. Fine-tuned systems are a credible basis for assisted assessment, potentially as one rater in a consensus process; they are not a replacement for the reviewer.
