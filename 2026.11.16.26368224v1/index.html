Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 128 papers with 246 instances of power statements, 203 were correctly detected (true positives), 22 were missed (false negatives) and 21 were incorrectly detected (false positives). Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Marginal Significance works

List all sentences that describe an effect as ‘marginally significant’.

The marginal module searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The list of terms is a subset of those listed in a blog post by Matthew Hankins. The module returns all sentences that match terms describing ‘marginally significant’ results.

Some of the terms identified might not be problematic in some contexts, and there are ways to describe ‘marginal significance’ that are not detected by the module.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). Statcheck might still miss one-sided tests that are described in another sentence, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Abstract

Alzheimer's disease (AD) exhibits substantial biological and clinical heterogeneity, posing critical challenges for advancing precision medicine. Existing data-driven approaches often rely on modalityspecific clustering or predefined biomarker trajectories, limiting their ability to discover disease subtypes from incomplete, heterogeneous real-world data. In this study, we introduce ADSMoE, a mixture-ofexperts (MoE)-based multimodal representation learning framework that jointly identifies AD subtypes as latent disease signatures while accommodating incomplete modality combinations. ADSMoE integrates a Fleximodal MoE encoder that accommodates arbitrary combinations of available modalities with a novel subtype-specific MoE decoder to capture complementary disease characteristics through expert specialization. Using data from 5,422 participants across three independent cohorts, we trained ADSMoE on the ADNI cohort and externally validated the identified subtypes in NACC and UKB without model retraining. The proposed ADSMoE identified four AD subtypes with distinct neuroimaging patterns, genetic and proteomic associations, and clinical characteristics. Benchmarking analyses demonstrated robust subtype discovery and strong biological and clinical relevance, while external validation revealed consistent subtype-associated characteristics across independent cohorts. ADSMoE advances AD subtyping by uncovering biologically and clinically meaningful AD heterogeneity from incomplete multimodal data, providing a scalable framework for disease stratification, biomarker discovery, and future precision medicine applications in AD.

Introduction

Alzheimer's disease (AD) is a biologically and clinically heterogeneous neurodegenerative disorder with substantial variability in pathology and progression. As of 2025, AD affects approximately 7 million individuals in the United States, a number projected to nearly double to 13.85 million by 2060 1 . The rapidly increasing prevalence of AD presents significant challenges not only for affected individuals and their families but also for healthcare systems and society. Disease heterogeneity manifests across cognitive impairment, neurodegenerative patterns, biomarker profiles, genetic risk factors, and rates of clinical progression 2 , highlighting the need to identify biologically and clinically meaningful AD subtypes. Recent advances in multimodal features, such as neuroimaging and fluid-based biomarkers, have provided important opportunities to characterize AD heterogeneity 3 . For example, positron emission tomography (PET) measures of amyloid-β (Aβ) and tau 4, 5 , while magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)-based measures of structural and functional brain changes capture complementary aspects of AD pathology and neurodegeneration 6 . Blood-and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF)-based biomarkers 7, 8 further reflect molecular alterations associated with AD. Genetic studies have identified susceptibility variants in genes such as APOE and TREM2 9 and pathogenic mutations in APP, PSEN1, and PSEN2 linked to early-onset AD 10, 11 . Importantly, these biomarkers are not independent but interact across molecular, structural, and functional levels. Prior studies have reported that neuroimaging abnormalities are associated with genetic risk variants 12, 13 , and CSF biomarkers closely correspond to Aβ and tau pathology detected by neuroimaging 14 . Moreover, growing evidence indicates that blood-and CSF-based biomarkers are influenced by both APOE genotype and broader polygenic risk profiles 15, 16 . These complementary and interconnected biomarkers provide a strong biological foundation for multimodal characterization of AD heterogeneity.

Despite these advances, effectively integrating multimodal biomarkers into large-scale, real-world studies and clinical practice remains challenging. In longitudinal cohort and routine clinical settings, multimodal data are often incompletely acquired, as neuroimaging, fluid biomarkers, genetic profiling, and detailed clinical assessments are collected at different time points, under varying protocols, or only in selected patient subsets [17] [18] [19] [20] . Consequently, incomplete modality availability is the rule rather than the exception, leading to heterogeneous observations across individuals and constraining the characterization of AD heterogeneity and disease progression in real-world populations 5, 21, 22 . Recent developments in artificial intelligence (AI) approaches have improved our understanding of AD heterogeneity but remain limited in modeling AD subtypes as latent signatures jointly learned from heterogeneous multimodal data [23] [24] [25] . Current methods generally fall into three categories. First, progression-based frameworks, such as Subtype and Stage Inference (SuStaIn) and its extensions, disentangle disease stage from phenotypic variability by modeling biomarker evolution over time 20, [26] [27] [28] , but they rely on predefined progression trajectories and typically operate on a limited subset of consistently acquired biomarkers. Second, modality-centered subtype discovery approaches, such as GeneSGAN 29 , incorporate genetic information to improve biological interpretation but generally anchor subtype identification to a dominant modality, most commonly neuroimaging, while treating other modalities as auxiliary information. Consequently, the resulting subtypes represent modality-specific phenotypes rather than shared multimodal disease signatures. Third, recent multimodal deep learning frameworks based on autoencoders and transformers have demonstrated powerful representation learning across neuroimaging, genetic, molecular, and clinical data [30] [31] [32] [33] [34] [35] [36] [37] [38] . However, these models are primarily designed for prediction or classification tasks, with subtype identification performed only as a downstream analysis, and generally assume a fixed set of input modalities during training. These assumptions limit robustness to irregular modality availability and restrict applicability to large-scale, real-world cohorts, where missing or partially observed modalities are common. Overall, existing approaches either learn modality-specific representations or perform multimodal prediction without explicitly modeling disease subtypes as latent multimodal signatures emerging from heterogeneous and incomplete real-world observations.

Given the heterogeneous and incomplete nature of real-world AD data, the Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) framework provides a flexible, adaptive strategy for multimodal representation learning 39, 40 . By allowing different experts to specialize in complementary multimodal patterns, MoE naturally accommodates irregular modality availability and preserves complementary information across modalities. Previous studies have demonstrated the effectiveness of MoE architectures for multimodal representation learning, including multimodal embedding learning 41, 42 , multi-omics integration 43 , brain foundation models 44 , and learning under missing modalities 41, 45 . More recently, advanced MoE-based transformer architectures have further improved training stability and expert specialization 46 . In the context of multimodal AD data, we hypothesize that routed experts can capture disease characteristics and learn subtype-specific patterns, enabling biologically and clinically meaningful disease heterogeneity to emerge through multimodal representation learning.

In this work, we present ADSMoE (Alzheimer's Disease Subtyping via Mixture-of-Experts), an MoEbased multimodal representation learning framework for discovering AD subtypes from heterogeneous real-world data. Unlike existing approaches, ADSMoE integrates incomplete and irregular multimodal data through a flexible-modal MoE encoder and introduces a novel subtype-specific MoE decoder that learns disease patterns through reconstruction. Using ADSMoE, we identified four biologically and clinically distinct AD subtypes characterized by significant differences in neuroanatomical patterns, molecular signatures, genetic variation and clinical characteristics. These four subtypes were discovered by training ADSMoE model on the ADNI dataset and validated in independent NACC and UKB cohorts, with consistent multimodal characteristics. These findings establish a generalizable framework for discovering reproducible AD subtypes from incomplete multimodal data and demonstrate the potential of an MoEbased transformer autoencoder to capture AD heterogeneity. It provides a scalable foundation for precision diagnosis, biomarker discovery, and subtype-guided therapeutic development in Alzheimer's disease.

Results

Overview of the ADSMoE framework

ADSMoE is a novel MoE-based transformer autoencoder designed to identify AD subtypes using heterogeneous multimodal data, integrating imaging, genetics, clinical, and protein data from three large cohorts (i.e., ADNI, NACC, and UKB). As shown in Figure 1, ADSMoE comprises a modality-flexible transformer encoder (termed the Fleximodal 47 encoder) that can accept an arbitrary combination of input modalities, and an MoE-based transformer decoder that learns subtype-specific AD representations (Figure 1A). The encoder generates unified latent representations, which are used to initialize subtype structure through the expectation-maximization (EM) algorithm 48 and calculate decoder gate weights. These weights subsequently guide expert specialization within the decoder, allowing subtype-specific experts to progressively capture distinct multimodal disease patterns 40 . The decoder reconstructs latent representations and learns subtype patterns. Unlike conventional clustering approaches that assign patients solely according to feature similarity, ADSMoE learns subtype assignments jointly with multimodal representation learning through expert routing (Figure 1B). Potential AD subtypes, which are patient groups with different diseaserelated characteristics, are routed to subtype-specific experts, while severity is modeled separately to avoid being driven dominantly by disease stage. The model is optimized in three sequential phases (Figure 1C). First, normative modeling 49, 50 is introduced to quantify individual deviations from healthy reference while reducing the effects of disease-unrelated confounders, converting each modality into residuals. Second, the Fleximodal encoder is trained to learn unified multimodal representations. Finally, the model jointly optimizes all components (e.g., the encoder, decoder, and routing network) to specialize each expert in a subtype-specific disease pattern. After convergence, each AD patient is assigned to the subtype with the highest routing probability (Figure 1D), followed by post-hoc analyses that characterize each subtype's biological and clinical relevance. Overall, ADSMoE provides a flexible and unified framework for discovering AD subtypes from heterogeneous multimodal data and characterizing their distinct biological signatures across independent cohorts.

Study cohorts and characteristics

In this study, we collected AD cohorts from three large data resources: the Alzheimer's Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI) 51 , the National Alzheimer's Coordinating Center (NACC) 52, and the UK Biobank (UKB) 53 , which collectively provide complementary multimodal data, including clinical, genetic, protein, and image data. For ADNI, we included all participants. For NACC and UKB, we included cases with at least two modalities. We ended up with 2,035, 1,635, and 1,752 participants from ADNI, NACC, and UKB, respectively. Although the NACC and UKB have substantially more eligible participants with AD, we randomly selected 876 individuals, the same as ADNI. As shown in Table 1, participants with AD were predominantly older adults across all cohorts, with most individuals aged between 65 and 84 years (74.9% -93.3%), and a similar mean age of approximately 75 years. Female participants accounted for a lower proportion in ADNI (39.3%) but a slightly higher proportion in the NACC and UKB cohorts (54.3% and 55.1%). APOE ε4 carriage was common across cohorts. The proportion of individuals carrying two copies of APOE ε4 alleles varied substantially, ranging from 7.8% in NACC to 22.4% in UKB. The three cohorts also differed in multimodal data availability, providing complementary evaluation settings for ADSMoE. ADNI involved all selected modalities with varying completeness, NACC mainly contributed to clinical and imaging data, whereas UKB provided comprehensive genetic and protein biomarker data but relatively limited MRI measurements. These heterogeneous modality combinations reflect the incomplete and irregular data commonly encountered in real-world AD studies and provide a suitable benchmark for evaluating the robustness and generalizability of ADSMoE. Additional cohort characteristics are provided in the Supplementary Table S1.

Model performance

Benchmark evaluation

We first benchmarked several encoders to identify the optimal structure for multimodal representation learning by discriminating individuals with AD from cognitively normal (CN) participants using ADNI dataset. As shown in Figure 2A, FlexMoE achieved the best overall performance, with the highest results in both the validation (accuracy = 0.906, F1 = 0.890, AUC = 0.936) and the test sets (accuracy = 0.892, F1 = 0.871, AUC = 0.938), while maintaining computational efficiency (192.0 s per run). Although FuseMoE showed competitive predictive performance, it required substantially longer runtime (309.4 s per run). In contrast, ResNet and LiMoE were more computationally efficient (12.5 s and 51.1 s per run), but consistently underperformed across all evaluation metrics. As a result, we selected FlexMoE as the multimodal encoder for ADSMoE. We next evaluated subtype discovery on ADNI by comparing ADSMoE with several representative methods (Figure 2B). Because ground-truth AD subtype labels are unavailable in real-world cohorts, we evaluated subtype quality using the Davies-Bouldin index (DBI) 54 and a geneticsinformed metric, i.e., the number of significantly associated single-nucleotide polymorphisms (denoted as N-Asso-SNPs) 29 . Lower DBI indicates better clustering compactness and separation, while higher N-Asso-SNPs suggests stronger genetic relevance of the identified subtypes. Additional evaluations under varying multimodal missingness, including different proportions of genetic variants, region of interest (ROI)-level MRI features 55 , clinical variables, and AD-related protein biomarkers, are presented in Supplementary Figure S1. Results are reported as mean ± standard deviation (SD) from five-fold cross-validation. Across all comparisons, ADSMoE consistently achieved the strongest genetic association signals (N-Asso-SNPs = 189 ± 16) while maintaining a low DBI (4.24 ± 0.74), indicating genetically related and well-separated subtype structures. In comparison, GeneSGAN identified substantially fewer associated SNPs (100 ± 9) and exhibited poorer clustering quality (DBI = 22.57 ± 0.46). CDEC produced highly compact clusters (DBI = 0.46 ± 0.00) but yielded limited genetic associations (N-Asso-SNPs = 59 ± 18), suggesting that geometric compactness alone does not necessarily correspond to biologically meaningful subtypes. EDESC had intermediate performance (N-Asso-SNPs = 64 ± 6, DBI = 13.75 ± 0.48), whereas classical clustering methods (Kmeans and spectral clustering) performed consistently worse across metrics. Overall, ADSMoE achieved the strongest subtype-genetic associations among the compared methods while maintaining competitive clustering quality.

Hyperparameter sensitivity analysis

To assess the robustness of ADSMoE, we performed a sensitivity analysis. ADSMoE demonstrated stable subtype discovery performance across different hyperparameter configurations (Figure 2C). Varying the number of experts from 16 to 32 in the encoder showed only modest changes in both N-Asso-SNPs and DBI, while N-Asso-SNPs decreased more when changing to 24 experts. When increasing the number of MoE layers to four in the encoder, we found it slightly improved cluster compactness (lower DBI) but did not strengthen genetic associations, suggesting diminishing returns from deeper encoder architectures. Increasing decoder depth to three layers of MoE reduced N-Asso-SNPs, whereas DBI remained lower with more decoder MoE layers, which shows a difference between biological relevance and clustering quality. Finally, varying the top-k routing strategy had little effect on DBI but influenced genetic association strength, with top-2 routing achieving the highest N-Asso-SNPs.

Contribution of ADSMoE modules

We further performed an ablation study to evaluate the contribution of key modules of ADSMoE. As shown in Figure 2D, the full model achieved the highest N-Asso-SNPs (189 ± 16) while maintaining a low DBI (4.24 ± 0.74). Removing normative modeling, which reduces confounders through residualization, produced a noticeable reduction in N-Asso-SNPs (132 ± 15) and increased DBI (4.32 ± 0.81), showing that normative modeling is critical for identifying disease-related subtype representations. In contrast, removing EM initialization resulted in the largest deterioration in clustering performance, yielding both reduced N-Asso-SNPs (159 ± 18) and the highest DBI (5.33 ± 0.47), highlighting the importance of clustering initialization. Moreover, eliminating either pretraining or the severity head reduced N-Asso-SNPs (142 ± 16 and 141 ± 19, respectively), highlighting their contributions to meaningful subtype discovery. Removing pretraining also increased DBI (4.85 ± 0.57), suggesting that pretraining improves clustering quality.

Overall, these results show that each component contributes to the overall performance of ADSMoE.

Characterization of ADSMoE-derived AD subtypes Latent representation and modality robustness

Using ADSMoE, we identified four multimodal subtypes in the ADNI dataset. We visualized the latent embeddings generated by the FlexMoE encoder using principal component analysis (PCA) 56 , t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (t-SNE) 57 , and uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) 58 . As shown in Figure 3A, the four identified subtypes formed distinguishable clusters using PCA, although partial overlap was observed between neighboring subtypes, reflecting the continuous nature of AD heterogeneity. Similar subtype separation was consistently observed using t-SNE and UMAP (Supplementary Figure S2), supporting the robustness of the learned latent representations. We further investigated whether subtypes were associated with specific modality combinations by summarizing subtype distributions across different patterns of available modalities (Figure 3B), where, P, C, G, and I denote protein, clinical, genetic, and image modalities, respectively. All four subtypes were represented across multiple modality combinations rather than being confined to a single data configuration. PCGI was the most common modality combination in S1 (36.7%), S2 (33.9%), and S4 (43.8%), whereas CGI was the most common in S3 (40.9%). These findings indicate that ADSMoE learns subtype-specific disease representations that are robust to heterogeneous multimodal availability, rather than simply reflecting missing-data patterns or modality composition.

Multimodal characterization of identified subtypes

To characterize the biological and clinical profiles of the identified AD subtypes, we compared Polygenic Risk Score (PRS) 59 , cognitive performance, cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) biomarkers, and ROI patterns (Figure 3C-F). Overall, the four identified subtypes exhibited distinct genetic, clinical, and neurobiological profiles, supporting the biological relevance of the subtype discovery. As shown in Figure 3C, genetic risk differed across subtypes, with S3 showing the highest PRS overall (1.324 ± 0.961), whereas S1 exhibited the lowest (-0.155 ± 0.977) genetic risk. Consistent with this pattern, S3 also demonstrated the greatest cognitive impairment (Figure 3D), characterized by higher Alzheimer's disease assessment scales (ADAS) 60 and lower mini-mental state examination (MMSE) scores. In contrast, S1 showed the least impaired performance, while Subtype 2 (S2) and Subtype 4 (S4) displayed intermediate levels of cognitive dysfunction. Subtype-specific differences were also observed in CSF biomarkers and neuroimaging features. As shown in Figure 3E, p-Tau concentrations were higher in S2-S4 than in S1, whereas Aβ42 levels were highest in S3. Analysis of imaging-derived regional ROI z-score patterns across major brain regions further revealed distinct neuroanatomical signatures across subtypes (Figure 3F). S3 showed widespread reductions in ROI z-scores across multiple cortical regions, whereas S1 exhibited values higher than the other subtypes on average. In contrast, S2 displayed relatively lower z-scores in frontal and temporal regions but higher values in occipital regions, while S4 showed more pronounced reductions in parietal and occipital regions. In addition, demographic characteristics also differed among subtypes (Supplementary Table S2). S4 contained relatively younger participants (71.9 ± 7.5), whereas S2 included older participants on average (76.4 ± 6.8). S3 had more males (54.4%), while S4 (50.6% male) showed a more balanced sex distribution. Additional differences were observed in racial composition and educational attainment, indicating that the identified subtypes capture heterogeneity extending beyond molecular and neuroimaging characteristics.

Biological and clinical signatures of AD subtypes Subtype-specific structural MRI patterns

We assessed subtype-specific structural MRI patterns by ROI-level comparisons of cortical volume for each identified subtype, using Welch's two-sample t-tests (one-vs-rest). The resulting subtype-specific ROI t-statistic maps are presented in Figure 4A. Overall, the four subtypes exhibited distinct spatial patterns of cortical volume variation, supporting the presence of heterogeneous neuroanatomical phenotypes. S3 demonstrated predominantly negative t-statistics across widespread cortical regions, with marked differences in the frontal, temporal, and parietal lobes, as well as in the insula, hippocampus, cingulate, and supramarginal regions, indicating broadly reduced cortical volumes compared with the remaining participants. In contrast, S1 exhibited widespread positive t-statistics across frontal, temporal, parietal, and occipital regions, suggesting relatively preserved cortical volumes. S2 also showed predominantly positive t-statistics, particularly in frontal and occipital regions, with relatively smaller differences in temporal regions. S4 displayed a more regionally heterogeneous pattern, characterized by positive t-statistics in temporal regions and negative values in portions of the frontal, parietal, and occipital cortices. These subtype-specific t-statistic maps highlight heterogeneous neuroanatomical patterns across subtypes, indicating that the identified subtypes are associated with distinct spatial profiles of cortical volume variations. Additional pairwise subtype comparisons are provided in the Supplementary Figure S3.

Prevalence of medical conditions

Differences in the prevalence of medical history conditions were also observed across the four identified subtypes (Figure 4B). According to the results, S4 exhibited the highest prevalence of psychiatric conditions (43.3%) and smoking history (50.0%), whereas S1 showed the highest prevalence of cardiovascular disease (69.4%). In contrast, hepatic disorders, alcohol abuse, and drug abuse were uncommon across all subtypes. Other common conditions, including musculoskeletal disorders and head, eye, ear, nose, and throat conditions, were observed across subtypes but varied more modestly in prevalence.

Major surgical procedures were most prevalent in S2 (75.3%) and least prevalent in S3 (69.9%). Overall, these findings demonstrate that the ADSMoE-derived subtypes are associated with clinically distinct comorbidity profiles, further supporting their clinical relevance.

Subtype-specific genetic associations

To investigate the genetic basis of the identified AD subtypes, we evaluated SNP-subtype associations using likelihood-ratio tests (LRTs) comparing multinomial logistic regression models fitted with and without each SNP (Figure 4C). The Manhattan plot revealed a pronounced enrichment of association signals on chromosome 19, where multiple variants exceed the genome-wide significance threshold. These associations were dominated by the APOE region, including rs429358 and rs7412 (APOE), together with neighboring variants in TOMM40 (rs34095326), APOC1 (rs439401), and NECTIN2 (rs11668861), highlighting the central role of this genomic region in distinguishing AD subtypes. Beyond chromosome 19, variants in TRIB1 (rs115038899) and LAT (rs111708809) reached the suggestive significance threshold, and variants in BIN1 (rs35114168), FRMD4A (rs2446581), MS4A3 (rs501945), and SLC24A4 (rs8008388) were significant after FDR correction. Although these associations were less significant than those observed at the APOE locus, they were distributed across multiple chromosomes, suggesting that the identified subtypes are influenced by polygenic genetic architectures rather than a single dominant susceptibility locus.

Subtype-specific protein associations

We further compared protein abundances across subtypes using one-way ANOVA to characterize molecular differences among the identified subtypes. The results are shown in Figure 4D, and we found that several proteins showed significant subtype-dependent differences. STAU1 showed the strongest association among all measured proteins, followed by DCC, VTI1A, CRAC1, TCAL3, CK2-A2:B, ESAM.2, TMX3, and TACC3. Other significantly associated proteins are involved in diverse biological pathways, such as immune and inflammatory signaling (e.g., RIPK2, CC134), lipid metabolism (e.g., ApoB), and cellular stress or translational regulation (e.g., IF4E2). The broad functional diversity of these proteins suggests that the identified AD subtypes differ across multiple molecular pathways rather than a single biological process, further supporting their biological heterogeneity.

Multimodal association and interpretation

Cross-modal associations

To investigate cross-modal relationships underlying the identified subtypes, we analyzed associations among genetic variants, protein biomarkers, neuroimaging features, and clinical variables. All features were adjusted for age and sex prior to integration to minimize potential confounding effects. The resulting network revealed extensive links across modalities (Figure 5A), with genetic variants exhibiting the most extensive cross-modal connectivity, particularly with structural MRI features. Multiple AD-associated variants, including those within the APOE locus (e.g., APOE, APOC1, and NECTIN2), showed widespread associations with cortical regions spanning the frontal, temporal, and parietal lobes. These findings suggest that subtype-specific genetic variation is closely linked to distributed patterns of brain structural alteration. For proteins, STAU1 showed a positive similarity with rs429358 (0.713) and a negative similarity with the left superior parietal MRI feature (-0.722). Clinical variables also demonstrated prominent cross-modal associations. The tremor measure was positively associated with MRI features in frontal, temporal, and parietal regions. The 18 years of education also exhibited associations with the left superior parietal MRI feature and several proteins, including DCC, further supporting the influence of demographic and clinical factors on subtype-specific neuroanatomical variation. The complete similarity matrix is provided in the Supplementary Data.

Survival analysis

To evaluate whether time to AD diagnosis differed across the identified subtypes, we retrospectively compared time to disease conversion (CN → AD) in each subtype using Kaplan-Meier analysis (Figure 5B). The four subtypes exhibited significantly different conversion trajectories (log-rank test: 𝑝 = 0.012).

During early follow-up, the survival curves largely overlapped, although S2 showed a modestly faster decline in event-free probability within the first five years. The curves began to diverge during mid-followup (approximately 5-7 years), with S1 demonstrating the earliest and most rapid decline, whereas S3 and S4 maintained higher event-free probabilities for a longer duration. By the end of follow-up (~14-16 years), event-free probability approached zero across all subtypes, resulting in the convergence of survival curves. These findings indicate that the identified subtypes exhibit distinct temporal patterns of observed time to AD diagnosis intervals. Additional Kaplan-Meier analyses for CN → mild cognitive impairment (MCI) 61 and MCI → AD transitions are provided in Supplementary Figure S4.

Multimodal feature importance

To identify the multimodal features driving subtype classification, we computed SHAP values for one-vs-rest prediction models and summarized the top-ranked features for each subtype (Figure 5C). Across all subtypes, MRI-derived regional measures dominated the top-ranked features, with genetic variants and CSF proteins among the top predictors for some subtypes. Each subtype exhibited a distinct combination of predictive features. S1 was primarily characterized by cortical thickness in the right and left middle temporal gyri and the right fusiform gyrus, with complement receptor type 1 also among the topranked features. S2 was distinguished by cortical thickness in the left superior and inferior parietal lobules and the right paracentral lobule, and variants involving rs17014873 and rs35627364. For S3, all 20 topranked features were cortical thickness measures, led by the right inferior temporal and bilateral middle temporal regions. S4 combined right entorhinal, right superior parietal, and left precuneus cortical thickness, with the rs72924659 variant and DCC and STAU1. Notably, several features consistently ranked among the most important predictors (e.g., left inferior parietal and right precuneus cortical thickness) across multiple subtypes. Overall, these findings suggest that subtype identification is driven by coordinated multimodal biomarker signatures.

External validation of AD subtypes Subtype characterization in the NACC cohort

We applied ADSMoE model to the independent NACC cohort without retraining or fine-tuning to evaluate the generalizability of the identified AD subtypes. Consistent with the subtype characteristics observed in ADNI, significant differences in cognitive performance of identified AD subtypes (S1-S4) were observed in the NACC cohort (Figure 6A). These differences were assessed using five commonly used cognitive measures including Clinical Dementia Rating -Sum of Boxes (CDR-SB) scores 62 , Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) scores 63 , delayed story recall 64 , animal fluency 65 , and Trail Making Test B 66 completion times, which assess cognitive function and impairment similar to those evaluated in ADNI. S3 showed the greatest cognitive impairment, characterized by higher CDR-SB, lower MoCA scores, poorer delayed story recall and animal fluency, and longer Trail Making Test B completion times. In contrast, S1 and S2 exhibited better cognitive performance, whereas S4 showed intermediate impairment. MRI analyses revealed neuroanatomical patterns consistent with those in ADNI (Figure 6B), with S1 showing widespread positive ROI t-statistics, S3 exhibiting predominantly negative values across cortical regions, and S2 and S4 displaying more region-specific patterns. Genetic differences were also observed (Figure 6C), with APOE ε4 carriage varying significantly across subtypes (χ² test, p = 0.029), providing genetic evidence independent of the inputs used for subtype assignment. S3 exhibited the highest proportion of APOE ε4 carriers (69%), compared with S4, S1, and S2 (56%, 44%, and 47%). Medical history profiles also showed the highest prevalence of cardiovascular conditions in S1 (21.8%) and the highest prevalence of smoking history in S4 (47.9%), consistent with the subtype rankings in ADNI (Figure 6D). Additional demographic characteristics among the four subtypes in the NACC cohort are in Supplementary Table S3.

Subtype characterization in the UKB cohort

We further applied our proposed and trained ADSMoE to the independent UKB cohort without retraining or fine-tuning to evaluate the cross-cohort generalizability of the identified AD subtypes. Neuroimaging analyses revealed neuroanatomical patterns consistent with those observed in ADNI and NACC (Figure 6E), with S1 showing predominantly positive ROI t-statistics and S3 exhibiting widespread negative values across brain regions. Genetic association analyses identified prominent signals on chromosome 19 (Figure 6F), particularly within the APOE and TOMM40 regions, aligned with the genetic associations observed in ADNI cohort. Additional associations were also identified at established AD susceptibility loci, including rs3818361 in CR1 and rs11763230 in the EPHA1-AS1 region. Proteomic association analyses also uncovered significant molecular differences among subtypes (Figure 6G), with CEA16 and VTI1A showing the strongest associations, along with several proteins also identified in ADNI, such as STAU1, DCC, VTI1A, and Apo B. These findings demonstrate that ADSMoE-derived subtypes retain consistent neuroanatomical, genetic, and proteomic characteristics across independent cohorts, supporting their biological relevance and the generalizability of the framework to heterogeneous multimodal datasets. Additional demographic characteristics among the four subtypes in UKB cohort are provided in Supplementary Table S4.

Discussion

In this study, we developed ADSMoE, a flexible multimodal framework for discovering AD subtypes from heterogeneous real-world data via expert-based representation learning. A major challenge in real-world AD research is that multimodal data are inherently heterogeneous and frequently incomplete, resulting in the exclusion of large numbers of participants when conventional multimodal methods require complete observations or a single representation pathway. ADSMoE addresses this challenge by leveraging all available modalities without extensive imputation or discarding subjects. By combining flexible multimodal encoding with a subtype-specific MoE decoder, the framework accommodates partially observed multimodal data while capturing distinct disease patterns. Unlike conventional subtype discovery methods that rely on predefined disease categories or modality-specific clustering strategies, ADSMoE learns disease subtypes as emergent properties of multimodal representation learning. More importantly, expert specialization enables the model to preserve heterogeneous disease patterns rather than collapsing disease heterogeneity into a shared representation.

ADSMoE demonstrates robust, reproducible multimodal representation learning and subtype discovery across model configurations and experimental conditions. The Fleximodal MoE-based encoder achieved a favorable balance between predictive performance and computational efficiency. Furthermore, ADSMoE identified stronger subtype-genetic associations while maintaining competitive clustering quality. Sensitivity analyses further showed that the learned subtype structure remained stable across a broad range of model configurations, indicating that subtype discovery was not driven by specific hyperparameter choices. This robustness likely arises from the complementary effects of normative modeling, deep representation learning, and expert specialization, which together enhance latent representation stability and separability. Additionally, compared with Gene-SGAN's genetically associated imaging subtyping, ADSMoE advances toward a unified multimodal framework for AD subtyping. These findings indicate that ADSMoE learns robust and biologically relevant multimodal representations, providing a foundation for modeling disease heterogeneity in AD.

A notable finding of this study is the convergence of multiple data modalities toward a coherent foursubtype organization. The identified subtypes exhibited consistent differences across structural neuroimaging, genetic variation, protein biomarkers, clinical characteristics, and survival rates from CN to AD, supporting their biological validity. Subtype 1 was characterized by relatively preserved baseline cognition, higher cortical volumes, and lower p-tau levels than other subtypes, resembling a minimalatrophy phenotype, yet showed faster long-term progression to AD. Subtype 2 represented an older subgroup with moderate cognitive impairment, relatively preserved neocortical volumes, showing potential similarities to a limbic-predominant AD phenotype 67 . Subtype 3 exhibited the highest PRS, the most severe cognitive impairment, and the most extensive cortical atrophy, accompanied by elevated expression of lipid-associated proteins, indicating a typical AD neuroanatomical pattern 67 . Lastly, Subtype 4 showed intermediate cognitive impairment with relatively higher temporal cortical volumes and lower volumes in portions of the frontal, parietal, and occipital cortices. It was also distinguished by limited genetic risk signals and lower expression of AD-and inflammation-related proteins, including Apo C-I and CEA16.

Together with its comparatively slower clinical progression, these findings support a resilience-associated subtype in which preserved brain integrity and reduced molecular burden may mitigate disease progression 68.

The biological relevance of the identified subtypes is further supported by their associations with established AD-related genetic variants and protein biomarkers. Among all SNPs associated with the identified subtypes, variants within the APOE locus, including rs429358 and rs7412, showed the strongest subtype-specific associations, consistent with their well-established roles in AD susceptibility, brain atrophy and cognitive decline 69 . Additional subtype-associated variants mapped to genes involved in amyloid processing (CLSTN1) 70 , tau secretion (FRMD4A) 71 , microglial activation (MS4A3) 72 , endocytosis (BIN1) 73 , and neuroinflammatory pathways (SEMA3F, MEF2C, HLA-DQA1, and LAT) 74 , indicating that distinct biological mechanisms contribute to different subtype profiles. Similarly, subtype-enriched proteins, such as STAU1, DCC, VTI1A, and ApoB 75, 76 , participate in protein transport, immune regulation, and lipid metabolism, further supporting subtype-specific molecular signatures. Collectively, these genetic and proteomic associations indicate that the identified subtypes are underpinned by distinct biological pathways, supporting the ability of ADSMoE to uncover mechanistically meaningful disease heterogeneity.

Importantly, the generalizability of ADSMoE was further supported by external validation using independent NACC and UKB cohorts. Despite differences in multimodal data availability, the identified subtypes retained several clinical and biological characteristics observed in ADNI. In NACC, Subtype 3 consistently exhibited greater cognitive impairment, widespread reductions in regional brain volumes, and the highest prevalence of APOE ε4 carriage, indicating the consistency of its clinical, neuroanatomical and genetic characteristics across cohorts. Similarly, MRI analyses in UKB revealed comparable subtypespecific patterns. Genetic analyses further identified prominent associations within the APOE region, consistent with those observed in ADNI, alongside additional signals involving established AD susceptibility loci (e.g., CR1). Proteomic analyses further revealed overlapping subtype-associated proteins, including STAU1, VTI1A, DCC, CEA16, and ApoB, although molecular profiles differed between cohorts. These findings suggest that ADSMoE can transfer learned multimodal representations across independent cohorts, while preserving clinically and biologically meaningful subtype characteristics, supporting its potential for generalizable AD heterogeneity modeling.

This study has several limitations. First, although the framework was evaluated across three independent cohorts, further validation in larger and more diverse populations and healthcare systems are needed to establish its robustness and generalizability. Second, our cross-cohort harmonization did not fully account for platform-and biospecimen-specific differences, particularly between CSF and plasma proteomic measurements, potentially affecting the comparability of molecular associations. Third, while the identified subtypes demonstrated distinct longitudinal clinical trajectories, subtype discovery primarily relied on cross-sectional multimodal measurements, and incorporating longitudinal multimodal data may further improve subtype resolution and capture dynamic changes in disease progression. Fourth, although the identified genetic and proteomic associations support the biological relevance of the discovered subtypes, experimental validation is needed to establish their mechanisms and potential causal contributions to AD progression. Finally, integrating additional data modalities, such as plasma multi-omics, digital biomarkers, and wearable sensor data, may further enhance subtype characterization and support future precision medicine applications.

In summary, this study presents a unified, multimodal representation learning framework for characterizing AD heterogeneity using real-world data. By jointly learning disease subtypes as multimodal signatures through a MoE architecture, ADSMoE accommodates incomplete modality combinations and enables biologically informed patient stratification. Across three independent AD cohorts, the four identified subtypes exhibited consistent differences in neuroanatomical characteristics, genetic and proteomic associations, and clinical profiles, providing evidence for their biological and clinical relevance. More broadly, the proposed framework provides a scalable foundation for subtype-specific biomarker discovery, precision clinical trial design, and mechanism-guided therapeutic development for AD and other neurodegenerative disorders.

Methods

Data sources and study cohorts

We collected multimodal data, including neuroimaging, genetic, clinical, and proteomic measurements, from three large-scale cohorts: the Alzheimer's Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI), the National Alzheimer's Coordinating Center (NACC), and the UK Biobank (UKB). ADNI data were downloaded on June 15, 2025. The study included 5,422 participants across the three cohorts (Supplementary Table S1).

Participants with AD and CN were identified using ADNI diagnostic records (DXSUM) 77 , NACC clinical assessments 78 , and UKB health records (ICD-10 codes for AD) 79 . For external validation, we randomly selected eligible participants with AD from NACC and UKB to obtain sample sizes equal to that of the ADNI cohort. Random sampling was performed independently within each external cohort using a fixed random seed of 42. For each participant, we selected the image, clinical, and proteomic data to improve temporal alignment across modalities. CN participants served as reference populations for normative modeling to account for disease-unrelated variations and identify deviations from healthy reference. The ADNI cohort was used for model development and training, whereas NACC and UKB were reserved for independent external validation cohorts without model retraining or fine-tuning.

Multimodal data processing

Image data processing T1-weighted structural MRI scans were obtained from ADNI and from a previously curated NACC MRI dataset described by Pan et al. 80 These scans were then processed using a fully automated and standardized FreeSurfer (version 7.2.0) pipeline. The processing pipeline included N3 bias field correction, followed by anatomical registration and intensity normalization. Then, extracranial tissues were removed via automated skull stripping. The skull-stripped images then underwent white matter segmentation and cortical surface reconstruction. Anatomical parcellation was performed using the Desikan-Killiany (DK) atlas 81 , while subcortical structures were automatically segmented. The resulting regional morphometric features were then extracted from the FreeSurfer outputs. For the UKB cohort, we used precomputed imaging-derived phenotypes (IDPs) generated using FreeSurfer, including cortical measures based on the DK white-surface parcellation and volumetric measures derived from automated segmentation. All imaging features were stored in a subject-level matrix indexed by participant identifiers.

Genetics data processing

Genetic features were derived from whole-genome sequencing (WGS) data in ADNI and imputed genotype data in the UKB. To improve cross-cohort consistency and reduce the feature space, analyses in both datasets were restricted to a curated set of AD-associated SNPs obtained from the GWAS Catalog (N = 5,343) 82 . For ADNI, per-chromosome variant call format (VCF) files from WGS data were processed, and genome-wide variant quality control was performed on autosomal chromosomes (1-22) using vcftools 83 , including filters on minor allele frequency (MAF ≥ 0.01), per-variant call rate (≥ 0.95), and Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium (HWE P ≥ 1×10⁻⁶). Variants with insertion or deletion alleles were excluded. Following quality control, variants were further restricted to the curated AD-associated SNP list. For the UKB, imputed genotype data from the imputation release were used in BGEN format. The same curated SNP list was applied by extracting variants from chromosome-wide BGEN files using bgenix, followed by conversion to PLINK2 84 format and restriction to participants to the analytic cohort. Imputed genotypes were hard-called using a fixed probability threshold of 0.1 and exported as compressed VCF files. For both cohorts, filtered VCFs were processed with scikit-allel 85 , and diploid genotypes were converted into an additive allele-count representation (0/1/2) based on the number of alternative alleles, yielding subject-byvariant matrices indexed by participant identifiers, which served as genetic feature inputs.

Clinical data processing

Clinical features were derived from routinely collected phenotypic data in each cohort, including demographic characteristics, neurological examinations, medical history, and vital signs. Prior to modeling, non-informative variants (e.g., participant identifiers) and common study-specific missing codes (e.g., -4) were removed or treated as missing. For measurements reported in different units, values were converted to a consistent unit system before feature construction. Continuous variables were then normalized using a standard scaler to ensure comparable numerical scales, whereas categorical variables were transformed into binary indicators using one-hot encoding. To avoid unstable high-dimensional expansions, categorical fields with high cardinality (>=20) were excluded. After preprocessing, continuous and one-hot encoded categorical variables were combined into participant-level clinical feature matrix as model input.

Proteomic data processing

Protein abundance measurements were used as the proteomic modality. For ADNI, CSF protein measurements generated using the SomaScan v4.1 aptamer-based proteomics platform (~7,000 protein targets) were used in the form of relative fluorescence unit (RFU) values. For UKB, plasma protein measurements generated using the Olink Explore proteomics platform (~3,000 protein targets) were analyzed using the provided normalized protein expression (NPX) values. Within each cohort, proteins with more than 20% missing values or near-zero variance across participants were excluded. The remaining protein measurements were log-transformed where appropriate and standardized using z-score normalization to ensure comparable numerical scales.

Cross-cohort harmonization

To improve comparability across ADNI, NACC, and UKB, modality-specific harmonization procedures were implemented using shared features identified across cohorts. Features with more than 20% missing values or low variance (variance < 0.1) were excluded. Missing values in the remaining continuous features were imputed using a k-nearest neighbors (KNN) imputer (k = 5). Missingness in categorical variables was encoded using a unified missing code. Modality-specific harmonization procedures were applied as follows.

For image data, 189 shared MRI features were identified across cohorts, and ComBat harmonization 86 was applied using ADNI as the reference batch, yielding a harmonized imaging matrix across the three cohorts. For clinical data, continuous variables (e.g., vital signs) were harmonized using ComBat and subsequently combined with the remaining encoded categorical variables, resulting in a merged clinical feature matrix containing 57 variables. For genetic data, variants were merged across cohorts based on shared reference SNP identifiers (rsIDs), yielding a harmonized genetic feature matrix comprising 937 SNPs. For proteomic data, protein measurements were standardized to z-scores using StandardScaler, followed by ComBat harmonization to account for cohort-specific differences. A total of 126 matched proteins were used for downstream modeling.

Subtype assignment

Final subtype discovery was determined after model training. For each participant with AD, the trained decoder generated routing probabilities 𝑞 6 across the 𝐾 subtype-specific experts, and the subtype with the highest routing probability was assigned as the initial label. We set 𝐾 = 4, consistent with previous studies identifying four distinct neuroimaging-based AD subtypes 90 . To assess the stability of identified subtypes, model training was repeated multiple times with different random seeds comprising at least 80% of participants. Because expert indices are arbitrary across training runs, subtype labels were aligned to a reference run using Hungarian matching algorithm 91 . The final subtype of each AD participant was determined by majority voting across aligned runs, and the assignment confidence was quantified as the proportion of runs agreeing with the final subtype label.

Model evaluation and experimental settings

Model comparison

To evaluate multimodal representation learning, we compared three MoE-based encoders: FlexMoE (Flexible Mixture-of-Experts) 47 , LiMoE (Language-Image Mixture of Experts) 42 , and FuseMoE (Mixtureof-Experts Transformers for Fleximodal Fusion) 41 , and a conventional convolutional neural network ResNet 92 . FlexMoE and FuseMoE are specifically designed for flexible multimodal learning. We used AD vs. CN classification as a benchmark task to assess the discriminative capability of the learned diseaserelevant multimodal representations. For AD subtype discovery, we compared ADSMoE with representative classical and deep clustering methods, including GeneSGAN (Gene-guided weaklysupervised clustering via generative adversarial networks) 29 , CDEC (contrastive deep embedded clustering) 93 , EDESC (efficient deep embedded subspace clustering) 94 , K-means 95 and spectral clustering 96 . These approaches represent different strategies for integrating representation learning with clustering or identifying latent patient subgroups. Model comparisons were conducted under multiple experimental settings to assess robustness to feature dimensionality and incomplete multimodal observations. Specifically, we evaluated (1) varying proportions of genetic features (i.e., 50%, 75%, and 100% of the SNP feature set); (2) missingness in selected neuroimaging regions (i.e., missingness of entorhinal, hippocampus, precuneus features); (3) missingness across different categories of clinical data (e.g., demographics, medical history and neurological assessments); and (4) varying proportions of proteomic features (i.e., 50%, 75% and 100% of the protein feature set). These experiments were designed to examine the sensitivity of ADSMoE and competing methods to changes in feature availability across modalities.

Evaluation metrics

We evaluated models in two settings. First, to assess multimodal representation learning, we reported binary classification metrics on the validation and test sets, including accuracy (Acc), F1-score (F1), and the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC). Computational efficiency was evaluated using wall-clock runtime (seconds). Second, to evaluate AD subtyping performance (Figure 2B), we assessed clustering quality using the Davies-Bouldin index (DBI) 54 , with lower values indicating more compact and better-separated clusters. Additionally, to evaluate the biological relevance of the identified subtypes, we quantified genetic associations using the number of subtype-associated single-nucleotide polymorphisms (N-Asso-SNPs) with AD. Following prior imaging-genetics subtyping studies (i.e., GeneSGAN), SNPsubtype associations were assessed using likelihood-ratio tests (LRTs) comparing multinomial models with and without each SNP. N-Asso-SNPs was defined as the number of SNPs with LRT p < 0.05 (K -1 degrees of freedom).

Experimental settings

The model was implemented in PyTorch and trained on a single NVIDIA L4 GPU (24 GB), with the threephase training pipeline taking approximately 3 minutes per run. Performance was assessed using five-fold cross-validation. The encoder and decoder used 16 tokens per modality, a hidden dimension of 128, and 8 attention heads, with 3 and 2 transformer blocks, respectively. The encoder employed 16 experts with top-2 routing, while the decoder used 4 subtype experts with soft mixture routing (dropout = 0.1). We optimized the model using Adam (learning rate 1×10 -4 , weight decay 1×10 -5 , batch size 32): 40 epochs of multimodal representation learning, followed by 30 epochs of joint training. After training on ADNI, the model was frozen and applied to NACC and UKB using cohort-specific residuals, without retraining or fine-tuning. More hyperparameter settings are provided in Supplementary Table S5.

Statistical Analysis

To characterize subtype-specific neuroanatomical patterns, ROI-level differences were assessed using twosample Welch's t-tests 97 , comparing each subtype with all remaining participants. ROIs were ranked by the absolute t-statistic (|𝑡|) for visualization. To test SNP-subtype associations, LRTs were performed using multinomial logistic regression models with subtype labels as the dependent variable and SNP genotype as the predictor. Models were fitted using the limited-memory Broyden-Fletcher-Goldfarb-Shanno (L-BFGS) 98 optimizer with a maximum of 1,000 iterations. The LRT statistic was evaluated using a chi-square distribution with K-1 degrees of freedom, where K denotes the number of subtypes. Genetic association analyses were adjusted for age and sex. Additionally, subtype-specific proteomic associations were assessed by comparing protein measurements across the identified subtypes using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), with an omnibus F-test applied across all subtypes. Multiple testing was controlled using the Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate 99 (FDR; q < 0.05). For genetic associations, the conventional genome-wide significance threshold (P = 5 × 10⁻⁸) and suggestive significance threshold (P = 1 × 10⁻⁵) were also used as reference threshold. For longitudinal analyses, time to AD diagnosis was defined as the interval in years between the baseline assessment and the first recorded AD diagnosis. Subtype labels assigned using data from the first recorded AD diagnosis were linked retrospectively to each participant's earlier CN or MCI visit. Only participants with an observed CN-to-AD or MCI-to-AD transition were included, so there was no right censoring. Kaplan-Meier survival curves 100 were estimated using the lifelines package, and subtype differences were assessed using a multivariate log-rank test. Conversion times exceeding 20 years were excluded from the analysis. Cross-modal associations were visualized using a DIABLO-based Circos plot, retaining component-based similarities with absolute values greater than 0.4.
