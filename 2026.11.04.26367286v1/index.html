How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. 1. Introduction

The decision a non-specialist makes after looking in an ear is usually not a diagnosis but a referral: does this ear need an ENT opinion? We built and deployed a model for that decision, and report here what it achieves and where it fails.

Disorders of the middle and external ear are among the most common reasons for primary care consultation worldwide, and most are treatable when identified. Chronic suppurative otitis media and cholesteatoma carry a risk of permanent hearing loss and intracranial complication when they go unrecognised, while conditions such as cerumen impaction and myringosclerosis need little or no intervention. Distinguishing between these on otoscopy is a learned skill, and in settings where ENT specialists are few, the clinician holding the otoscope is frequently not the clinician best placed to interpret what they see.

Deep learning on otoscopic images has been studied for several years, and reported accuracies are often high (Cha et al., 2019; Başaran et al., 2020). Three gaps recur in that literature and motivated the present work.

First, most published models are framed as closed-set classifiers over a fixed condition list, and are evaluated with accuracy or macro F1. Neither quantity describes the error that matters clinically. A model that is right about which disease is present, but occasionally confident that a diseased ear is normal, is more dangerous than one that is vague about the disease but reliably flags abnormality. We therefore make the binary refer/do-not-refer decision the primary endpoint, fix its operating point in advance on a validation split, and report the differential diagnosis as secondary.

Second, reported probabilities are rarely calibrated. A clinician reading "82% confident" reasonably expects that to mean something about frequency. Without calibration it does not, and a screening aid whose confidence cannot be interpreted is difficult to use safely alongside clinical judgement.

Third, and most consequential for how published numbers should be read, several public otoscopic datasets are derived from video. The same ear then appears across long runs of frames. A random split, or a split grouped only by perceptual hash, places near-identical views of one ear on both sides of the partition and inflates the reported score. We encountered this directly: an earlier version of our own pipeline used hash-based grouping and left roughly half of one source's test images with a near twin in training. The figures reported here come from the corrected split, and the difference between the two is large enough that we treat it as a finding rather than a footnote.

A fourth consideration is what happens after development. A model validated once on retrospective public data says little about performance on a particular clinic's otoscopes, operators and patient mix. We therefore treat deployment as the beginning of measurement rather than its conclusion: every result is reviewed by a surgeon, those verdicts accumulate as a prospective record of local performance, a scheduled backtest re-runs the model in service against them, and retraining is gated on both a frozen test set and an explicit human approval. This paper reports the development and retrospective validation of NHREF OtoScan, a screening aid that recommends ENT referral from a single otoscopic image, names the most likely of twelve conditions with a calibrated differential, and displays the image regions driving the finding. We describe the leakage and confounding controls in enough detail to be reproduced, report performance stratified by source dataset, and describe the deployed system and its review loop. We make no claim of prospective clinical validation; the system is research software, is not a medical device, and is used only with mandatory specialist review.

2.2 Field-of-view normalisation

The three source datasets frame the ear differently: one applies a hard black circular mask, one fills the frame edge to edge, and one sits between the two. Because 8 of the 12 classes are drawn from a single source, that framing is close to a label. Before normalisation we measured 99% of images from one source as having pure black corners against 17% from another, a cue sufficient for a network to score well by identifying the dataset rather than reading the ear.

Every image therefore passes through one normalisation function, in four steps: 1.

Locate the illuminated field of view and crop to it. A pixel counts as illuminated above a luminance of 20, chosen because the darkest source retains roughly that value outside its circular mask. 2. Centre-crop to a square. 3. Resize to the 224 × 224 px network input. 4. Set every pixel outside one fixed inscribed circle to black, at a radius of 0.95 of the half-width. That radius was chosen from the measured radial luminance profile of each source: all three keep their content inside 0.95, while the harder mask edges of two of them fall between 0.95 and 1.0.

After step 4 the region outside the circle is identical for every image from every source and can no longer carry information about provenance.

This function is implemented once, in a single module, and imported by both the training pipeline and the inference service. That is a deliberate design constraint rather than an implementation detail: a preprocessing pipeline reimplemented on the serving side is a standing opportunity for training-serving skew, and here the two cannot drift apart because there is only one definition. The training pipeline caches the normalised copy of each image, so the network sees bytes produced by the same code path that will run in the clinic.

2.3 Partitioning and near-duplicate control

Near-duplicate images are not spread evenly across the three sources, and the difference is stark. In source A the same ear recurs across long runs of near-identical frames, consistent with extraction from oto-endoscopic video: 672 of its 1,040 similarity groups hold more than one image, and its largest holds 36. Sources B and C are predominantly singletons, with largest groups of 3 and 6 images respectively. Any split that treats images as independent will therefore place near-identical views of the same source A ear on both sides of the partition, and the resulting test score measures memorisation rather than generalisation.

Our first implementation grouped images by perceptual hash. This proved inadequate. A perceptual hash catches only frames that are near-pixel-identical; as the otoscope moves, successive frames of the same ear drift past the hash threshold while remaining obviously the same ear to a human observer. Auditing that split, roughly half of source A's test images had a near twin in training, and the scores for that source were correspondingly inflated. We report this because the failure is invisible in the headline metric, since the model simply looks better, and because the same hazard applies to any otoscopic dataset built from video.

The corrected procedure groups by semantic rather than pixel similarity: 5. Each normalised image is embedded using generic ImageNet MobileNetV2 features. These are pretrained features, not features learned from this dataset, so grouping cannot be contaminated by the labels. 6. Any two images of the same class with cosine similarity ≥ 0.93 are joined, and groups are formed as the transitive closure (connected components) of that relation.

7. Whole groups are assigned to exactly one split, so every frame of a given ear stays together.

Two checks constrain the threshold. At 0.93, no group exceeds one tenth of its class, which bounds the risk that transitive joining has chained genuinely distinct ears into one component. After splitting, the maximum similarity between any test image and any same-class training image was 0.92999, confirming the constraint holds in the delivered partition. Because that grouping compares images only within a class, a further review was carried out across classes: every test and validation image was compared with every training image irrespective of label, using the same features and the same 0.93 threshold. Its results are given in section 3.2.

Images were then split 70/15/15 by group under a fixed random seed (1337), giving 3,326 training, 719 validation and 718 test images. The validation split carried every decision that required looking at data: architecture and fine-tuning choices, early stopping, the calibration temperature, and the referral threshold. The test split was scored once, after all of those were frozen. No test image contributed to any choice reported in this paper.

The complete manifest, which records for every image its path, source folder, source dataset, class, group identifier and split, is distributed with the code so that the partition can be audited or reproduced exactly.

2.4 Model architecture and training

We used MobileNetV2 (Sandler et al., 2018) initialised from ImageNet weights, with a global average pooling layer, dropout at 0.3, and a single softmax dense layer over the 12 classes. The architecture was chosen for deployability: the target installation is an ordinary clinic computer with no GPU, and the pooled-linear head also yields exact class activation maps, described below.

Training ran in two stages. The backbone was first frozen and the head trained for 10 epochs with Adam at a learning rate of 1×10⁻³. The backbone was then unfrozen from the block_9_expand layer onward and fine-tuned for a further 18 epochs under a decaying learning-rate schedule. Batch size was 16, constrained by the four-core CPU and available memory of the development machine. Class imbalance was handled with square-root inverse-frequency class weights. Checkpoint selection used validation macro F1; the best epoch was 26, with validation macro F1 of 0.716.

Augmentation was chosen to match the invariances of the clinical setting rather than applied generically: • Rotation through any angle, and horizontal flip.

Otoscope orientation is arbitrary, so every orientation is label-preserving. The fixed circular field of view is invariant under rotation, so no information from outside the circle can leak in.

• Zoom in only, up to 12%. Zooming out would draw a black ring inside the circle, a pattern the service never produces at inference.

• Brightness (±20 levels) and contrast (0.85-1.15). Illumination varies widely between devices and operators.

• Saturation 0.9-1.1 and hue ±0.02. Colour is diagnostic in this domain (erythema, discharge, keratin debris), so chromatic augmentation is deliberately narrow; aggressive hue shifts would destroy signal rather than add robustness.

After augmentation the fixed circular mask is reapplied, so the network always sees exactly the field of view the service will present. Training took approximately 40 minutes on four CPU cores.

2.5 Test-time augmentation, calibration and the referral threshold

Orientation averaging. At inference, logits are averaged over all eight dihedral transforms of the image (four rotations, each with and without reflection) before the softmax. The clinical justification is that the result should not depend on how the otoscope happened to be held; the empirical justification is that validation macro F1 rose from 0.716 for a single view to 0.733 with averaging. The property is asserted in the automated test suite, so a future change that breaks orientation invariance fails the build rather than degrading silently in service.

Temperature scaling. Following Guo et al. (2017), a single scalar temperature was fitted on the validation split alone, by grid search minimising negative log likelihood, giving T = 1.2. Temperature scaling preserves the ranking of classes and therefore cannot change which condition is named; it changes only how confident the stated probability is.

The referral score. The probability that an ear is abnormal is defined as one minus the calibrated probability of the normal class. This is deliberately not a separately trained binary head. Deriving the screening decision from the same distribution that produces the differential guarantees the two can never disagree: a system cannot recommend no referral while naming cholesteatoma as the leading finding.

Threshold selection. The referral threshold was chosen on the validation abnormal ears as the highest threshold whose sensitivity still met the 97% target, which maximises specificity subject to meeting that target, giving 0.2320. In implementation the abnormality scores of the validation abnormal ears are sorted in ascending order, at most floor((1 -0.97)n) of them are permitted to fall below the threshold, and the threshold is set at the next score above that point. The comparison is inclusive: an ear whose abnormality score equals the threshold is referred. Where scores tie at the threshold value the realised sensitivity therefore meets or exceeds the target rather than falling below it, so ties resolve toward referral. The 97% target was set before the test split was scored, as a clinical policy decision: in screening for conditions that include cholesteatoma, a missed abnormality is materially worse than an unnecessary specialist opinion. Thresholds for four alternative targets were computed the same way on validation and are reported in Results as a sensitivity analysis; the deployed value remains the 97% point, and changing it is a one-line configuration change requiring no retraining.

Low-confidence flag.

A finding is marked low-confidence when the margin between the top two classes is below 0.15, or when the entropy of the distribution exceeds 80% of its theoretical maximum. This flag qualifies the named condition only; it never suppresses or alters the referral recommendation.

The resulting behaviour is asymmetric by design. Where the model is uncertain whether an ear is normal, the decision resolves toward referral, and the interface states that it has done so.

2.6 Explanation of findings

The network ends in global average pooling followed by a single linear layer, the arrangement that yields class activation maps (Zhou et al., 2016). Under it the class activation map is not an approximation of the model's reasoning but an algebraic identity: the logit for a class is exactly the spatially pooled inner product of the final feature map with that class's weight vector. Projecting the feature map onto the same weight vector therefore recovers, per spatial location, that location's additive contribution to the logit.

This matters practically. Gradient-based attribution methods estimate what a different architecture might have attended to, and can disagree with the prediction they purport to explain. Here the explanation and the prediction come from one forward pass and cannot diverge. The map is computed from the unrotated view even when orientation averaging is active, so the highlighted regions align with the image the clinician is shown.

The map is rectified at zero, normalised to its peak, and rendered as a blue tint over the normalised image. The interface presents it beside the image as the model received it, so a clinician can see both what the model looked at and what it was given to look at, the latter being how field-of-view problems, such as an image dominated by canal wall, become visible.

2.7 Deployed system and clinical governance

The model is deployed as a web service. A surgeon signs in, enters the patient's hospital record number, age, sex and the ear examined, then uploads an otoscopic image or captures one directly from a USB video otoscope or endoscope. Analysis takes approximately one third of a second on CPU. The result is written to the patient record with timestamp, clinician and the model version that produced it.

Mandatory review.

Every result is reviewed. The surgeon either confirms the finding, selects the correct condition from the list, or records a diagnosis outside the twelve classes as free text. Reviews are append-only. This serves two purposes: no automated output stands as a clinical record without specialist endorsement, and the accumulated verdicts constitute a prospective measurement of performance on this clinic's own patients and equipment, which retrospective public data cannot provide.

Daily backtest.

Once a day the model in service is re-run over every reviewed case and compared against the surgeons' verdicts. Cases that a model was retrained on, and copies of original dataset images, are excluded so that no model is ever scored on its own training data. The dashboard raises an alert when recent referral sensitivity falls below 90%, or when recent agreement falls more than 10 percentage points below earlier agreement, subject to a minimum case count. The backtest runs in a separate process and never modifies the model.

Approval-gated retraining.

Retraining may be started by an administrator once at least 50 reviewed cases exist, and follows a fixed protocol. Approximately 30% of reviewed patients form a reserved evaluation cohort that is excluded from training and calibration. Membership is fixed by a hash of the patient's identifier rather than drawn afresh at each run, so the same patients are reserved every time and a patient reserved once is never later trained on. Splitting by patient also keeps both ears and repeat visits for one patient on a single side of the split. Any uploaded image identical to an image in the frozen validation or test set is excluded from training, so that the comparison cannot be contaminated. The candidate is calibrated and evaluated exactly as the incumbent was, then both are scored on the held-out local patients. A candidate that is worse than the incumbent on either the frozen test set or the local patients cannot be approved. A candidate that passes still requires explicit administrator approval, which switches the active model without a restart and regenerates the validation report.

We chose supervised, gated retraining over continuous online learning deliberately. A model that updated itself from every clinician click could be degraded by a small number of mistaken reviews with no one noticing, and in a clinical setting silent degradation is the failure mode that matters most.

Privacy and auditability.

Patients are identified by hospital record number only; names, dates of birth and contact details are never stored. Images are held outside the web root under random names, because clinic filenames frequently contain patient names, and uploaded filenames are never logged or retained. Stored images are capped at 200 KB, re-encoded only when larger, with the analysed image being the stored image so that record, backtest and retraining all reference identical bytes. Sign-in, examination, review, account change, deletion and model approval are each written to an append-only audit log. The service runs as a dedicated database role owning only its own database.

At the time of writing the system is deployed and operational but has not yet accumulated the reviewed cases required for a first retraining run, and no prospective performance figures are therefore reported in this paper.

2.8 Statistical analysis

The primary endpoint was referral sensitivity and specificity on the held-out test split at the pre-specified threshold, with abnormal defined as any of the eleven non-normal classes. Binomial proportions are reported with 95% Wilson score intervals (Wilson, 1927), which behave better than the normal approximation at the proportions near 1 that arise here.

Discrimination independent of threshold is summarised by AUROC for abnormal versus normal.

Secondary endpoints were twelve-class macro F1, balanced accuracy, overall accuracy, coarse accuracy with the five chronic subtypes merged, and per-class precision, recall and F1 with support. Macro-averaging weights every class equally and is therefore the more demanding summary given the class imbalance; we report accuracy alongside it for comparability with the literature, not as the headline figure.

Calibration was assessed by expected calibration error over equal-width confidence bins and by negative log likelihood, each computed before and after temperature scaling.

Performance was additionally stratified by source dataset, restricted to the four conditions appearing in more than one source. This is the analysis most directly relevant to generalisation: where a condition exists in several sources, consistent recall across them indicates the model is reading the ear rather than the acquisition style.

Group-aware intervals. Because near-duplicate frames make images within a similarity group statistically dependent, referral sensitivity and specificity were also given group-aware intervals by a cluster bootstrap: the similarity groups of the test split were resampled with replacement 2,000 times under a fixed seed, and the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of the resulting distributions taken as the interval.

All reported figures were produced by a single scripted evaluation run over the frozen test split, which also regenerates the validation report distributed with the code. No figure in this paper was computed by hand or transcribed, and the script can be re-run to reproduce every value.

Analyses were performed in Python 3.11 with TensorFlow 2.15 and NumPy. No formal sample size calculation was performed, as the study used the entirety of three fixed public datasets; the resulting precision is reported as confidence intervals throughout, and is explicitly poor for the smallest classes.

The target sensitivity of 97%, fixed on validation before the test split was scored, was met almost exactly on test (97.0%), indicating the operating point transferred without recalibration.

The 15 missed abnormal ears are the result that matters most for safety, and their composition is reassuring. Eleven were myringosclerosis, a calcified plaque of the tympanic membrane that is usually benign and often an incidental finding. Two were acute otitis media, one cerumen impaction, and one fell in the pooled Other findings class. No cholesteatoma was missed, and no chronic suppurative otitis media was missed. The 53 false positives represent normal ears sent for an ENT opinion they did not need. At a prevalence of 69.8% abnormal in this test set the resulting PPV is 90.2%, but that figure will fall in a screening population with lower disease prevalence, and should not be carried over to such a setting unchanged.

An AUROC of 0.974 indicates that discrimination between abnormal and normal is strong independently of where the threshold is placed, so the specificity cost reported here is a consequence of the deliberately aggressive operating point rather than of weak underlying separation.

Group-aware uncertainty. The 718 test images fall into 363 similarity groups, so observations within a group are not independent and the Wilson intervals in Table 4, which assume independence, understate uncertainty. A cluster bootstrap over those groups, resampling them with replacement 2,000 times, gives a 95% interval of 93.8 to 99.6% for referral sensitivity and 66.0 to 83.4% for specificity, against the Wilson intervals of 95.1 to 98.2% and 69.4 to 80.8%. The group-aware intervals are the wider and the more appropriate of the two, and the figures in Table 4 should be read with that in mind.

Cross-class near-duplicate review.

The grouping procedure compares images only within a class, so a separate review compared every test image with every training image irrespective of label. Eleven of the 718 test images (1.5%) had a training image of a different class at or above the 0.93 similarity threshold, and three of those pairs were effectively identical at a cosine similarity of 1.000. Seven pairs lay within source B and four between sources A and B, and they concentrate on four training files: one image labelled normal matched five test images labelled acute otitis media, one labelled acute otitis media matched three test images labelled normal, one matched two test images labelled otitis externa, and one labelled other findings matched a test image labelled chronic suppurative otitis media. These are duplicate or near-duplicate images carrying inconsistent labels rather than further views of one ear. Excluding all eleven moves referral sensitivity by less than a tenth of a percentage point, from 97.01% to 96.96%, and raises specificity from 75.58% to 76.64%, so the reported figures are not materially affected. The direction is what should be expected: a near-twin in training that carries a different label teaches the wrong answer for that image rather than inflating its score.

The perfect score should be distrusted. Chronic otitis media (unspecified) achieved precision and recall of 1.000 on 91 test images. This class is drawn from a single source dataset. A perfect score on a single-source class is more plausibly a signature of that source's acquisition style than evidence of diagnostic skill, and it should not be quoted as such.

Myringosclerosis shows a characteristic asymmetry.

Precision is 1.000 but recall only 0.632: when the model names myringosclerosis it is always right, but it misses more than a third of cases. This single behaviour accounts for 11 of the 15 referral misses. Clinically the consequence is mild, since myringosclerosis is usually benign, but it is the dominant failure mode of the system and is stated here plainly.

The smallest classes are not usable as reported. Otitis externa (recall 0.167 on 6 images), chronic suppurative OM (0.200 on 10) and Other findings (0.400 on 5) have support too small for the point estimates to be meaningful. These figures establish only that the model does not reliably recognise these conditions; they do not establish how badly.

Normal ear recall is high and precision moderate (0.899 / 0.774), which is the expected signature of a system tuned to over-refer: the model is reluctant to assign normal, so when it does so it is usually correct, and some abnormal ears are pulled into the normal class, which is exactly the error the referral threshold is set to intercept.

3.4 Calibration and choice of operating point

Calibration. The fitted temperature was 1.2, indicating the uncalibrated network was mildly overconfident. On the test split, expected calibration error fell from 0.0498 to 0.0430 and negative log likelihood from 0.391 to 0.380. The improvement is modest because the model was not badly miscalibrated to begin with; the value of the step is that a stated confidence can now be read as an approximate frequency, which is the premise on which a clinician can weigh the model's output against their own impression.

Low-confidence flag. The flag fired on 4.0% of test images. Accuracy was 87.2% on unflagged cases against 31.0% on flagged ones. The flag therefore does useful work: it identifies a small subset where the named condition is close to unreliable, and it does so without touching the referral recommendation.

Input characteristics. For reference when assessing whether a new site's images resemble the training distribution, the corpus had median brightness 136 (1st-99th percentile 69-188) and median Laplacian sharpness 17.2 (1st-99th percentile 3.0-564). The wide sharpness range reflects genuinely variable focus quality across sources.

Runtime. Inference, including the eight-view orientation averaging, takes approximately one third of a second per image on an ordinary CPU, with the model occupying about 280 MB in memory. No GPU is required at training or inference, which is what makes deployment on routine clinic hardware practical.

4. Discussion

Principal findings. A compact model running on ordinary CPU hardware met a pre-specified 97% referral sensitivity on a held-out test split, with 75.6% specificity and AUROC 0.974. The failure modes are clinically legible: of 15 missed abnormal ears, 11 were myringosclerosis, a usually benign finding, and no cholesteatoma or chronic suppurative otitis media was missed. Twelve-class macro F1 of 0.686 is modest, and we regard it as the honest figure for this corpus rather than a disappointment, since it reflects six classes with fewer than 20 test images and five overlapping chronic subtypes.

On comparison with published accuracies. Reported accuracies for otoscopic deep learning are frequently higher than ours, and we would caution against reading that difference as a difference in model quality. Our own pipeline produced materially better numbers before its near-duplicate control was corrected. Because public otoscopic datasets include video-derived material, and because perceptual hashing does not catch frames that drift as the otoscope moves, a split can appear sound while leaving half of one source's test images with a near twin in training. We can speak only to our own case, but the lesson generalises: in this domain the reported score is a function of the partitioning method, and papers that do not state how near-duplicates were handled are difficult to compare against. We report the grouping method, the similarity threshold, the maximum test-to-train similarity achieved (0.92999) and the full manifest, so that ours can be checked.

Source dependence is the main scientific caveat.

The gap between 95.5% and 50.0% recall for acute otitis media across two sources is larger than the gap between most published models. Field-of-view normalisation was effective against the framing confound, having been introduced precisely because 99% of one source had black corners against 17% of another, but it cannot neutralise differences in sensor, optics, illumination and compression. Any single-centre validation of an otoscopic model, including a future one of ours, should be read with this in mind.

Design choices framed by clinical use. Three decisions follow from treating the referral decision as primary. Deriving the abnormality score as one minus the calibrated probability of normal, rather than training a separate binary head, makes it structurally impossible for the screening recommendation to contradict the differential. Fixing the operating point on validation to a sensitivity target, rather than reporting the best achievable accuracy, means the deployed behaviour was specified as policy before it was measured. And resolving uncertainty toward referral, while saying so in the interface, matches the asymmetry of harms in this setting.

The explanation design follows the same logic. Because the network ends in average pooling and one linear layer, the activation map is an identity rather than an approximation, so the clinician is shown where the evidence actually came from and not an estimate of where it might have. Displaying the normalised image alongside it also surfaces preprocessing failures, which would otherwise be invisible.

Deployment as measurement.

The most defensible claim available from retrospective public data is a narrow one, and we have tried not to exceed it. What the deployment adds is not performance but measurement: mandatory specialist review turns routine use into a prospective record of local accuracy, the daily backtest detects degradation against those verdicts, and retraining is gated on both a frozen test set and explicit human approval. We chose this over continuous learning because a model that updates itself from every click can be degraded by a handful of mistaken reviews without anyone noticing, and silent degradation is the failure mode least likely to be caught in clinical use.

Comparison with clinical practice. We did not compare the model against clinicians, and the comparison the system is designed for is not model-versus-expert but non-specialist-with-tool versus non-specialist-alone. That study has not been performed, and the figures here do not substitute for it.

5. Limitations

No prospective validation. Every figure in this paper comes from retrospective public data.

The system has not been evaluated on images from the deploying clinic's own equipment, and no prospective performance data existed at the time of writing. This is the single most important limitation and the reason the system is used only with mandatory specialist review.

A prospective, patient-level cohort is reserved and withheld from retraining and calibration so that this evaluation, when it is carried out, is independent of the data the model is fitted to.

Source dependence.

Recall varies substantially by source dataset (Table 8). Performance on unfamiliar otoscopes should be assumed closer to the weaker columns than to the pooled figures.

Single-source classes. Eight of twelve classes are drawn from one source each. For these, acquisition style and condition are confounded and cannot be separated within this corpus. The perfect score for chronic otitis media (unspecified) is the clearest instance and should not be quoted as diagnostic performance.

Small classes. Otitis externa (6 test images), chronic suppurative OM (10), cholesteatoma (9), chronic OM active (13) and Other findings (5) have support too small for reliable estimation. Their intervals are wide and are not reported as point estimates to be relied upon.

In particular, the absence of missed cholesteatoma is encouraging but rests on 9 test images and does not establish a low miss rate.

Pooled and overlapping classes. Other findings combines foreign bodies, pseudomembranes and ventilation tubes, which share no visual signature and were pooled only because each was too small to learn. The five chronic subtypes overlap in the source labelling, with the unspecified class functioning as their parent.

Otomycosis is absent.

No source contains otomycosis images, so fungal infection cannot be recognised and would be forced into another class. Clinicians can record it only as a free-text diagnosis.

One image, one ear. Clinical assessment draws on history, examination of both ears, and often audiometry. This system sees a single static image, and its outputs should be weighed accordingly.

Prevalence dependence. The test split is 69.8% abnormal. Predictive values, particularly PPV, will differ in a screening population with lower prevalence, and the reported 90.2% should not be transferred to such a setting.

No clinician comparison.

The model was not benchmarked against clinicians of any grade, and no study of clinician-plus-model performance has been conducted.

Label provenance.

Ground-truth labels are those supplied by the source datasets. We did not re-adjudicate them, and the accuracy ceiling is therefore set by the quality of the original labelling, which we cannot independently verify. The cross-class review in section 3.2 shows the problem directly: eleven test images have a near-duplicate in the training set under a different label, three of them effectively identical, concentrated in source B. These are inconsistencies in the source collections rather than errors introduced here, but they bound the accuracy any model trained on this corpus can demonstrate.

6. Conclusions

A MobileNetV2 classifier with orientation averaging, temperature scaling and a validation-fixed referral threshold achieved 97.0% referral sensitivity and 75.6% specificity on 718 held-out otoscopic images, with AUROC 0.974 for abnormal versus normal. Missed abnormalities were concentrated in myringosclerosis, and no cholesteatoma was missed in this test set.

Two methodological points carry beyond this model. First, near-duplicate control determines the reported score in otoscopic datasets built from video: perceptual hashing was insufficient in our pipeline, and the corrected feature-similarity grouping produced materially lower, and more credible, numbers. Second, performance varies substantially by source dataset even after the framing confound is removed, so single-corpus figures should be read as upper bounds for unfamiliar equipment.

The system is deployed with mandatory ENT review, a scheduled backtest against clinician verdicts, and retraining gated on both a frozen test set and explicit human approval. Its purpose in that deployment is to begin measuring prospective performance on local patients and equipment, which retrospective public data cannot establish. Until that evidence exists, the system is a screening aid under specialist supervision and is not a diagnostic device.
