Using voxel-level deep learning, we derived whole-brain and nine regional brain-age gaps (BAGs) spanning cortical, subcortical and cerebellar regions from T1-weighted MRI in 46,254 UK Biobank participants.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

A single brain-age estimate may obscure regional differences in clinical vulnerability. Using voxel-level deep learning, we derived whole-brain and nine regional brain-age gaps (BAGs) spanning cortical, subcortical and cerebellar regions from T1-weighted MRI in 46,254 UK Biobank participants. Genetic correlation estimates in 41,067 participants (r g = 0.56-0.93) exceeded phenotypic correlations across all 45 pairs, yet regional profiles varied across 24 hospital-recorded diagnoses. Oligodendrocyte annotations predominated for whole-brain and cerebellar BAGs, whereas annotations for caudate BAG included astrocyte and vascular classes.

In the Alzheimer's Disease Neuroimaging Initiative, temporal BAG showed stronger associations with cerebrospinal fluid Aβ42 and cognition than whole-brain BAG when modelled together. A regional model modestly improved discrimination of observed conversion from mild cognitive impairment to Alzheimer's disease during available follow-up compared with a wholebrain model. Polygenic scores showed cross-cohort associations with hypertension and osteoporosis. Regional BAGs provide complementary clinical information within a shared polygenic architecture.

Introduction

Brain ageing is a spatiotemporally heterogeneous process characterized by region-specific variability in the timing and magnitude of structural changes across the lifespan [1] [2] [3] . Brain-age models estimate an individual's apparent brain age from neuroimaging and summarize deviation from chronological age as the brain-age gap (BAG) [4] [5] [6] . A higher BAG has been associated with mortality, mild cognitive impairment (MCI) and dementia, Alzheimer's disease (AD)-related biomarkers, and subsequent clinical decline [7] [8] [9] [10] . A single whole-brain BAG, however, may obscure regional variation in age-and disease-related brain changes 5, 6, [11] [12] [13] [14] . Regional BAGs may therefore capture clinical and genetic information not represented by a whole-brain estimate.

Previous brain-age studies commonly used predefined morphometric features, such as cortical thickness and regional volumes, or voxelwise grey-matter measures with conventional machinelearning models 13, [15] [16] [17] [18] . Regional models have identified disorder-specific BAG patterns and provided evidence for the heritability of regional BAGs 13, 18 . Deep-learning models can learn spatial features directly from three-dimensional MRI, although many still yield a single wholebrain age estimate 6, 19, 20 . Other studies have inferred regional contributions to global brain-age predictions using post hoc feature attribution 15, 21, 22 . Voxelwise local-age models provide another approach by generating spatially resolved age maps that can be averaged within anatomical regions to derive regional estimates 12, 14, 23, 24.

Genome-wide association studies (GWASs) of whole-brain BAG have identified associated loci and genetic relationships with neurological, psychiatric and systemic traits 25, 26 . Multimodal studies have compared the genetic architecture and cell-type enrichment of grey-matter, white-. matter and functional-connectivity BAGs 27 . Recent GWASs have also identified genetic associations with deep-learning-derived local brain age across cortical and subcortical regions 28, 29 . However, the degree of genome-wide genetic sharing among whole-brain, cortical, subcortical and cerebellar BAGs remains incompletely characterized. Their potential to complement whole-brain BAG in clinical associations and cell-type enrichment, and their genetic relationships with leukocyte telomere length and proteomic age, also require clarification.

Here, we trained separate three-dimensional convolutional neural networks (3D-CNNs) to learn spatial features directly from whole-brain and atlas-masked regional T1-weighted MRI volumes in UK Biobank, yielding one whole-brain and nine regional BAG phenotypes. We assessed associations with hospital-recorded diagnoses in UK Biobank and with AD biomarkers and cognition in the Alzheimer's Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI), where we also evaluated discrimination of observed MCI-to-AD conversion. We used these BAGs as quantitative phenotypes for GWAS and post-GWAS analyses and evaluated their polygenic scores in UK Biobank, All of Us and ADNI.

Results

Study overview

We combined imaging, clinical and genetic analyses across three cohorts (Fig. 1a-d). In the UK Biobank, we derived one whole-brain and nine regional BAG phenotypes from 46,254 imaging participants. Models were trained using four-fold cross-validation in 25,642 participants meeting prespecified disease-exclusion criteria and evaluated in an independent test set of 20,612 participants. BAG denotes bias-corrected predicted age minus chronological age; prediction accuracy was evaluated before bias correction (Methods). GWASs included 41,067 UK Biobank imaging participants with genotype data and self-reported White British ethnicity. Post-GWAS analyses included gene mapping, heritability and genetic correlation estimation, cell-type enrichment, polygenic risk score (PRS) derivation and validation, PRS-based phenome-wide association studies (PheWAS) in UK Biobank and All of Us and exploratory bidirectional Mendelian randomization. The framework was then applied to ADNI for domain-adapted clinical analyses, exploratory genetic concordance analyses and polygenic follow-up.

Region-wise brain-age prediction

We trained separate voxel-level 3D-CNNs for the whole brain and nine anatomical regions (Fig. 1a; Supplementary Fig. 1). In UK Biobank test participants without selected diagnoses (n = 10,776), the whole-brain model achieved a mean absolute error (MAE) of 2.39 years (Pearson r = 0.93), and regional MAEs ranged from 3.17 to 4.43 years (r = 0.73-0.87). Corresponding MAEs in participants with selected diagnoses (n = 9,836) were 2.51 and 3.29-4.45 years, with regional age correlations of r = 0.72-0.85 (Supplementary Table 1). In UK Biobank participants both with and without selected diagnoses, regional models showed lower MAEs than those reported in the regional studies summarized in Supplementary Table 1. Diagnosis-specific correlations with chronological age were generally higher than those reported by Kaufmann et al. 18 (Supplementary Table 2). After fine-tuning in cognitively normal (CN) ADNI participants (n = 1,179), cross-validated MAEs were 3.46 years (r = 0.80) for the whole-brain model and 3.87-5.10 years (r = 0.49-0.75) for regional models (Supplementary Table 1). Diagnosis-specific correlations and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) are reported in Supplementary Table 2.

Disease-associated regional BAG profiles

We compared BAGs between cases and controls across 24 hospital-recorded diagnoses in the independent UK Biobank test set, adjusting for age and sex (Methods; Supplementary Note 1).

Nineteen of the 24 diagnoses were associated with higher BAG in at least one phenotype (q < 0.05, Benjamini-Hochberg (BH) correction across the ten BAG phenotypes separately within each diagnosis; Fig. 2a; Supplementary Fig. 3; Supplementary Table 3). All seven metabolic diagnoses were associated with higher BAG in at least nine of the ten phenotypes. Caudate BAG had the largest adjusted case-control difference among the ten BAGs for six of the seven metabolic diagnoses, including type 1 diabetes (5.91 years) and type 2 diabetes (3.31 years). Bipolar disorder, anxiety disorders and both addiction-related diagnoses were associated with higher BAG in all ten phenotypes. Multiple sclerosis showed the largest differences, reaching 7.52 years for parietal BAG.

We compared the adjusted case-control difference in each regional BAG with that in wholebrain BAG across 24 UK Biobank diagnoses (nine regions per diagnosis; 216 comparisons).

When expressed in years, regional differences were significantly larger in 82 comparisons. After dividing each BAG by its control-group standard deviation, only parietal BAG in multiple sclerosis showed a significantly larger case-control difference than whole-brain BAG, whereas regional differences were significantly smaller in 58 comparisons (Supplementary Table 3, continued).

In ADNI, participants with MCI or AD had higher BAGs than cognitively normal participants across all ten phenotypes after adjustment for age, sex and MRI field strength (all q < 0.05; Fig 2a; Supplementary Table 3). All ten BAGs were positively associated with baseline diagnostic stage (all q < 10 -27 ), with the largest coefficient for temporal BAG (Supplementary Table 6).

AD biomarkers and cognition

We assessed each BAG against seven AD-related measures: amyloid and flortaucipir tau positron emission tomography (PET), cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) amyloid-β 1-42 (Aβ42) and tau phosphorylated at threonine 181 (p-tau181), Clinical Dementia Rating-Sum of Boxes (CDR-SB), 13-item Alzheimer's Disease Assessment Scale-Cognitive Subscale (ADAS-Cog13) and Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE). Models adjusted for age, sex and APOE ε4 allele count. All 70 associations were significant after false discovery rate (FDR) correction, with higher BAGs associated with greater amyloid and tau burdens, lower CSF Aβ42, higher CSF p-tau181 and poorer cognitive performance (Fig. 2b; Supplementary Table 4).

Temporal BAG had the largest absolute coefficient in single-BAG models for six of the seven outcomes. A 1-s.d. higher temporal BAG was associated with 160.0 pg ml -1 lower CSF Aβ42 and a 4.16-point higher ADAS-Cog13 score (both q < 10 -24 ; Supplementary Table 4).

For each outcome, we included temporal and whole-brain BAGs in the same regression model and compared their coefficients. Temporal BAG was more strongly associated with lower CSF Aβ42 (coefficient-contrast q = 2.0 × 10 -4 ) and poorer performance on all three cognitive measures (q = 0.0019-0.032) than whole-brain BAG. Whole-brain BAG was more strongly associated with higher CSF p-tau181 than temporal BAG (q = 0.017), whereas their associations with amyloid and tau-PET did not differ significantly (Supplementary Table 5).

Discrimination of observed MCI-to-AD conversion

We compared regional and whole-brain BAG models in 303 observed MCI-to-AD converters and 718 participants whose diagnosis remained MCI during follow-up (n = 1,021). The regional model achieved an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) of 0.722 versus 0.674 for the whole-brain model (ΔAUC = 0.048; 95% bootstrap CI, 0.019-0.077). The temporal-only model had a similar AUC (0.721; Fig. 2c). The regional-whole-brain AUC difference was smaller under stricter follow-up criteria (ΔAUC = 0.034; 95% CI, -0.002 to 0.069; Supplementary Note 9; Supplementary Data 1).

Shared and phenotype-restricted GWAS associations

GWASs of the ten BAG phenotypes in UK Biobank identified genome-wide significant associations across multiple chromosomes (Fig. 3a; Supplementary Fig. 4). Functional Mapping and Annotation of GWAS (FUMA) identified 125 distinct lead variants across these analyses; cross-phenotype locus merging yielded 64 genomic risk loci (Supplementary Table 25). Of the 64 loci, 43 were detected in only one BAG GWAS at P < 5 × 10 -8 , whereas 21 were detected in two or more (Supplementary Table 25). The strongest association was with thalamic BAG at 17q21.31 (rs2732689; P = 5.7 × 10 -38 ). This locus overlaps the MAPT-containing inversion region, which has been associated with brain structure and neurodegeneration 30, 31 . The region was associated with all ten BAGs and accounted for approximately 85% of genome-wide significant variant-phenotype pairs (Supplementary Table 25). A locus at 6p21.1, annotated to RUNX2, reached genome-wide significance for caudate, putaminal, thalamic and whole-brain BAGs. Genome-wide significant associations were also observed in the extended major histocompatibility complex (MHC) region for frontal, insular and temporal BAGs (Supplementary Table 25, continued).

Of the 125 lead variants, 42 had GWAS Catalog mapped-gene annotations, defining 35 mapped gene groups (Supplementary Table 11). These groups comprise one or more annotated genes, not independent loci or established causal genes. A group was assigned to a BAG when an annotated variant from the pooled lead set appeared in that BAG's FUMA candidate-variant results (Supplementary Note 10). Eighteen groups were assigned to only one BAG. Four mapped gene groups-WNT3, MAPT, LINC02210-CRHR1 and ARL17B/MAPK8IP1P1-were detected in all ten BAGs and arose from the same extended 17q21.31 locus.

Mapped gene profiles also varied across BAG phenotypes (Fig. 3b; Supplementary Table 11).

Caudate BAG yielded the most mapped gene groups, followed by the frontal lobe and putamen; the occipital and parietal BAGs yielded no groups detected in that phenotype alone; and four of the nine cerebellar groups were detected only for cerebellar BAG, the highest such proportion across BAG phenotypes, consistent with reported differences between cerebellar and cerebral ageing 32, 33.

We assigned 444 distinct GWAS Catalog reported traits to 27 curated Experimental Factor Ontology (EFO) categories across the mapped gene groups (Supplementary Table 12).

Neuroimaging traits were the most frequent annotations in both phenotype-restricted and shared groups. Cardiometabolic annotations were concentrated in phenotype-restricted groups, largely reflecting SLC39A8 (Supplementary Fig. 7; Supplementary Note 3; Supplementary Table 13).

Shared polygenic architecture and cell-type enrichment profiles across BAGs

Single-nucleotide polymorphism (SNP)-based heritability was significant for all ten BAG phenotypes. Across the nine regional BAG phenotypes, the estimates ranged from 0.105 for the occipital lobe BAG to 0.184 for the caudate BAG, with a mean of 0.136. The whole-brain BAG showed a comparable estimate (h2 = 0.115, S.E. = 0.018; Supplementary Table 14). Only caudate BAG had significantly higher heritability than whole-brain BAG after BH correction across nine comparisons (difference=0.069; S.E.=0.016; q = 1.1 × 10 -4 ; Supplementary Table 14, continued).

All 45 pairwise genetic correlations among the ten BAG phenotypes were positive (r g = 0.564-0.927) and significant after FDR correction. Correlations were particularly high among the frontal, insular, occipital, parietal, putaminal and temporal BAGs (r g = 0.790-0.927). Genetic correlation estimates exceeded the corresponding phenotypic correlations (r = 0.335-0.601) in every pair (Fig. 4a; Supplementary Table 15).

Genetic correlations with the first principal component (PC1) of leukocyte telomere length measurements 34 were negative for all ten BAGs (r g = -0.122 to -0.013), with those for wholebrain and cerebellar BAGs remaining significant after BH correction across 21 external-ageing tests (q = 0.014 and 0.016, respectively). In contrast, the ProtAge-204 proteomic-age gap 35 showed positive genetic correlation estimates with eight of the ten BAGs (r g = -0.082 to 0.169), although none survived after FDR correction (smallest q = 0.061, frontal BAG; Fig. 4a).

ProtAge-204 and telomere length PC1 were inversely genetically correlated (r g = -0.189, q = 0.0012; Supplementary Note 6; Supplementary Table 15).

. FUMA cell-type enrichment analysis identified 173 significant BAG-annotation associations across five BAGs after Bonferroni correction within each BAG. Of these, 142 met the withindataset retention criteria (Methods; Supplementary Table 16).

Oligodendrocyte annotations accounted for all 14 whole-brain associations and 111 of 120 cerebellar associations. Caudate BAG retained five annotations: two astrocyte, two fibroblast/vascular leptomeningeal cell (VLMC) and one vascular annotation without subtype resolution. Temporal BAG retained two oligodendrocyte precursor cell (OPC) annotations, and thalamic BAG retained one fibroblast/VLMC annotation. No annotations were retained for frontal, parietal, occipital, insular or putaminal BAGs, or for neuronal or pericyte/mural classes (Fig. 4b; Supplementary Table 16).

Cross-cohort associations of BAG polygenic risk scores

We derived ten BAG PRS using SBayesRC and GWAS summary statistics from 30,800 White British UK Biobank imaging participants (Methods). All ten PRS were positively associated with their corresponding measured BAGs in the non-overlapping held-out UK Biobank sample (n = 9,997). In univariable models, adjusted R² ranged from 1.37% for occipital BAG to 3.83% for caudate BAG (Supplementary Table 17; Supplementary Fig. 8).

We conducted a PRS-based PheWAS in three relatedness-pruned samples excluded from PRS derivation: White British UK Biobank non-imaging (n = 359,334) and held-out imaging (n = 9,997) participants, and European-ancestry All of Us participants with complete covariates (n = 109,774; Supplementary Table 24). At q < 0.05 after BH correction within each PRS and sample, the non-imaging analysis identified 191 associations across 84 phecodes; 185 associations linked higher PRS to higher disease odds. In All of Us, we identified 14 significant regional BAG PRS-. phecode associations involving eight phecodes, all linking higher PRS to higher disease odds (Fig. 5; Supplementary Tables 18 19 20). Nine regional PRS-phecode associations were significant in both the UK Biobank non-imaging and All of Us samples, with concordant positive directions, spanning hypertension, essential hypertension and osteoporosis (Supplementary Tables 18 19 20).

In the non-imaging sample, higher PRS for five BAGs were associated with higher odds of type 2 diabetes (q = 2.1 × 10 -4 to 0.021; Supplementary Table 18). In the held-out imaging sample, higher temporal BAG PRS was associated with lower odds of phlebitis and thrombophlebitis, including its lower-extremity subtype (two associations; Supplementary Table 18).

In ADNI, PRS-BAG correlations were positive for all ten phenotypes but modest (r = 0.045-0.152; n = 1,693), with eight nominally significant associations (Supplementary Fig. 9a; Supplementary Table 21). No PRS association with AD or MCI status survived after FDR correction (n = 1,845; Supplementary Fig. 9b; Supplementary Tables 22 and 23).

Bidirectional Mendelian randomization

Exploratory bidirectional Mendelian randomization (MR) identified eight inverse-varianceweighted (IVW) associations passing trait-and direction-specific Bonferroni correction and four additional FDR-only associations. IVW estimates linked doctor-diagnosed diabetes and higher body mass index (BMI) to higher whole-brain BAG, and hypertension to higher caudate BAG.

With thalamic BAG as the exposure and hypertension as the outcome, the estimate was negative. These estimates were not consistently supported by sensitivity analyses (Supplementary Figs. 10 and 11; Supplementary Note 12; Supplementary Data 2).

Discussion

Brain-age models trained separately on three-dimensional regional MRI inputs revealed substantial shared genetic influences alongside variation in clinical associations and cell-type annotations. Regional MAEs were lower than those reported in the regional studies summarized in Supplementary Table 1, although comparisons are descriptive because samples, regional definitions and evaluation designs differed. In ADNI models containing both temporal and whole-brain BAGs, temporal BAG was more strongly associated with CSF Aβ42 and cognition, whereas whole-brain BAG was more strongly associated with CSF p-tau181. Their associations with amyloid and tau-PET did not differ significantly. Regional models showed a modest advantage over whole-brain BAG for discriminating observed MCI-to-AD conversion, but combining regions did not clearly outperform temporal BAG alone. In UK Biobank, regional case-control differences were often significantly larger than whole-brain differences in years, but rarely so after scaling each BAG by its control-group standard deviation. Together, these findings support complementary clinical information from regional and whole-brain BAGs, with their relative strengths varying across clinical outcomes. Jawinski et al. 26 reported a genetic correlation of 0.74 between grey-and white-matter BAGs, indicating substantial but incomplete genetic overlap. Recent cortical local-age GWAS work distinguished broadly distributed from spatially restricted variant effects 28 . We extended the comparison to genome-wide genetic sharing among whole-brain, cortical, subcortical and cerebellar BAGs. Genetic correlations exceeded phenotypic correlations for every pair; the lower phenotypic correlations may reflect non-genetic variation and measurement error. The recurrent 17q21.31/MAPT signal coexisted with loci and mapped gene groups detected in one or a few BAGs, although detection in a single phenotype does not establish a region-specific effect.

Oligodendrocyte enrichment in whole-brain and cerebellar BAGs is consistent with white matter contributions to structural brain ageing 36, 37 and evidence linking oligodendrocyte dysfunction to age-related white matter pathology 38 . Other retained annotations included astrocytes and fibroblast/VLMCs for caudate BAG, OPCs for temporal BAG and fibroblast/VLMCs for thalamic BAG. Comparing BAGs derived from the same participants and imaging modality complements previous multimodal analyses 27 (Fig. 4b; Supplementary Table 16). These are differences in observed enrichment profiles; enrichment differences between BAGs were not formally tested.

Genetic correlation estimates were negative with telomere length and mostly positive with ProtAge-204. In the relatedness-pruned UK Biobank and All of Us samples, regional BAG PRS shared positive associations with hypertension, essential hypertension and osteoporosis.

However, no PRS association with AD or MCI diagnostic status in ADNI was significant after multiple test correction. Exploratory MR findings involving metabolic traits were not consistently supported by sensitivity analyses and do not establish causal effects.

Several limitations warrant consideration. Genetic analyses were restricted to self-reported White British UK Biobank participants or genetically inferred European-ancestry participants in ADNI and All of Us, leaving cross-ancestry generalizability untested. Differences in external GWAS sample sizes also limit comparisons of statistical significance.

. Cross-sectional T1-weighted MRI and broad atlas masks cannot resolve within-person regional ageing trajectories, and disease-related changes such as multiple-sclerosis lesions may contribute to BAG. Protein sampling preceded MRI by a mean of 8.60 years, so phenotypic correlations between ProtAge-204 and BAGs do not compare contemporaneous ageing measures (Supplementary Note 6). ADNI was smaller and clinically enriched, and nine of ten BAGs were associated with MRI field strength, although temporal-whole-brain contrasts and diagnosticstage associations were similar in 3 T-only analyses (Supplementary Tables 5 and 6). Restriction to quality-control-passed flortaucipir scans reduced the primary tau-PET sample, while differences between tracers limited interpretation of the pooled tau-PET analysis. The attenuated conversion-discrimination advantage under stricter follow-up requirements warrants independent longitudinal validation.

In summary, whole-brain and regional BAGs share substantial polygenic influences but show regional variation in their observed clinical associations and cell-type enrichment annotations.

Methods

Ethical approval

All research was conducted in accordance with relevant ethical regulations. This study used the UK Biobank Resource under Application 45227; UK Biobank received ethical approval from the North West Multi-centre Research Ethics Committee. ADNI was approved by the institutional review boards of all participating institutions, and the All of Us Research Program was approved by its institutional review board. All participants provided written informed consent.

Statistics and reproducibility

Analyses included participants meeting the eligibility criteria for each cohort and analysis. Major cohort subsets are summarized in Supplementary Table 24; analysis-specific sample sizes are reported in the corresponding result tables and Supplementary Data 1.

Unless otherwise specified, tests were two-sided. Tests of nonzero SNP heritability and MAGMA tests for positive cell-type enrichment were one-sided. Multiple testing was controlled using the BH procedure at FDR < 0.05 within the families defined for each analysis. BHadjusted P values are denoted by q. Genome-wide significance was set at P < 5 × 10 -8 , and celltype enrichment used Bonferroni correction within each BAG phenotype. All confidence intervals were 95%. Analysis-specific statistical methods, validation procedures and software versions are provided in the corresponding Methods sections and Supplementary Notes. Brain-age modelling included 46,254 UK Biobank participants: 25,642 for training and 20,612 for independent testing. BAG GWAS and post-GWAS analyses included 41,067 imaging participants with self-reported White British ethnicity after genotype quality control. ADNI included 2,986 imaging participants: 1,179 cognitively normal participants for fine-tuning and 1,807 for inference (1,303 with MCI, 420 with AD and 84 with significant memory concern). Genetic analyses in ADNI and All of Us were restricted to genetically inferred European ancestry. All of Us relatedness pruning retained 112,080 candidates; excluding 2,306 participants with missing sex left 109,774 with complete covariates. Related-disease exclusions and sex restrictions further reduced phenotype-specific sample sizes (Supplementary Table 24).

Image preprocessing

UK Biobank provided skull-stripped T1-weighted images nonlinearly registered to Montreal Neurological Institute 152 (MNI152) space 39 . ADNI scans underwent N4 bias-field correction, brain extraction and nonlinear registration to MNI152 using the Advanced Normalization Tools Python interface (ANTsPy v0.5.4) 40 . Images from both cohorts were resampled to a common 128 × 128 × 128 grid. After resampling and before regional masking, ADNI image intensities were . normalized by scaling the 99th percentile of positive voxel values to 105, without upper clipping.

The MNI structural atlas 41,42 defined nine regional inputs: frontal, parietal, temporal and occipital lobes; insula; putamen; caudate; thalamus; and cerebellum.

Region-ise brain-age prediction and BAG derivation

Separate 3D-CNNs predicted chronological age from whole-brain and each regional MRI input using a previously described architecture 20 (Supplementary Fig. 1). Regional atlas labels were binarized, dilated by four voxels on the atlas grid and resampled to the image grid by nearestneighbour interpolation. The same mask was applied to every participant, setting voxels outside the region to zero. Models were trained in 25,642 UK Biobank participants selected using previously reported disease-exclusion criteria 20 . Within each fold, parameters were optimized on the other three folds, and the checkpoint with the lowest held-out-fold MAE was retained. The independent test set (n = 20,612) was used for neither optimization nor checkpoint selection; test predictions were averaged across the four models. In both cohorts, accuracy was assessed using uncorrected predicted ages (MAE and Pearson r; Supplementary Table 1). Diagnosis-specific correlation intervals used the Fisher z transformation, treating fitted predictions as fixed (Supplementary Table 2). Exclusion criteria and training settings are given in Supplementary Note 4.

Age-bias correction coefficients were estimated from pooled fold-held-out training predictions and applied unchanged to all UK Biobank participants 43 . BAG was corrected predicted age minus chronological age, with positive values indicating an older-appearing brain (Supplementary Note 5; Supplementary Fig. 2).

Disease-associated regional BAG profiles across the UK Biobank and ADNI cohorts UK Biobank hospital inpatient ICD-10 records were mapped to phecodes v1.2. Cases required at least one qualifying record; 24 phenotypes had at least 30 cases in the independent test set. Selfreported illness codes were not used. Records before and after MRI were included, so these were cross-sectional associations with recorded diagnoses (Supplementary Note 1).

Cases were compared with 10,776 test-set participants without selected diagnoses. Controlselection criteria differed from the training-exclusion criteria (Supplementary Notes 1 and 4). For each diagnosis, we fitted a linear regression of BAG on case status, adjusting for age and sex.

The case coefficient represented the adjusted case-control difference in BAG years. Breast-and prostate-cancer comparisons were sex-restricted and did not include sex as a covariate. Regional and whole-brain case coefficients were compared in the same participants, in years and after scaling each BAG by its control-group standard deviation. These contrasts used standard errors clustered by participant. An expanded-control sensitivity analysis added fold-held-out predictions from 17,910 training participants without selected diagnoses, giving 28,686 controls.

The same sex restrictions applied throughout (Supplementary Note 1; Supplementary Table 3, continued). ADNI analyses compared MCI (n = 1,232) and AD (n = 398) separately with cognitively normal participants (n = 1,122), adjusting for age at MRI, sex and field strength (1.5 T or 3 T). We excluded 149 participants with missing MRI field strength and one additional participant whose sex was not coded as male or female. Two-sided Wald tests and 95% confidence intervals were calculated using heteroskedasticity-robust standard errors (HC3). BH correction was applied across ten BAGs within each diagnosis, including the expanded-control analysis. Regional-whole-brain contrasts were corrected across nine regions within each diagnosis, separately for each scale.

Domain-adapted brain-age estimation in the ADNI cohort

ADNI models used the same masks and architecture, initialized from the UK Biobank checkpoint for the corresponding target and fold. All layers were fine-tuned in 1,179 cognitively normal participants using shuffled four-fold cross-validation with a fixed seed. As in UK Biobank, checkpoints were selected by held-out-fold MAE (Supplementary Note 4). Fold-heldout predictions in cognitively normal participants were used to assess accuracy and estimate ADNI-specific bias correction. Predictions for other participants were averaged across the four models. Correction coefficients estimated in cognitively normal participants were applied unchanged to all participants before BAG calculation.

Discrimination of observed MCI-to-AD conversion

Baseline was defined as each participant's first MRI visit matched to a diagnosis in the ADNI diagnostic summary (DXSUM). Eligibility required MCI at that visit. Conversion required a subsequent AD diagnosis at a visit with MRI. After exclusions, the analysis included 1,021 participants: 303 converters and 718 non-converters whose diagnosis remained MCI throughout the available diagnostic record. At least one post-baseline diagnostic visit was required, but no minimum follow-up duration or fixed conversion window was imposed in the primary analysis.

Logistic regression models included age and sex together with either whole-brain BAG or selected regional BAGs. Performance was evaluated using stratified ten-fold outer crossvalidation. Within each outer-training fold, inner five-fold cross-validation selected the number of regional predictors, k = 1-9, with regions ranked using training participants only. Ranking used the univariate AUC of regional BAG residuals obtained using whole-brain adjustment coefficients estimated in cognitively normal participants; the prediction models used the biascorrected regional BAGs rather than these residuals. Predictors were standardized using training data only. Fixed-k models were evaluated descriptively.

AUCs were calculated from pooled outer-fold predictions. Conditional on precomputed BAGs, 1,000 participant-level bootstrap replicates repeated ranking, k selection, standardization, fitting and held-out evaluation to obtain percentile confidence intervals for the regional-whole-brain AUC difference. Copies of each resampled participant remained in the same fold at every crossvalidation level. Sensitivity analyses used all nine regional BAGs in a ridge model, with penalty selection by inner three-fold cross-validation, or required at least 24 months of follow-up for non-converters (Supplementary Note 9; Supplementary Data 1).

Genome-wide association and functional annotation analyses

BAG GWASs included 41,067 White British UK Biobank participants and used SAIGE v1.1.8 for null-model fitting and v1.1.9 for association testing 44, 45 . Each BAG was rank-based inversenormal transformed across the full sample before additive association testing adjusted for age, sex and ten genetic principal components. A dense genetic relationship matrix modelled relatedness with leave-one-chromosome-out analysis. UK Biobank imputation v3 BGEN files on GRCh37 46 yielded 35,784,891 tested variants per phenotype after requiring minor allele count ≥10 and missingness ≤15%. Locus definition and annotation retained 9,458,475 variants with analysis-sample minor allele frequency (MAF) ≥0.01 and mfi imputation quality (INFO) ≥0.8 (Supplementary Note 10; Supplementary Table 25).

FUMA v1.8.2 47 used the 1000 Genomes Phase 3 European reference to identify independent significant variants (P < 5 × 10 -8 ; linkage disequilibrium r 2 < 0.6) and lead variants (r 2 < 0.1). Independent signals within 250 kb were merged into genomic risk loci. The MHC interval (GRCh37 chr6:29,614,758-33,170,276) was excluded from FUMA annotation, including leadvariant and locus definition. This exclusion did not apply to GWAS association testing or MAGMA gene analysis.

Mapped gene groups were defined by distinct GWAS Catalog MappedGene annotations rather than genomic intervals. Lead variants were pooled across the ten BAG GWASs. A group was detected in a BAG phenotype when at least one variant from this pooled set carried the group annotation in that phenotype's FUMA candidate-variant results (P < 0.05 and r2 ≥ 0.6 with an independent significant variant). Groups detected in one phenotype were classified as unique, and those detected in two or more as shared (Fig. 3b). Genotype processing and annotation details are provided in Supplementary Note 10, and trait-category curation in Supplementary Note 3. Exploratory GWASs were also performed in 1,693 ADNI participants of genetically inferred European ancestry. UK Biobank lead variants selected from the common biallelic variant set ProtAge-204 was trained locally using the fixed 204-protein panel of Argentieri et al. 35 , with protein quality control and k-nearest-neighbour imputation described in Supplementary Note 6.

The proteomic-age gap from out-of-fold predictions was age-bias corrected and inverse-normal transformed before GWAS in 38,806 UK Biobank participants with self-reported British ethnicity and genotype data. Genetic correlations were estimated using cross-trait LDSC with unconstrained intercepts. BH correction was applied jointly across 21 correlations: ten BAGs with each external ageing measure and the correlation between the two ageing measures (Supplementary Table 15).

Phenotypic correlations between ProtAge-204 and BAGs used the bias-corrected gaps before inverse-normal transformation in 3,784 participants. Partial correlations adjusted for sex, age at protein sampling and the protein-MRI interval, with BH correction across ten BAGs (Supplementary Note 6; Supplementary Table 15).

Cell-type enrichment analysis

The FUMA cell-type module (FUMA v1.8.2; MAGMA v1.10) 50 tested whether genes with greater cell-type expression specificity showed stronger gene-level BAG associations.

Bonferroni correction was applied within each BAG across dataset-cell-type tests.

Significant annotations were retained when they were the only significant annotation in their dataset or met FUMA's within-dataset conditional criteria. Cross-dataset pairwise conditional analyses assessed dependence but did not change retention. Source annotations were harmonized into 15 cell classes (Supplementary Note 7; Supplementary Table 16).

Polygenic risk scores and cross-cohort phenome-wide association analyses

PRS for the ten BAGs were derived using SBayesRC (GCTB v2.5.2) 51, 52 , with the UK Biobank European HapMap3 LD reference and baseline-LD v2.2 annotations 53 . SBayesRC was configured with the default five-component SNP-effect mixture prior, with relative variance multipliers of 0, 0.001, 0.01, 0.1 and 1 and annotation-dependent mixture probabilities. Each deposited BAG weight set was generated using a single MCMC chain with 3,000 iterations, 1,000 burn-in iterations, a thinning interval of 10, random seed 20260821 and four threads. PLINK 2.0 was used for quality control and scoring 54, 55 . GWASs were repeated with the same model specification in 30,800 White British imaging participants, and variant weights were held fixed in all target samples.

In the relatedness-pruned non-overlapping held-out sample (n = 9,997), each inverse-normaltransformed BAG was regressed on its corresponding PRS, standardized using that sample's mean and standard deviation, in a univariable linear model. We reported adjusted R² (Supplementary Table 17; Supplementary Fig. 8).

PheWAS used relatedness-pruned White British UK Biobank non-imaging (n = 359,334) and held-out imaging (n = 9,997) samples, and European-ancestry All of Us participants with complete covariates (n = 109,774). UK Biobank non-imaging participants were outside the 46,254-participant imaging cohort. In both UK Biobank targets, participants related to any PRSderivation participant at KING kinship > 0.0884 were excluded, followed by greedy withintarget pruning at the same threshold. All of Us pruning used the All of Us v7 precomputed relatedness exclusion list; no pair in the supplied kinship file remained among retained candidates (Supplementary Note 11; Supplementary Table 24). ICD-10 records were mapped to phecodes v1.2 and aggregated to the first decimal place. Cases required at least one qualifying record, and related-disease control exclusions were applied in all three samples. Each PRS-phecode association was tested by logistic regression adjusted for age, sex and ten genetic principal components. UK Biobank PheWAS age was 2018 minus birth year; All of Us age was elapsed days from birth to 1 January 2023 divided by 365. Sex-specific phecodes were restricted to the designated sex and did not include sex as a covariate; ambiguous phecode 751.1 remained unrestricted.

Models used complete cases and required at least 30 cases after exclusions and sex restrictions.

Unsuccessful fits were excluded. Coefficients and odds ratios are per one-unit increase in the unstandardized summed PRS. BH correction was applied within each PRS and sample (Supplementary Note 11; Supplementary Tables 18 19 20).

In ADNI, Pearson correlations related each PRS to its corresponding bias-corrected baseline BAG in 1,693 participants (Supplementary Table 21). Clinical-label analyses included 1,845 of 1,856 genotype-QC-passed participants, excluding 11 with missing age or baseline diagnosis.

Logistic models compared AD and MCI separately with cognitively normal status, adjusting for age, sex, ten genetic principal components and APOE ε4 allele count. Odds ratios are per oneunit increase in the unstandardized summed PRS. Sensitivity analyses excluded APOE ε4 allele count from the covariates. BH correction was applied separately across the 20 tests in each analysis (Supplementary Tables 22 and 23).

SNP-based heritability and correlations among BAGs

SNP-based heritability and pairwise genetic correlations were estimated using LD Score regression (LDSC v1.0.1; commit aa33296) 48, 49 and 1000 Genomes European-ancestry LD scores. Heritability refers to inverse-normal-transformed BAGs (Supplementary Table 14; Supplementary Note 6). Regional-whole-brain heritability differences used the same 41,067 participants and regression SNPs. Standard errors were estimated by a paired jackknife over 200 shared genomic blocks, accounting for covariance between estimates. Two-sided normal-theory P values were BH-corrected across nine comparisons (Supplementary Table 14, continued). Genetic correlations were estimated for all 45 BAG pairs with unconstrained cross-trait intercepts to account for sample overlap and correlated errors. Phenotypic correlations were unadjusted Pearson correlations between inverse-normal-transformed BAGs in the same 41,067 participants. BH correction was applied separately to the 45 genetic and 45 phenotypic correlations (Supplementary Table 15).
