Reporting p values imprecisely (e.g., p < .05) reduces transparency, reproducibility, and re-use (e.g., in p value meta-analyses). Best practice is to report exact p-values with three decimal places (e.g., p = .032) unless p values are smaller than 0.001, in which case you can use p < .001.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (e.g., “decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

What we found

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Parkinson's disease (PD) and related α-synucleinopathies are defined by the spread of Lewy body (LB) pathology across vulnerable brain regions, yet how substantia nigra (SN) myeloid cells respond remains unresolved. Using SOX10-based oligodendrocyte depletion and CD11b-based myeloid enrichment, we profiled 169,799 brain myeloid cells and nuclei from 96 donors spanning the LB continuum, including 86,427 from the SN, and integrated them with AMP PD for a total of 298,592 profiles. Across eight conserved states, α-synucleinopathy is associated with a bidirectional signature: upregulated glycolytic and proteostatic-stress programs alongside downregulated lysosomal and mitochondrial pathways. Microglia shift from P2RY12-high sensome cells toward an HSP90AA1-high proteostatic stress state, a transition that intensifies at late Braak LB stages. Immunohistochemistry confirmed that HSP60 + proteostatic microglia state proportions increase with SN neuron loss. A rare NUCKS1-high state (~1% of myeloid cells), more abundant in late-stage disease, carried the strongest PD polygenic-risk loading. The PD-protective rs3732765-A allele, a microglial cis-eQTL that lowers P2RY12 expression, was nominally associated with a higher proportion of P2RY12-high microglia and higher lysosomal program activity. These findings frame microglial involvement as structured and genetically tractable, nominating the P2RY12 genetic axis and NUCKS1-high microglia for mechanistic studies.

Introduction

Parkinson's disease (PD) and dementia with Lewy bodies (DLB) are defined by the spread of α-synuclein pathology through vulnerable brain regions, culminating in the degeneration of substantia nigra (SN) dopaminergic neurons and the motor features of PD 1, 2 . Genetic evidence, however, increasingly implicates brain myeloid cells: PD risk variants are enriched in microglial regulatory elements, and myeloid-expressed genes falling into the mitochondrial and proteolysosomal pathways are dysregulated by disease 3, 4 . Microglia rapidly remodel their transcriptional programs in response to protein aggregates and neuronal injury [5] [6] [7] , yet whether this capacity is preserved, exhausted, or maladaptive in the degenerating human SN remains unknown.

Resolving this question requires deep transcriptomic sampling of SN myeloid cells, and here prior studies have been limited. Across five recent studies, fewer than 70,000 SN myeloid nuclei have been recovered in total [8] [9] [10] [11] [12] , with individual studies capturing 4,000 to 30,000, insufficient to resolve state heterogeneity, quantify abundance shifts across disease stages, or power genetic analyses. Deep myeloid profiling has been achieved in Alzheimer's disease through enrichment-based approaches (PsychAD) 13 , but PD studies have relied on standard nuclear preparations that yield only 5-15% microglia. The Accelerating Medicines Partnership Parkinson's Disease (AMP PD) recently described microglial remodeling along Braak Lewy body (LB) stages using brainstem, pallidal, and cortical nuclei 6 , but their resource did not include the SN. Hence, no existing PD resource combines myeloid enrichment, cross-cohort harmonization, and the donor scale needed for systematic analysis. As a result, the field lacks a unified molecular taxonomy of human SN myeloid cells and a clear view of the transcriptional programs engaged by α-synuclein pathology.

We addressed this gap with the myeloid arm of an Atlas of Integrative Omics of Neuroimmunity in α-synucleinopathy (AION-myeloid), an integrated atlas of 169,799 human brain myeloid profiles from 96 unique donors spanning the full spectrum of LB pathology. Using SOX10-based oligodendrocyte depletion and CD11b-based myeloid enrichment, we recovered 86,427 substantia nigra myeloid transcriptional profiles from α-synucleinopathy and neurotypical control donors, exceeding all prior PD SN datasets combined. Additional sampling from amygdala and cortex (83,372 transcriptional profiles), together with integration of 128,793 myeloid nuclei from the AMP PD consortium 6 , provides coverage of brain regions variably affected across Braak LB stages. Together, these data support a systematic molecular dissection of SN myeloid biology in PD: defining a consensus cell state taxonomy, tracking state-specific abundance shifts across the LB pathology continuum, identifying transcriptional programs that scale with pathological progression, and linking myeloid states and gene programs to PD genetic risk.

Results

Taxonomy of α-synucleinopathy brain myeloid cells

AION-myeloid profiles human brain myeloid cells with two complementary enrichment strategies: SOX10-depleted nuclei isolated by fluorescence-activated nuclei sorting (FANS) from cortex (CTX), amygdala (AMY) and substantia nigra (SN), and CD11b-enriched cells isolated by magnetic-activated cell sorting (MACS) from CTX and SN. For the abundance and transcriptional analyses, we restricted the cohort to the α-synucleinopathy spectrum. To avoid confounding by unrelated pathologies, we removed donors with progressive supranuclear palsy (PSP), amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) or Alzheimer's disease neuropathologic change (ADNC), retaining 36,214 of 37,310 SOX10-depleted myeloid nuclei and 133,585 CD11b-enriched myeloid cells. AION-myeloid was designed to extend existing PD single-cell resources such as AMP PD by adding myeloid enrichment, regions not covered in the AMP PD data used here (AMY, SN) and matched donor genotypes. We therefore integrated it with 128,793 AMP PD myeloid nuclei from CTX, globus pallidus interna (GPI) and dorsal motor nucleus of the vagus (DMNX). This yielded a harmonized dataset of 298,592 myeloid cells and nuclei from 96 AION-myeloid and 97 AMP PD donors (Table 1, Fig. 1a,c), with brain region included as a covariate to account for uneven regional coverage across sources. For the genetic analyses, we retained PSP, ALS and ADNC donors to preserve power, using all 113 genotyped AION-myeloid donors (72.6% European genetic ancestry; 260,844 myeloid nuclei and cells) across brain regions; AMP PD was excluded because donor genotypes were not available.

Unsupervised clustering and marker-based annotation revealed seven major cell-type clusters in AION (nuclei; Fig. 1b; Supplementary Fig. 1a): neurons, astrocytes, myeloid cells, oligodendrocyte precursor cells (OPCs), oligodendrocytes, T-cells, and vascular cells. We then performed in silico subsetting and unsupervised clustering of the myeloid populations in AION (nuclei). This yielded AION-myeloid (nuclei), a dataset comprising eight myeloid states, each defined by distinct transcriptional programs and named for a representative marker gene (Fig. 1d, Supplementary Fig. 1b). These included seven states of microglial identity (P2RY12-high, SPP1-high, HSP90AA1-high, TMEM163-high, PCDH9-high, NUCKS1-high, and B2M-high), and one state of border-associated macrophages (CD163-high). Several of the top state markers are established PD risk genes, each expressed in a largely state-restricted pattern (Supplementary Fig. 1c), a partitioning we revisit when assessing polygenic risk across states.

These states were reproducible across cohorts and their identity was robust to modality. Using the contrastive learning method CONCORD 14 to propagate state labels across datasets, Pearson correlation of each state's top 50 markers indicated that the states replicated across our myeloid cohorts (Supplementary Fig. 2i-k). In a matched-donor subset of AION-myeloid, a linear mixed model (see Methods) showed that nuclei and cells differed only in genes from cytoplasmic pathways, the compartment absent from nuclei (Supplementary Fig. 3).

Over-representation analysis (ORA) of Gene Ontology Biological Process (GO:BP) terms across the top 50 marker genes per state assigned seven of the eight states to previously described myeloid transcriptional programs: P2RY12-high (M1) corresponds to neuronal surveillance (i.e., sensome functions) 15 ; HSP90AA1-high (M2) to proteostatic stress and the adaptive response to unfolded proteins 16 ; SPP1-high (M3) to MHC-II and ribosomal enrichment associated with phagocytic functions 17 ; TMEM163-high (M4) to cell migratory and inflammatory programs 18 ; PCDH9-high (M5) to synapse regulation 19 ; CD163-high (M6) to perivascular macrophages and immune/inflammatory signaling 20, 21 ; and B2M-high (M8) to phagocytosis-induced neuroinflammation 22 (Supplementary Fig. 4). NUCKS1-high (M7) microglia are the exception: they co-express markers spanning sensome (P2RY12), phagocytic (SPP1), and inflammatory (TMEM163) states (Fig. 1d) and show no enrichment for any canonical biological pathway (Supplementary Fig. 4), consistent with a hybrid or transitional identity rather than a discrete transcriptional program. Across our three cohorts and both modalities (nuclei and cells), the NUCKS1-high state accounts for 0.5-1.3% of myeloid cells (Supplementary Fig. 2e). Although small, it remains a discrete, reproducible population rather than a technical artifact: it clusters stably (Supplementary Fig. 2i-k), is de-enriched for apoptosis and cell-death markers (Supplementary Fig. 5), and its abundance largely is unrelated to technical covariates (Supplementary Fig. 6). To our knowledge, no existing literature describes the function of NUCKS1-high microglia in health or disease.

To look for a program below genome-wide resolution, we used the state's defining marker as a lead. NUCKS1 is a chromatin-associated factor involved in homologous recombination, DNA repair and cell-cycle progression [23] [24] [25] , with no described role in microglia. Targeted ORA of the 50 top state markers against curated cell-cycle gene sets showed that NUCKS1-high microglia were the only state enriched for a synthesis (S)-phase signature (Supplementary Fig. 7), pointing to a proliferative or DNA-replicative component. We next tested whether the abundance of this population shifts with disease stage. 1.

Sensome microglia are depleted while proteostatic stress and S-phase microglia expand in α-synucleinopathy

We first assessed whether α-synucleinopathy alters myeloid state composition across brain regions. We modeled cell-state abundance with crumblr 26 , which applies a variance-stabilizing transformation to cell-type proportions with precision weights that account for the compositional structure of the data. We fit linear mixed models with dream 27 , adjusting for age, sex, brain region, post-mortem interval and sample-level quality metrics, with a random intercept for donor (Methods). Braak LB stages were binned into control (Braak LB stage 0), intermediate (Braak LB stages 1-4), and late (Braak LB stages 5-6).

We then tested intermediate-vs-control and late-vs-control contrasts separately. Both contrasts recovered largely the same directional shifts across all three datasets, with effect sizes generally larger at the late stage: P2RY12-high microglia were depleted, while HSP90AA1-high and NUCKS1-high microglia expanded. These shifts reached significance in the merged late-vs-control analysis (P2RY12-high: logFC = -0.40, FDR = 0.092; HSP90AA1-high: logFC = 0.58, FDR = 0.031; NUCKS1-high: logFC = 0.56, FDR = 0.011; Fig. 2a-c; Supplementary Fig. 8). In the merged dataset, the intermediate-vs-control contrast was directionally concordant, with smaller effects that did not reach significance (all FDR ≥ 0.1). This is consistent with a milder shift at the intermediate stage and fewer intermediate donors, although HSP90AA1-high expansion was already detectable at the intermediate stage in AMP PD. Other states showed variable or smaller effects, including an expansion of SPP1-high microglia in AMP PD and modest depletion of PCDH9-high and B2M-high microglia in the merged late-vs-control analysis (Fig. 2a-c; Supplementary Fig. 8).

If α-synucleinopathy drives sensome microglia into the proteostatic-stress state, the two populations should be inversely coupled across donors, and more strongly so in disease. Consistent with this, P2RY12-high and HSP90AA1-high proportions were negatively correlated at the donor level, with the strength of the association between the two states differing by disease status: no significant correlation in controls (Spearman ρ = -0.15, P = 0.23) and moderate in α-synucleinopathy donors (ρ = -0.37, P = 2.4 × 10⁻⁵; Fig. 2d-e). This suggests microglial compositional remodeling occurs predominantly within the disease state.

To validate this shift at the protein level, we performed triple chromogenic immunohistochemistry (IHC; IBA1/HSP60/ALDH1L1) on SN from twelve Neuropathology Brain Bank & Research CoRE at Mount Sinai (NPBB) donors (Fig. 2g-i; Supplementary Fig. 9). HSP60 was chosen as the readout because its gene, HSPD1, is among the most strongly upregulated genes in the HSP90AA1-high state (Fig. 2f), and because HSP60 is a mitochondrial chaperonin that, when released, acts as a danger signal to the innate immune system 28 . HSP60 co-localized with IBA1+ microglia, and the proportion of IBA1+ microglia co-expressing HSP60 increased with SN neuron-loss severity (Supplementary Fig. 9). In the eleven donors with both IHC and sn/scRNA-seq data from SN (one slide excluded because of tissue damage), the proportion of proteostatic stress microglia rose with neuron loss in both IHC (IBA1 + /HSP60 + ; 17% with no SN neuron loss to 41% with severe SN neuron loss, P = 0.019) and sn/scRNA-seq (HSP90AA1-high microglia; 11% to 45%, P = 0.001), with no detectable difference between the two methods (P = 0.39; Supplementary Fig. 9). Together, these results provide an orthogonal, protein-level confirmation that the proteostatic-stress (HSP90AA1-high) state expands with dopaminergic neuron loss in the degenerating SN.

After adjusting for LB pathology, myeloid state abundance differed markedly by brain region, whereas sex and age effects were modest (Supplementary Figs. 10 11). Regionally, HSP90AA1-high microglia were enriched in the DMNX (logFC = 0.59, P = 4.4 × 10⁻⁹) and GPI (logFC = 0.30, P = 0.004) and depleted in cortex (logFC = -0.68, P = 3.7 × 10⁻¹⁵; AMP PD, each region vs. all others), with concordant estimates in the merged dataset (Supplementary Fig. 10a-b). By sex, males (n = 73) showed higher HSP90AA1-high and females higher TMEM163-high abundance. The direction was consistent across all three datasets and the merged analysis, but the effects reached only nominal significance (HSP90AA1-high, AION-myeloid (nuclei): logFC = 0.73, P = 0.048; TMEM163-high, merged: logFC = -0.42, P = 0.018) and did not survive FDR correction (Supplementary Fig. 11a-b). After adjusting for pathology, age was not associated with HSP90AA1-high or P2RY12-high abundance in any dataset (all P ≥ 0.1; Supplementary Fig. 11c-d). This suggests that apparent age-related shifts in these states largely reflect the co-accumulation of Lewy pathology with age. The strongest age association was a decrease in B2M-high microglia (merged: logFC = -0.12, P = 0.004). No sex or age association survived FDR correction.

Together, these data show a reorganization of the myeloid compartment in α-synucleinopathy: the balance shifts away from sensome (P2RY12-high) microglia that survey the parenchyma toward HSP90AA1-high microglia engaged in the heat-shock response, while the rare NUCKS1-high population expands. This shift is stronger in late Braak LB stage donors, is reproduced across datasets, and sits on a regional baseline in which HSP90AA1-high microglia are most abundant in the early-affected DMNX and least abundant in cortex.

Microglia in α-synucleinopathy exhibit coordinated metabolic-proteostatic remodeling and lysosomal decline

To characterize transcriptional changes associated with α-synuclein pathology, we performed pseudobulk differential gene expression (DGE) analysis using a linear mixed model 27 with donor as a random effect, comparing α-synucleinopathy to control samples at both myeloid-wide and state-specific levels, adjusting for age, sex, post-mortem interval, brain region, dataset, and additional technical covariates (Methods). Myeloid-wide analysis identified 105 upregulated and 89 downregulated genes (FDR < 0.05, |log 2 FC| > 0.5; Fig. 3a, Supplementary Table 1). ORA of the top differentially expressed genes at FDR < 0.1 revealed a bidirectional pattern (Fig. 3b). Upregulated genes were enriched for glycolytic metabolism and Heat Shock Response (HSR); downregulated genes were enriched for lysosomal modules, electron transport chain (ETC) pathways, and interferon signaling. Targeted module score (i.e., the z-scored mean of a pathway's genes) testing confirmed these shifts at the donor level: HSR and F6P Phosphorylation pathway genes increased in expression across pathology and Braak LB stages (P < 0.01; Fig. 3c,d, Supplementary Fig. 12a-b).

We previously 29 positioned metabolic reprogramming downstream of proteostatic stress in peripheral monocytes from PD patients. We applied the same internally curated gene sets to brain myeloid states, targeting PD-relevant cellular functions and resolving discrete functional programs more finely than broad GO biological-process terms. We first tested whether HSR and glycolytic pathway expression were correlated across donors. Indeed, their activity was tightly linked (Pearson r = 0.68, P = 1.5 × 10 -27 ; Fig. 3e).

To assess whether state-specific contributions drove these global myeloid DGE patterns, we fit a linear mixed model on state-subsampled cells and nuclei and performed ORA on the state-level DEGs against the curated functional panel (Supplementary Fig. 13a-b, Supplementary Table 2). Because this panel is a small, hypothesis-driven set rather than a discovery database, we report nominal p-values.

Enrichment was concentrated in the three states carrying the largest DE signal (P2RY12-high, HSP90AA1-high, SPP1-high microglia). SPP1-high microglia showed the strongest upregulation, a coordinated stress and innate-immune axis spanning the mitochondrial/ER heat shock response (HSR; DNAJA1, HSPD1; P = 0.004) and cytosolic nucleic-acid sensing (dsRNA/DNA sensors and effectors; TRAF3, STAU2; P = 0.013), while sensome P2RY12-high microglia upregulated interferon-stimulated genes (ZC3HAV1, NLRC5; P = 0.012). Upregulation of HECT-domain E3 ligases (ubiquitin-proteasome system) was a recurring feature across the P2RY12-, HSP90AA1-, SPP1-, and CD163-high states, though below nominal significance. Downregulation converged on clearance and cytoskeletal machinery: CD163-high perivascular macrophages downregulated lysosome biogenesis/maturation/fusion (STX8, SUMF1; P = 0.014) alongside weaker reductions in myosin motors and RAB effectors, and P2RY12-high microglia downregulated actin-binding proteins (CFL1, AIF1; P = 0.026); SPP1-and HSP90AA1-high microglia showed weaker, trafficking-and protease-oriented lysosomal downregulation (RAB effectors and lysosomal proteases/enzymes, respectively). The remaining low-DE states (TMEM163-high, PCDH9-high) produced only isolated enrichments, and NUCKS1-high microglia yielded too few DEGs to overlap the panel, showing no enrichment in either direction.

We next asked whether microglial remodeling in α-synucleinopathy reflects a brain-specific or a systemic myeloid response. To this end, we performed ORA using published DEG signatures from peripheral monocytes of idiopathic PD patients (Mattei et al. 29 ). Pathway directionality was conserved: glycolysis and HSR programs were upregulated, while lysosomal pathways were downregulated, in both brain myeloid cells and peripheral monocytes (Pearson's r = 0.49 for upregulated pathways; Pearson's r = 0.35 for downregulated pathways; Supplementary Fig. 14). This convergence suggests a shared myeloid stress signature operating across tissue compartments in PD.

Together, these findings indicate that α-synucleinopathy is associated with a coordinated metabolic and proteostatic shift in brain myeloid cells. The concurrent downregulation of ETC genes alongside upregulation of glycolytic and HSR programs, whose activities were tightly correlated across donors, is consistent with a metabolic adaptation to compromised mitochondrial homeostasis and proteostatic stress, accompanied by a shift toward glycolysis. This pattern parallels the coupled proteostatic and metabolic reprogramming reported in peripheral PD monocytes, where reduced mitochondrial function is evident both transcriptionally and functionally, and its directionality was conserved across compartments here. Suppression of lysosomal and trafficking pathways, concentrated in phagocytic populations, further points to a reduced capacity for aggregate clearance. The state-resolved analysis, though limited by sparse per-state signal, was broadly consistent with this global pattern rather than pointing to a single dominant state: the proteostatic and innate-sensing arm was strongest in SPP1-high microglia, lysosomal and trafficking suppression in CD163-high perivascular macrophages, and an interferon-stimulated signature in sensome P2RY12-high microglia.

Heat-shock and lysosomal gene programs correlate with Lewy body pathology

Previously, Marshe et al. 30 noted that cluster-based analyses can impose sharp boundaries on microglial variation that is partly continuous. To complement our state-level analysis, we used Spectra 31 to model microglial expression as a set of additive gene programs rather than discrete clusters. Spectra is a pathway-guided factorization method that identifies co-regulated transcriptional modules, here referred to as Spectra factors. Factors were labeled by their seeding gene sets; ORA on top-loading genes was used to annotate factors and to characterize de novo factors not anchored to prior knowledge. Factor activity was aggregated per donor and compared between α-synucleinopathy and control donors using mixed models adjusting for age, sex, PMI and data type (i.e., nuclei and cells; Methods). Of 20 factors tested, 7 differed between groups (FDR < 0.1). Six were lower in α-synucleinopathy: lysosomal enzymes, nucleases and proteases, mitochondrial quality control, Complex V (ATP synthase) and, notably given prevailing activation models, pathogen recognition (adjusted differences -0.11 to -0.21 SD; FDR < 0.05; Fig. 4a-f). The HSR factor was the only factor higher in α-synucleinopathy (+0.32 SD; FDR = 0.076; Fig. 4g; Supplementary Table 4).

One factor departed from this pattern only when resolved by state. PKC signaling did not differ between groups across all myeloid cells (-0.07 SD; FDR = 0.15), but was increased in NUCKS1-high microglia (+0.59 SD; P = 0.01), whereas it tended to decrease in all other states (-0.11 SD). This divergence between NUCKS1-high microglia and the remaining states was significant (pathology-by-state interaction, +0.70 SD; P = 1.9 × 10 -8 ; n = 48 donors with NUCKS1-high microglia; Fig. 4h). State-resolved trajectories for all factors across Braak LB stages are shown in Supplementary Fig. 15. Factor activity differed between brain regions for 17 of 20 factors (FDR < 0.1), whereas the α-synucleinopathy effect differed between regions for only three (GEFs, PI3K signaling kinases and mitochondrial quality control; region-by-pathology FDR < 0.1). For all other main-figure factors, the α-synucleinopathy effect was consistent across regions (interaction FDR ≥ 0.1; Supplementary Table 5).

α-synucleinopathy donors thus show lower expression of the lysosomal and mitochondrial machinery that supports degradation and energy supply, together with lower expression of pathogen-recognition modules. Rather than a primed or activated phenotype, this pattern points to reduced sensing and clearance capacity. The concurrent rise in the HSR factor indicates proteostatic stress, consistent with the expansion of HSP90AA1-high microglia (Fig. 2) and the differential expression results described above (Fig. 3).

HSP90AA1-and NUCKS1-high microglia are enriched for Parkinson's disease polygenic risk

To determine whether microglial states differentially capture genetic architecture, we applied single-cell disease relevance scoring (scDRS 32 ) using neurodegenerative and neuropsychiatric GWAS summary statistics together with non-neurological traits (rheumatoid arthritis, height) as comparators [33] [34] [35] [36] [37] [38] [39] . NUCKS1-high microglia showed significant enrichment for PD polygenic risk (FDR < 0.05; Fig. 5a); HSP90AA1-high microglia showed nominal enrichment that did not survive multiple-testing correction. Among neurodegenerative traits, this enrichment was specific to PD: neither population was enriched for AD, ALS or PSP risk, and no other myeloid state showed PD enrichment. Several states were enriched for non-neurological traits (Fig. 5a), indicating that myeloid state enrichment is not PD-specific per se. Notably, this within-myeloid signal was not evident in whole-brain single-cell analyses that include all cell types (Hirata et al., in preparation), likely reflecting the dilution of within-lineage heterogeneity by the broader transcriptional variance across cell types-a limitation of disease score approaches applied to heterogeneous datasets.

We next asked whether specific transcriptional programs mediate genetic risk. Factor-level scDRS revealed that PKC signaling, Other Kinases, and HSR factors were significantly associated with PD risk (FDR < 0.05 for PKC and Other Kinases factors, P < 0.05 for HSR factor; Fig. 5b). PKC signaling activity was highest in NUCKS1-high microglia (Fig. 4h), linking this population's genetic risk enrichment to a specific functional axis. Examination of PD risk genes revealed that NUCKS1-high microglia expressed elevated levels of NUCKS1, TLR2, TBC1D8, and SNCA (Supplementary Fig. 1c)-genes involved in DNA damage response 23 , innate immune sensing of α-synuclein 40 , endosomal trafficking 41 , and the pathological substrate itself 42 . HSP90AA1-high microglia instead showed high expression of BAG3, a co-chaperone regulating autophagy and proteostasis 43, 44 , consistent with this state's nominal PD risk association operating through a distinct, proteostatic axis. These risk genes loaded highly on the ontological supercategories of the corresponding factors (Supplementary Fig. 16), tying each myeloid state's genetic signal to its implicated functional program.

NUCKS1-high microglia thus emerge as a genetically implicated population in PD: they carry significant polygenic risk enrichment, expand in α-synucleinopathy (Fig. 2c), and express TLR2, a major receptor for extracellular oligomeric α-synuclein, and the endosomal regulator TBC1D8 alongside NUCKS1 (Supplementary Fig. 1c). Genetic variation in this state may thus influence PD risk through coupled modulation of innate sensing of α-synuclein and vesicular processing, converging on the PKC signaling program preferentially active in this population.

The PD-protective P2RY12 eQTL rs3732765 is associated with microglial state composition and lysosomal factor activity

Having identified the transcriptional programs that carry PD polygenic risk, we asked whether individual PD risk variants influence myeloid state composition. We tested the lead variants of 30 microglial cis-eQTLs that colocalize with PD GWAS signals from Nalls et al. (2019) 45 or the more recent GP2 meta-analysis 39 in SingleBrain and MiGA 3, 46 , comparing the two opposing genotypes of each variant (e.g. A/A vs G/G) for the abundance of all eight myeloid states across 113 genotyped donors, adjusting for pathology, brain region, and donor covariates with a donor random effect (Methods; Supplementary Table 6). No test result was significant, the full results for all 30 variants and eight states are reported in Supplementary Fig. 17 and Supplementary Tables 6-7. Because no association was expected to survive correction at this sample size, we focused on a single prespecified hypothesis.

Several features of the MED12L/P2RY12 locus make it the most tractable in our cohort and motivated targeted follow-up of rshttps://paperpile.com/c/MUVuwM/82ped732765. rs3732765 is the top cis microglial eQTL for P2RY12 in MiGA, with the A allele downregulating P2RY12 expression, and the eQTL colocalizes with the PD GWAS signal at MED12L/P2RY12 3 . The same A allele confers reduced PD risk in a GWAS meta-analysis (β = -0.06, P = 2.4 × 10⁻¹⁰) 45.

Because rs3732765 acts as a cis-eQTL for P2RY12, the defining marker of the P2RY12-high state, we specified the abundance of P2RY12-high microglia as the primary outcome for this variant before testing. Consistent with this hypothesis, A/A donors showed a higher proportion of P2RY12-high microglia than G/G donors across brain regions (logFC = 0.62, P = 0.027; 18 A/A vs 57 G/G donors; Fig. 6a). The effect was directionally consistent in SN alone but was not significant with only 4 A/A donors (logFC = 0.79, P = 0.16; Supplementary Table 6). It was weaker under an additive model (P = 0.18; Supplementary Table 7), suggesting that the shift is most apparent with two copies of the A allele. The protective allele is thus associated with a higher proportion of P2RY12-high microglia, a state that is less abundant at higher Braak LB stages, where HSP90AA1-high cells predominate (Fig. 2c). This positions the locus as a candidate link between PD genetic risk and the stage-associated remodeling of microglial states.

To examine this remodeling directly, we modeled the CLR-based transition score from P2RY12-high to HSP90AA1-high microglia, which increased with pathology group (β = 0.55, P = 0.031; Fig. 6b). The genotype-by-pathology interaction estimate was negative, consistent with a smaller increase in A-allele carriers, but did not reach significance (β = -0.64, P = 0.13).

The association with genotype was more evident at the level of lysosomal enzyme factor activity, which is lower in microglia from donors with α-synucleinopathy (Fig. 4c). Residual factor activity, adjusted for disease status and donor covariates, increased with A-allele dosage in both P2RY12-high (β = 0.18, P = 0.027, n = 54) and HSP90AA1-high microglia (β = 0.24, P = 0.008, n = 54; Fig. 6c), with the larger effect in the stress-associated state. Together, these data nominate rshttps://paperpile.com/c/MUVuwM/82ped732765-A as a microglial cis-regulatory variant that lowers P2RY12 expression (MiGA 3 ), associated here with a higher proportion of P2RY12-high microglia, and higher lysosomal factor activity in both sensome and proteostatic stress-associated states.

Discussion

We present AION-myeloid, a myeloid-enriched single-cell atlas of the human SN and connected brain regions which, integrated with AMP PD, comprises 298,592 myeloid profiles (Fig. 1a; Table 1). Myeloid enrichment recovered more SN myeloid profiles than prior PD SN studies combined and resolved eight states that replicated across cohorts and isolation methods (Fig. 1c,d; Supplementary Fig. 2i-k). Three findings emerge: α-synucleinopathy is associated with a shift from sensome toward proteostatic-stress microglia; this shift is accompanied by lower expression of clearance and sensing programs rather than a classical activation signature; and PD genetic risk maps onto specific states and programs within this landscape.

The most consistent compositional change was a lower proportion of P2RY12-high sensome microglia and a higher proportion of HSP90AA1-high microglia, with larger effects at late Braak LB stages (Fig. 2c; Supplementary Fig. 8). The two states were inversely correlated in α-synucleinopathy donors but not in controls (Fig. 2d,e). At the protein level, HSP60 + microglia increased with SN neuron loss in parallel with the sequencing-based HSP90AA1-high fraction (Fig. 2f-i; Supplementary Fig. 9). If this reflects state conversion, sensome microglia would lose their surveillance program and enter a proteostatic-stress state under α-synuclein burden, rather than being replaced by a distinct population. This interpretation fits the reduced sensing and clearance programs described below. However, because the design is cross-sectional and the data compositional, this anti-correlation may reflect state conversion, selective loss or expansion, or differential turnover; distinguishing these will require trajectory, lineage-tracing or perturbation data. Independently of pathology, HSP90AA1-high microglia were more abundant in the DMNX and GPI than in cortex (Supplementary Fig. 10), raising the possibility that regional myeloid composition relates to regional susceptibility to Lewy pathology, a relationship our data cannot resolve. Sex and age effects were modest and did not survive multiple-testing correction (Supplementary Fig. 11a).

At the transcriptional level, α-synucleinopathy was associated with higher expression of heat-shock-response (HSR) and glycolytic genes, whose activities were tightly coupled across donors, and lower expression of lysosomal, electron-transport and interferon genes (Fig. 3a-e). Gene-program analysis gave a concordant picture: programs for lysosomal enzymes, nucleases, proteases, mitochondrial quality control, Complex V and pathogen recognition were lower in disease, and only the HSR program was higher (Fig. 4a-g). Together, these data describe microglia with reduced degradative, bioenergetic and sensing capacity under proteostatic stress, rather than a primed or pro-inflammatory phenotype. In terms of the question posed at the outset, these data argue against a preserved response and are most consistent with an exhausted rather than a hyperactivated, maladaptive state, although exhaustion and maladaptation are not mutually exclusive. Furthermore, whether this reduced capacity is a consequence of accumulated aggregate burden or a pre-existing limitation that contributes to impaired α-synuclein clearance cannot be determined from post-mortem tissue. The same directional pattern was present in peripheral monocytes from people with PD 29 (Supplementary Fig. 14). Because circulating monocytes have little direct contact with parenchymal aggregates, this concordance is compatible with a systemic or intrinsic component of myeloid dysfunction in PD, although the moderate pathway-level correlation and the differences between cohorts and assays warrant caution. NUCKS1-high microglia were the only state significantly enriched for PD polygenic risk (Fig. 5a). Because this state is rare (0.5-1.3% of myeloid cells), we characterized it before interpreting its disease association: it expressed canonical microglial markers (Supplementary Fig. 1b), was recovered across cohorts and modalities (Supplementary Fig. 2e,i-k), was not enriched for apoptosis markers (Supplementary Fig. 5), and was largely unrelated to technical covariates (Supplementary Fig. 6). NUCKS1-high microglia were more abundant in late-stage α-synucleinopathy (Fig. 2c), were the only state enriched for an S-phase signature (Supplementary Fig. 7), and showed higher PKC-signaling activity in disease, opposite to all other states (Fig. 4h), a program itself associated with PD risk (Fig. 5b). Their risk enrichment is carried by elevated expression of PD risk genes including NUCKS1, TLR2, TBC1D8, and SNCA (Supplementary Figs. 1c, 16); the scDRS signal and the risk-gene expression are therefore two views of the same observation rather than independent evidence. The S-phase signature raises the possibility that NUCKS1-high cells represent a proliferative compartment, consistent with local microglial renewal in affected regions, but this remains to be tested. Abundance changes were also regionally heterogeneous, higher in SN but lower in the DMNX and GPI (Supplementary Fig. 10b). Whether NUCKS1-high microglia are protective, pathogenic or bystanders remains open.

At the single-variant level, the PD-protective rshttps://paperpile.com/c/MUVuwM/82ped732765-A allele, the lead microglial cis-eQTL for P2RY12 in MiGA, which lowers P2RY12 expression and colocalizes with the MED12L/P2RY12 GWAS signal 3 , was associated with a higher proportion of P2RY12-high microglia (Fig. 6a) and with higher lysosomal-enzyme program activity in P2RY12-high and HSP90AA1-high microglia in the SN (Fig. 6c). The genotype-by-pathology interaction on the P2RY12-high-to-HSP90AA1-high balance was directionally consistent with attenuation but not significant (Fig. 6b). These associations are nominal, rest on few A/A donors, and were tested as a single prespecified hypothesis within a panel reported in full (Supplementary Fig. 17; Supplementary Tables 6 7); they require replication in larger genotyped cohorts and functional validation.

This study carries limitations. First, the design is cross-sectional, so associations with Braak LB stage cannot establish temporal order or causality. Second, abundance estimates are compositional, so the lower abundance of one state is partly the counterpart of higher abundance of others. Third, we combined FANS-sorted nuclei, MACS-enriched cells and AMP PD nuclei; although dataset, data type and brain region were modeled as covariates, isolation method is partly confounded with brain region, and tissue dissociation can induce ex vivo stress signatures, which may particularly affect rare states and cross-regional comparisons. Fourth, the genetic analyses have limited power and scope: the rs3732765 findings are nominal, and scDRS enrichment neither establishes causality nor is exclusive to PD, as other states were also enriched for non-neurological traits (Fig. 5a). Fifth, the IHC validation included 12 donors with few controls. Finally, we did not compare α-synucleinopathy with other proteinopathies, so whether these patterns are specific to α-synuclein remains to be determined.

Together, these findings describe a structured remodeling of human brain myeloid states in α-synucleinopathy, from sensome toward proteostatic-stress microglia with reduced clearance and sensing programs, and connect PD genetic risk to a rare NUCKS1-high state and to the P2RY12 locus. These states and programs are candidates for mechanistic testing in human cellular models.

Methods

Human tissue donors

De-identified case and control postmortem brain tissues were obtained from two sources. Fresh-frozen and formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissue from frontal cortex, occipital cortex, and midbrain at the level of the red nucleus was provided by the Mount Sinai Neuropathology Brain Bank & Research CoRE (RRID:SCR_027565). Fresh-frozen tissue from frontal cortex and amygdala was provided by the Netherlands Brain Bank (NBB). NBB postmortem donors were stratified by rs3732765-A genotype. For single-nucleus assays, 40 donors were procured by the NBB, with 51 respective tissues across two regions (11 amygdala, 40 cortex). 58 donors (all substantia nigra) were procured by the NPBB. Isolation of nuclei from brain cells for RNA and multiome assay

Nuclei were isolated using the S2 Genomics Singulator 100 (two to four tissue samples per cartridge, each from a different individual) in combination with a previously described Tween-20 lysis buffer 47, 48 . Substantia nigra was dissected from the surrounding midbrain parenchyma prior to nuclei isolation. We used 20-100mg of tissues per donor (same amounts for each donor per run), varying by the brain region processed. All buffers were ice cold and supplemented with 1:200 RNAse inhibitor (RRI; Takara). All centrifugation steps were performed in a swinging bucket rotor centrifuge at 600 × g for 10 minutes at 4°C. Briefly, tissues were automatically dissociated in 3mL tween lysis buffer with DTT (1:1000, Sigma Aldrich) and rinsed with S2 Genomics Nuclei Storage Reagent. The nuclei suspension was spun down with max brake, resuspended in diluted Debris Removal Solution (S2 Genomics), and spun down again with slow brake. The pelleted nuclei were then resuspended in 300 µL blocking buffer (5% Gibco BSA), filtered through a 30 µm mesh, and incubated for one hour with SOX10-AF647 (1:1000; abcam, Cat. #ab270151) and NeuN-PE (1:1000; Sigma Aldrich, Cat. #FCMAB317PE) antibodies. Prior to fluorescence-activated nuclei sorting (FANS), volumes were brought up to 1.5 mL with blocking buffer and stained with DAPI (Sigma Aldrich, Cat. #MFCD00012681). Nuclei were then FAN-sorted in blocking buffer coated 5mL tubes using a SOX10-/NeuN+ and SOX10-/NeuN-gating strategy (oligodendrocyte depletion) previously described 49.

Sorted nuclei fractions were spun down, counted manually using a SYBR Green I nucleic acid gel stain (Cat. #50513), and resuspended in a loading buffer (1% Gibco BSA) volume appropriate to reach concentrations of 1500 nuclei per µL (10x Genomics 3' v3.1 RNA chemistry) or 3000-5000 nuclei per µL (10x Genomics Multiome ATAC + RNA chemistry; multiome data not analyzed here). For RNA, targeted recovery was 30,000 nuclei. For multiome, targeted recovery was 10,000 nuclei. GEMs and cDNA libraries were prepared according to protocol (10x Genomics). All libraries were sequenced at Azenta using the NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina) at a depth of 30,000 reads per nucleus.

Human postmortem brain dissociation and CD11b+ myeloid-cell enrichment

All centrifugation steps were performed at 4°C, and all tissue-handling steps were carried out on ice unless otherwise indicated. We optimized a previously established protocol for microglia isolation from mouse brain for use with fresh postmortem human brain tissue. This approach was designed to minimize technical and biological artifacts introduced by tissue dissociation and cell isolation, as previously described 29.

Fresh dissociation buffer, hereafter referred to as PBS+, was prepared on the day of isolation and consisted of PBS supplemented with 0.5% BSA, 25 mM HEPES, and 2 mM EDTA. Percoll was pH-adjusted to 7.3-7.4 and kept at 4°C. Isotonic Percoll was prepared immediately before use by mixing nine parts pH-adjusted Percoll with one part 10X PBS. Human brain tissue was transferred to a pre-chilled Petri dish containing ice-cold PBS+ and minced into small fragments using sterile scalpels. The minced tissue was transferred into a pre-chilled 15 mL Dounce homogenizer containing 12 mL ice-cold PBS+ and mechanically dissociated using a loose pestle. Homogenization was performed with gentle up-and-down strokes while rotating the pestle, until no large tissue fragments remained, while avoiding excessive mechanical stress. The resulting cell suspension was transferred to a 50 mL conical tube and centrifuged at 450 × g for 10 minutes.

After centrifugation, the supernatant was removed and the pellet was resuspended in PBS+ to a final volume of 12 mL. Four milliliters of isotonic Percoll were added and mixed thoroughly. Sixteen milliliters of PBS+ were then carefully overlaid onto the Percoll-containing suspension by slowly pipetting along the wall of the tilted tube to preserve phase separation. Samples were centrifuged at 3000 × g for 10 minutes. The upper phase and myelin-containing interface were carefully aspirated, leaving approximately 5 mL above the pellet to minimize loss of low-density cells. Ten milliliters of ice-cold PBS+ were then gently added along the wall of the tube without directly disturbing the pellet. Tubes were gently tilted to mix the remaining Percoll with the wash buffer, and cells were pelleted by centrifugation at 500 × g for 8 minutes. The resulting pellet contained the total brain cell fraction after myelin removal.

For enrichment of CD11b+ myeloid cells, the total brain cell pellet was resuspended in 360 µL MACS buffer consisting of PBS supplemented with 1% BSA. Thirty-five microliters of CD11b nanobeads were added, and cells were incubated for 15 minutes at 4°C. Cells were then washed with 2 mL MACS buffer and pelleted at 400 × g for 8 minutes. The pellet was resuspended in 500 µL MACS buffer and applied to LS columns mounted on a Miltenyi quadroMACS magnet. Columns were washed three times with 3 mL MACS buffer, and CD11b+ cells were eluted with 3.5 mL MACS buffer for downstream analyses.

DNA isolation and genotyping

Genomic DNA was extracted from the frontal cortex, amygdala, or substantia nigra and genotyped using the Illumina Infinium Global Screening Assay as previously described 3.

Computational preprocessing

Alignment of sequencing reads from each multiplexed sample batch was done using the CellRanger suite (v9.0.1) against the hg38 reference genome. We leveraged the CellBender tool (v0.3.0) 50 to remove ambient RNA and empty droplets from each 10x run. We used cellSNP-lite (v1.2.2) 51 to detect SNPs directly from our snRNA-seq data. Subsequently, vireoSNP (v0.5.8) 52 was applied to demultiplex pooled snRNA-seq samples by integrating observed allele counts with matched donor genotypes, enabling barcode donor assignment and genetic doublet detection. Genotype concordance was verified via QTLtools-mbv (v1.3.1) 53 . As a first-pass QC, nuclei from each 10x snRNA-seq run were removed if they had fewer than 15 genes (permissive threshold, as empty droplets and ambient RNA were already removed with CellBender and nuclei were FAN-sorted), more than 15% mitochondrial content, or genotype discordance (nuclei with unassigned donors or with a doublet log-likelihood > 3.84 were removed). For scRNA-seq, the cells from each 10x run were removed if they had fewer than 200 genes or more than 20% mitochondrial reads. Scanpy's (v1.11.1) built-in scrublet 54, 55 function was applied to each 10x sn/scRNA-seq run to remove expression doublets.

sn/scRNA-seq processing and analysis pipeline

A total of 452,290 cells and 423,445 nuclei passed the initial QC steps and were merged into two separate datasets for downstream analysis. Normalization and clustering were performed using Scanpy and Pegasus (v1.10.2) 56 . Counts were normalized to library size and log-transformed. The top 2,000 variable genes were selected with the pg.highly_variable_features() function in Pegasus. Technical effects from library size and number of nuclei or cells were regressed out using the pg.regress_out() function. Batch effects from each different 10x run were addressed with the Harmony algorithm via the pg.run_harmony() function.

Dimensionality reduction was performed by first applying Principal Component Analysis (PCA) on the variable genes. We constructed a k-nearest neighbors (k-NN) graph (k=30) and then applied Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP; 30 nearest neighbors, min_dist=0.3, spread=1.0), using the top principal components (PCs) that cumulatively captured > 95% of the variance explained. Clusters were identified using the Leiden algorithm. These steps were executed using the Pegasus functions pg.highly_variable_features(), pg.pca(), pg.neighbors(), pg.umap(), and pg.leiden().

Each barcode was annotated with a class-level cellular identity by annotating each cluster through visual inspection of cell-type markers (markers are in Supplementary Fig. 1). Myeloid nuclei and cells were then in silico selected and underwent a re-analysis with the same pipeline described above. Non-myeloid nuclei or cells were filtered out. Myeloid nuclei (AION cohort) were used as a discovery cohort and subclustering was performed to identify distinct myeloid states. Clusters were manually annotated into myeloid states based on top marker gene expression and labeled by a representative marker (e.g., P2RY12-high), set in roman type to denote cell populations rather than gene expression.

Integrated myeloid dataset embedding and visualization

To create an integrated myeloid embedding of all microglial cells and nuclei, we joined our AION-myeloid dataset with that of AMP PD 6 and SingleBrain (consisting of Fujita et al. 57 , Bryois et al. 58 , Gabitto et al. 59 , & Mathys et al. 60 ). Expression matrices were subsetted to 15,642 genes common between all cohorts, and features selected using the top 3000 most variable genes of the in-house cells and nuclei. CONCORD 14 was then used with the individual IDs as the CONCORD domain to transfer the 8 myeloid labels to the SingleBrain collection, using default parameters. AION-myeloid and AMP PD data was then extracted from the joint object. A 2D UMAP was then created in the joint CONCORD embedding space for subsequent visualization of the myeloid clustering.

Neuropathological staging

Owing to the small number of early-and medium-stage donors, early and medium Braak LB stages were combined into a single intermediate group for abundance and transcriptional analyses; contrasts were therefore intermediate-vs-control and late-vs-control.

Differential abundance analysis

Differential abundance analysis was performed using crumblr (v0.99.19) 26 as previously described 6 . Briefly, the aggregateToPseudoBulk() function from the dreamlet package (v1.4.1) 61 was applied to derive a pseudobulk object for each cohort that contained all state-level count assays, including normalized cell counts. A crumblr object was generated with the crumblr() and cellCounts() functions. To test for abundance changes across different conditions, we applied the following formulae with the dream() function from the variancePartition package 27 : composition ~ (pathology_group) + (braak_bins) + scale(age) + (sex) + (brain_region) + scale(PMI) + (1|participant) + log(n_genes) + log(n_counts) + scale(mito_ribo) Cell composition ~ (braak_recode_binary) + (braak_bins) + scale(age) + (sex) + (brain_region) + scale(PMI) + (1|participant) + log(n_genes) + log(n_counts) + scale(mito_ribo) Cell composition ~ (rs3732765_num) + (braak_lb_bins) + (braak_bins) + scale(age) + (sex) + (brain_region) + scale(PMI) + (1|participant) + log(n_genes) + log(n_counts) + scale(mito_ribo)

Here pathology_group encoded pathology status as a binary variable and braak_recode_binary encoded a temporary Braak LB stage binary variable where each pathological stage was tested against controls (0: Control, 1-4: Intermediate, 5-6: Late). Braak_bins represented Braak stages (0: Control, < 4: less than 4, ≥ 4: ADNC). Participant was used as the donor ID. brain_region contained DMNX, substantia nigra, amygdala, GPI, and cortex. Participant was modeled as a random effect, while all other variables were modeled as a fixed effect.

Differential gene expression analysis

We utilized dream for differential expression analysis applied on our pseudobulk objects. To test for expression changes across pathology and isolation methods (nuclei vs cells), we applied the following respective formulae with the dream() function from the variancePartition package 27.

Gene expression ~ (pathology_group) + (braak_bins) + scale(age) + (sex) + (brain_region) + scale(PMI) + (1|participant) + log(n_genes) + log(n_counts) + scale(percent_mito) + scale(mito_ribo) Gene expression ~(dataset) + (braak_lb_bins) + (braak_bins) + scale(age) + (sex) + (brain_region) + scale(PMI) + (1|participant) + log(n_genes) + log(n_counts) + scale(mito_ribo)

Over-representation analysis (ORA)

Using clusterProfiler (v4.14.6) and a custom R function 3 we applied Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment and ORA on top cell-state markers, global and cell-state-specific differentially expressed genes (DEGs). Briefly, we performed a pairwise enrichment analysis across two collections of gene sets. For each pair, we removed missing values and computed the number of overlapping genes. We then constructed a 2-by-2 contingency table representing shared and non-shared genes relative to a specified background of genes and applied a one-sided Fisher's exact test to test for over-representation of the overlap. For each comparison, we recorded the gene set identifiers, sizes, overlap count, odds ratio, nominal P-value, and the list of overlapping genes. Multiple testing correction was performed using the Benjamini-Hochberg method. Each top marker or DEGs set was tested against GO:BP terms or manually curated gene sets implicated in Parkinson's disease 29.

Chromogenic Immunohistochemistry Multiplex (IHC)

The paraffin-embedded sections of frontal cortex and midbrain (5µm thickness) were pretreated using Cell Conditioning CC1 antigen retrieval buffer (Tris/Borate/EDTA buffer, pH 8.0-8.5, 950-224, Roche Diagnostics), and incubated in rounds of staining with primary antibodies against the mitochondrial marker HSP60 [EPR18245-93] 1:5000 (abcam #ab190828), microglial marker IBA1 1:500 (Thermo Fisher Scientific #PA5-27436), astrocyte marker ALDH1L1[3E9] 1:700 (abcam #ab56777) and secondary antibody Multimer HRP OmniMAP-Anti Mouse and Multimer HRP OmniMap-Anti Rabbit (760-4310 & 760-4311, Roche Diagnostics). The detection was performed using DISCOVERY Purple HRP Kit (ALDH1L1), DISCOVERY Green HRP Kit (HSP60), and DISCOVERY Yellow HRP Kit (IBA1) (Cat # 760-229, 760-271, 760-250, Roche Diagnostics). Sodium citrate antigen retrieval (pH 6.0) was used between rounds to remove the antibody from the previous round and avoid cross-reactivity. Hematoxylin and Bluing reagent (760-2021, 760-2037, Roche Diagnostics) were used as a nuclear counterstain.

Whole-slide digital images were acquired at 40X magnification using the Aperio GT450 high-resolution slide scanner (Leica Biosystems).

Whole-slide image processing and cell detection

Triple-chromogen brightfield whole-slide images (IBA1, yellow; HSP60, green; ALDH1L1, purple; scanned at 40X, 0.26 µm/px) were analyzed in QuPath v0.7.0. Each slide was set to the Brightfield (Other) image type and color-deconvolved using per-slide stain vectors: unit optical-density (OD) vectors were derived from RGB values sampled in single-positive regions for each chromogen, with a per-slide background (white) reference, and a reference vector set (donor D079) was applied to slides for which a dedicated, spectrally clean calibration could not be obtained. The substantia nigra pars compacta was annotated manually on each slide. Within this region, cells were detected with the QuPath watershed cell-detection routine operating on the optical-density sum (requested pixel size 0.5 µm, background radius 8 µm, Gaussian σ 3 µm, intensity threshold 0.10 OD, minimum/maximum area 20/1200 µm 2 , cell expansion 4 µm); watershed post-processing (object splitting) was disabled so that ramified microglia were retained as single objects rather than fragmented. For every detected cell, mean, maximum and standard-deviation OD were recorded for each deconvolved channel, together with morphological features (area, circularity, calipers, eccentricity).

Cell classification.

Cells were classified per slide to accommodate between-slide staining differences. Lineage-marker positivity was determined by fitting a two-component Gaussian mixture model (expectation-maximization) to the per-cell mean OD of IBA1 and of ALDH1L1 and taking the density crossing point of the two components as the positivity threshold. Neuromelanin-laden neurons-an endogenous broadband pigment that three-chromogen deconvolution cannot spectrally separate-were identified with a QuPath object classifier (random-trees) trained on operator-labeled pigmented-neuron and non-pigment examples drawn from multiple slides and applied across the cohort; because neuromelanin and glial identity are mutually exclusive, classifier-positive cells were assigned to a Neuromelanin class and excluded from glial analysis. Cells positive for both IBA1 and ALDH1L1 (a biologically implausible microglia-astrocyte double identity) were flagged as ambiguous and excluded. HSP60 positivity, used to define proteostatically burdened microglia and astrocytes, was assessed on the per-cell maximum HSP60 OD rather than the mean, because mitochondrial HSP60 is punctate and broadly expressed, rendering the mean unimodal and unstable across slides; a two-component Gaussian mixture was fit to the maximum-OD distribution of the lineage-classified glia and the threshold was placed a fixed fraction (0.35) of the way from the component crossing toward the baseline mode. Cells were thereby assigned to microglia (IBA1 + ), proteostatic microglia (IBA1 + /HSP60 + ), astrocytes (ALDH1L1 + ), proteostatic astrocytes (ALDH1L1 + /HSP60 + ), HSP60 + -only, negative, neuromelanin + neurons, or ambiguous. Detection and classification were applied identically to all 12 slides by batch scripting the QuPath project, and per-cell measurements were exported for downstream analysis in R v4.4.3.

Cross-modality comparison.

In donors with both SN immunohistochemistry (IHC) and SN sn/scRNA-seq data (n = 11), we calculated the proportion of proteostatically burdened microglia as IBA1⁺HSP60⁺ / IBA1⁺ cells (IHC) and as M2: HSP90AA1-high / all myeloid cells (sn/scRNA-seq). One library was used per donor; for donors profiled by both methods, the library with more myeloid cells was used. Both measurements were analyzed in a single quasibinomial regression of cell counts on SN neuron-loss grade (0-3;0 = None,1 = Mild,2 = Moderate,3 = Severe), measurement method (IHC or sn/scRNA-seq), and whether this relationship differed between methods, with each donor counted as one unit. We report the modeled percentage of HSP90AA1-high microglia at no and at severe SN neuron loss for each method, and tested whether the rise with SN neuron loss differed between them.

Spectra analysis

To discover biologically informed gene programs that were dysregulated across disease states, we employed a latent factor approach called Spectra (v0.2.0) 31 . Spectra integrates prior knowledge by building gene-gene similarity graphs from pathway annotations, constructing separate graphs for each cell-type (or cell-state) and a global graph. It jointly learns cell-and gene-specific parameters that represent distributions over gene expression programs, encouraging genes connected in the graph to have similar representations. We annotated each latent factor using our manually curated gene sets and then collapsed the factors into a non-redundant set of gene programs based on semantic similarity.

Factor scoring. For each gene program, cell-level activity was computed as the mean score across its constituent factors, winsorized at the 99th percentile of absolute values, transformed with a sign-preserving log1p (sign(x)•log(1 + |x|)) and z-scored across all cells. Cells with ambiguous or missing donor assignment were excluded.

Pathology and Braak LB association.

Cell scores were averaged per donor and data type (fresh cells or nuclei; AMP PD nuclei were treated as nuclei), retaining units with ≥20 cells. For each program we fitted a linear mixed model, score ~ pathology group + data_type + age + sex + PMI + (1 | participant), using lme4 and lmerTest with Satterthwaite degrees of freedom; a linear model was used when no donor contributed more than one unit. P values were adjusted across the 20 programs using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure, and programs with FDR < 0.1 were considered significant. Of 193 donors, 3 were excluded for having fewer than 20 myeloid cells in every data-type unit and 2 for missing PMI, leaving 188 unique donors. Braak LB stages were binned as Control (0), Intermediate (1-4) and Late (5-6) and modeled in the same way, testing each stage against Control and a per-stage linear trend.

State-resolved analysis.

To test programs within microglial states, scores were averaged per donor, data type and state (≥10 cells) and modeled as score ~ pathology group + (1 | participant). Because rare states are not captured in every donor, fixed covariates were omitted at this level to avoid excluding donors with sparse representation. Whether the pathology effect in a given state differed from that in the remaining states was assessed with a pathology-by-state interaction term in a model including all states and a donor random intercept.

Brain-region effects. Region dependence was assessed on scores averaged per donor, data type and brain region (≥20 cells), using score ~ brain_region + pathology_group + data_type + (1 | participant), with an additional region-by-pathology term to test whether the pathology effect differed between regions. Omnibus F-tests were adjusted across programs using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure (Supplementary Table 5).

Single-cell disease relevance scoring (scDRS)

To calculate combined expression of disease-associated genes identified through GWAS summary statistics, we employed scDRS (v1.0.2) https://paperpile.com/c/MUVuwM/4jXDB and MAGMA 62 . The scdrs compute-score command was run with default settings, except that 500 control scores were computed. For downstream analysis, we aggregated cell-scores across state-, Spectra factor-, and pathology levels using the scdrs perform-downstream command with default settings.

Selection of PD risk variants and genotype-state association testing

Candidate variants were drawn from the eQTL-GWAS colocalization results of SingleBrain 46 , which include colocalization of single-nucleus microglial eQTLs and of a reanalysis of MiGA microglial eQTLs https://paperpile.com/c/MUVuwM/82ped (primary and conditionally independent signals) with PD GWAS summary statistics from Nalls et al. (2019) and GP2 (2025; proxy and clinical cases, and clinically defined cases only) 39, 45 . We retained locus-gene pairs with a posterior probability of a shared causal variant (PP4) > 0.8 in either microglial eQTL set and, for each, selected the lead eQTL variant, yielding 49 unique variants. 30 of these 49 unique variants passed imputation quality and a minimum number of homozygous donors per genotype group and were tested. Because the additive model does not require homozygous donors in both groups, 43 microglial eQTL variants and 136 GP2 (2025) 39 lead variants were tested under that model (Supplementary Table 7).

Donors were genotyped on the Illumina Global Screening Array (GSA). Genotypes were imputed using the TOPMed r3 reference panel on the NIH BioData Catalyst TOPMed Imputation Server [63] [64] [65] . Imputed variants were filtered to an imputation quality of R² ≥ 0.3 and were taken as hard calls (hg38) coded as alternate-allele dosage. Ancestry information was available for all 113 donors in the AION cohort, comprising 82 European (EUR; 72.6%), 18 African (AFR; 15.9%), 8 Admixed American (AMR; 7.1%), 3 East Asian (EAS; 2.7%), and 2 South Asian (SAS; 1.8%) individuals. Genetic principal components (gPCs) were computed with PLINK v2.0 66 (--pca) across all 113 donors on 363,963 variants with minor allele frequency ≥ 5%, retained after linkage disequilibrium pruning (--indep-pairwise 50 5 0.2; 50-variant windows, step size 5, r 2 < 0.2). The first two gPCs were included as covariates in all genotype-based models to account for population structure.

For each variant, donors homozygous for the alternate allele were compared with donors homozygous for the reference allele, excluding heterozygotes; an additive model across all three genotypes was fitted as a supplementary analysis. Myeloid state abundances were transformed to centered log-ratios with crumblr 26 and modeled with dream (variancePartition 27 ), adjusting for gPCs 1-2, Braak stage, Braak LB stage, age, sex, brain region, post-mortem interval, log-transformed gene and UMI counts, mitochondrial read fraction, and mitochondrial and ribosomal gene counts, with a random intercept for donor. Analyses were run across all brain regions and in SN alone; variants with fewer than two donors in either genotype group were not tested. P values were corrected using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure across all variant-state tests within each variant set and region.

Statistical analysis

Analyses were performed in R (v4.4.3) and Python unless otherwise stated. Unless otherwise noted, P values were corrected for multiple testing using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure. Significance thresholds were FDR < 0.05 for differential gene expression and scDRS, and FDR < 0.1 for differential abundance and Spectra factor analyses. Nominal P values are reported for the hypothesis-driven curated-pathway ORA of state-specific DEGs, for the prespecified rs3732765 analysis, and for sex and age associations, which did not survive correction. In figures, asterisks denote FDR thresholds and dots or filled points denote nominal P < 0.05, as specified in each legend.
