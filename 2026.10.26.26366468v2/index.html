Ethics Approval Check

Based on the following text, we would expect an ethics approval statement, and it was present:

Participants are recruited into two parallel arms of 800 each: individuals reporting psychedelicrelated challenges and individuals reporting meditation-related challenges, defined as distress or impairment persisting after the acute effects of a psychedelic experience or period of meditation practice have subsided.

Each subsequent component is nested within the preceding one: longitudinal participants are drawn from the cross-sectional sample, and diary participants from the longitudinal sample. presents the study design and timeline.

Invitations to the voice diary component will be issued on a rolling basis to any participant in the psychedelics arm eligible for longitudinal assessment until all n = 40 diary participants are enrolled; these invitations will be made within the first week after baseline completion, with diaries beginning within two weeks of baseline completion.

Participants are then asked to rank the types practiced from most to least, and report cumulative lifetime hours of practice.

In addition, participants are queried about the typical daily meditation schedule they maintained at the time of the precipitating event; and, where a meditation app was used, its name.

Psychedelic-arm participants are asked about psychedelic-specific coping actions (e.g., changing dose or compound, stopping use, reducing or increasing use, or repeating the same psychedelic experience unmodified or modified); meditation-arm participants are asked about contemplative coping strategies (e.g., attention regulation, acceptance or surrender, self-inquiry, reappraisal or application of views, stopping practice, reducing or increasing practice).

In addition, cross-arm use of remedies is assessed: psychedelic-arm participants are asked about contemplative coping strategies for post-psychedelic challenges, and meditation-arm participants are asked about psychedelic use for meditation-related challenges.

At the end of the survey, participants are invited to submit their full account in writing, along with their understanding of why their experience occurred and which responses and remedies they found most and least helpful.

Diary participants are purposely sampled from the longitudinal cohort on the basis of recency and persistence of difficulties: eligible participants report onset of their index experience within the previous six months and ongoing symptoms that are less than 70% resolved.

Consistent with prior work using naturalistic narrative speech prompts (Kaplan et al., 2025), participants are instructed to “try to talk about this in a stream-of-conscious way for about 5 minutes.

Participants are invited to take breaks and resume at their convenience, and those who do not complete are contacted up to three times to identify barriers and provide reminders.

The second is the observational design: participants are not randomized, precluding causal inference.

Participants are clearly informed of study expectations, encouraged to commit to completion, and supported through reminders and follow-ups led by dedicated study personnel.

Because the study involves reporting the use of substances that are currently illegal in several countries, the requirement for written documentation of consent has been waived, on the grounds that a signed consent document would constitute the principal record linking participants to the study and would therefore represent the primary risk of breach of confidentiality.

• Hypothesis 1a: The 59 categories of phenomenology that were catalogued in the Varieties of Contemplative Experience Project (VCE; Lindahl et al., 2017) will be represented in this sample but will be statistically explained by a lesser number of distinct but correlated factors.

• Hypothesis 1b: The duration, severity, and impact on mental health and functioning will range from mild to severe (e.g., > 10-year duration, inability to work, hospitalization, suicidality).

Research Question 2: Given the overlapping phenomenology, benefits, mechanisms and delivery systems of meditation and psychedelics, do the types and course of challenges and adverse effects also overlap? How are they similar or different?

statistical_tests Research question 1: Descriptive information about the kinds of meditation and psychedelic-related lasting difficulties will be analyzed both quantitatively and qualitatively. Qualitative analysis will follow the procedures in the VCE study (Lindahl et al., 2017), which utilized the technique of content analysis to create a codebook defining the range of phenomena that participant’s report (Schreier, 2012). Quantitative analyses will involve reporting descriptive statistics of scales measuring experience phenomenology and impact.

Research Question 2: Independent sample t tests will be used to investigate whether meditation or psychedelic-related difficulties differ across quantitative measures of their characteristics and longitudinal course.

Research Question 3: Standard validated measures of identified clinical syndromes will be used to determine whether meditation or psychedelic-related difficulties match or diverge from clinical criteria or symptom patterns for known clinical syndromes (e.g., trauma symptoms, depersonalization, diagnoses measured by the SCL-90R).

Research Question 4: Multiple regression will be used to identify influencing factors (preexisting factors, phenomenology factors, and response factors) that are predictive of the level of duration, impairment, and severity of meditation or psychedelic-related difficulties. Variables for each type of influencing factor (preexisting, phenomenology, response) will be entered into multiple regression models for each outcome. A control variable for study arm (meditation vs. psychedelic) will be added to all regression models.

Exploratory analyses will also examine whether certain preexisting factors predict symptom phenomenology, and whether certain symptom phenomenology predicts the helpfulness of certain responses/remedies. Exploratory path analyses will be used to examine indirect effects between preexisting factors, phenomenology, responses and outcomes (impairment/duration).

Research Question 5: Qualitative descriptions of remedies will be categorized using content analysis based on participant’s reports of what was most helpful to them. Quantitative endorsements of remedies will be combined with any new categories of remedies stemming from the qualitative data. Next, descriptive statistics will be used to examine participant’s ratings of the effectiveness of each category of remedy.

• Mental health history checklist: This check list assesses the presence of personal and/or family psychiatric illness and trauma history. These factors have been found to be distal risk factors for developing meditation/psychedelic-related adverse effects.
• Adverse Childhood Experiences (ACE; Felitti et al., 1998): Childhood adversity is a commonly recognized risk factor that contributes to vulnerability across a range of mental and physical health domains. It is therefore an important individual difference to assess in meditation/psychedelic-related adverse experiences.
• Set and Setting questionnaire (under development, see measure development methods section for item sources): These questions ask about contextual factors present at the time of onset, or when meditation/psychedelic-related adverse effects first started. These set and setting variables have been found or hypothesized to be proximal risk factors for developing meditation/psychedelic related adverse effects and their severity.

SURVEY SECTION 3:
MEDITATION OR PSYCHEDELIC-RELATED CHALLENGES (PHENOMENOLOGY)

The following measures were included as part of the item-pool for development of the Comprehensive Assessment of Meditation/Psychedelic-related Challenges (CAMC/CAPC-372). Furthermore, validated questionnaires were included in their entirety in order to compare and contrast meditation/psychedelic-related adverse effects with known clinical syndromes (e.g., PTSD, DPDR), and/or constructs that have been used in prior research (e.g., mystical experience).
• Meditation/Psychedelic-Related Adverse Effects Scale (MRAES/PRAES; Britton, 2018; Simonsson et al., 2023): This questionnaire consists of 11 items, which query specific meditation-related adverse effects that have been derived based on prior research. It is a check-list of known adverse effects and the rationale for including it in this study is to identify which of these effects are being experienced by current participants, and to establish correspondence of these known types of adverse effects with other standardized measures.
• Symptom Checklist-90 (SCL-90; Derogatis, 1992): This questionnaire assesses 9 domains of clinically relevant symptoms (e.g., anxiety), as well as global functioning indices. The rationale for this measure is to establish a standardized assessment of a variety of standardized mental health symptoms that individuals might experience in association with meditation/psychedelic -related adverse effects.
• Metacognitive Process of Decentering Scale, Short Form (Hanley et al., 2020): This questionnaire assesses participant propensity toward Decentering, a documented metacognitive effect of meditation and psychedelic use whereby one recognizes a distinction between oneself and one’s thoughts. There is overlap between decentering and certain components of dissociative experiences, making this an important construct to measure when assessing varied responses to meditation.
• Cambridge Depersonalization Scale (CDS; Sierra & Berrios, 2000): Depersonalization, a type of dissociation, was previously reported in qualitative research about meditation/psychedelic–related adverse effects, and is a plausible correlate of meditation/psychedelic related adverse effects since many meditation techniques and psychedelic communities focus on changing one’s relationship to one’s self- concept. Therefore, the Cambridge Depersonalization Scale will be used to assess depersonalization in a standardized way.
• The Mystical Experience Questionnaire (MEQ-30; Barrett et al., 2015): this questionnaire measures a construct that includes changes to the sense of self, changes in perception of time and space, feelings of ineffability, spiritual appraisals, and positive emotion. Many of these characteristics are found in both meditation and psychedelic use and have been found to be predictors of clinical outcomes.
• HPPD Questionnaire (Locke, 2022): Hallucinogen Persistent Perceptual Disorder is a DSM-5 diagnosis characterized by the reexperiencing of certain perceptual symptoms after the use of a psychedelic substance. This questionnaire assesses common perceptual symptoms of this disorder and is included due to the similarities of these symptoms with qualitative descriptions of meditation-related adverse effects.
• Post-Traumatic Stress Disorder Checklist, Trauma-Related Altered States of Consciousness (PCL-TRASC; Frewen et al., 2017): The experience of meditation/psychedelic-related adverse effects may be traumatic. Altered states of consciousness have been reported in association with meditation/psychedelic-related adverse effects specifically, and may be associated with trauma symptoms more broadly. For this reason, we will use the PCL-TRASC to assess trauma-related altered states (e.g., dissociation) in particular, as they may arise as a result of meditation/psychedelic- related adverse effects.
• Religious and Spiritual Struggle Scale (Exline et al., 2014): Individuals often practice meditation or take psychedelics as part of a religious or spiritual framework. When they experience adverse effects, this may contribute to religious or spiritual struggle which, in turn, may exacerbate other challenges.
• Selected items or subscales were also used from the following questionnaires:
• The Psychotomimetic States Inventory (PSI; Mason et al., 2008) is a measure of psychotic-like acute subjective drug effects. The cognitive disorganization, delusional thinking, mania, and perceptual distortion subscales were included in the present research in order to capture changes in executive functioning, cognition, delusional thinking, affective lability, and perception that were described through previous qualitative research on meditation-related challenges (Lindahl et al., 2017) but were not sufficiently captured by other questionnaires.
• The Altered States of Consciousness Rating Scale (AOV; Studerus et al., 2010) is a measure of altered states of consciousness that has been extensively used in research on psychedelic-related subjective experiences. The most recent version of the scale includes 11 subscales, 5 of which were included in the present research: impaired control and cognition, elementary imagery, complex imagery, changed meaning of percepts, and audio-visual synesthesia. These subscales were included to measure changes in executive functioning, loss of agency, and visual imagery that were not sufficiently captured by other questionnaires yet were described in previous qualitative research on meditation-related challenges (Lindahl et al., 2017).
• The Self and Self-Boundary Loss Characteristics Scale (Canby et al., in preparation): is a measure of changes to one’s sense of self and/or the diminishment of the boundaries between self and objects, other people, and/or the world. The present research includes subscales measuring a sense of unity with all things, boundary diminishment with others, boundary diminishment with objects, loss of self, and loss of the sense of ownership of thoughts and feelings. These experiences have been described previously in the context of meditation-related challenges (Lindahl & Britton, 2019) and are thus important to include in the present research.
• The Inventory of Non-Ordinary Experiences (INOE; Taves et al., 2023) is a measure of experiences that may be considered religious, mystical, psychopathological, or anomalous. It is designed to measure the interaction of mind and culture and thus does not make assumptions about how these experiences are interpreted. Items measuring unusual experiences that were not captured by other questionnaires (e.g., lucid dreaming, paralysis, possession experiences) were included in the present research in order to measure a comprehensive range of experiences.
• The Challenging Experiences Questionnaire (CEQ; Barrett et al., 2016): is a measure of acute challenging psychedelic experiences. Items measuring specific fears of losing one’s mind and experiencing one’s own death were included due to a lack of coverage of these topics from other questionnaires.
• The Thalbourne Kundalini Scale (TKS; Thalbourne, 1999) is a measure of the energy-like psychophysiological phenomena that has been termed “kundalini.” Selected items were included from the scale measuring involuntary movements, somatic energy-like sensations, and thermal changes when these phenomena were not captured by other scales.

SURVEY SECTION 4:
POST-DIFFICULTY INFLUENCING RESPONSES AND REMEDIES
4.1 Appraisals Frameworks and Explanations: This section asks about how participants interpret or make sense of their experience. Participants are asked whether they have encountered various interpretive frameworks to make sense of their meditation or psychedelic-related difficulties and the degree to which each of these frameworks were helpful or harmful. Participants are asked about encountering both spiritual/religious frameworks and scientific/biological/trauma-related frameworks.
4.2 Social Responses:
• Social Reactions Questionnaire-Shortened (SRQ-S; Ullman et al., 2017): This is a 16-item measure assessing different kinds of responses to the disclosure of meditation-related adverse effects. Previous research has found that the ways in which others respond to self-disclosures about harmful effects experienced within established social institutions can have important influence on persons’ recovery from harmful events. However, little is known about how these kinds of responses influence recovery from meditation-related adverse effects. This questionnaire will help identify types of social responses as a determinant of outcomes and severity of meditation related adverse effects.
• Institutional Betrayal and Support Questionnaire (Rosenthal et al., 2016): Like the Social Responses Questionnaire, this scale assesses different kinds of responses to disclosure of meditation-related adverse effects. This scale is more extensive, and also more tailored to the ways in which institutions respond to harms that happen under their purview. Responses to this scale can help to identify systemic and structural determinants of differences in the course and outcome of meditation-related adverse effects.
4.3 Remedies. In response to focus group recommendations to provide solutions along with problems, this study aims to provide information about what types of remedies or treatments are most and least helpful for enduring psychedelic difficulties. Participants provide both free responses as well as ratings of therapies, medications, alternative medicine treatments and/or integration support services that they tried, so that any information about negative effects will be paired with evidence-based information about available solutions.
SURVEY SECTION 5:
IMPACT AND OUTCOMES
• Impacts checklist assesses various types of impacts that may result due to meditation/psychedelic-related adverse effects, including inpatient hospitalization, urgent care/emergency room visit; suicide attempt or self-harm, psychotropic medication, outpatient services, and involvement of law enforcement.
• World Health Organization Disability Assessment Schedule (WHODAS; Ustun, 2012): This is a standardized measure of functional impairment that can help to identify areas of impairment that are comparable to impairment from other causes.
• The Patient-Reported Outcomes Measurement Information System Scale - Global Health (PROMIS-GH; Elsman et al., 2021): this is a brief assessment of global health and functioning, which is typically used to assess whole health and wellbeing including psychological, physical, and relational aspects of health. As an index of general health, this measure is important for understanding and interpreting the general impacts of meditation-related adverse effects on individuals’ wellbeing.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. Abstract

Introduction: Psychedelics and meditation are increasingly used for therapeutic and wellness purposes. However, research into adverse effects (AEs) has been limited, without adequate investigation of the nature, clinical characteristics, and diversity of persisting challenges following psychedelic use and meditation practice. This study aims to better characterize the clinical and contextual features of these challenges, describe their longitudinal course, and compare psychedelic-related challenges to meditation-related challenges, selected as comparator conditions given previously characterized phenomenological and contextual overlap between the two.

This is a mixed-methods, enriched-sample, active-comparator (psychedelics vs. meditation) observational study comprising (1) a cross-sectional retrospective survey (N = 1,600), (2) a prospective 12-month longitudinal design involving six bimonthly surveys (n = 800), (3) 12 weeks of weekly ambulatory assessments combining quantitative symptom ratings with narrative voice report (n = 40), and (4) qualitative interviews (n = 60).

Participants are recruited into two parallel arms of 800 each: individuals reporting psychedelicrelated challenges and individuals reporting meditation-related challenges, defined as distress or impairment persisting after the acute effects of a psychedelic experience or period of meditation practice have subsided. Data collection includes a tailored item pool aimed at capturing the symptoms and phenomenology of these challenges in detail, developed from qualitative research and collaboratively with stakeholders with lived experience. The World Health Organization Disability Assessment Schedule (WHODAS 2.0) and measures of agent/exposure, set, setting, and social response are administered to characterize biopsychosocial correlates and functional impacts of AEs. Initial analyses will describe the phenomenology, course, and functional impact of psychedelic-and meditation-related challenges, and evaluate the structure and psychometric properties of the novel item pool.

The study will produce a publicly available de-identified dataset supporting subsequent analyses, such as correspondence with recognized clinical syndromes, trajectory characterization, and cross-arm comparison of measurement structure, reported in registered reports or preregistrations.

Introduction Overview and orientation

This protocol describes an observational, active-comparator naturalistic study of psychedelic-related and meditation-related adverse effects (AEs). Psychedelics and meditation have both shown evidence of efficacy for addressing multiple difficult-to-treat psychiatric symptoms in clinical populations and for enhancing wellbeing in adults (Griffiths et al., 2016; Goldberg et al., 2022; Hoge et al., 2023; Mitchell et al., 2021; Raison et al., 2023). There is also growing evidence, however, that both may produce adverse impacts among a subset of users and practitioners (Breeksema et al., 2022; Simonsson et al., 2023; Goldberg et al., 2022; Van Dam et al., 2025). The therapeutic effects of both have been attributed to alterations in consciousness and heightened neuroplasticity (Holas & Kamińska, 2023; Kishon & Cycowicz, 2025), and although AEs have been recognized in both domains, their nature, chronicity, and correlates remain poorly understood and subject to under-reporting in both the psychedelic (Cheung et al., 2025; Marinis et al., 2025; Olofsson et al., 2026) and meditation (Wong et al., 2018; Van Dam et al., 2025) literatures.

Characterizing unexpected and unwanted effects in psychedelics and meditation is complicated by the fact that both practices can produce intense, difficult, or disorienting experiences during the acute period, many of which resolve without lasting consequence. This protocol therefore focuses on effects with persistent or lasting impact. Throughout this protocol we use adverse effect (AE) to denote distress or functional impairment that persists after the acute effects of a psychedelic experience or a period of meditation practice have subsided: challenging, difficult, or distressing sequelae that impact everyday functioning (Britton et al., 2021; Ward et al., 2026). Defined as such, these effects are neither rare nor trivial. Among individuals with a history of psychedelic use across naturalistic contexts-including recreational, ceremonial, and therapeutic use outside of clinical trials-an estimated 6.4-8.9% report difficulties or functional impairment persisting beyond one day (Simonsson et al., 2023; Olofsson et al., 2026), and 1.3% report difficulties lasting more than a year (Olofsson et al., 2026). Comparable figures are observed among meditators: in population-based samples, impairment lasting longer than one day was reported by 3.8-6.0% of participants (Goldberg et al., 2022; Van Dam et al., 2025), with 1.2% reporting impairment lasting one month or longer (Goldberg et al., 2022). Reported prevalence varies considerably, however, depending upon assessment method and definition of AEs used (Britton et al., 2021). A recent review reports that 25-87% of meditators have experienced AEs, according to less conservative definitions, with 3-37% experiencing AEs that entail functional impairment-though the proportion of these experiencing lasting harms, rather than only transitory difficulties, remains undetermined (Matko & van Dam, 2026).

The remainder of this introduction develops the rationale for the study's design: the state of research into psychedelic-and meditation-related difficulties, measurement gaps present in both fields, and the logic of using meditation-related challenges as a comparator condition for post-psychedelic challenges, before stating the study's research questions. A discussion of the state of research in both of these respective areas-as well as their outstanding needs-follows below.

Psychedelic-related difficulties: the state of research

Classic psychedelics, including psilocybin, lysergic acid diethylamide (LSD), dimethyltryptamine (DMT), and related serotonergic compounds, produce marked, temporary alterations in perception, cognition, and sense of self, and have been investigated as adjuncts to psychotherapy for depression, post-traumatic stress, and substance use disorders (Griffiths et al., 2016; Mitchell et al., 2021; Goodwin et al., 2022). Although they remain Schedule I controlled substances in the United States, several have received FDA breakthrough-therapy designation, are the subject of active Investigational New Drug applications, and their use in the general population has grown substantially in recent years (RAND, 2026). As interest in therapeutic potential and general public use grow in parallel, so do the stakes for a rigorous characterization of the harms that can follow these experiences.

Evidence of such potential for harm has accumulated alongside the evidence of benefit (Breeksema et al., 2022; Simonsson et al., 2023), but its measurement remains uneven. The assessment of acutely challenging experiences during psychedelic sessions is comparatively well-established (Barrett et al., 2016); the assessment of post-psychedelic difficulties, however, remains underdeveloped in two respects. A recent systematic review found that under 25% of psychedelic clinical trials conducted since 2006 used any systematic AE reporting. Furthermore, adverse events in clinical trials have typically been captured through standardized medicalcoding frameworks such as MedDRA (Brown et al., 1999), which were developed for conventional drug trials and are poorly suited to describing phenomena that psychedelics may produce, such as subjective and perceptual disturbances, disruptions to the sense of self, and interpersonal or psychotherapeutic sequelae (Palitsky et al., 2024a), and may therefore miss the effects of greatest clinical relevance. Moreover, much of what is known about enduring difficulties outside of clinical trials is based on retrospective self-reports without repeated longitudinal follow-up, such that inference is limited by recall bias and the absence of documentation regarding the timing and context of onset (Shiffman, Stone, & Hufford, 2008).

Meditation-related difficulties: the state of research

Meditation has yielded a more developed, albeit still limited, literature on AEs.

Meditation here refers to a broad family of contemplative practices that train attention, cognition, affect, and self-related processing. Rather than adopting a tradition-specific definition, we characterize meditation in mechanistic terms following Dahl, Lutz, and Davidson (2015), who distinguish attentional, constructive, and deconstructive families of practice, and Dorjee (2016), who frames contemplative practice in terms of the mind's metacognitive self-regulatory capacity and associated modes of existential awareness. This mechanistic framing spans secular mindfulness-based programs and their contemplative-tradition antecedents. Lindahl et al. (2017) produced a uniquely detailed and standard-setting systematic mapping of meditation-related difficulties, conducted in the Varieties of Contemplative Experience Study. This mixed-methods investigation identified 59 experiential categories of meditation-related challenge across affective, cognitive, perceptual, somatic, conative, social, and sense-of-self domains.

Historically, most quantitative meditation research has assessed adverse experiences using single items or narrow negative-affect indices rather than instruments designed to capture the full range of reported phenomena (Van Dam et al., 2025). Where harms have been monitored in trials at all, monitoring has been inconsistent and adverse-event reporting correspondingly sparse: A systematic review of mindfulness-based stress reduction and cognitive therapy trials found that only a small proportion of eligible RCTs reported monitoring for adverse events at all, and that reported adverse-event rates in those that did were under 1% (Wong et al., 2018). A growing body of multi-item and phenomenological work has begun to address this gap (Britton et al. 2021; Goldberg et al., 2022; Schlosser, 2019; Van Dam et al., 2025), but the evidence base remains small, and prospective data on the emergence and resolution of these difficulties are scarcer. A parallel prospective cohort study now underway in a general population of beginning meditators (Ward et al., 2026) will estimate the incidence and functional impact of these effects as they arise, a design complementary to and coordinated with the present study.

Meditation-related challenges as comparator condition for psychedelics

Meditation-related AEs overlap with psychedelic-related AEs in both phenomenological and contextual characteristics, which is what makes meditation the most informative available comparator condition (Grob & Grigsby, 2022; Millière et al., 2018; Palitsky et al., 2024b). What has not been established systematically and empirically is the precise nature of this overlap: where these challenges converge in their features, course, and impact, and where they diverge.

The present study's parallel, enriched-sample design is built to answer these questions.

A need for improvements in measurement and characterization of AEs

Considered together, these two literatures are characterized by a number of common deficits, described in a recent integrative review (Palitsky et al., 2024b), which proposes an agenda for advancing AE research in both domains. Specifically, AE measurement strategies require improvements in detail and comprehensiveness, grounding in the experiences of affected individuals, orientation to real-world impact, standardization, and representativeness. To help address some of these needs, this project aims to improve the content validity (i.e., comprehensiveness, relation to phenomenology, grounding in stakeholder experiences) and advance the methodological rigor of AE assessment related to meditation and psychedelics use.

This study offers considerable granularity in assessing the range of phenomenology that AEs related to these practices may produce, including dissociation, perceptual changes, somatic disturbances, and existential distress-above and beyond the narrow set of general symptoms such as anxiety or depressed mood. It remains unclear whether meditation-related and postpsychedelic challenges constitute variants of recognized clinical syndromes, on one hand, or phenomena not captured by existing nosology, on the other. The assessment approach described in this protocol embeds established measures of clinical symptoms, including depression, anxiety, dissociation, somatic symptoms, alongside novel, phenomenologically-oriented items. This design enables future benchmarking of psychedelic-and meditation-related challenges against recognized syndromes in pre-registered analyses.

There is also a need for chronological assessment beyond cross-sectional self-report, in AE research related to meditation and psychedelics. Longitudinal research methods make it possible to prospectively assess the course, duration, and persistence of these challenges, as well as longitudinal predictors of improvement and worsening. Addressing this lacuna is a central rationale for the longitudinal component of the present design.

The present study

This protocol describes a mixed-methods, enriched-sample observational study of psychedelic-related and meditation-related challenges, comprising parallel cross-sectional, longitudinal, qualitative interview, and intensive weekly mixed-method diary components (described in Materials and Methods). Notably, several of the symptom batteries and set-andsetting measures were co-developed with stakeholders with lived experience of these challenges and grounded in existing research characterizing AEs (Sánchez et al., 2021; Boness et al., 2025).

The study is designed to yield a uniquely detailed dataset characterizing these challenges and their impacts, and to serve as a foundation for a pre-registered program of subsequent analyses.

Three sets of research questions guide the initial analyses planned for this study.

The first of these is: What are the phenomenological features of psychedelic-and meditation-related challenges, and what is the structure of these features? Initial analyses will describe the Comprehensive Assessment of Psychedelic-related Challenges (CAPC; see below for description) and Comprehensive Assessment of Meditation-related Challenges (CAMC; see below for description) batteries and evaluate their structure and psychometric properties using exploratory and confirmatory factor analysis.

The second set of questions is: How are these challenges experienced by those affected, what are their impacts on day-to-day life, how do these impacts unfold over time, and to what extent are they associated with functional impairment? These questions are addressed through qualitative interviews, weekly mixed-method diaries collected via a smartphone-administered ecological momentary assessments using Fabla (Kaplan et al., 2025), and the WHO Disability Assessment Schedule (WHODAS 2.0; Üstün et al., 2010).

The third set of questions is: To what extent do psychedelic-related and meditationrelated challenges converge or diverge in their features, course, and impacts? The parallel twoarm design permits direct descriptive comparison across all study components. Additional questions related to this research program will be addressed in pre-registered analyses and registered reports (see Open Science strategy). Beyond these initial and planned analyses, a central aim of this study is to establish a publicly available anonymized dataset intended to serve as a resource for the field. The breadth of assessment described here supports a variety of research questions concerning meditation-and psychedelic-related AEs, their contexts, and impacts The dataset and associated materials will be made available to researchers (see Open Science Strategy).

Materials and Methods

Study design

This is a two-armed (psychedelics and meditation) mixed-methods observational study. It comprises a cross-sectional retrospective online surveys (n = 800 per arm; N = 1,600), which serve as the baseline for longitudinal follow-ups among a subset of participants with current unresolved symptoms from recent-onset AEs (n = 400 per arm; N = 800), consisting of six bimonthly surveys over 12 months. Qualitative interviews will be conducted with a subsample of participants (n = 30 per arm; N = 60), and an additional subset of n = 40 participants drawn from the longitudinal sample will be invited to complete weekly ambulatory assessments comprising quantitative symptom ratings and a narrative voice diary for 12 weeks. Each subsequent component is nested within the preceding one: longitudinal participants are drawn from the cross-sectional sample, and diary participants from the longitudinal sample. presents the study design and timeline.

An enriched-sample design was chosen to support detailed characterization of these challenges. General-population cohort studies can estimate how frequently such difficulties occur, but at realistic incidence rates they yield too few affected individuals to characterize the structure of those difficulties in depth: a cohort of several hundred beginning meditators produces on the order of dozens of adverse-effect cases (Ward et al., 2026), insufficient for detailed analyses of phenomenology or quantitative approaches that can identify symptom clustering. Solely recruiting individuals who have already experienced psychedelic-or meditation-related challenges concentrates the sample on the phenomena of interest and permits the detailed, multi-method assessment described below, although it forecloses analyses of prevalence of these AEs in the general populations of meditators or psychedelics takers.

All components of the study will be conducted online, with surveys delivered via the Qualtrics survey platform, interviews conducted over the Zoom teleconferencing platform, and weekly assessments collected via the Fabla app (Kaplan et al., 2025).

Participants

This research will recruit N = 1,600 participants into two equal study arms (n = 800 each), identified by primary precipitating exposure (psychedelics vs. meditation).

Inclusion in either arm requires an experience the participant believes was caused by the psychedelic use or meditation practice, and which persisted after the acute effects of the substance had subsided or beyond a single meditation session, and either (1) caused distress, (2) negatively impacted the participant's life or functioning, or (3) required additional support or treatment. The definition is common to both arms, differing only in the precipitating event.

Psychedelic-arm AEs must be precipitated by either a "classical" psychedelic (5-HT2A receptor agonist), such as LSD, psilocybin, ayahuasca, DMT, 5-MeO-DMT, or mescaline, or by compounds that have been culturally classed with psychedelics, such as MDMA, ibogaine, or ketamine, in line with prior research on subjective and community use features of psychedelics (Palitsky et al. 2024a). Cannabis, though sometimes grouped with psychedelics, differs from the above compounds in its patterns of use, legal status, and neuropharmacology, and carries recognized independent risks associated with its use (Volkow et al., 2014), and is therefore not included. Eligibility for the meditation arm was not limited to any prespecified set of practices or traditions, although these were assessed in the study measures.

Sample stratification targets were developed to support the recruitment of participants with ongoing AEs into the longitudinal study, such that n = 400 participants (50%) in each arm must have experienced <70% improvement in AE symptoms and have had their AEs onset within the past 12 months. For the psychedelics arm, stratification targets also support adequate representation of different psychedelics, comprising at least 150 participants with AEs related to each: psilocybin, LSD, and MDMA. Because of the existing use of ketamine in legal therapeutic settings, its occasional inclusion among psychedelics, and the corresponding need to assess its AEs, ketamine is included in this study; however, because the number of ketamine users may far exceed that of other psychedelics, the stratified sampling approach caps the number of individuals with ketamine-related AEs at n = 150 total. Because other psychedelics (e.g., mescaline) are expected to be used less prevalently, no minimum sampling quota is set for AEs related to these substances.

Participants who fail validity checks or who appear not to be participating in good faith will be excluded. These procedures are described below.

Recruitment, screening, and compensation

Recruitment

Participants will be recruited through several channels. Direct outreach will be made to clinical and academic centers conducting clinical trials, asking them to share study flyers and information with participants who have completed their research studies. Outreach will also be made to community organizations, advocacy networks, and educational entities, some of which provided formative input into the design of this study as part of community-engaged research.

These include peer support and special-interest organizations; organizations that support individuals experiencing recent or enduring psychedelic-and meditation-related AEs; and participants receiving psilocybin services at Oregon licensed psilocybin service centers, recruited with the support of community-engaged organizations informed about this study. Further recruitment will occur through public appearances, lectures, and presentations (e.g., podcasts), as well as news bulletins from psychedelic-focused organizations (Palitsky et al., 2025). In the case that recruitment milestones prove difficult to reach, paid targeted social media campaigns may be used for further recruitment. Participants will be recruited from countries where psychedelic use has been decriminalized or legalized in any jurisdiction, pending country-specific approval by the Emory University IRB and international recruitment guidelines; current recruitment includes the United States, Canada, and Australia. Recruitment is planned to continue for 36 months in order to reach adequate sample size and to account for attrition and rapidly changing access to psychedelics internationally.

Data collection procedure

Screening and participant verification

Potential participants for each arm will complete a preliminary screening instrument specific to that arm, querying age, experience of psychedelic-or meditation-related AEs, and willingness to complete a follow-up live screening call. Those who meet initial eligibility criteria will be invited to a live screening conducted over teleconference with study team researchers, typically 15 minutes in length. In this screening, eligibility will be assessed through a set of open-ended questions aimed at discerning participants engaging in research in good faith from fraudulent participants. These will take the form of a free-flowing conversation aimed at eliciting background information that would demonstrate lack of the kinds of experiences that would qualify a participant for this study. These screenings will also provide opportunities to answer detailed questions about the study, offer transparency, and ensure that participants have a direct point of contact with the study team. During study screenings, if participants have had AEs in the context of both meditation and psychedelics, they will be asked to identify one as primary (e.g., due to temporal precedence or subjective impact) and will be enrolled in the corresponding arm of the study. In addition to live screening, survey data will be examined for indicators of nongenuine responding, including failed attention checks, internally inconsistent or impossible responses, implausible response times, and duplicate or automated submissions (Louis & Thompson, 2025; cf. verification procedures described in Ward et al., 2026). Participants identified through these procedures will be contacted when feasible and excluded from analysis if their responses cannot be verified.

Survey administration

The baseline survey is expected to take approximately 90-120 minutes. Participants may complete the baseline survey across more than one session, with responses retained and the survey required to be completed within 30 days of starting. All participants will be sent up to three reminder emails and check-ins for each assessment in the study timeline if they have qualified for the study but have not yet begun to participate, or have begun an assessment but have not yet completed it.

Follow-up surveys for longitudinal study waves are expected to take approximately 30-60 minutes, with analogous completion windows and check-in procedures as the baseline survey.

Longitudinal and weekly diary enrollment

Checks will be conducted weekly to identify participants meeting criteria for longitudinal follow-up (Participants). Those participants will be contacted within one week and invited to participate in the longitudinal portion of the study. Invitations will be sent to complete a followup survey two months after completion of the baseline survey, and every two months thereafter for one year. Invitations to the voice diary component will be issued on a rolling basis to any participant in the psychedelics arm eligible for longitudinal assessment until all n = 40 diary participants are enrolled; these invitations will be made within the first week after baseline completion, with diaries beginning within two weeks of baseline completion.

Baseline survey

Baseline surveys comprise several published instruments, as well as novel items for the assessment of constructs for which published instruments are insufficient or nonexistent. The latter include batteries focused on set and setting, and on the symptoms and phenomenology of AEs.

Participants' individual-difference and demographic characteristics will include age, dominant hand, gender, sexual orientation, race and ethnicity, national origin and country of current residence, relationship status, rurality, education, current occupation, socioeconomic status (MacArthur Scale of Subjective Social Status; Adler et al., 2000), military service background, religious affiliation, involvement, and observance, and experience as a psychedelic facilitator, guide, or therapist. Participants will also report personal and family psychiatric history. Early childhood adversity will be assessed via the ACEs questionnaire (Felitti et al., 1998; 10 items, yes/no/prefer not to answer).

Use and practice history. Participants in both arms report their lifetime history with the relevant practice; items are parallel across arms except where noted. An open-ended item assesses age of first psychedelic use or first meditation practice. Prior motivations are assessed via a 12-item checklist in each arm (e.g., "For social reasons (e.g., my friends were doing it)" in the psychedelic arm; "To gain insight into yourself or parts of your life/self-knowledge" in the meditation arm).

Psychedelic arm.

The prior psychedelic experiences is assessed via Likert-type items (never, once, 2-5 times, 6-10 times, 11-20 times, 21-50 times, 51-100 times, over 100 times) for each of: psilocybin, LSD, ayahuasca, DMT (other than ayahuasca), peyote, mescaline (other than peyote), ketamine, MDMA, and ibogaine, with three open-ended "other" options.

Meditation arm.

Prior meditation experience is assessed by asking participants to indicate, from a list of 25 traditions including "no tradition," which they have practiced in, and from a list of 18 practice types (e.g., "observing the body (body scan)," "embodied movement practice", "contemplation of sacred texts"), which they have practiced at any point. Participants are then asked to rank the types practiced from most to least, and report cumulative lifetime hours of practice. An additional item asks whether participants have used wearable devices to assist their practice-whatever practice was specified.

Both arms.

Participants report the contexts in which they have engaged in the practice, using an 8-option checklist (e.g., "At a social event, party, rave, nightclub, festival, or concert", for the psychedelic arm; "In a meditation teacher training program", for the meditation arm; "On a multi-day retreat, for both") with an open-ended "other" option, and rank their endorsed contexts by frequency. Participants who have attended multi-day retreats report how many they have attended and the duration of the longest; psychedelic-arm participants who took psychedelics as part of a spiritual tradition or retreat describe that tradition in an open-ended item. To account for the existence of meditation and psychedelics co-use, both arms query ongoing and historic meditation practice and psychedelics use.

Responses to these items are used to narrow the options presented in the Agent, Set, and Setting Questionnaire below by means of survey logic, such that only previously-endorsed lifetime history responses are re-administered with reference to the index experience.

Agent, Set, and Setting Questionnaire. A novel measure for the assessment of the agent, set, and setting at the time of onset of difficulties is included in the baseline questionnaire for both arms. Questionnaire items were developed based on a review of prior literature (Aday et al., 2021; Aixalà, 2022; Breeksema et al., 2022; Carhart-Harris et al., 2018; Haijen et al., 2018; Johnson et al., 2008; Perkins et al., 2021; Russ et al., 2019; Strassman, 2022). The measure comprises 80 items across sections assessing the agent, the individual's mindset at the time of the index experience ("set"; 36 items), and the context in which it occurred ("setting"; 50 items in the psychedelic arm, 45 in the meditation arm). The full set of agent, set, and setting items is included in Tables S1 and S2.

Agent. Items assessing the agent focus on the nature of the exposure associated with the onset of difficulties (the index experience).

In the psychedelic arm, participants indicate which psychedelic(s) they took prior to onset, using the response options presented in their use history; estimate how many times they had previously used that compound; specify the method of consumption; and estimate the dose ("Low dose (enough for you to feel a weak psychedelic effect)," "Moderate dose (enough for you to feel psychedelic effects)," "High dose (enough for you to feel extremely strong psychedelic effects)," or "Not sure"), with the option to specify an exact dose if known. Further items ask how frequently they were taking psychedelics at the time, whether they were concurrently microdosing, and whether the index experience was precipitated by multiple doses.

In the meditation arm, participants indicate which of the 18 practice types they were engaged in at the time of onset and the amount of each (from "Very little" to "Very much"), and which was their primary practice, as well as which of the 25 traditions they were practicing in and which was primary, if any. In addition, participants are queried about the typical daily meditation schedule they maintained at the time of the precipitating event; and, where a meditation app was used, its name. The arms differ in one respect of consequence: in the meditation arm, difficulties need not arise from a single, datable index experience, and items accommodate onset that is gradual or cumulative across a period of practice.

In both arms, participants report use of other substances and prescription medications shortly before or during the index experience, including timeline and dosage.

Set.

Items assessing mindset at the time of the index experience comprise a 5-6 item questionnaire assessing preparatory behaviors, a 12-item checklist assessing participants' intentions for either psychedelics or meditation (e.g., to improve physical health, for social reasons, general self-improvement, to gain insight into oneself), and two sets of Likert-type items (1 = strongly disagree,7 = strongly agree) adapted from Perkins et al. (2021). The first set includes 6 items querying mindset around the time of the experience (e.g., occurrence of a recent major stressor; preoccupation with other issues in life; sense of preparedness; mental and physical health; clarity of intention). The second set includes 17 items querying mindset during the experience, assessing perceived control; acceptance or surrender versus resistance; redirection ability; and intense affective states such as fear, despair, and suffering.

Setting.

Items assessing setting focus on the overall context of the experience (e.g., therapeutic program or therapy, spiritual or religious ceremony or practice, social event; retreat or group-practice context in the meditation arm), the social context (presence of a guide, leader, teacher, sitter, or other participants, the quality of interaction with them, and that person's expertise or professional role), the environmental context (e.g., safety, comfort, availability of support), the post-experience context (e.g., presence of integration), and additional supports available shortly before or after the experience (e.g., counseling, yoga, tai chi). Items measuring the quality of relationships with others during the experience were adapted from established measures of therapeutic alliance: the Session Rating Scale (Duncan et al., 2003) and the Working Alliance Inventory (Horvath & Greenberg, 1989).

Influencing factors at onset of difficulties. Additional factors surrounding the onset of difficulties, which are not properties of the agent, the participant's mindset, or the context of the experience, are assessed separately and include the following: Timing. Participants report the recency of the index experience and, in the meditation arm, how recently difficulties began, how long after establishing a regular practice they emerged, whether they emerged within the first 50 hours of practice, and how long the daily schedule reported above had been maintained at the time of onset.

Health behaviors. Participants in both arms report any changes in diet, food intake, or sleep patterns in the period leading up to the index experience.

Circumstances at onset. Participants report their age and relationship status at the time of onset, whether they had children, and whether they were pregnant, had recently given birth, had recently miscarried or terminated a pregnancy, or were trying to conceive.

Assessment of symptoms and phenomenology: the CAPC and CAMC. Symptoms and experiences that are part of the AEs are assessed using a novel item pool administered in two variants: the Comprehensive Assessment of Psychedelic-related Challenges (CAPC) and the Comprehensive Assessment of Meditation-related Challenges (CAMC). The two variants comprise the same items, differing in instructions and wording that specify the psychedelic or meditation context. To enable the development and validation of a measurement model from this pool, the CAPC/CAMC is described here as an assessment battery, containing a mixture of validated scales and individual items.

This item pool comprises 270 Likert-type items (1 = not at all,5 = extremely) designed to capture 59 previously-identified categories of symptoms and experiences across seven domains derived from qualitative research (Lindahl et al., 2017): cognitive, perceptual, affective, somatic, conative, sense of self, and social. It combines 261 items retained or adapted from existing measures, with 9 novel items developed for this study. Items for all CAPC/CAMC scales were either generated in focus groups and/or interviews with meditators and/or psychedelic users with lived experience of enduring negative effects, or were adapted from preexisting scales. Items were rated for inclusion or modification by meditator/psychedelic stakeholders, by researchers from multiple disciplines (religious studies, psychology, etc.), and by a psychometrician in a modified Delphi process (Fink et al., 1984) to yield the final set of items. The full item pool is detailed in S3 Table.

Existing measures included within the CAPC/CAMC.

Established measures included within the CAPC/CAMC. Items drawn from existing instruments are listed in an appendix. With three exceptions noted, these instruments are administered in part rather than in whole, and response options were modified throughout for consistency with the rest of the CAPC/CAMC; published subscale scores are therefore not recoverable for partially administered measures, which contribute items to the pool rather than scores. Where an instrument has been further adapted, the adaptation is noted, since evidence of reliability and validity pertains to scores obtained for a particular purpose and population rather than to instruments as fixed properties.

The Generalized Anxiety Disorder Scale (GAD-7; Spitzer et al., 2006) is administered in full and includes 7 Likert-type items assessing symptoms consistent with generalized anxiety disorder. The Inventory of Depression and Anxiety Symptoms-II (IDAS-II; Watson et al., 2012) assesses psychological symptoms across 18 domains, of which 15 are included, yielding 79 items: Appetite Gain (3), Appetite Loss (3), Checking (3), Dysphoria (10), Euphoria (5), Ill Temper (5), Insomnia (6), Lassitude (6), Mania (5), Ordering (5), Social Anxiety (6), Suicidality (6), Traumatic Avoidance (4), Traumatic Intrusions (4), and Well-Being (8). The Panic, Claustrophobia, and Cleaning subscales are not included.

The remaining embedded measures assess loneliness (7 items from the UCLA Loneliness Scale, Version 3; Russell, 1996); dimensions of altered states of consciousness ( 10 2024). Two further items were written for this study adapting the Metacognitive Processes of Decentering Scale (Hanley et al., 2020), and two adapting the Symptom Checklist-90-Revised (Derogatis, 1994).

Finally, the Religious and Spiritual Struggles Scale (Exline et al., 2014) is administered as part of the item pool in a modified 25-item form, with modifications made for relevance to spiritual-but-not-religious participants and to non-Abrahamic traditions (e.g., adding "or a higher power" to items referring to God).

Novel items included in the CAPC/CAMC.

A total of 9 novel items were developed for this study, derived from input from researchers and stakeholders with lived experience of AEs, and from the 59 categories identified by Lindahl et al. (2017). Example items include "I developed a fear of meditation/psychedelic or meditation/psychedelic-related things" and "I experienced insights about the nature of reality that made me distressed or afraid."

Post-difficulty responses and remedies.

Influencing factors occurring after or in response to the onset of difficulties are assessed as follows.

Appraisals: interpretive frameworks and explanations encountered.

Participants provide an open-ended narrative of their understanding of what caused the difficulties, followed by a checklist of explanatory frameworks they may have encountered from others: scientific or neuroscientific explanations; medical or psychiatric diagnostic explanations; spirituality and religion; trauma-related explanations; spiritual emergency (e.g., kundalini awakening); a normal part of the path or of spiritual progress; spiritual insight; purification (blockages, negative energy, sin, karma, evil spirits); incorrect practice or inappropriate psychedelic use; and preexisting psychiatric condition, plus two write-in options. For each, participants indicate whether they encountered it and, if so, rate how often (1 = sometimes to3 = very often) and how helpful or harmful it was (-3 = extremely harmful to +3 = extremely helpful), with optional comments including who provided the explanation.

Responses.

Participants report whether they disclosed their difficulties to others (e.g., partner, family, friends, community, healthcare professionals, teachers or guides, organizational leadership, "I didn't tell anyone," other), with optional comment, and rate the helpfulness of anyone they disclosed to (-3 to +3). Psychedelic-arm participants are asked about psychedelic-specific coping actions (e.g., changing dose or compound, stopping use, reducing or increasing use, or repeating the same psychedelic experience unmodified or modified); meditation-arm participants are asked about contemplative coping strategies (e.g., attention regulation, acceptance or surrender, self-inquiry, reappraisal or application of views, stopping practice, reducing or increasing practice). In addition, cross-arm use of remedies is assessed: psychedelic-arm participants are asked about contemplative coping strategies for post-psychedelic challenges, and meditation-arm participants are asked about psychedelic use for meditation-related challenges.

Impact and chronicity.

Participants report whether their difficulties are currently ongoing, how long their difficulties or symptoms lasted (ordinal,0 = ≤1 day to8 = >10 years), how recovered they currently feel (slider,0 = not at all recovered to100 = totally recovered), and how long they felt impaired as a result (0 = ≤1 day to8 = >10 years).

Consequences and service utilization.

Participants endorse any consequences experienced as a result of their difficulties from a list including unemployment or inability to work; leaving school; moving back in with parents; applying for disability or unemployment benefits; suicidal ideation or attempt; urgent care or emergency department visit; psychotropic medication; law enforcement or legal involvement; outpatient care; inpatient medical treatment or hospitalization; and other. Where applicable, number of hospitalizations and total days hospitalized are queried.

Functional impairment.

An adapted version of the WHODAS-II (Üstün et al., 2010), comprising 36 Likert-type items (0 = none,4 = extreme), assesses impairment of daily living and functioning over the past 30 days across the domains of cognition, mobility, self-care, getting along with others, life activities, and social participation. Overall functioning is additionally assessed with a single-item slider referring to the period when difficulties were most intense (0 = 0% functioning,100 = 100% functioning).

Spiritual and religious factors. In addition to the measures of religiosity described above, and to the Religious and Spiritual Struggles Scale administered within the CAPC/CAMC, participants complete a modified version of the Spiritual Bypass Scale (Fox et al., 2017), comprising 13 Likert-type items (1 = strongly disagree,4 = strongly agree). Modifications were made for relevance to spiritual-but-not-religious participants and to non-Abrahamic traditions, analogous to those made to the Religious and Spiritual Struggles Scale.

Assessment of causality likelihood.

Although self-report assessment of causality is imperfect, this study employs a series of questions across eight criteria designed to address some limitations of self-reported attribution and to evaluate the likelihood that reported experiences were causally related to psychedelics or meditation rather than to alternative causes (Britton et al., 2021; Lindahl et al., 2017). Items query whether pre-existing conditions worsened after exposure; whether experiences occurred on more than one occasion; whether they lessened after stopping or decreasing exposure; whether they reappeared with resumption or increased exposure; whether the participant believes the experiences were caused in whole or in part by exposure; whether experiences first appeared during or following exposure; and whether plausible alternative causes could wholly account for the experience, with an optional openended description. This variable additionally serves as a validity control: participants scoring below the cutoff for attribution of their AEs to psychedelics or meditation are excluded from analysis.

Qualitative and free-response items. Open-response items appear at regular intervals throughout the sections described above, giving participants an opportunity to comment on any standardized assessment they have just completed. At the end of the survey, participants are invited to submit their full account in writing, along with their understanding of why their experience occurred and which responses and remedies they found most and least helpful. These responses support descriptive characterization and validity checking and may be subjected to content-analytic coding as part of a mixed-methods analytic strategy.

Longitudinal follow-up surveys

The longitudinal follow-up surveys, administered every two months (a total of six times) after completion of the baseline survey to participants endorsing ongoing difficulties, employ a subset of the baseline measures: the CAPC/CAMC and the WHODAS-II. They additionally include the Multidimensional Scale of Perceived Social Support (Zimet et al., 1988), comprising 12 Likert-type items (1 = very strongly disagree,7 = very strongly agree) assessing perceived support from significant others, family, and friends.

At alternating timepoints, participants complete different baseline sections: at 2, 6, and 12 months they complete the Remedies section, tailored to assess what remedies they have currently tried; at 4, 8, and 10 months they complete the Responses and Appraisals sections, tailored to track social responses received during the preceding period.

Weekly mixed-method symptom and narrative diaries

A subset of n = 40 participants is invited to complete a weekly assessment for 12 weeks.

Diary participants are purposely sampled from the longitudinal cohort on the basis of recency and persistence of difficulties: eligible participants report onset of their index experience within the previous six months and ongoing symptoms that are less than 70% resolved. The assessment is administered via Fabla, a multimedia ecological momentary assessment platform (Arconada Alvarez et al., 2026 , Kaplan et al., 2025). Participants receive a brief orientation and app training, including a practice survey and an opportunity to ask questions about the app or the items. Weekly assessments are administered at 7-day intervals at a day and time of each participant's choosing (Fig 1). Each weekly survey remains open for 24 hours, with reminder push notifications delivered at 6-hour intervals until survey completion via Fabla. Adherence is monitored by the research team, with two consecutive missed entries triggering a check-in call.

Each weekly survey takes approximately 10-15 minutes. The first two items are voicerecorded prompts: "Thinking specifically about your [psychedelic or meditation] related challenges: how have things been going this week?" and "Tell us, more generally, about your week."

Consistent with prior work using naturalistic narrative speech prompts (Kaplan et al., 2025), participants are instructed to "try to talk about this in a stream-of-conscious way for about 5 minutes. Do not worry about pauses, stutters, or having the right things to say. We are interested in hearing anything about this that comes to mind." For both narrative diary items, an option to "type my response instead" is provided for participants who prefer to write rather than speak about their experience. Participants then complete the IDAS-II Dysphoria and Well-Being scales (18 items), three items from the Sheehan Disability Scale (Sheehan, 1983) assessing functional impairment, with the total-work-days-lost item removed, and a single item assessing overall sleep quality (1 = terrible,10 = excellent). In many cases, the stems of these items were modified to query, "During the last week", given the specific time horizon of this assessment.

Qualitative interviews

Participants (n = 30 per arm; N = 60) representing a range of settings, symptom types, use contexts, and compounds, and including participants with both resolved and unresolved symptoms, are invited to participate in qualitative interviews. Interviews use a semi-structured format to evaluate (1) phenomenology, (2) interpretive frameworks, (3) impacts, and (4) contributing factors, including coping strategies, elaborating on methods used by Lindahl et al. (2017) to study meditation-related AEs. Interviews last approximately 60-90 minutes and are conducted after participants have completed the other study components for which they are eligible.

Analysis Plan Scope of analyses reported in this protocol

This protocol describes the study design, the core outcomes, and a defined set of initial descriptive and psychometric analyses, all of which will be preregistered as described in Open Science strategy. Beyond data validation and preliminary descriptive characterization of the data, subsequent analyses will be independently pre-registered whenever possible.

Planned initial analyses

The initial analyses comprise descriptive characterization of the sample and their difficulties, psychometric evaluation of the CAPC/CAMC batteries, description of functional impairment, thematic analysis of the qualitative and narrative data, and side-by-side description across arms. These are the analyses the study will report first; they illustrate what the dataset supports rather than exhausting its analytic potential.

Analyses are to be conducted separately within each arm before any combined analysis.

Because psychedelic-and meditation-related AEs are expected to overlap, the effective sample available for evaluating a given item pool may exceed the n = 800 recruited into the corresponding arm; the extent of this overlap is not yet known, and will be reported descriptively.

Descriptive characterization. Descriptive statistics will be reported for all study variables: sample composition; the compounds, practices, and contexts associated with onset; endorsement frequencies and severity distributions across the 59 categories comprising the batteries; duration, chronicity, and current status of difficulties; consequences and service utilization; and the remedies participants attempted with their rated helpfulness. Remedy data are characterized descriptively, including whether the profile of remedies attempted differs by the characteristics, severity, or chronicity of difficulties; analyses treating remedies as predictors of outcome are deferred.

Structure of the CAPC/CAMC item pool. Analyses specified at this stage of investigation will examine the structure of the CAPC/CAMC item pool. Endorsement rates, variability, and inter-variable associations will be examined first. Then, variables and standalone items showing minimal variability will be flagged and documented, rather than removed.

Exploratory factor analysis will then be conducted within each arm, with parallel analysis used to bound the number of factors retained alongside considerations of factor interpretability, followed by confirmatory factor analysis in a holdout sample. Specification of rotation, estimation, and retention criteria will be documented in the pre-registration for this analysis.

Functional impairment. WHODAS-II scores will be described by domain and in aggregate, within and across arms, together with the single-item overall functioning rating.

Associations between impairment and the characteristics of participants' difficulties will be reported descriptively.

Qualitative analyses. Interview transcripts will be analyzed thematically and using an adaptive, inductive-deductive approach (Layder, 1998). Diary narrative data are automatically transcribed using AWS transcription integration. Transcripts will be checked and corrected as needed by a trained research assistant to verify accuracy, and will be analyzed using an adaptation of the structured tabular approach to thematic analysis for brief texts (Robinson, 2022), suited to the volume and brevity of weekly entries. Narratives and detailed free-responses provided at various points throughout the cross-sectional and longitudinal survey forms may also be retained for thematic analyses.

Cross-arm description. Symptom profiles, impairment, course, and remedy patterns will be described side by side across arms, providing the first empirical account of where psychedelic-and meditation-related difficulties converge and diverge. Formal evaluation of whether measurement structures are parallel across arms, in the form of multigroup confirmatory factor analysis and tests of measurement invariance, is deferred, as it depends on the measurement model being established first.

Sample size calculation

Sample size was determined by the requirements of the full analytic program described in the Open Science strategy below, including analyses to be reported in subsequent registered reports, rather than by the initial analyses alone.

A sample of n = 800 per arm was selected to support the factor-analytic work described above. Current standards suggest validation samples of at least 300 participants for each stage of exploratory and confirmatory analysis (Clark & Watson, 2019); n = 800 permits splitting each arm into training (n = 400) and testing (n = 400) sets while retaining adequate power in both after attrition. Power analysis for regression models indicates that n = 480 provides greater than 95% power to detect small effects (f² of .2). Additionally, this sample size ensures adequate power to support possible future measure-development work, currently beyond the scope of this protocol.

For the longitudinal component, power was estimated through Monte Carlo simulation (Muthén & Muthén, 2002) using the R package simr, generating 500 samples of n = 240 to account for 40% attrition, with four repeated measures to account for 33% attrition. Models were fitted using lme4 and included time, a longitudinal predictor, their interaction, a level-2 variable with a cross-level interaction, and three additional level-2 covariates, assuming random intercepts. Simulations yielded greater than 95% power to detect an effect of .2.

The enriched longitudinal target of n = 400 per arm, with approximately 100 participants at each of 3, 6, 9, and 12 months since onset, was set to provide adequate representation across the range of compounds, practices, contexts, and symptom presentations expected among participants with recent and ongoing difficulties.

Amendments will be approved by the Emory University IRB, logged on the OSF page, and noted in any publication bearing on affected data. Study materials, such as the protocol, assessment batteries, measure documentation, codebooks, and analysis code, will be deposited openly in the study's Open Science Framework repository, with analysis code posted at the time of each associated publication. Requests for access to qualitative material will be considered individually, subject to institutional review board approval, an executed data use agreement, and confirmation that the proposed use falls within the scope of participant consent. Requests may be directed to the corresponding author.

Discussion

Significance

AEs can occur after both psychedelics and meditation, but their nature is not well understood clinically, hampering efforts to measure, prevent, and treat negative impacts. Current detection paradigms underspecify subjective, SERT, perceptual, self-related, and interpersonal attributes, despite their reported importance to individuals with lived experience of AEs (Argyri et al., 2025; Palitsky et al., 2024a Palitsky et al., , 2024b)). Thus, data that can support an empirically grounded, clinically useful understanding of AEs are needed. This study addresses that need by generating a large, multi-method, longitudinal dataset characterizing these psychedelic-and meditationrelated AEs in detail. Complementing qualitative work that has documented AEs falling outside standard clinical-trial detection paradigms (Lindahl et al., 2017; Argyri et al., 2025), it is designed to support the identification of clinically meaningful presentations of psychedelic-and meditation-related difficulties, examination of contributing factors, and detection of factors associated with improvement, persistence, or deterioration, analyses that will be reported in the pre-registered series described above. It further enables the examination of symptom cooccurrence patterns, which can allow for the detection of phenotypes related to psychedelic and meditation AEs, a goal that current AE dictionaries such as the CTC-AE_5 and MedDRA do not readily accommodate.

Difficulties following psychedelics and meditation are sometimes described as elements of an ultimately favorable trajectory, in which challenging experiences carry latent transformative benefits and ultimately yield improvement for those who experience them (Statharakos et al., 2025). This study will generate evidence bearing on trajectories that contribute to benefit as well as to harm, which can allow for an empirical test of the "it gets worse before it gets better" adage. However, rather than categorizing difficulties into harmful or beneficial, the primary focus of this research is to bolster the development of frameworks for supporting individuals who experience AEs, providing care seekers and clinicians with evidence to reduce harm and to support recovery and growth.

The comparison of psychedelic-and meditation-related difficulties across the two arms may help clarify how deliberate practices and psychoactive compounds might differentially relate to subsets of AEs in what is otherwise an often-overlapping corpus (Palitsky et al., 2024b).

Nevertheless, the observational design precludes attributing differences to pharmacological action specifically. A clearer account of convergence and divergence will support better detection and response in both contexts.

Implementation challenges

Several operational considerations bear on execution. The principal challenge is recruiting and enriching the sample to meet the 50% target for recent and ongoing difficulties.

Although the study uses an enriched convenience sample, the varied legal status of psychedelics and the often-private nature of meditation practice may produce uneven recruitment rates between arms. Identifying participants whose difficulties began within the past twelve months requires rigorous screening, as many individuals do not categorize their experiences as adverse until considerable time has passed.

The reliance on multiple digital platforms introduces specific considerations for data quality and retention. Given the advertised compensation and an online survey ecosystem saturated with automated and disingenuous responding, the study implements live video screening calls with all prospective participants. Participant burden is also substantial: the baseline survey is estimated at approximately 90-120 minutes. Participants are invited to take breaks and resume at their convenience, and those who do not complete are contacted up to three times to identify barriers and provide reminders.

Longitudinal attrition presents a related risk. With a twelve-month duration and six bimonthly follow-ups, the study is powered to withstand 40% attrition. The team must nonetheless manage the possibility that participants with the highest levels of functional impairment may be the most likely to discontinue, which would bias longitudinal findings; this is partially addressed through personalized contact to identify barriers to completion. Fabla's design has been purposely built to maintain engagement for long duration studies. A virtual avatar, Fabio, acts as a companion throughout the study nudging and encouraging the participant to complete entries. Preliminary evidence from multiple studies using the app supports that participants really like the avatar element, which paired with additional gamification elements aims to reduce study fatigue. Additionally, the platform enables study coordinators to actively monitor participants and send push notification nudges if entries are being missed. Lastly, a preliminary study comparing Fabla to a text-entry based EMA method found Fabla three times preferable over typed method (Kaplan et al., 2025).

Limitations

Several limitations attend the design. The first is self-report and retrospective bias: participants may not report accurately on current psychological phenomena (Paulhus & Vazire, 2007), and recollection is influenced by present experience (Ross, 1989).

Longitudinal data collection somewhat reduces retrospective and recall bias, though self-report bias remains. The second is the observational design: participants are not randomized, precluding causal inference.

The inclusion of a comparator arm helps distinguish effects associated with meditation from those associated with psychedelics, but does not establish causation.

Generalizability is limited linguistically and culturally. Restriction to English-speaking participants in three countries limits inference to other cultural or global contexts in which these practices are common. There is little evidence that the effects of psychedelics, or related AEs, are uniform across cultures, and such conclusions should not be drawn from these findings.

Moreover, the enriched sampling design of this study limits inferences about the prevalence of psychedelic-and meditation-related challenges at a population level. Given this sampling strategy, it must be emphasized that participants cannot be taken as representative of psychedelic users or meditators in general.A final limitation is temporal scope. Although one year exceeds the follow-up period of prior studies of psychedelic-or meditation-related challenges, and this study includes more longitudinal assessments than any to date, a one-year window nonetheless restricts interpretation at longer timescales. Longer follow-up would be required to capture the full trajectory of enduring difficulties.

Risks to completion

The principal risk is low enrollment or high attrition. This is mitigated through a coordinated and flexible recruitment and retention strategy: all research partners contribute to recruitment, progress is monitored regularly, and compensation or procedures are adapted as needed. Participants are clearly informed of study expectations, encouraged to commit to completion, and supported through reminders and follow-ups led by dedicated study personnel.

Recruitment is further strengthened by established networks in the psychedelic and contemplative research and practice communities and by structured compensation with bonuses for longitudinal completion. The design accounts for moderate-to-high attrition, permits extension or targeted external recruitment if required, and places key measures early in the battery so that core data are retained even where completion is partial.

Ethics and safety

Ethical approval has been granted by the Emory University Institutional Review Board (#2025P011692). All participants provide informed consent prior to enrollment.

Because the study involves reporting the use of substances that are currently illegal in several countries, the requirement for written documentation of consent has been waived, on the grounds that a signed consent document would constitute the principal record linking participants to the study and would therefore represent the primary risk of breach of confidentiality.

Identifiable information, including email addresses used for longitudinal follow-up, is stored on encrypted servers at Emory University and linked to survey data only via a study identifier. Speech data collected via Fabla are securely and automatically transmitted through HTTPS requests to Emory University's Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA)-eligible Amazon Web Services (AWS) infrastructure. Participants retain the ability to review their recordings via the app at all times, and can autonomously choose to delete past voice recordings from the app as well as from research records.

Recruiting a population characterized by distress or impairment requires careful ethical management. The informed consent process and the initial video screening call are designed to give participants clear information and an opportunity to ask questions before beginning the survey, and to establish a direct point of contact with the study team. Participants may skip any item, pause and resume the survey, or withdraw at any point without penalty.

Study status

At the time of writing, approximately 200 of the targeted 800 cross-sectional surveys have been completed in the psychedelic arm, and approximately 60 of the targeted 800 in the meditation arm. Enrollment into the longitudinal follow-up is underway in both arms. N = 9 interviews have been completed for the interview arm of the study.

As of the present writing (October 2026), no results have been generated, and no analyses have been undertaken.

Participant recruitment and data collection both began in summer 2025; recruitment is expected to be completed by the end of 2028, data collection is expected to be completed by the end of 2029, and initial results are expected by the end of 2028.

Dissemination

Results will be published in peer-reviewed journals and shared with participating communities, including the peer-support, advocacy, and educational organizations that contributed formative input to the study's design. Given the community-engaged origins of several study components, findings will be returned to those communities in accessible formats rather than through academic publication alone.

Beyond the analyses reported by the study team, de-identified quantitative data and study materials will be made available to the broader research community under the tiered access model described above, so that other investigators may build on the dataset in future work on psychedelic-and meditation-related challenges. Access to qualitative and audio data is more restricted, reflecting the re-identification risks these data carry.

Registered reports and pre-registrations arising from the study will be posted to the OSF repository as described above.
