Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. Abstract

Background Age-related hearing loss is the leading sensory deficit in older adults, yet audiometric thresholds at conventional frequencies often poorly predict speech understanding.

Two competing hypotheses have emerged: extended high-frequency (eHF) hearing loss beyond 8 kHz may unmask variance in speech performance, while cochlear synaptopathy, inferred via auditory brainstem response (ABR) wave I amplitude reduction, may degrade temporal coding independent of audiometry. This study aims to identify the best predictors of speech in noise deficits in the general population.

Methods This is a cross-sectional analysis of 526 ears from 263 tinnitus-free adults (178 [67.7%] women and 85 men based on self-reported gender) enrolled in the Swedish Tinnitus Outreach Project, a population-based cohort study. Participants underwent air-conduction audiometry from 0.5 to 16 kHz, loudness discomfort levels, distortion product otoacoustic emissions, click-evoked ABR, and standardized speech-in-noise and word recognition testing in a clinical setting. Their associations with age and speech outcomes were evaluated using multivariable regression models.

Results eHF pure-tone average was the most age-sensitive auditory metric, accounting for 64% of age-related variance, compared with 16% for PTA4 (the pure tone average of audiometric thresholds at 0.5, 1, 2, 4 kHz). eHF PTA thresholds (pure-tone average at 10-16 kHz) predicted speech-in-noise performance substantially better than PTA4, otoacoustic emissions, or ABR measures. ABR wave I amplitude did not significantly contribute to speech outcomes, even in high-reliability recordings (ICC = 0.96), whereas prolonged wave I latency was associated with poorer word recognition, particularly in older adults. In young adults, delayed wave I latency minimally affected word recognition, but in older adults an increase in latency from approximately 1.0 to 1.8 ms was associated with about a 27-percentage-point reduction in word recognition scores.

These findings identify eHF audiometry as a dominant clinical predictor of both cochlear aging and speech outcomes, and challenge the translational relevance of ABR-based synaptopathy markers in heterogeneous human populations, with implications for hearing assessment, clinical trials, and early intervention targeting in aging cohorts.

Introduction

Hearing loss is typically defined as a loss of >20 dB hearing levels (HL) at the pure tone average of audiometric thresholds of 0.5, 1, 2, 4 kHz (PTA4) [1]. Based on this gold-standard, hearing loss affects over 1.5 billion people globally, with prevalence rising steeply after age 50. Between 1990 and 2019, global moderate-to-complete hearing loss increased 79.1%, from 225.3 million to 403.3 million, with projections suggesting 2.45 billion affected by 2050 [1]. Beyond auditory function, hearing loss has emerged as a potentially modifiable risk factor for cognitive decline and dementia [2]. Cross-sectional studies show strong associations of mild cognitive impairment evidencing impaired functional and anatomical connectivity once corrected for β-Amyloid deposition [3]. Population-based cohort studies reveal that hearing loss associates with incident cognitive impairment and dementia [4]. However, in systematic reviews and meta-analyses that have evidenced a strong relationship between cognitive impairment or dementia, 80% of the studies limited their auditory assessment to 4 kHz [5]. Despite its clinical importance, the PTA4 poorly predicts real-world communication ability and speech understanding in challenging environments [6]. In addition, clinical pure-tone audiometry (up to 8 kHz) explains limited variance in word recognition and speech-in-noise (SPiN) performance, leaving clinicians unable to accurately predict who will struggle most with communication [7]. As a consequence, hearing loss is often detected only once communication is impacted, leading to a delay of near 10 years until interventions (e.g., hearing aids) occur [8], although these could potentially prevent cognitive decline [9].

Difficulties in understanding speech may occur in presence of a normal audiogram. Early detection of SPiN deficits may thus contribute to earlier interventions for hearing and cognitive interventions [10]. Interestingly, recent work shows that eHF pure-tone averages (10) (11) (12) (13) (14) (15) (16) robustly predict word and phoneme recognition in challenging acoustic environments [11]. Since cochlear damage from aging, noise exposure or ototoxicity preferentially affects the cochlear base, where frequencies above 8 kHz are encoded [12], measurement of extended highfrequency (eHF) thresholds (> 8 kHz) may reveal clinically relevant cochlear pathology that conventional PTA misses. Recent studies have suggested a contribution of cochlear synaptopathy (CS) to "hidden hearing loss" and SPiN deficits [13] [14] [15]. CS derives from preclinical work showing that moderate noise exposure can selectively destroy high-threshold, lowspontaneous-rate auditory nerve fibers at the inner hair cell synapse without elevating auditory brainstem thresholds, albeit decreasing the auditory brainstem response (ABR) Wave 1 amplitude of the auditory brainstem response [16] [17] [18]. This synaptic pathology theoretically persists even after threshold recovery, potentially explaining deficits in speech understanding in noisy environments despite normal or near-normal quiet thresholds. Post-mortem histopathology of human temporal bones found cochlear nerve degeneration to be associated with poorer word recognition performance [19]. Likewise, in young healthy individuals with normal hearing thresholds up to 16 kHz, those with lower ABR Wave I amplitudes had poorer performance on word recognition in noise or with time compression and reverberation [20], a correlation supported by an independent study from Bramhall et al. [21]. However, the validity of the ABR Wave I amplitude as a biomarker for CS and/or SPiN has been debated [22, 23]. Thus, whether a reduction in Wave I amplitude may serve a noninvasive proxy of CS or SPiN in humans remains to be established.

While prior studies have examined eHF [11], DPOAE [24, 25], or ABR markers [21] in isolation, or in small [26, 27], highly selected samples [20], the present analysis is, to my knowledge, the first to directly address these two hypotheses against one another: eHF hearing loss versus ABR Wave I amplitude as an index of SPiN in a large, unselected general population cohort with comprehensive, quality-controlled audiometric and electrophysiological data. The analysis relies on data from the Swedish Tinnitus Outreach Project (STOP) but focuses on healthy adults without tinnitus in order to convey a clearer understanding of the relationship between these features and SPiN in absence of tinnitus. Here, eHF pure-tone audiometry (10-16 kHz), highreliability ABR recordings with rigorous quality control, DPOAEs and speech recognition measures (word and phoneme recognition in noise) were investigated. Classical multivariate statistical modeling was used to assess the independent explanatory power of eHF thresholds vs ABR markers after accounting for conventional audiometry and demographic factors.

Materials and Methods

Ethics statement

The study was approved by the Regional Ethics Review Board in Stockholm (Dnr: 2016/1468-31/2); all participants provided written informed consent.

Study Population and design

This report is a cross-sectional analysis of data collected at a single clinical assessment visit.

Adult participants (>18 years old) were drawn from the Swedish Tinnitus Outreach Project (STOP) [28], a population-based cohort recruited 2016-2020 via the LifeGene national health study [29]. As a result, the cohort naturally includes a substantial proportion of tinnitus-free adults who volunteered as part of the broader population-based study. LifeGene invited all of their participants (N = 26,282) to join STOP, of whom 5,593 completed the STOP survey (2,588 reported no tinnitus, 1,131 occasional and 1,466 constant tinnitus, 403 were uncertain and 5 did not answer). Tinnitus-free participants (no tinnitus on any occasion, confirmed at the clinical session) were selected for this analysis to avoid confounding of ABR and audiometric measures by tinnitus-related central changes [28, 30]. The prevalence of tinnitus in the LifeGene cohort and the characterization of these participants has been previously reported [31] [32] [33]. Compared with tinnitus participants in STOP [28], the tinnitus-free subsample did not differ significantly in age (p = 0.21), gender ratio (p = 0.44), or conventional audiometric thresholds (PTA4, p = 0.19), suggesting they are broadly representative of the wider STOP population in the domains relevant to this study. Participants did not receive any financial compensation.

The participant flow diagram is reported in Figure 1. The present analysis included 263 participants (N = 526 ears) who (1) reported no tinnitus (neither occasional nor constant) (2) completed audiometry from 0.125-16 kHz, (3) completed speech-in-noise testing (N = 511 ears); click ABR was recorded in a subset of 168 participants (N = 336 ears). All were Swedish residents, and comprised 87% individuals with Swedish origin. Participants were highly educated (75% University level) and none were hearing aid or cochlear implant users. The ESIT screening questionnaire [34, 35] was added to the platform in November 2018 and was answered by a subset of participants (n = 32 missing). This allowed to determine that none were diagnosed for the following ear dysfunctions: ear barotrauma, Ménière's Disease, Acoustic Neuroma, and Otosclerosis. Less than 2% of the participants had acoustic trauma, chronic otitis, or sudden hearing loss). Chemotherapy and head and neck radiotherapy was reported by 5 participants.

Prevalence of other diseases is reported in Table S1.

Gender was self-reported in the STOP survey using item 2 of the Tinnitus Sample Case History Questionnaire (TSCHQ; response options male/female) for all 263 participants (178 women, 85 men) [36]. Sex assigned at birth was additionally recorded with item A2 of the ESIT-SQ [34] for 231 participants and agreed with self-reported gender in 226. Because the TSCHQ item does not distinguish gender identity from sex assigned at birth, analyses refer to self-reported gender, and no inference on sex-linked biological mechanisms is made. Study size was determined by the number of eligible tinnitus-free participants who completed the clinical assessment during the recruitment period. No a priori sample-size calculation was performed for the present analysis.

Sex and Gender Equity in Research (SAGER) and Strengthening the Reporting of Observational

Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines were used to ensure clear, transparent and complete reporting [37, 38].

Audiometry and otoacoustic emissions

Consenting tinnitus and non-tinnitus participants located in the Stockholm area or Skåne and who had answered large tinnitus survey [28] were invited for a battery of auditory measurements in a clinical setting. For external validity, auditory measurements were performed on 927 participants between August 2016 and December 2019 at three different sites: two different hospital audiology clinics in Stockholm (Karolinska Sjukhuset or KS Rosenlund, n = 778; and KS Solna, n = 120) and at the Department for Logopedics, Phoniatrics, and Audiology at Lund University (n = 29).

For internal validity, the auditory measures and ABRs were collected by four licensed audiologists.

The session started with a short interview, confirming the participants' tinnitus statuses, and otoscopy and tympanometry (Madsen OTOflex 100 tympanometer, Otometrics; #8-04-04900, GN Resound AB, Göteborg) was performed. The DPOAEs were measured using the Madsen Capella Speech-in-noise (SPiN) testing was performed using the clinical standard method and material in Sweden, presenting 50 CNC-words in +4dB SNR per ear at a level judged comfortable by the participants [39]. Participants repeated back isolated words (WS; 50 key words) and individual phonemes (PS; 3 phonemes per word, accuracy scored by consonant clusters). WS was quantified as the percentage of words in noise repeated correctly, and PS was estimated as percentage of phonemes in noise repeated correctly. Both measures were converted to percent correct (0-100%), where higher scores indicate better performance.

The LDLs were assessed after SIN at all threshold frequencies. A 1.5-second pure tone was presented at 60 dB HL, increased by 5 dB, and presented again. The subject was instructed to inform the tester if the tone presented would have been uncomfortable to listen to for a longer period (1-2 minutes). The session concluded with measurements of the ABR, detailed below.

Auditory Brainstem Response (ABR)

Two setups for the ABR recordings were used in this study, the Madsen EP200 Chartr (ICS Chartr EP 200, Otometrics; #8-04-12701, GN Resound AB, Göteborg) and the Eclipse (Interacoustics, Stockholm) with ER-3A insert earphones and gold-wrapped tip trodes. The settings for both systems were identical, with high and low pass filters of .1 and 3 kHz, respectively, with 100 µs click stimuli of alternating polarity presented at 9.1 clicks/s at 90 dBnHL through insert earphones, with contralateral masking of -40 dB relative to the stimulus ear. Each recording consisted of 2,000 accepted clicks. The participants were relaxed in a reclined position in a dimly lit room during the recording. An audiologist identified relevant ABR features, i.e., wave I, III, and V peaks and troughs, then double-checked and, if necessary, corrected by two independent licensed audiologists blinded to the age of the participant. Amplitude was defined as peak to trough. In the present study, 190 non tinnitus ears were collected using the Chartr EP (Otometrics) and 146 ears with the Eclipse (Interacoustics). ABR Waves (N = 331, n = 5 missing) were classified as having good morphology (n = 151, 46%), fair morphology (n = 31, 9%), no clear wave forms for waves I (n = 2, 1%), III (n = 2, 1%), V (n = 4, 1%), and poor morphology (n = 141, 43%). In our previous study [28], test-retest reliability was established for these two devices: ICC for latencies 0.77-0.96 (good-to-excellent), ICC for amplitudes 0.26-0.47 (poor) on Chartr, 0.18-0.48 (poor) for Wave III and V but 0.96 (excellent) for Wave I amplitude on Eclipse.

Statistical analysis

Age-related trajectories were modeled for all audiometric, OAE, speech, and ABR measures using separate polynomial regressions with age as the predictor and each variable [Pure-tone average at 0.5, 1, 2, and 4 kHz (PTA4), eHF pure-tone average at 10-16 kHz, DPOAE amplitude at 5,273 Hz, Word Score (WS), Phoneme Score (PS), Wave I amplitude, Wave I latency, Wave III latency, Wave V latency] as the outcome. For each measure, linear, quadratic, and cubic age models with age mean-centered were fitted, and the best functional form using hierarchical likelihood ratio tests (LRT) were selected in a sequential testing framework: linear models were compared to quadratic models, and if the quadratic term was significant (p < 0.05), quadratic models were compared to cubic models. The polynomial order was selected based on this stepwise LRT approach rather than AIC, to prioritize biological interpretability and avoid overfitting [40]. R² , LRT p-values comparing nested models, and the selected polynomial order for each relationship were reported. Wave I amplitude analyses were restricted to the Eclipse subset, whereas all other variables used the full cohort. The best-fitting age functions from these models were then used to generate the smooth age trajectories.

Univariate linear regression models were constructed with WS or PS as the outcome and audiometric (PTA4, eHF PTA, OAE), or ABR (Wave I amplitude, Wave I latency, Wave III latency, Wave V latency) predictors separately, using centered continuous predictors. Polynomial terms for each predictor were selected using the same hierarchical LRT approach (a = 0.05), testing linear vs. quadratic, then quadratic vs. cubic forms sequentially. Models were evaluated using R² , LRT p-values, and parameter estimates (b, SE, t, p), ensuring consistency in polynomial selection across univariate and multivariable frameworks [40]. Covariates were chosen a priori on subjectmatter grounds rather than by data-driven variable selection: age and gender were treated as potential confounders, auditory measures were entered in prespecified blocks, and data-driven selection was limited to the polynomial order of each predictor. The Age x Gender interaction on WS and PS was examined in an exploratory model including age, age 2 and gender. For the multivariable analysis predicting WS and PS, a prespecified four-phase hierarchical modeling strategy was followed, with each phase compared with the previous one using nested-model F tests: Multicollinearity was assessed via variance inflation factors (VIF < 5 for all models). Robust heteroscedasticity-consistent standard errors (HC3) from the sandwich-car framework were used because Breusch-Pagan tests indicated variance heterogeneity (studentized test for the final latency models: WS, χ²(17) = 35.04, p = 0.006; PS, χ²(17) = 43.49, p < 0.001), and HC3-based inferences are reported for all regression coefficients [41] [42] [43]. Model fit was assessed using adjusted R² , and diagnostic residual analyses in the complete-case subset (n = 298 ears), with sensitivity checks confirming that exclusion of extreme outliers did not materially change the pattern of significant predictors. Statistical significance was defined as a = 0.05 (two-tailed), and p-values are reported in scientific notation for values < 0.0001 in the main and supplementary tables.

Unit of analysis, missing data and sensitivity analyses.

The unit of analysis was the ear, and both ears of a participant were entered as separate observations when available. Because HC3 standard errors do not account for the correlation between the two ears of a participant (between-ear correlation for WS, r = 0.63), the final multivariable models were re-estimated with participant-clustered jackknife (CR3) robust standard errors with Satterthwaite small-sample degrees of freedom [44, 45]. A further sensitivity analysis added recording device as a covariate to the latency models. Missing data were not imputed: each analysis used all ears with non-missing values for the variables involved, and the final multivariable models were fitted to ears with complete data on all predictors and outcomes (complete-case subset, n = 298 ears from 163 participants, 135 of whom contributed both ears).

Age groups for descriptive stratification were defined a priori in 10-year bands (<35, 35-44, 45-54, 55-64, 65-74 and ≥75 years). For the WS-PS slope comparison, the complete-case subset was dichotomized at its median age (46 years).

All analyses were performed in R version 4.4.0 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing) using the tidyverse (v2.0.0) for data wrangling, car (v3.1-2) for regression diagnostics and HC3 standard errors, sandwich (v3.0-2) for robust variance estimation, clubSandwich for cluster-robust (CR3) inference, broom for model summaries, and ggplot2 (v4.0.0) with patchwork (v1.2.0) for figure making.

Results

Cohort characteristics

The STOP cohort comprised 526 ears from 263 participants aged 20 to 84 years (mean ± SD: 49.2 ± 13.6 years; Table 1), comprising 178 women (67.7%) and 85 men (32.3%). The study population was overall highly educated and with low disease prevalence (Table S1). Participants were measured in a clinical environment with a sound proof booth, with EC marked clinical devices.

Overall, the cohort exhibited clinically normal hearing thresholds (cut-off 20 dB HL) in the conventional frequency range (PTA₄ 0.5-4 kHz: 5.2 ± 10.7 dB HL), which is the clinical goldstandard used by the WHO for classifying hearing impairment severity [1]. In contrast, substantial variability was found in eHF hearing loss (eHF PTA 10-16 kHz: 36.5 ± 22.5 dB HL, range: -12.2 to 78.0 dB HL). Loudness Discomfort Levels (LDLs) plateaued near ceiling values of the medical device (75.6-93.9 dB HL). Distortion Products of Otoacoustic Emissions (DPOAEs), a measure of outer hair cell (OHC) health and function, at 5.273 kHz averaged 19.8 ± 10.1 dB signal to noise ratio (SNR). Speech perception performance (ranging from 0 to 100% accuracy) was tested on 511 ears with hearing abilities in the standard speech weighted noise (+4 dB SNR) was robust, with mean word scores (WS) of 75.1 ± 10.3% and phoneme scores (PS) of 89.2 ± 6.0%. Auditory brainstem response measurements (available for 336 ears; Table 1, Fig. 1) were performed on two different medical devices: the Chartr EP (Otometrics) and the Eclipse (Interacoustics). In our former study, test-retest reliability was moderate (ICC>0.5) for all Wave latencies in both devices, but poor (ICC<0.5) for all Wave amplitudes, with the exception of the Eclipse Wave I amplitudes being excellent (>0.9) [28]. As a consequence, only Wave I amplitudes for the Eclipse are reported (0.4 ± 0.2 µV, n = 140 ears), and the Wave latencies (from both devices) were 1.4 ± 0.2 ms for Wave I, 3.6 ± 0.2 ms for Wave III, and 5.4 ± 0.4 ms for Wave V. The I-V interwave latency was 4.0 ± 0.3 ms (Table 1). Audiometry and OAEs were available for all 526 ears (263 participants), speech scores for 511 ears (257 participants), ABR latencies for 321-324 ears (167-168 participants) and Eclipse Wave I amplitude for 140 ears (72 participants); 298 ears from 163 participants had complete data for the final multivariable models (Fig. 1).

Auditory performance declines with age

Age-stratified analyses revealed progressive deterioration across auditory domains (Fig. 2a, Figure S1, Table S2): eHF thresholds worsened from 13.8 ± 15.2 dB HL in participants <35 years to 69.0 ± 6.2 dB HL in those ≥75 years. In contrast, PTA4 remained within normal range according to WHO standards (cut-off = 20 dB HL), even in the older group (≥75 years: 18.5 ±11.3 dB HL).

LDLs had near ceiling effects and tolerance to loud sounds at 8 kHz increased with age (Fig. 2b).

Both PTA and LDLs showed device limitations in the eHF with maximal dB HL values achieved for 10 kHz (93 dB HL); 12.5 kHz (85 dB HL); 4 kHz (75 dB HL) and 16 kHz (55.5 dB HL). DPOAEs (hereafter referred to as OAEs), showed also an age-progressive decline, most evident for the 5.273 kHz, with a 24.5 ± 8.4 dB SNR in participants <35 years to 7.9 ± 10.1 dB SNR in those ≥75 years (Fig. 2c). WS and PS declined from 78.3 ± 6.5% to 55.6 ± 19.4%, and 90.9 ± 3.7% to 77.8 ± 12.3% respectively, across the same age ranges (Fig. 2d,e The mean and standard error of the mean (SEM) latency values obtained from the two devices using equal settings are reported for each age group (Fig. 2g-i). Nonlinear Relationships Between Auditory Function and Aging

Guided by the non-linear, age-stratified patterns in Fig. 2, each audiometric and physiological variable were modeled as a function of age using univariate polynomial regression (linear vs. quadratic vs. cubic fits; Fig. 3, Table S5). Consistent with a former study from Bugannim et al. [6], PTA₄ showed a modest but significant quadratic increase with age (R² = 0.158, quadratic p = 0.0057). In contrast, eHF PTA exhibited a much steeper age-related elevation with high overall fit (R² = 0.641) but no additional improvement from adding a quadratic term (quadratic p = 0.548), indicating an approximately linear worsening of eHF thresholds across the adult lifespan (Fig. 3a,b), corroborating former work from Motlagh Zadeh et al. [11]. OAE amplitude declined with age in a curvilinear fashion (R² = 0.237, quadratic p = 0.009; Fig. 3c), while both WS and PS showed pronounced accelerating decline captured by strong quadratic components (WS: R² = 0.296, quadratic p <0.0001; PS: R² = 0.283, quadratic p <0.0001), confirming an inverted-U relationship published by Bugannim et al. [6], whereby speech perception performance initially improves with age before plateauing or declining (Fig. 3d,e). The Age 2 coefficients were negative for both outcomes (WS: β = -0.013, p < 0.001; PS: β = -0.0077, p < 0.001), indicating that gains diminish progressively across the lifespan (Table S6). Gender exerted only marginal effects on speech perception. When age was included in the model, women performed modestly better than men (WS: +1.82 points, p = 0.034; PS: +1.00 point, p = 0.05), though the gender effect on phoneme scores was marginally significant. Critically, the Age x Gender interaction was not significant for either outcome (WS: p = 0.271; PS: p = 0.067), providing no clear evidence that age-related trajectories of speech perception differed between women and men (Table S6), although the study was not powered to detect such interactions. Among ABR measures, Wave I amplitude (Eclipse only) decreased with age but was adequately described by a linear fit (R² = 0.195, quadratic p = 0.893, Fig. 3f), whereas Wave I, III and V latencies all displayed statistically significant quadratic age effects (Wave I: R² = 0.153, p = 0.007; Wave III: R² = 0.102, p = 0.048; Wave V: R² = 0.127, p = 0.028), consistent with subtly increased delay at older ages (Fig. 3g-i, Table S5). These polynomial fits confirm that several key predictors (PTA₄, OAE, WS/PS, and brainstem latencies) exhibit non-linear age relationships, providing the rationale for the subsequent univariate polynomial analyses of WS and PS and for including higher-order terms only where formally justified in the multivariable models.

Influence of audiometric measures on speech perception.

To directly answer the primary research question and identify which peripheral measures best predict SPiN outcomes in this population cohort, a hierarchical multivariable regression model was constructed, informed on the preceding univariate analyses (Figures 2 3) that established the agerelated trajectories of each measure and their bivariate relationships with speech outcomes, ultimately providing the justification for predictor selection and polynomial terms in the final models.

To optimize model specification, whether audiometric and physiological predictors exhibited nonlinear relationships with speech perception outcomes was systematically evaluated using likelihood ratio testing (LRT), fitting univariate polynomial models (linear, quadratic, cubic) for each predictor against word score (WS) and phoneme score (PS). EHF pure-tone average (eHF PTA, 10-16 kHz) was the single strongest peripheral predictor of speech performance (Fig. 4, Table S7): cubic models explained substantially more variance in WS than conventional PTA₄ (R² = 0.345 vs. 0.221; cubic p = 0.0014 for eHF PTA, p = 0.047 for PTA₄; Fig. 4a,b), with an even larger advantage for PS (eHF PTA R² = 0.361 vs. PTA₄ R² = 0.247; eHF PTA cubic p = 0.0001, PTA₄ quadratic p <0.0001; Fig. 4g,h). Both audiometric measures showed non-linear relationships with speech outcomes (cubic for eHF PTA; cubic for PTA₄ with WS and quadratic with PS), consistent with non-linear effects of hearing loss on speech perception rather than a simple monotonic decline.

Critically, eHF PTA outperformed PTA₄ by 12.4 percentage points for WS (R² difference = 0.124) and 11.4 percentage points for PS (DR2 = 0.114), indicating that eHF thresholds capture speechrelevant cochlear damage that is only partly reflected in conventional audiometry. Together with the steeper age-related deterioration of eHF PTA compared to PTA₄ (Fig. 3a,b), this pattern supports the clinical added value of incorporating eHF measures into routine assessment. In contrast, OAE amplitude at 5.273 kHz exhibited a linear relationship for WS and cubic for PS (WS: R² = 0.196, linear p < 0.0001; PS: R² = 0.219, cubic p < 0.0001; Figure S2, Table S7), indicating a roughly proportional decrement in speech scores as OHC function decreases. These findings suggest that, in this cohort and listening condition (+4 dB SNR), eHF is sufficient to index the cochlear pathology that matters for speech, and OHC-specific measures offer limited incremental value for prediction beyond eHF PTA; consistently, none of the OAE terms was significant in the multivariable models (Table S9). The corresponding relationships between WS/PS and ABR measures are illustrated in Fig. 4c-f,i-l 4f,l, Table S7). Together, these polynomial fits indicate that eHF PTA is a dominant, at least in part, predictor of speech outcomes in this cohort, accounting for 34-36% of univariate variance in speech scores (substantially more than conventional audiometry or any ABR measure), yet leaving the majority of variance (~65%) unexplained by peripheral auditory measures alone. The strong cubic relationships for eHF PTA and Wave III latency justify the use of higher-order terms in subsequent multivariable models, in which eHF PTA, OAE and Wave III latency entered with cubic terms, PTA₄ with a quadratic term, and Wave I and V latency as linear terms.

Incremental Contributions of Hearing Loss, ABR, and Interactions to Speech Models

Blockwise model progression analyses confirmed that each predictor domain made a significant incremental contribution to explaining variance in speech outcomes (Table S8). For WS, adding hearing loss measures to an age/gender baseline model increased R² from 0.23 to 0.29 (DR2 = 0.064, p = 0.0016), with further gains when ABR latencies (R² = 0.34, DR2 = 0.049, p = 0.0011) and the Age x Wave I latency interaction (R² = 0.36, DR2 = 0.022, p = 0.0021) were included. PS showed a similar pattern but with larger improvements from hearing loss and ABR blocks: R² rose from 0.24 (age/gender only) to 0.33 with hearing loss (DR2 = 0.092, p = 9.7×10⁻⁶), to 0.40 with ABR latencies (DR2 = 0.067, p = 1.5×10⁻⁵), and to 0.42 after adding the interaction (DR2 = 0.016, p = 0.006). These stepwise gains indicate that peripheral hearing loss, brainstem timing, and age-dependent interactions each explain unique components of speech variance beyond demographics alone. In the Eclipse-only sequence (n = 122 ears), adding Wave I amplitude (WS: DR2 = 0.002, p = 0.57; PS: DR2 = 0.003, p = 0.44) and the Age x Wave I amplitude interaction (WS and PS: DR 2 < 0.001, p ≥ 0.69) did not improve model fit (Table S8).

In contrast, PS showed only linear age effects without significant quadratic acceleration (age 2 b = -0.0041, 95% CI -0.009 to 0.001, p = 0.113; Table S9). Specifically, with other predictors held at their mean, the model-implied WS decline was approximately 0.1 percentage points per year at age 50 and 0.6 percentage points per year at age 75, whereas the corresponding PS declines were smaller (approximately 0.04 and 0.25 points per year) and the quadratic term was not significant. This differential trajectory indicates that complex linguistic processing (word recognition) is more age-vulnerable than basic acoustic perception (phoneme identification).

Multivariable polynomial regression models identified distinct predictor patterns for word versus phoneme recognition. Unstandardized coefficients showed that, contrary to predictions from the cochlear synaptopathy hypothesis [16] [17] [18] whereby lower Wave I amplitude is associated with speech recognition deficits [15, 20], Wave I amplitude, the putative neural marker of cochlear inner hair cell-auditory nerve synapse loss, showed no significant relationship to either WS (b = 2.35, 95% CI -7.81 to 12.52, p = 0.647) or PS (b = 2.28, 95% CI -3.95 to 8.51, p = 0.470; Fig. 5b, Table S10) in the Eclipse-only model (n = 122 ears from 68 participants), where Wave I amplitude reliability is excellent (ICC = 0.96) [28]. Furthermore, the Age x Wave I amplitude interaction was not significant for either outcome (WS: b = -0.110, 95% CI -1.386 to 1.165, p = 0.864; PS: b = 0.134, 95% CI -0.705 to 0.972, p = 0.752; Fig. 5b, Table 2). Collectively, these results indicate that ABR Wave I amplitude shows no detectable association with word or phoneme recognition in presence of background noise, even in the highest-quality, most reliable recording subset. In striking contrast, Wave I Latency that reflects more auditory nerve conduction velocity rather than neural synchrony demonstrated a predictive value for WS but not PS (WS: b = -9.92, 95% CI -19.40 to -0.45, p = 0.040; PS: b = -4.93, 95% CI -11.01 to 1.15, p = 0.112; Fig. 5b, Table S9).

Each additional millisecond of Wave I latency was associated with approximately 10-point decline in Word Score and 5-point decline in Phoneme Score, with WS showing approximately twice the sensitivity to neural timing delays. Critically, the Age x Wave I latency interaction was substantially larger in magnitude for WS compared to PS (Fig. 5b). Specifically, WS showed a significant Age x Wave I latency interaction (b = -0.788, 95% CI -1.484 to -0.093, p = 0.027), indicating that older adults experience greater speech perception decline as Wave I latency increases (Fig. 5b, Table 2, Table S9). The PS interaction was not significant being substantially weaker in magnitude (b = -0.401, 95% CI -0.844 to 0.042, p = 0.076), with the WS age-interaction effect approximately 1.97 times the magnitude of the PS effect (coefficient ratio; Fig. 5b, Table 2, Table S9); the difference between outcomes was not formally tested. With participant-clustered (CR3) standard errors, the Age x Wave I latency interaction remained significant for WS (95% CI -1.51 to -0.06, p = 0.035) and non-significant for PS (p = 0.096), and Wave I amplitude remained unrelated to either outcome in the Eclipse-only model (WS: p = 0.65; PS: p = 0.50; Table S11). Adjustment for recording device did not alter the interaction (WS: b = -0.79, p = 0.027), although the Wave I latency main effect for WS became marginal (p = 0.061; Table S11). In the Eclipse-only model, the Age x Wave I latency estimate for WS was of similar magnitude (b = -0.83) but imprecise (95% CI -2.99 to 1.34, p = 0.450; Table S10). This dissociation suggests a differential age-dependent vulnerability: while both word and phoneme recognition are sensitive to peripheral neural transmission delays, the agedependent amplification of neural timing vulnerability is approximately twice as pronounced for word recognition, suggesting task-specific mechanisms of aging. These findings collectively suggest that delayed neural transmission (reflected in increased Wave I latency), rather than synapse loss (reflected in decreased amplitude), is more closely associated with age-related speech perception decline, although the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference. The selective age-amplification of the Wave I latency effect for word recognition (vs. phoneme recognition) indicates that complex linguistic processing may depend more heavily on the precision of early neural timing information than basic acoustic-phonetic processing.

Surprisingly, Wave III latency cubed, representing higher-order (cubic) nonlinearity in central auditory brainstem processing, significantly predicted both outcomes with large, positive effects (Fig. 5c, Table S9). Specifically, WS was predicted by Wave III Latency 3 (b = 81.78, 95% CI 11.07 to 152.50, p = 0.024), and PS was similarly predicted (b = 67.42, 95% CI 7.74 to 127.11, p = 0.027; S9; participant-clustered CR3: WS, p = 0.049; PS, p = 0.051; Table S11). With participant clustering, inference for this term rested on few effectively informative participants (Satterthwaite df ~ 11), so the cubic Wave III effect should be interpreted with caution. The comparable magnitude of Wave III Latency 3 effects between outcomes (difference in coefficients = 14.36 points, approximately 18% relative difference) contrasts sharply with the substantially differential Age x Wave I latency effects (coefficient ratio = 1.97-fold difference). This dissociation explains that higher-order (cubic) central auditory processing effects impact word and phoneme recognition similarly, indicating shared reliance on central auditory complexity, whereas, in contrast, the age-dependent neural timing vulnerability, specifically the age-amplified impact of peripheral wave initiation delays (Age x Wave I latency), shows outcome-specific vulnerability that is unique to word-level processing. This pattern suggests that aging selectively compromises mechanisms dependent on early neural precision (Wave I timing), whereas cubic nonlinearities in central processing (Wave III cubic term) are inherently task-invariant. Finally, Wave V latency showed no significant associations in the multivariable model (WS: b = -0.673, 95% CI -3.591 to 2.246, p = 0.650; PS: b = -0.335, 95% CI -1.831 to 1.161, p = 0.660), indicating that the most distal brainstem response does not provide independent predictive value when upstream measures (Wave I latency, Wave III latency) are included in the model (Table S9).

Age-Stratified Trajectories Reveal Selective Word Score Vulnerability

Predicted trajectories across four age strata (45, 55, 65, 75 years) reveal that while young adults (45y) maintain stable performance across Wave I latency values (WS predicted 74.7-81.7%, PS predicted 89.5-93.0%), older adults (75y) show marked declines as Wave I latency increases, with WS dropping to 56% and PS to 80% at the maximum observed Wave I latency (Fig. 5d, Table S12). This 27-percentage-point drop in WS (from 82.3% to 55.6%) versus 13-percentage-point drop in PS (from 93.8% to 80.4%) demonstrates that peripheral neural timing delay effects increase with advancing age, with word-level processing showing approximately 2.0 times greater vulnerability compared to phoneme recognition. The age-stratified trajectories visually demonstrate the Age x Wave I latency interaction. While young adults' performance remains relatively insensitive to Wave I latency across the observed range, older adults show substantially steeper slopes, indicating that age unmasks or amplifies the neural timing dependency of speech recognition.

Dissociation Between Word and Phoneme Recognition Across Age Groups

I tested whether word and phoneme scores show differential relationships with age and neural timing by constructing multivariable polynomial regression models with interaction terms. Despite high overall correlation (Pearson r = 0.934, p < 0.001), WS and PS show dissociable aging patterns and neural correlates with WS, but not PS, exhibiting accelerated age-related decline and ageamplified vulnerability to neural timing delays. Yet, both outcomes were equally driven by central processing measures as indicated by Wave III latencies (Fig. 5c). This dual-process profile suggests that word recognition is preferentially vulnerable to age-related neurobiological changes and early neural timing deficits, while phoneme recognition remains more robust to age-dependent mechanisms, despite both being sensitive to central auditory complexity. Age-stratified analysis revealed selective word score vulnerability despite the high bivariate correlation. Young adults (≤ 46 years, the median age of the complete-case subset; n = 153 ears) clustered tightly in the upperright quadrant (high WS, high PS) with a regression slope (0.48, 95% CI 0.42-0.54, p < 0.001; Fig. 5e,f). Older adults (older > 46 years, n=145 ears) showed greater scatter and were concentrated in the lower-left region (lower WS, lower PS) with a steeper slope (0.58, 95% CI 0.50-0.65, p < 0.001; Fig. 5e,f), although the difference in slopes did not reach significance (D = 0.09, 95% CI -0.001 to 0.19, p = 0.051). The steeper point estimate in older adults (0.58 vs 0.48) suggests stronger WS-PS coupling with age, whereby phoneme recognition changes more steeply relative to word score, a pattern compatible with differential age-related vulnerability of complex linguistic versus acoustic-phonetic processing.

Discussion

This cross-sectional analysis of 526 ears from 263 tinnitus-free adults reveals three central findings regarding age-related speech perception decline. First, eHF PTA (10-16 kHz) is the dominant predictor of speech recognition, explaining 34-36% of variance in both word and phoneme scores, substantially more than conventional audiometry or any physiological measure.

Second, ABR Wave I amplitude contributes no additional explanatory power, inconsistent with the "hidden hearing loss" hypothesis even when tested in speech-weighted noise (+4 dB SNR) where these effects should be most evident [13, 20, 47]. Third, Wave I latency reveals age-dependent vulnerability specific to complex linguistic tasks (word recognition), suggesting that neural conduction slowing, rather than synapse loss, may contribute to age-related speech deficits, though this finding requires validation. Collectively, these results support eHF audiometry as a primary clinical tool for predicting speech outcomes in aging populations and challenge the translational relevance of ABR-based synaptopathy indices.

Past studies have attempted to address the peripheral contributors to SPiN deficits, either focusing on eHF PTA [11], DPOAE [24, 25], or ABR markers [21] in isolation, or in small [26, 27], highly selected samples [20]. Of those rare studies that used eHF PTA, DPOAE and ABR in relation to SPiN [26, 27], these were not covering older age groups, and insufficient in sample size to detect the non-linearities presented herein. The present contribution is thus methodological (testing all mechanisms together) and population-based (all age groups with substantial audiometric variability). In the context of prior literature, our findings partially confirm those from Motlagh Zadeh et al. where eHF hearing enhances SPiN [11]. The linear worsening of eHF hearing with aging observed here is outstanding for a single auditory measure explaining 64% of the variance. Unlike other measures that show accelerating (quadratic) decline, eHF PTA declines linearly, steadily and predictably throughout the lifespan making it a reliable biomarker of cochlear aging far better than any other conventional audiometry, all below 30%. Since eHF PTA tracks cumulative cochlear damage with such fidelity, and since speech relies on intact cochlear function across the whole frequency spectrum, eHF PTA naturally emerges as the dominant speech predictor. Based on the differential age-related trajectories (eHF PTA R² = 0.641 vs. PTA R² = 0.158), mean eHF thresholds reach 25 dB HL (mild hearing loss) at around 40 years of age, whereas mean PTA₄ remains below this level across the entire age range sampled (20-84 years), providing a critical window for early intervention and hearing conservation counseling.

Our finding that eHF PTA (10-16 kHz) explains 34-36% of speech variance in univariate polynomial models (R² = 0.34-0.36), substantially more than conventional PTA₄ (22-25%), aligns with emerging evidence that standard audiometry systematically underestimates cochlear damage in aging populations [11]. Speech contains substantial high-frequency acoustic cues extending into the 8-16 kHz range, with eHF energy accounting for approximately 10-20 dB below mid-frequency peaks when analyzed using physiologically-relevant equivalent rectangular bandwidth (ERB) filters [48, 49]. Fricative contrasts (/s/ vs. /ʃ/), stop consonant bursts, and voice pitch harmonics all carry linguistically important information in this range, with the maximum audible low-pass cutoff for speech reaching approximately 13 kHz in young normal-hearing listeners [48]. Critically, speech radiation patterns are frequency-dependent: eHF energy radiates primarily toward the front of a talker, whereas low-frequency energy is omnidirectional [50]. In ecological "cocktail party" scenarios where target talkers face the listener but interfering talkers are rotated away, access to eHFs (up to 20 kHz) significantly improves SRTs by 1.4-2.0 dB relative to speech low-pass filtered at 8 kHz [51]. This frequency-dependent directivity makes eHF energy a salient cue for auditory object formation, facilitating both detection and segregation of target talkers from background talkers [50, 51]. Traditional laboratory studies with all talkers facing the listener artificially equalize eHF energy across speech sources, obscuring these contributions [49, 52]. Our findings demonstrate that eHF loss predicts speech outcomes even in controlled (4 dB SNR) conditions, suggesting that cumulative loss of eHF phonetic information and reduced segregation cues is clinically meaningful across diverse acoustic environments. The high prevalence of undetected eHF loss among young adults with self-reported listening difficulties [53] supports extending routine audiometry beyond 8 kHz, particularly for individuals presenting with unexplained speech-in-noise complaints, ototoxicity exposure, or noise-related occupations.

The fact that ABR wave I amplitude did not account for variability in speech-in-noise performance contrasts with predictions that cochlear synaptopathy (CS) would be a primary driver of SPiN deficits [18, 21]. While animal studies convincingly demonstrate that noise exposure causes permanent loss of auditory nerve fiber (ANF) synapses with inner hair cells, leading to reduced ABR wave I without threshold elevation [16, 54, 18], human translation has proven challenging [22]. Recent human temporal bone studies provide direct anatomical evidence for age-related neural degeneration contributing to poor word-discrimination scores among individuals with similar audiometric thresholds [19, 55, 56]. However, multiple large-scale human studies have failed to find consistent relationships between putative physiological markers of CS and speechin-noise performance [57] [58] [59] [60] [61]. The impact of CS on hearing may be most pronounced early in the pathological cascade, before substantial hair cell loss. Early-life noise exposure accelerates agerelated cochlear synaptopathy in mice, with effects spreading from high-frequency regions initially affected to lower frequencies as animals age [62]. This noise-accelerated synaptopathy precedes threshold elevation by months, but once animals reach an advanced age (~20 months postexposure), threshold shifts and OHC loss emerge, converging with aging-only pathology. This suggests CS may be an early vulnerability marker, but its relative contribution to hearing impairment diminishes once threshold-elevating pathologies (e.g., OHC loss, strial degeneration) accumulate. Despite mixed behavioral evidence [57] [58] [59] [60] [61], computational models strongly support CS impairing supra-threshold temporal coding [13, 15, 47]. Gerbils with noise-induced CS display impaired neural discrimination of vowel-consonant-vowels in background noise at high sound levels (75 dB SPL), which was surprisingly improved at moderate levels (60 dB SPL), something reproduced in a phenomenological model [63]. A fundamental challenge is the inability to directly measure CS in living humans since all proposed markers are indirect and influenced by multiple cochlear pathologies. While CS undoubtedly occurs in aging and noise-exposed human cochleas, our study joins growing evidence that CS alone does not account for large inter-individual variability in speech-in-noise performance among individuals with clinically normal audiograms.

Multiple peripheral pathologies (synaptopathy, demyelination, strial degeneration, OHC dysfunction) and central factors (cognitive control, attention, neural gain) likely interact to determine real-world listening abilities.

In contrast to Wave I amplitude, Wave I latency appears as a stronger predictor of speech pathology. The selective interaction between Wave I latency and age for word recognition (WS, p = 0.027) but not phoneme recognition (PS, p = 0.076) reflects differential temporal integration demands: word-level comprehension requires extended temporal windows (400-600 ms) encompassing multiple phonemes, making it vulnerable to conduction delays that accumulate across longer sequences, whereas phoneme recognition operates within brief acoustic windows (50-100 ms) where conduction delays have minimal impact. Indeed, this dissociation is consistent with hierarchical processing models wherein word-level comprehension requires precise temporal integration across multiple phonemes and syllables-a process that becomes increasingly vulnerable when peripheral timing delays compound with age-related central processing decline.

In contrast, phoneme recognition relies on shorter temporal integration windows and benefits from high acoustic-phonetic redundancy, rendering it relatively robust to peripheral timing variability even in older age [64] [65] [66] [67]. These findings suggest that peripheral neural timing precision, rather than synaptic loss (as measured by Wave I amplitude), represents the ratelimiting step for complex speech understanding in aging. These findings suggest that age-related speech deficits are more closely associated with slowed auditory nerve conduction than with cochlear synaptic loss inferred from Wave I amplitude. In support of these findings, acute demyelination of auditory nerve fibers in mice using a transient ablation of Schwann cells leads to a hidden hearing loss phenotype with normal thresholds with both a decrease in Wave 1 amplitude and increased Wave 1 latency in absence of synapse loss [68]. Our data in aging humans, however, reveal a different profile: ABR Wave I amplitude shows no association with speech-in-noise performance, whereas Wave I latency and its interaction with age were associated with word recognition, particularly in older adults, but not with phoneme recognition. This suggests that in heterogeneous human cohorts, the temporal imprecision resulting from decreased peripheral conduction may be more tightly coupled to complex speech deficits than the overall reduction in neural output, and that amplitude-based 'synaptopathy' markers may lack specificity for the age-related mechanisms at play. WS appears to represent higher-level, plasticity-dependent speech comprehension, while PS reflects more fundamental, robust acoustic processing. This dissociation has important implications for age-based intervention strategies. Our best models explain 32-38% of speech variance, leaving ~65% unexplained. What could account for this residual? Working memory, attention, processing speed, and executive function all contribute to speech understanding, especially in challenging conditions [69, 70]. Age-related cognitive decline may co-occur with hearing loss but follow an independent trajectory [71], and cognitive reserve may compensate for peripheral deficits [72]. Our models did not include cognitive assessments and thus future work incorporating measures like the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) adapted to individuals with hearing impairment [73] or digit span could partition variance between sensory and cognitive domains. Cortical auditory processing, phonemic decoding, lexical access, predictive coding, is entirely unobserved in click ABRs, which reflects only the first 10 ms of the auditory pathway.

Cortical evoked potentials (e.g., P1-N1-P2 complex, mismatch negativity) or speech-evoked envelope following responses may capture variance orthogonal to audiometry [58, 74]. Aging affects cortical temporal processing, neural noise, and speech-specific encoding [75], potentially explaining inter-individual differences in speech perception despite similar audiograms. In addition, vocabulary size, phonemic familiarity, native language experience, and lifetime auditory enrichment all modulate speech recognition [76, 77]. Comprehensive models integrating eHF audiometry, cognitive assessments, cortical evoked potentials, and speech-in-noise testing across diverse listening environments (reverberation, competing talkers, audiovisual) are needed to fully map the determinants of real-world communication ability in aging.

Women obtained modestly higher word and phoneme scores than men after adjustment for age, but gender did not contribute independently in the fully adjusted models, and there was no clear evidence that age-related trajectories of speech perception differed between women and men. These null interactions do not establish equivalence: women were over-represented (67.7%), which reduces precision for men and for interaction terms, and only self-reported gender was available, so sex-linked biological contributions to cochlear aging cannot be separated from gender-related exposures such as occupational noise.

Strengths and Limitations

The main strengths of this study are its population-based design with a large, broadly-recruited cohort focusing on healthy tinnitus-free individuals (N = 263 adults across the adult lifespan), the use of comprehensive and multi-domain audiometric characterization (audiometry 0.125-16 kHz, OAE, click-ABR, and speech outcomes), rigorous quality control of ABR recordings (double-blind waveform identification, test-retest analyses), and the use of robust statistical models with pre-specified hierarchical structure and heteroscedasticity-consistent standard errors. However, the use of a tinnitus-focused cohort to study tinnitus-free individuals may introduce volunteer bias, since participants may have selfselected based on hearing concerns even in the absence of tinnitus. Other limitations include the cross-sectional design, which precludes causal inference about whether eHF loss precedes speech decline, something that would require larger efforts in population screens to answer this question. The restriction to Swedish-speaking, highly educated urban adults, limits generalization.

Generalizability is further limited to tinnitus-free adults able to complete the full test battery, to the age range sampled (20-84 years), and to the specific transducers, stimulus protocols and device output limits used here; the over-representation of women (67.7%) also limits precision for men.

Analyses were performed at the ear level, although participant-clustered standard errors did not change the main conclusions (the Wave III latency³ effect on PS became borderline, p = 0.051), the two ears of a participant are not independent observations. The complete-case multivariable sample (298 of 526 ears) may differ from the full cohort, and Wave I amplitude could only be analyzed in the smaller Eclipse-only subset (122 ears), which limits precision for amplitude and its interaction with age. In addition, the use of a single, moderately challenging SNR (+4 dB) may not reveal synaptopathy effects present at lower SNRs [13]. Finally, the high WS-PS correlation (r = 0.934) means that most main effects are inevitably replicated across both outcomes, however, the incremental scientific value justifying the retention of both measures resides primarily in the differential Age and Wave I Latency interaction.

Implementation of Extended Audiometry in Clinical Practice and Clinical Trials

Whether in clinical practice or in clinical trials, implementation of the eHF measures may differ depending on the purpose. In clinical practice, standard pure-tone audiometry covers 0.125-8 kHz and is performed with commercially available, CE-marked audiometers. Extending the range to 10, 12.5, 14 and 16 kHz requires an audiometer with extended high-frequency capability together with a transducer calibrated for that range according to ISO 389-5 (for instance the Sennheiser HDA 200, which is widely available), a configuration offered by many current clinical audiometers, including the Madsen Astera 2 used here. The marginal cost is therefore the examiner's time, approximately 5-10 additional minutes per ear, rather than new hardware, which makes routine eHF screening feasible in clinical audiology, occupational health and hearing conservation programs. Since the purpose in this setting is to identify individuals who may have speech-innoise difficulties rather than to quantify a threshold exactly, a device-limited "no response at maximum output" remains clinically informative, and the reduced maximum output at 14 and 16 kHz apparent in the older age groups (> 55 years; Fig. 2a) does not compromise the screening decision. However, age-related reference values remain incomplete above 8 kHz, since ISO 7029:2017 gives expected medians only up to 12.5 kHz and for information only, so interpretation at 14 and 16 kHz has to rely on published normative data (Motlagh Zadeh et al. [11], supplementary materials). In resource-limited settings, eHF screening could be prioritized for individuals with unexplained speech-in-noise complaints, a history of ototoxic exposure, or occupational noise exposure, where the clinical yield is likely to be highest. For multi-site clinical trials, the requirements may differ because the measurement must be comparable across sites and over time rather than merely informative for one patient. The maximum output attainable at 14 and 16 kHz is substantially lower than at conventional frequencies and varies with the audiometer-transducer combination, so thresholds become censored at the device limit, not that of the ear. Whereas I acknowledge that identical equipment across centers is rarely achievable in investigator-initiated trials, this wouldn't be critical within a single site where censoring is a constant, however, across sites equipped differently, the censoring would shift between centers, and pooled eHF means, prevalences and change scores would reflect more the distribution of equipments' ceiling effect rather than that of hearing. Such details need to be evaluated during study design, prior the first enrollment.

In conclusion, the present study confirms eHF pure-tone audiometry as a dominant clinical predictor of speech recognition performance and challenges the hypothesis that cochlear synaptopathy, as reflected in ABR wave I amplitude changes, contributes meaningfully to speech deficits in this large population. In contrast, ABR wave I latency, a marker of peripheral neural conduction timing, shows an age-dependent association with word recognition, particularly in older adults, suggesting that slowed neural timing rather than reduced response amplitude is more tightly linked to complex speech decline in aging. Routine extension of hearing assessment beyond 8 kHz may provide clinically valuable information regarding speech prediction and communication prognosis, particularly in older adults and those with noise exposure history.

Future studies examining hearing-cognition relationships should routinely include eHF audiometry, alongside latency-based brainstem timing measures, to avoid an incomplete characterization of hearing status.
