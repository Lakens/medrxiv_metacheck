Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). Statcheck might still miss one-sided tests that are described in another sentence, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. Abstract

Background: Racism shapes the maternity care and outcomes of Black, African, Caribbean and mixed-Black women in the UK. Services monitor inequities through outcomes and experience data, yet evidence of racism often results in inaction, and existing explanations rely mainly on staff perspectives. We drew on Black mothers' and community advocates' accounts to explain what stops monitoring of racism from leading to action, and how they propose evidence of racism should be recorded so that it does. Methods: This co-produced qualitative study was underpinned by critical realism and Black feminist thought. Four focus groups were held in London from June to July 2026 with nine Black, African, Caribbean and mixed-Black mothers who used NHS maternity services and seven community advocates. Lived experience co-researchers co-led the study design, data collection and analysis. We used critical realist thematic analysis to propose mechanisms explaining participants' accounts, each tested against rival explanations.

Results: We propose three mechanisms: credibility discounting, evidence neutralisation and containment of change. Participants described Black women's accounts being discounted until someone else raised the same concern, consistent with testimonial injustice. Recorded evidence appeared to be neutralised through audit culture, as concerns were acknowledged or deflected without action. Change that would shift power or accountability appeared to be contained, consistent with institutional racism. Participants also described harm from staff who shared their racial identity, linked to internalised racism intersecting with language and migration, and to workplace cultures in which Black staff are also dismissed; we interpret this harm as a possible outcome of these mechanisms. Participants proposed that services account for their response to each concern, supported by community-generated evidence, independent observation, qualitative routes and ethnicity-disaggregated data. This would require protection for those who raise concerns and sustained funding and backing for action.

Conclusions: Our findings suggest more evidence alone is unlikely to address racism in maternity services. An independently reviewed record of how services respond to each concern could show when they do not act. Change is more likely where services shift power and resources to community organisations working with Black families, while remaining accountable for acting on evidence of racism.

Word count: 7663 words 1. BACKGROUND

Racism shapes the maternity care and outcomes of Black, African, Caribbean and mixed-Black women in countries where they are racially minoritised [1] [2] [3]. In the UK, Black women are up to three times more likely than white women to die during pregnancy or up to six weeks after birth [3]. Racism operates as a system that distributes opportunity and worth according to how people are racialised [4]. In maternity care, this system is visible in Black women's accounts of their concerns being dismissed, ignored or disbelieved [5] [6] [7]. A national investigation across twelve English National Health Service (NHS) organisations found racism ingrained throughout maternity services [8].

Much of the policy response relies on monitoring, by which we mean the routine ways services collect evidence of racism. These include complaints, patient experience surveys and the NHS Maternity and Neonatal Equalities Dashboard, which reports outcomes and experience by ethnicity and deprivation [9]. These approaches can show where care differs between groups or record individual concerns, but they say little about the decisions behind those differences or how services should improve. England's national maternity and neonatal investigation by Baroness Amos identified racism as a 'critical maternity safety issue', recommending that inequalities data be captured as safety intelligence and escalated to board level [8]. This approach assumes that recorded evidence prompts action. However, one NHS organisation was aware of poorer experiences and outcomes for racially minoritised women for over a decade, while initiatives to address them were inconsistently resourced [10].

Existing research partly explains why evidence goes unused. Studies of patient feedback and speaking up in the NHS suggest that staff are more likely to act on concerns when they value patient input and have the autonomy and resources to respond [11], and that staff may not raise their own concerns about safety or racism when they expect a defensive response and limited change [12, 13]. Formal institutional commitments to anti-racism, such as policies and written values, can also take the place of action, as stating a commitment may be treated as equivalent to realising the change it promises [14]. Although patient-reported experience measures of obstetric racism and respectful maternity care have been developed [15, 16], such evidence may do little if the barriers lie in how it is handled. Independent investigations have documented families' concerns being dismissed [8, 10, 17], but research examining why concerns are not addressed has drawn largely on staff accounts. These explanations have rarely been examined from the perspective of women and families who raise concerns about racism. Black mothers hold direct knowledge of how their concerns were responded to, while community advocates observe how concerns are handled across many families and services [18]. Co-production, in which researchers, practitioners and communities jointly conduct research [19], offers a way to bring this knowledge into decisions about how evidence of racism is collected and used. This study therefore asks, from the perspective of Black mothers and community advocates: (1) what appears to stop the monitoring of racism in maternity care from leading to action; and (2) how evidence of racism should be recorded so that it does.

2. METHODS

>METHODS

2.1. Study design and theoretical approach

We co-produced a qualitative focus group study underpinned by critical realism, which holds that reality exists independently of our knowledge of it and distinguishes between experiences, the events experienced and the causal mechanisms that produce them [20, 21].

Because the social world is an open system, these mechanisms operate as tendencies rather than certainties [22], and our knowledge of these mechanisms is fallible and open to revision [20]. Social structures such as institutional racism are considered underlying mechanisms, which can be explored and inferred through qualitative methods [22, 23].

Our study was also guided by Black feminist thought, which treats Black women's lived experience as a criterion of meaning and holds that their experience of racism and sexism together gives them a particular view of how oppression operates. This knowledge is built and tested through dialogue with other Black women [24]. Critical realism complements this by explaining the mechanisms through which interlocking systems of oppression produce harm, including those that the people affected or institutions may not recognise [25]. This emphasis on dialogue informed our choice of focus groups. Groups who share identities can provide a more comfortable setting to discuss experiences [26], and experiences may only be recognised as racism when discussed with others who have had similar ones [27]. Group interaction also allows participants to build on each other's accounts [28]. Black feminist thought also informed our co-produced approach, in which five lived experience coresearchers from Black, African, Caribbean and mixed-Black backgrounds contributed to the study design, topic guide, facilitation, coding and interpretation, as reported using the GRIPP2 short form (Additional file 1) [29].

However, combining the two creates tension. Black feminist thought treats the knowledge of people whose voices are rarely heard as authoritative, whereas critical realism holds that racism exists whether or not it is recognised, so researchers may need to look beyond participants' own explanations [25, 30, 31]. We addressed this by sharing responsibility for exploring mechanisms and rival theories with the lived experience co-researchers during coding and interpretation. Key findings were shared with participants for review, which further refined our interpretations.

Study reporting adheres to the Standards for Reporting Qualitative Research (Additional file 2) [32].

2.2. Our positionality and reflexivity

SA, a South Asian woman and doctoral researcher, works for a national anti-racism organisation in health. This gave insight into how services use evidence of racism, but her organisation's commitment to anti-racism risked leading her to read participants' accounts as evidence of racism before other explanations had been considered. SA also does not share participants' experiences of anti-Black racism or of NHS maternity care. SA therefore kept reflexive memos and developed interpretations with the five lived experience co-researchers, who had used NHS maternity care or supported Black families as advocates and had worked with SA for two years. Their own experiences, discussed in fortnightly meetings, informed findings only where participants' accounts supported them. RW, a South Asian woman and patient and public involvement professional, supported recruitment and coding.

SA's supervisors reviewed and challenged interpretations; HW is a white British woman and clinical public health academic focused on improving research and healthcare for marginalised populations, PA a white British woman and researcher in healthcare quality and safety, and SD a mixed-race Southeast Asian woman, NHS obstetrician and reproductive health researcher. As the co-researchers were the only Black members of the team, their judgement was central to interpreting accounts of anti-Black racism.

2.3. Study setting

The study took place in London, where a higher proportion of residents are Black, African, Caribbean and mixed-Black than elsewhere in England [33]. Participants could therefore describe care across several maternity services, including those that have committed to addressing racism.

2.4. Ethics approval

The study received a favourable opinion from the Imperial College Research Ethics Committee (ref. 8208475). Participants gave signed electronic consent and could withdraw up to 14 days after their session, although contributions to a group discussion could not always be removed. Confidentiality was maintained unless a serious risk was disclosed, and participants were signposted to NHS complaints routes where relevant.

2.5. Participants and recruitment

We purposively sampled maternity service users aged 18 or over who had used London NHS maternity services within the past five years and self-identified as Black, African, Caribbean or mixed-Black. All participants identified as mothers, and so we refer to them as such. We also recruited community advocates of any ethnicity who currently support Black families in London during pregnancy, birth or the postnatal period. Participants were recruited through community and professional networks, social media and word of mouth.

Eligibility was confirmed through an online screening questionnaire and a phone call with SA or RW, which also screened for fraudulent participation. Mothers and advocates took part in separate groups so that advocates' knowledge of services would not shape how mothers described their own care.

We assessed sample adequacy using information power dimensions [34]. Our aim was narrow, concerning what happens to evidence of racism in maternity care in a defined population and setting, and our sample was specific to it. Established theories also guided retroduction towards specific mechanisms. Dialogue was rich, as lived experience coresearchers co-facilitated the groups and participants readily described their own experiences unprompted. Two of the groups were smaller, which allowed each participant more time to speak and may have created the safety needed to discuss experiences of racism more quickly [35]. Our cross-group analysis called for more data, but the narrow aim, specific sample and quality of dialogue offset this.

2.6. Data collection and processing

Four focus groups were held in June and July 2026, two with Black mothers and two with advocates. Each was facilitated by two lived experience co-researchers with SA and RW, all trained in trauma-informed facilitation and qualitative research, who debriefed after each session. The semi-structured topic guide, co-produced by SA and the lived experience coresearchers, asked about good and fair maternity care, what evidence could show racism, and how evidence could be captured and used; it was revised for clarity after the first two groups (Additional file 3). Participants received example questions and support resources in advance.

Participants could attend in person at a community venue in south London or online on Zoom (Table 3); in-person groups were recorded with a dictaphone. Sessions lasted 90 to 120 minutes and were conducted in English. Ground rules were agreed, and questions allowed participants to describe scenarios rather than their own experiences of racism, although many shared their own. Participants received a £50 thank-you voucher.

Recordings were pseudonymised, stored on encrypted servers and professionally transcribed after a 14-day withdrawal period. SA checked transcripts against the recordings and removed identifying details before analysis.

2.7. Coding and data analysis

We used critical realist thematic analysis as described by Fryer [21], which aims to produce causal explanations of events rather than descriptions of experience and draws on existing theory to assess and strengthen explanations derived from the data [36]. Following this distinction, participants' accounts record their experiences, codes consolidate these into events (what happened), and themes explain the mechanisms through which these events occur [21]. We followed Fryer's five steps (Table 1), moving between them iteratively.

Codes were consolidated through abduction, reinterpreting the events participants described through theoretical concepts from the literature [22, 36]. Themes were developed through retroduction, working back from those events to the structures and mechanisms that could have produced them [37, 38]. We organised the mechanisms using Jones's levels of racism [39], with other theories to explain how racism operates at each level. For each consolidated why evidence was not being used, their own causal explanations were the starting point.

In this exchange, self-advocacy shifted from an individual responsibility to a response to services that withhold information and do not listen. It is therefore an unreliable safeguard for women least able to advocate for themselves. Later, one mother suggested that being dismissed teaches women 'to ask for less so that they don't feel like a burden' (P9, mother).

Another described holding back a complaint because 'it's going to be one word against the other' (P7, mother).

One advocate described Black fathers who spoke on behalf of their partners being seen as aggressive and removed from the ward rather than heard. An advocate also described a woman awaiting rehousing who said she felt too unwell to be discharged and was told that staying would not get her housing; she was later found to have a major infection (P15, advocate). Where harm occurred, the burden of being heard could also be turned back on parents. One advocate witnessed staff at times blaming parents after babies had died: 'They would say, "She didn't say she was in pain. She didn't say it loud enough. She didn't come in enough times when her baby stopped moving."' (P13, advocate). In one example, a concern that services had ignored was acted on quickly once an advocate raised it: 'the same admin team who hadn't replied for weeks, then got her an appointment straight away' (P16, advocate). Conversely, one mother described a midwife in a later pregnancy who knew her history and, without her knowing, called the ward to ensure staff looked after her (P2, mother). Continuity of carer may help concerns to be heard, although here too a professional spoke for the woman.

We tested three rival explanations. Professional hierarchy could explain some dismissals, as most of those dismissed were service users speaking to clinicians, and staff concern about the extra work a request might create could explain others. Neither explains why a receptionist with no clinical authority treated white and Black women differently while they waited for the same service, or why Black fathers advocating for their partners were seen as aggressive rather than worried. A third, clinical judgement, does not explain why the woman with a major infection was dismissed. We therefore interpret this mechanism as testimonial injustice, in which a speaker is given less credibility because of prejudice about their social group [45], enacted through interpersonal racism [39]. Participants' accounts point to one source of this prejudice, images of Black women as strong, able to bear pain and aggressive, which Collins calls controlling images [24]. That a white obstetrician's intervention changed how one mother was treated suggests whiteness acted as a credential, so that the obstetrician was listened to where the mother had not been [46]. Mothers who expect not to be believed may also narrow what they say [47], which may explain why women learned to ask for less. For monitoring, a concern that is not believed is unlikely to be recorded, and where harm occurs, responsibility may be placed on Black women themselves.

3.2.2. 'Sorry, and bye-bye': neutralising evidence through audit culture

Where concerns were recorded, participants described three ways in which evidence was neutralised. In the first, which we term absorption, services responded to evidence without changing care. This resembles organisational decoupling, in which formal structures such as complaints processes confer legitimacy while remaining separate from practice [48], a process that is often racialised, allowing equity commitments to coexist with unchanged care [46]. After complaining, one mother received a written apology and a call from a lead midwife, with the harm attributed to a busy period. Another completed a feedback questionnaire, 'but still yet they did it to somebody else' (P4, mother). Advocates described women's accounts reaching governance meetings without leading to change, leaving many with 'a, "Sorry, and bye-bye," and being expected to go into the world with all the trauma and all the pain' (P12, advocate). Where advocates raised concerns, responses resolved the individual cases of those who attended service-led feedback meetings, leaving out 'all the others who didn't come' (P16, advocate).

In the second, which we term deflection, services used low numbers of complaints or average figures to suggest there was no problem, pointing to their own data to show they were not outliers. Mothers noted that fewer complaints from Black families reflected long, difficult processes, with women 'so fatigued... that they just can't be arsed to report it' (P8, mother). An advocate described how openness within a service did not reach board-level discussions: '... There is a gaslighting culture amongst even themselves. So I'll go to an internal meeting and they'll be more open on where something went wrong, and then you go to the board meeting, and they're, "Oh, we did this, we did that." So there is this culture of, "Yes, it went wrong, but we never really admit it."' -P16 (advocate)

The third, raised in both advocate groups, was target distortion, in which indicators used in decision-making distort the processes they monitor [49], so that 'the numbers are good on your tables, but a lot of women are destroyed' (P11, advocate).

Two advocates, P15 and P16, developed an explanation together: P16: 'Because they're just so scared…' P15: 'Do you think if upper management financially lost out, that they'd be more…?' P16: 'But they gain money from meeting targets that show that they're, socalled, doing all of…' P15: 'No, flipping it, inverting it. So for you to gain that money…' P16: 'You'd have to have evidence of certain outcomes.' P15: 'And if the lived experience that's coming through negates that, that's a lot of evidence, like you're saying.'

Their exchange moved from leaders' fear to incentives, which, they suggested, reward services for appearing to meet targets rather than for changing care. Participants also attributed this pattern to staffing shortages and to a defensive culture in which, 'when they're on the spot... they'll never ever really say, "We messed up"' (P16, advocate). As a rival explanation, defensive culture may explain why services avoid admitting fault more generally [8, 10], but participants' accounts suggest evidence of racism faces additional barriers.

Complaints about racism often rested on one woman's account against a professional's.

Two mothers described the evidence services required before acting: P9: '…systemically they can make the evidence threshold so high or so unrealistic that even when you submit the evidence, it's not enough for them to take accountability or to act.' P8: '…Like you said, the thresholds… When will it ever be enough? Which I think one concern is one concern too many if no one has listened to you… they just stack up all this data and they don't actually do anything with it.'

This suggests that collecting data may itself stand in for action. During analysis, the lived experience co-researchers proposed that staff may also be excused by a visible record of anti-racism work, such as completed training. We treat this as part of neutralisation, because diversity initiatives can serve a performative function while leaving the distribution of power unchanged [14]. Extending participants' explanations, we interpret neutralisation as institutional racism operating through audit culture [39, [50] [51] [52]. In audit culture, processes designed to demonstrate accountability turn evidence of racism into evidence of responsiveness, or set thresholds it cannot meet, while staffing pressures and fear of repercussions offer credible reasons why nothing could be done.

3.2.3. Containing change that would shift power or accountability

Even where evidence of racism was accepted, participants described anti-racism work that left existing power and accountability unchanged, such as commissioning reports or cultural competency training, as more likely to go ahead. Change that would shift who controlled resources, or make staff answerable to Black women, was more often held back, most clearly where funding that could have gone to community organisations was kept within the NHS: 'I've personally seen, in services before, where there is money... that could be given to Black and brown projects or the community as such, and instead what will happen is the NHS will decide to internally take on that budget... So, the money is there, but the allocation isn't happening in the right way.' -P14 (advocate)

Another advocate described a grant for Black maternal health in London that stalled after a disagreement between consultants and commissioners: 'everybody who applied for the grant has just been left in hold, there's no communication' (P10, advocate). Participants described recommendations being held back in the same way; an advocate also noted that a recent independent review of a trust's maternity services included a chapter on racism but no recommendation to tackle the issues raised [10].

Mothers offered possible explanations for this pattern. One located the decision not to act with senior leaders who 'probably don't look like any of us in this room... So, if it doesn't affect them, or their community, or their people, why would they care?' (P4, mother). Another suggested that current arrangements suited those who ran services, since 'nobody is going to hold you accountable if you mistreat a Black woman' (P9, mother).

Others attributed the lack of action to money, a rival explanation that participants themselves contested. One mother suggested that the problem 'comes down to cost and money' (P7, mother), but another questioned this, replying: 'I disagree on the money thing, because some of these things don't cost money.

Manners, kindness, and respect are all free... I also think it's because it benefits them. The way how things are, it doesn't change their privilege, it doesn't change their income, it doesn't change nothing.' -P9 (mother)

One mother argued that while services did not act, funding for equity work should be diverted to Black-led care, because 'it's not really fair to tell people to, "Oh, hang on. We're just going to keep on with this racism a little bit longer," when it's a risk to life' (P8, mother).

She also suggested that inaction could be countered when 'someone from your community in a high-up position' took the issue on 'as a moral thing to do', although this would rely on individual commitment rather than accountability. An advocate also questioned why funding could be found during the COVID-19 pandemic but not for racism in maternity care. This comparison, together with accounts of funding being retained or left unspent and of no-cost changes such as respectful care not being made, suggests that the barrier lay in who controlled resources rather than whether they existed. We interpret this as institutional racism [39], in which routine processes maintain unequal access to power and resources without any individual needing to intend harm. As the mothers could not observe how funding decisions were made, this interpretation rests mainly on advocates' accounts from inside services and on the pattern of what did and did not go ahead.

3.2.4. Harm from staff who share a racial identity

As Figure 1 shows, the three mechanisms together may reproduce racism in maternity care, and when women raise concerns about the resulting harm, those concerns may be discounted, neutralised and contained in turn. Beyond well-documented harms [3, 5, 7], we focus on a less examined one, harm from Black staff, described in three groups. We interpret this harm as an outcome of the same mechanisms, which leave poor care of Black women with few consequences for staff of any ethnicity. In the first group, Black African mothers who had recently arrived in the UK described being treated worse by Black staff who had lived in the UK for longer: '...when I went for this sugar test, it was a Black woman that was acting as if she is racist... They believe that once they have stayed here for a long time, and they can speak like the whites, so they try to treat us that have just come, that we still speak, like, you know this person is from Africa, the way they treat us is very, very bad.' -P6 (mother)

Others in the group agreed, although one mother, born in the UK, disagreed. Advocates explained the same behaviour through the system Black staff work in, with one describing how staff respond to the accountability they face: 'When I come into the system, they're telling me, "Hush, hush.

Come on, you can manage," because I look like their niece, I look like their sister, I look like their cousin... When a white woman comes in who they know will complain and who may give them problems, they treat her like she's the princess of the world, and that is because they're in a system that will put them into trouble if they don't treat that white woman that way.' -P12 (advocate)

Another advocate traced this treatment to a culture set from the top: Mothers and advocates explained this harm in two ways, which we interpret as different levels of the same process. Mothers' accounts suggest internalised racism, in which a hierarchy based on proximity to whiteness positioned women by their time in the UK and the way they spoke [39]. Here, racialisation intersected with migration [53]. Advocates' accounts locate the harm in the system staff work in, where complaints carry consequences unequally and staff are dismissed as women are. This also reflects the national investigation's finding that bullying, racism and discrimination by senior leaders and clinicians affect safety [8].

Internalised racism may influence which women staff treat less well; that the three mechanisms allow this treatment to continue rests mainly on advocates' accounts.

3.3. Mothers' and advocates' proposals on evidencing racism in maternity care

Participants' proposals focused on making visible what services decided, whose accounts were believed and what happened after a concern was raised, rather than on new measures of racism. We synthesised them into one framework (Figure 2), with accountability for the service's response to each concern at its centre; this responds to neutralisation by making the decision not to act visible. The remaining proposals are grouped by the mechanism they counter. Community-generated evidence, produced by people and in spaces women trust, and qualitative routes, which ask women directly about racism and record their own accounts, counter discounting. Independent observation and ethnicity-disaggregated service delivery data counter neutralisation; the first moves the judgement of whether evidence is sufficient outside the service, and the second addresses deflection. Conditions for action counter containment, while conditions for safety allow evidence to be raised at all. 3.3.1. Accounting for the response to racism Participants argued that services should account for what they did with evidence of racism; one mother proposed the most concrete form: 'I would say it should be recorded every time a woman asks for something or raises a concern... And what also needs to be recorded on the professional side is if they acted on that information and if they didn't act on it, why didn't they act on it?' -P9 (mother)

Another mother in the same group argued that staff who had to record a decision not to act would be less willing to make that decision. Advocates in both groups called for feedback to be taken back to services and for inspection of whether organisations had acted on it; one proposed that regulators ask for this evidence and count it towards a service's rating.

3.3.2. Producing and reading the evidence

The remaining proposals describe who should produce, witness and read this record.

Participants placed most weight on evidence produced outside the service, by communities or independent observers, because they did not expect services to recognise racism in their own records. One advocate explained this in terms of whose accounts are believed:

People will tell you. And when they tell you, we need to believe them... we've got the data of how many people are dying, but we haven't got the data of the near misses in that national data... They [services] need to listen, to believe us. We're not making it up, we're not trying to do a hype. It is what it is.' -P12 (advocate) Qualitative routes concerned what is asked and recorded. Advocates argued that services were 'not capturing the stories, the experiences' (P13, advocate), and that racism would go unrecorded unless women were asked about it directly. Mothers wanted a choice of routes, including questionnaires for those who 'might not want to talk face to face' (P1, mother).

Yet an account on its own remains open to being discounted as anecdotal, which may explain why participants paired it with independent observation and service data.

Independent observation addressed this by changing who witnesses care. Participants in several groups proposed an independent witness who could accompany women and see whether they were treated differently, likening this to mystery shopping, and an independent body to review concerns. This approach, however, relies on a third party being believed rather than changing who is believed.

Participants also wanted service data broken down by ethnicity, such as the timing of antenatal booking, referrals and access to services. Mothers and advocates, however, wanted different things from these data. Mothers saw numbers as part of a written record that would protect them from being disbelieved: 'always statistics, emails, letters, everything that you can seize in black and white, because then it can't be my word against yours' (P9, mother). Two advocates were wary of numbers replacing women's accounts, although both said they used ethnicity-disaggregated measures themselves. Advocates also wanted data on earlier decisions, such as offers of induction, since caesarean rates are 'the last stop' and earlier intervention 'gets explained away that that's normal'. Hospitals held these numbers, 'but what they don't have is the why, as in the honest why of it' (P15, advocate). Participants therefore tended to treat numbers as evidence of racism when read alongside the reasons behind them and by someone outside the service.

3.3.3. Conditions for safety and for action

Participants said recording alone would not be enough and described conditions for safety, so that evidence could be raised, and for action, so that it would be used. The first condition for safety was protection from backlash, as mothers described holding evidence back for fear it would affect their care. One proposed keeping a feedback log during pregnancy to hand in at the end: someone gives you negative feedback, you will feel a way, if you were a midwife, reading something that your patient is basically saying that they don't feel like you're meeting their needs, midway through the journey.

So, I guess it's something that we, as the parents, or mums, could keep with us, and then hand it in, maybe, at the end?' -P2 (mother) Advocates described the same fear among staff, as midwives who saw women treated unfairly could not escalate it without risk to themselves. Safety also meant support, as a traumatised mother should not be asked to relive her experience 'without her having that additional support to hold her' (P15, advocate). It also meant time, as advocates proposed that monitoring continue for two to three years after birth because 'it takes time to acknowledge that harm was experienced' (P11, advocate). Conditions for action concerned who backs the evidence and who pays for it. Advocates argued that evidence would lead to change only with sustained backing from those who hold power and money: '[...] you have to have a mandate and you have to have support higher up the ladder, like in commissioning services and ICBs (integrated care boards)... without that backing from high up, you won't get that far, or a lot of the work you would do would be noticed for a little while, it becomes flavour of the month or flavour of the week, and then what happens after that?' -P10 (advocate)

Advocates also called for sustained funding for the community organisations that collect evidence, often staffed by underfunded or part-time advocates, and for incentive schemes that penalise missed outcomes as well as rewarding targets met. Their remedies for containment were therefore redistributive, requiring a transfer of money and authority that better evidence alone would not achieve.

4. DISCUSSION

Drawing on the accounts of Black mothers and community advocates, we identified three mechanisms, credibility discounting, neutralisation and containment, that may help explain why evidence of racism in maternity care often does not lead to action. Unlike national investigations, which cover maternity care for all service user groups [8, 10], and research drawing largely on staff accounts [12, 13, 54, 55], this study offers an explanation of how evidence of racism in particular is handled, from the perspectives of Black mothers and community advocates.

The credibility discounting participants described is consistent with UK Black-led and community inquiries into English NHS maternity services [5, 7] and with evidence from other countries such as Canada, Brazil and the United States [58] [59] [60] [61]. Women in these studies also held back concerns; in one UK survey, around half of Black women who experienced poor care did not complain formally, often believing nothing would change or fearing worse care [7]. This suggests that complaint numbers understate racism before any concern reaches a service. Advocacy sometimes prompted action, but only by substituting the advocate's credibility for the woman's. This offsets discounting in individual cases without correcting it and risks individualising responsibility for navigating inequitable systems [18].

Our findings also bring together research on epistemic injustice, which shows how patients' testimony is given less credibility than it deserves [45, 56], and on audit culture, which shows how processes designed to demonstrate accountability can come to stand in for it [50] [51] [52].

Audit culture research has focused mainly on how organisations and professionals perform accountability, whereas our findings suggest that, for evidence of racism, what enters the audit also depends on whose testimony is believed. A record thinned by discounting is easier to absorb or deflect. In Jones's terms, interpersonal racism thins the record on which institutional racism then operates, so the levels compound [39]. Dashboards now report gaps between ethnic groups within each service [9], but where inequalities run through the whole system, a service whose gap matches the national pattern can still show it is not an outlier.

Linking incentives to such indicators also risks the target distortion participants described [49]. Outcome data also record harm only after it occurs, whereas women's own accounts may be the closest equivalent to near-miss reporting.

Failure to implement recommendations has been attributed to their volume, overlap and lack of a clear owner [8], and to constrained resources and overlapping demands for data [57], so that reporting can consume the capacity it is meant to direct [52]. These explanations treat inaction mainly as a question of capacity. Containment suggests that, for racism, inaction may also reflect who controls resources. Ray's theory of racialised organisations suggests that ordinary rules and routines can reproduce racism without anyone intending harm [46].

Our findings are consistent with this account, as services appeared to give Black women's concerns less weight, keep complaints processes separate from practice and leave control of resources unchanged.

Our findings suggest why remedies for defensive culture may not address racism. Efforts to address defensive culture in the NHS have focused on encouraging staff to speak up and services to be open when things go wrong [12, 13, 54], whereas participants' conditions for safety suggest that women who raise concerns need similar protection from backlash. For evidence of racism, the problem may lie earlier, as discounted concerns may not reach the processes these efforts rely on. Where services respond most to those expected to complain, greater openness may still leave Black women's concerns unaddressed. Framing racism as a patient safety issue may have similar limits, as it places racism within governance that relies on internal reporting. This governance sits alongside clinical negligence processes, which the national investigation found can create incentives to deny problems and challenge families' accounts [8]. These responses work within existing services, leaving unchanged who judges whether evidence is sufficient to act on.

Harm from staff who shared women's racial identity adds an intersectional dimension to these mechanisms, as the hierarchy mothers described operated within racial groups as well as between them [25, 53], which monitoring by broad ethnic category would miss. Advocates' accounts of Black midwives being dismissed in the same way as the women they cared for suggest that Black staff are themselves subject to the racialised organisations they work in [46]. Participants who reviewed the findings linked this harm to the organisation, as staff who face racism at work may treat women as they are treated. A more diverse workforce may not, on its own, change how Black women and families are cared for.

Participants' proposals respond to limits in existing measures. Patient-reported experience measures, including measures of obstetric racism [16], and the experience data that NHS maternity boards review [62] may undercount racism where Black women hold back concerns or are not believed, and they record what women experienced rather than what services did in response. The proposals address these limits only in part. A record of the response to each concern would still be produced within services that discount Black women's accounts, and like other accountability measures it could come to stand in for action [14, [50] [51] [52]. Independent observation substitutes a credible witness for the woman, as advocacy does, which leaves her own testimony no more likely to be believed [45]. These proposals also leave unchanged who controls the resources to act.

Participants' conditions for action are consistent with evidence that stable funding and power-sharing strengthen community advocates' capacity to challenge inequities, although advocacy alone cannot resolve systemic disparities [18]. Short-term or externally controlled funding can compromise the independence of community organisations [18], as can contracting through procurement and performance management [63]. Funding would therefore need to be long-term and leave these organisations free to challenge the services they work with. It should also avoid adding to the unpaid, emotionally taxing work of evidencing racism that already falls on Black women and advocates [64, 65].

4.1. Implications for policy, practice, and future research

Our findings suggest that more measurement of racism alone will not change care. To counter credibility discounting, services could ask women directly about racism and record their accounts alongside complaints and survey scores, as these accounts may be the only record of near misses. They could also invite women to raise concerns through community organisations, and for several years after their care has ended, as recognising harm takes time. To counter neutralisation, board reports on safety and experience data [8, 62] could record the response to each concern about racism and the reason for any decision not to act. Ethnicity data could cover earlier decisions such as referrals, with the reasons for them, using categories that reflect the diversity of women served [8]. Some participants proposed that regulators such as the Care Quality Commission review this record. Regulators are not neutral readers, however; the national investigation heard of families ignored in services rated 'good' and asked the Nursing and Midwifery Council to check staff fitness to practise referrals for racial bias [8].

Community organisations working with Black families should therefore review this reporting alongside regulators and share decisions on how services respond. To counter containment, commissioners and integrated care boards could move funding for gathering evidence of racism to community organisations that support Black families, on a long-term basis that protects their independence, while services remain accountable for acting on what they find. Services should also tell those who raised concerns what was done, as the belief that nothing will change leads women to hold concerns back [7]. Because we did not compare how services handled evidence of racism with other service failures, future studies could test whether these mechanisms are specific to evidence of racism, for example by comparing complaint outcomes by ethnicity and type of concern. Research with maternity staff and boards could show how the mechanisms operate inside services. Research with migrant Black women and Black staff could test whether the hierarchy described in one group operates through the suggested mechanisms. The participants' framework could be developed into a practical tool and evaluated, including the burden it places on women and advocates. Comparing health systems that fund and govern community advocacy differently could show whether moving funding to community organisations changes how evidence of racism is used.

4.2. Strengths and limitations

Lived experience co-researchers shaped the design, facilitation and interpretation, sharing the judgement between accounts that in critical realist research often rests with the researcher [30]. Co-facilitation by Black women may have made it easier for participants to describe anti-Black racism with a non-Black researcher present [26, 27]. Including mothers and advocates combined women's accounts of their own care with advocates' accounts of how services handle concerns across many families. The analysis aimed to explain rather than describe, and each mechanism was tested against rival explanations.

The mechanisms are proposed explanations, and we do not claim that they are exhaustive or that they operate in the same way in other settings. The study relies on four focus groups of mixed size in one city. It did not include staff or service perspectives, so the containing and neutralising mechanisms rest partly on advocates' and mothers' views of how services are run. Data collection coincided with the publication of reports from two independent investigations into NHS maternity care [8, 10], which may have shaped advocates' accounts.

We also did not compare how services handled evidence of racism with other service failures, so the claim that racism faces barriers beyond a general defensive culture rests on participants' accounts. Some participants knew the lived experience co-researchers or the first author, which may have made it more comfortable to name racism as an explanation and describe experiences that might otherwise have gone unreported. We addressed this through reflexive memos and shared interpretation with the lived experience co-researchers.

Finally, London has more community organisations working with Black families than most of England, so participants may have been better able to name racism than families elsewhere.

Several mechanisms, however, operate through national structures such as complaints processes and incentive schemes, and we argue transferability through these mechanisms rather than the representativeness of the sample [36].

5. CONCLUSION

Evidence of racism in maternity care is increasingly collected but seldom leads to action.

Black mothers' and community advocates' accounts suggest why. Black women's concerns may be discounted before they are recorded, recorded evidence absorbed or deflected through the processes services use to demonstrate performance, and change that would shift power or accountability withheld. More evidence alone is therefore unlikely to change care, as participants asked who decides when evidence is enough. They proposed that services record what they do with each concern, that this record be reviewed independently and that those who raise concerns be protected, making visible a decision not to act.

Change is more likely where services also shift power and money to community organisations working with Black families, while remaining accountable for acting on evidence of racism.
