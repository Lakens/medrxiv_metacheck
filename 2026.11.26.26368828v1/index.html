How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Background: Array-based methylation profiling with the Heidelberg classifier supports WHO-CNS5 classification of CNS tumors, but its substantial hands-on time, cost, centralized infrastructure and dependence on externally-hosted software limit broad implementation. Nanopore methylation sequencing combined with AI-based classification is a viable alternative, yet performance evidence required for implementation in adult CNS tumor diagnostics is limited. We test the equivalence of nanopore methylation profiling using the Sturgeon-v2 classifier against a WHO-CNS5-compliant integrated reference diagnosis incorporating array-based classification.

Methods: We compared Sturgeon-v2 classifications from nanopore sequencing of fresh-frozen tissue DNA with array-based reference diagnoses of matched FFPE tissue DNA, in two cohorts of 154 and 47 primary CNS tumors. Nanopore-derived copy-number alteration (CNA) profiles complemented Sturgeon-v2 predictions to resolve low-confidence calls. CNA-derived tumor cell fractions and histopathological evaluation determined tissue adequacy. Outcomes were categorized as concordant; discordant; additional analyses needed; or tissue inadequate for methylation profiling. Equivalence required the 95% upper confidence limit (95UCL) for discordance to be below 10%. Sturgeon-v2 classification and CNA analysis were implemented in the containerized pipeline NEURON.

Results: No discordant outcomes were observed (95UCL: 1.7%), with consistent performance across cohorts. Of 201 samples, 26 were inadequate due to low tumor purity or poor tissue quality. Of the remaining 175 samples, 167 were concordant with the reference diagnosis; eight required additional analyses.

Conclusion: Diagnostic equivalence and reproducibility across independent cohorts, together with the publicly available, locally-executable NEURON workflow, provide evidence and tools to support laboratory validation, accreditation and routine implementation of nanopore methylation profiling for adult CNS tumor classification.

Introduction

Rapid advances in elucidating the (epi)genetic basis of primary central nervous system (CNS) tumors have yielded major biological and clinical insights [1] [2] [3]. Although histopathological evaluation has long formed the cornerstone of CNS tumor diagnosis, it does not have the capacity to adequately capture the extensive biological heterogeneity of these neoplasms while subject to substantial inter-observer variability. An early example was the incorporation of the 1p/19q status in IDH-mutant glioma to distinguish astrocytomas from oligodendrogliomas, thereby resolving the diagnostic ambiguity associated with the former oligoastrocytoma tumor type [4] [5] [6]. Molecular diagnostics have become central to CNS tumor classification, with new molecular insights continuously refining tumor taxonomy and clinical decision-making [1, 7]. This shift is reflected in the fifth edition of the World Health Organization (WHO) classification of CNS tumors (WHO CNS5), which mandates integration of histopathological and molecular features for diagnosis [8].

Next to molecular approaches such as next generation sequencing panels, genome-wide DNA methylation profiling has emerged as a particularly powerful diagnostic tool. WHO CNS5 strongly emphasizes its use as a desirable, and in some cases an essential criterion for accurate classification [9, 10]. Additionally, application of methylation profiling has enabled the identification of novel tumor entities, as reflected in the continuous expansion of the random forest-based Heidelberg CNS tumor classifier, which now distinguishes 184 methylation subclasses (v12.8) [1].

In routine adult neuro-oncology practice, array methylation profiling is frequently applied to adulttype diffuse gliomas, with data uploaded to the Heidelberg platform for classification [1]. This provides an objective classification while simultaneously yielding diagnostically relevant copy number aberrations (CNA) and O6-methylguanine-DNA methyltransferase (MGMT) promoter methylation status. Previous studies have shown that DNA methylation profiling confirms the original (immuno)histochemical diagnosis in most CNS tumors and provides diagnostically relevant refinement or reclassification of 10-25% of particularly of diagnostically challenging tumors [11] [12] [13] [14]. However, array-based methylation profiling remains dependent on dedicated, capital-intensive microarray platforms, creating a need for diagnostically equivalent alternatives that require lower capital investment, offer greater workflow flexibility and allow local control over data analysis, thereby broadening access worldwide [15].

Recent advances in nanopore DNA sequencing offer a powerful alternative for molecular diagnostics. Low-coverage nanopore workflows coupled with machine-learning classifiers can rapidly achieve CNS tumor classification, including CNA profiles using sparse CpG methylation data [16] [17] [18]. Nanoporebased methylation and CNA profiling for CNS tumors have been validated in pediatric cohorts and used routinely in the intraoperative setting at several hospitals [19, 20]. In adults, feasibility has been shown [20], but the performance evidence needed for confident implementation and accreditation in routine diagnostics remains limited. Here, we formally test the diagnostic equivalence of a nanoporebased molecular diagnostic workflow on fresh frozen tissue (hereafter 'nanopore workflow'), against strict WHO CNS5-compliant integrated reference diagnoses (hereafter 'reference diagnosis') that incorporated routine histomolecular diagnostics, including Heidelberg FFPE-based array classification, in two independent retrospective cohorts of predominantly adult-type diffuse gliomas. The nanopore workflow is implemented in NEURON [21], a locally executable, containerized pipeline that automates methylation-based tumor prediction with Sturgeon-v2 (Vermeulen et al. medRxiv, 2026), genomewide CNA analysis and tumor purity quality control. The study is designed to reflect routine postoperative neuropathology practice and provides the performance evidence required for clinical implementation and accreditation.

Methods

Patient selection

We retrospectively assembled a Dutch cohort of 154 patients and Portuguese cohort of 47 patients, diagnosed with a primary CNS tumor, mostly comprising adult-type diffuse gliomas. In the Dutch cohort, eligible cases were selected based on prior DNA methylation profiling using the Illumina 850K EPIC or 900K EPICv2 array in combination with classification by the Heidelberg CNS tumor classifier (predominantly v11b4). To enable robust validation of the nanopore workflow against a reliable reference standard, the Dutch cohort only included cases with confident Heidelberg family scores (≥0.84 for v11b4 and/or ≥0.90 for v12.8) and fulfilling WHO CNS5 diagnostic criteria. Most of these cases (119/154; 77%) also achieved a confident Heidelberg class score. In addition, all reference diagnoses were independently confirmed using methylation data analysed with the NCI/Bethesda CNS tumor classifier v2.0 [22]. In the Portuguese cohort, case selection was based on fulfilment of WHO CNS5 diagnostic criteria, rather than methylation classifier confidence alone, allowing assessment of the feasibility of nanopore sequencing in a diagnostic setting, without pre-selection of cases with high-confidence methylation-based classifiers outcomes. 26/47 samples had a confident Heidelberg v12.8 family score, of which 19 samples had a confident Heidelberg v12.8 class score as well.

Where available, additional molecular information, including immunohistochemistry and nextgeneration sequencing (NGS) gene panel data, was considered during case selection to support a confident reference diagnosis.

Oxford Nanopore Technology (ONT) sequencing currently requires fresh-or fresh frozen tissue-derived DNA to achieve optimal throughput and data quality, in contrast to methylation array workflows for CNS tumor diagnostics which are generally performed on FFPE-derived DNA. Consequently, only patients for whom matched frozen tumor tissue was available were included. This design resulted in a patient-matched rather than DNA-matched comparison between workflows, precluding a direct oneto-one DNA comparison. For all patients included, comprehensive histomolecular data were available (Supplementary Table 1).

Tissue sectioning, DNA extraction and nanopore-sequencing

Samples for nanopore sequencing were processed as received from the neurosurgical procedure to reflect routine diagnostic practice, without preselection of tissue material. Representativity of frozen tumor material was assessed post nanopore sequencing, using haematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining performed on 4 µm sections, obtained immediately before and after sectioning of tissue obtained for genomic DNA isolation.

For each fresh frozen tissue sample, 5 unstained sections of 20 µm thickness were collected for DNA extraction. In samples where the maximum tissue diameter was <3 mm, 10 unstained sections of 20 µm were collected. Genomic DNA was isolated using the QIAamp DNA Micro Kit (Qiagen; cat nr: 56304) according to the manufacturer's instructions and eluted in 30 µL of nuclease-free water. DNA quantity was determined using a Qubit 3 fluorometer (Invitrogen, Q33216) using the Qubit dsDNA BR assay kit (Invitrogen, Q33230). For DNA fragmentation, a solution of 250 ng genomic DNA in 50 µL nuclease-free water was sheared at 6000 x g for 1 minute using a Covaris G-TUBE (SKU: 520079) to achieve a targeted fragment size of approximately 5 kb after DNA library preparation. After fragmentation, DNA was concentrated using a ThermoSavant SpeedVac (SPD111V-115) for ~30 minutes and reconstituted in 10 µL nuclease-free water. A total of 200 ng genomic DNA (in 10 µL) was required as input for library preparation with the ONT's Rapid Barcoding Kit 24 (RBK114.24), following the manufacturer's protocol.

In the Dutch cohort, samples were multiplexed with n=20 per PromethION flow cells (R10.4.1) and performed on an Oxford Nanopore Technologies' P2 solo device for 48 hours (MinKNOW 25.03.9). In the Portuguese cohort, samples were multiplexed with n=5 using minION (R10) flowcells on a gridION device.

Data processing and analysis

POD5 files generated by the Oxford Nanopore Technologies sequencer were processed on an HPC cluster using an in-house pipeline (https://gitlab.com/hg-aumc/bioinf/pipelines/sushi), using a GPU for basecalling and CPU for subsequent analyses. In short, basecalling was performed using Dorado (v0.7.2) with the high-accuracy model dna_r10.4.1_e8.2_400bps_hac@v4.2.0, enabling modified base detection for 5mC and 5hmC. Demultiplexing was performed using Dorado demux. Basecalled reads were aligned to the BSgenome.Hsapiens.NCBI.T2T.CHM13v2.0 reference genome using minimap2 (v2.26). Read-level quality control was conducted using NanoPlot and NanoLyse (v1.41.0), and summary metrics were compiled using MultiQC (v1.14).

Aligned bam files were sorted and indexed using Samtools (v1.18). Aligned bam files were analyzed using the NEURON pipeline [21], that performed Sturgeon prediction, CNA analysis, tumor purity estimation and reporting. In short, sturgeon is a CNS neural network classifier, originally based on the reference dataset published by Capper et al., 2018[11, 16]. Sturgeon-v2 has been trained on an extended dataset of nearly 10,000 methylation profiles and includes a redesigned classifier architecture (Vermeulen et al. medRxiv, 2026). CpG methylation calls were extracted using Modkit (v0.5.0, ONT). The resulting data were converted into the required input format for Sturgeon-v2 tumor prediction. Sturgeon-v2 predictions with a confidence score of ≥0.8 were considered confident. For all IDH-wildtype glioblastoma outcomes, 'family' scores are reported, which were obtained by summing the Sturgeon-v2 class scores, as it is currently only diagnostically relevant to diagnose a glioblastoma, rather than its molecular (sub)class (e.g. RTK1 or RTK2).

CNA analysis was performed using the QDNAseq package (v1.42.0), with a setting for sparse whole genome sequencing data by employing 500 kb bin sizes [23]. Read coverage was normalized against a 35x deep nanopore-sequenced Genome in a Bottle reference dataset [24] to correct for nanoporespecific mapping biases against the BSgenome.Hsapiens.NCBI.T2T.CHM13v2.0 genome. CGHcall (v2.68.0) was used to call CNAs [23, 25]. Bin annotations were generated using the BSgenome.Hsapiens.NCBI.T2T.CHM13v2.0 genome and were filtered to exclude low mappability regions, ENCODE blacklisted intervals, and bins with low complexity or insufficient base coverage [26]. Normalized read counts were segmented using the DNAcopy package (v1.80.0) to identify regions of genomic gain or loss [27] Tumor purity and ploidy were inferred from the CNA data using ACE (v1.24.0) [28], applying the squaremodel function for model fitting. This approach infers absolute copy number states and estimates the tumor cell percentage based on best-fit integer copy number solutions across segmented regions. When the model estimates 100% tumor fraction, a visual inspection of fits and CNA profile were performed. If the profile was entirely flat, the estimate was adjusted to 'below detection limit'. If not, the next best fit was used to estimate the tumor fraction. When ACE estimated the tumor purity below 20%, we deemed the CNA profile indicative of low tumor purity.

Statistical analyses

For each case, Sturgeon-v2 prediction was followed by nanopore-derived CNA profiling to assign a final nanopore workflow outcome. Outcomes were categorized as concordant or discordant compared to the reference diagnosis; or additional analysis required or tissue inadequate for methylation profiling.

Samples were considered concordant only when the nanopore workflow yielded a WHO CNS5compliant diagnosis, incorporating required defining molecular features such as IDH mutational status for astrocytoma and oligodendroglioma (the latter with 1p/19q codeletion) or class characteristic CNAs (gain of chromosome 7 combined with loss of chromosome 10 (+7/-10) and/or EGFR amplification) for glioblastoma, IDH-wildtype. Diagnostic equivalence was assessed by estimating the proportion of discordant samples across the combined cohorts. A one-sided 95% upper confidence limit (L) for this proportion was calculated using an exact binomial approach (Pearson-Clopper). Equivalence was defined a priori as L < 0.10.

Samples lacking a tractable nanopore workflow outcome, including low-confidence calls without low tumor purity estimates, or absence of required defining molecular features, were assigned the outcome 'additional analysis needed'. Samples failing quality control due to poor tissue quality or low tumor purity, as determined by post-sequencing neuropathological review and tumor purity estimation inferred by ACE, were assigned the 'tissue inadequate for methylation profiling' outcome. 'Additional analysis needed' and 'tissue inadequate for methylation profiling' samples were not considered as true discordant in equivalence testing.

Illumina EPICv2 arrays

To justify 'tissue inadequate for methylation profiling' and 'additional analysis needed' workflow outcomes, a subset of 23 DNAs of fresh frozen tissues, initially subjected to nanopore sequencing, underwent Illumina EPICv2 array profiling followed by Heidelberg v12.8 classification. As only one fresh frozen specimen was available per patient, this analysis provided information on the impact of intratumoral and sampling heterogeneity between the patient-matched samples and informed on whether Sturgeon-v2 low confident calls or control tissue predictions reflected insufficient tumor content of the fresh frozen specimens rather than true diagnostic disagreement. 200 ng DNA in 45 µL nuclease-free water was used as input material.

Results

General cohort characteristics

A total of 154 patients' CNS tumors were included in the Dutch cohort with the following reference diagnoses: astrocytoma, IDH-mutant (n=38), oligodendroglioma, IDH-mutant, and 1p/19q-codeleted (n=35), and glioblastoma, IDH-wildtype (n=50). In addition, an 'other CNS tumors' category was included, consisting of meningioma (n=22), atypical teratoid rhabdoid tumor (n=1), ependymoma (n=1 spinal, n=1 myxopapillary and n=1 posterior fossa group B), high-grade astrocytoma with piloid features (n=1), a medulloblastoma, SHH-activated (n=1), pilocytic astrocytoma (n=2) and pleomorphic xanthoastrocytoma (n=1). The independent Portuguese cohort included glioblastoma, IDH-wildtype (n=43), astrocytoma, IDH-mutant (n=1), medulloblastoma, SHH-activated (n=1) and CNS tumor with BCOR internal tandem duplication (n=2). Detailed clinical, molecular, and radiological characteristics are provided in Supplementary Table 2 for the Dutch cohort and Supplementary Table 3 for the Portuguese cohort.

The results presented below follow the structure of nanopore workflow, starting with primary methylation-based tumor prediction by Sturgeon-v2, followed by assessment of CNA profiles, ultimately comparing the final workflow outcome to the reference diagnosis.

Sturgeon-v2 performance

In the Dutch cohort, Sturgeon-v2 analysis yielded correct tumor predictions in 129/154 samples (83.8%) compared to the reference diagnosis (at the family level for glioblastoma, IDH-wildtype and class level for the rest of the entities). 124/154 samples (80.5%) exceeded the confidence threshold, while 5/154 samples (3.2%) were predicted correctly but did not reach the confidence threshold. 3/154 samples (1.9%) showed an incorrect prediction, one confident and two low-confident. In addition, 22/154 samples (14.3%) were predicted as control tissue, all with a confident prediction (Figure 1). Median confidence score was 0.99 (range: 0.47 -1.0). Across the Portuguese cohort, Sturgeon-v2 yielded 40/47 (85.1%) confident correct predictions, 6/47 (12.8%) predictions of control tissue (four confident and two low-confident) and 1/47 (2.1%) low-confident incorrect prediction (Figure 2). Median confidence score was 0.99 (range: 0.48 -1.0).

The three incorrect predictions in the Dutch cohort were observed in the following samples; sample 109 was confidently predicted to be an astrocytoma, IDH-mutant (Sturgeon-v2 confidence score: 0.99), whereas the reference diagnosis was oligodendroglioma, IDH-mutant, and 1p/19q-codeleted (CNS WHO grade 3). Samples 103 and 122 were predicted with low-confidence to be a ganglioglioma (Sturgeon-v2 confidence score: 0.47) and an astrocytoma, IDH-mutant (Sturgeon-v2 confidence score: 0.56), respectively, whereas the reference diagnosis of both was oligodendroglioma, IDH-mutant, and 1p/19q-codeleted (CNS WHO grade 2). In the Portuguese cohort, sample IPATIMUP0067 was predicted subependymal giant cell astrocytoma (SEGA) with low confidence by Sturgeon-v2 (confidence score 0.77), while the reference diagnosis was an IDH-wildtype glioblastoma.

For these four samples, alternative CNS tumor classifiers (CrossNN and MethyLYZR) yielded both correct and incorrect predictions as summarized in patient-level heatmaps (Supplementary Figure 1 and Supplementary Figure 2). All samples were further evaluated by integration of genome-wide CNA profiles as part of the nanopore workflow.

Refinement of Sturgeon-v2 predictions following integration of CNAs

Nanopore-derived genome-wide CNA profiles were evaluated across all samples, focusing on predefined, entity-characteristic diagnostically relevant CNAs. Namely, +7/-10 and/or EGFR amplification in glioblastoma, IDH-wildtype, and complete 1p/19q codeletion in IDH-mutant oligodendroglioma. CNA profiles were not used to support diagnostic interpretation in astrocytoma, IDH-mutant, or in the 'other CNS tumors' category, as no defining CNAs are currently specified for these entities. In addition, tumor cell percentages were inferred from the CNA profiles using ACE as a quality control metric. Samples were categorized as having diagnostically relevant CNAs, no diagnostically relevant CNAs or low estimated tumor purity.

Across the Dutch cohort, 75/154 samples (48.7%) showed diagnostically relevant CNAs, 64/154 samples (41.6%) did not show diagnostically relevant CNAs, and 15/154 samples (9.7%) were suggestive of low tumor purity based on ACE (Figure 1). Between the Sturgeon-v2 confident correct predictions (124/154), CNAs were diagnostically supportive in 68/124 samples (54.8%) and did not show diagnostically relevant CNAs in 56/124 samples (45.2%). Between the low-confident correct calls (5/154), CNAs were diagnostically supportive in 1/5 samples (20.0%) and did not show diagnostically relevant CNAs in 4/5 samples (80%). Control tissue predictions (all confident; 22/154) were frequently associated with low estimated tumor purity. Specifically, 15/22 control tissue predictions (68.2%) showed low estimated tumor purity, while 4/22 (18.2%) showed diagnostically relevant CNAs and 3/22 (13.6%) did not.

Across the Portuguese cohort, 32/47 samples (68.1%) showed diagnostically relevant CNAs, while 11/47 (23.4%) did not show any diagnostically relevant CNAs. The remaining 4/47 samples (8.5%) had CNA profiles indicative of low tumor purity as estimated by ACE (Figure 2).

Final nanopore workflow outcomes and diagnostic equivalence to the reference diagnosis

Across the Dutch cohort, 127/154 samples (82.5%) yielded a nanopore workflow outcome concordant with the reference diagnosis, 7/154 samples (4.5%) required additional analyses, and 20/154 samples (13%) were considered inadequate for methylation profiling by histopathological review and lowestimated tumor purity based on ACE. Excluding these QC failed samples, the workflow reached 94.8% concordance with the reference diagnosis (Figure 1). Finally, no discordant nanopore workflow outcomes were observed.

Across the Portuguese cohort, 40/47 samples (85.1%) yielded a nanopore workflow outcome concordant with the reference diagnosis, 1/47 samples (2.1%) needed additional analyses, and 6/47 samples (12.8%) were deemed inadequate for methylation profiling. Excluding these, the nanopore workflow reached 97.6% concordance with the reference diagnosis, while the remaining sample required additional analyses (Figure 2). Consistent with the Dutch cohort, no discordant nanopore workflow outcomes were observed in the validation cohort.

Across the combined cohorts, the 95% upper confidence limit (95UCL) for the true mismatch rate was 1.7%. To illustrate the diagnostic interpretation of the nanopore workflow, three cases are highlighted below.

Exemplary samples illustrating the nanopore workflow

Sample 2 was selected as a clear example of a confident correct Sturgeon-v2 prediction (confidence score: 1.0). CNA analysis revealed diagnostically supportive EGFR amplification and +7/-10, with an inferred tumor purity estimate of 85%. Sturgeon-v2 based its prediction on 31,646 CpG methylation sites (Figure 3A). Of note, while part of the tissue was necrotic, Sturgeon-v2 was still able to correctly and confidently predict this sample, which emphasizes its sensitivity. The final workflow outcome was concordant with the reference diagnosis. Sample 10 was selected to illustrate a control tissue prediction with a high Sturgeon-v2 score (confidence score: 0.91) while the reference diagnosis was oligodendroglioma, IDH-mutant, and 1p/19q-codeleted (Figure 3B). ACE estimated a tumor purity below 20%, based on the CNA profile. Sturgeon-v2 used 36,891 CpG methylation sites for prediction. Reassuringly, rather than predicting a (low)-confident tumor, Sturgeon-v2 confidently predicted this sample as control tissue, emphasizing its specificity. DNA-matched analysis using Heidelberg v12.8 confirmed Sturgeon-v2 prediction (Supplementary Figure 1). Therefore, this frozen tissue sample was deemed inadequate for diagnostic methylation profiling. Sample 109 was selected as it concerns the single confidently incorrect prediction by Sturgeon-v2, which predicted the sample to be an astrocytoma, IDH-mutant (confidence score: 0.99; Figure 3C). Sturgeon-v2 prediction used 44,965 CpG methylation sites, and the CNA-derived tumor purity estimation was 49%. The CNA profile showed a complete 1p/19q codeletion, which supports the oligodendroglioma reference diagnosis and resolves this sample towards a concordant final nanopore workflow outcome, as an astrocytoma, IDH-mutant cannot harbour a complete 1p/19q codeletion. Interestingly, Heidelberg v12.8 classification of this sample in a DNA-matched methylation array analysis showed subclassification of oligosarcoma (confidence score 0.90), despite a non-sarcomatous phenotype. In routine diagnostic practice, bona fide oligodendrogliomas, IDH-mutant and 1p/19qcodeleted may occasionally align with IDH-mutant astrocytoma on array-based methylation profiling, as previously described [29].

DNA-matched array profiling for validation of selected Sturgeon-v2 outcomes

To validate control tissue and incorrect Sturgeon-v2 predictions (confident or low-confident), microarray profiling with Heidelberg v12.8 classification was performed on the same fresh-frozen DNA used for nanopore sequencing. This analysis was done to determine whether these predictions reflected insufficient tumor-derived methylation signal or were attributable to tissue heterogeneity (n=23; 17 confident control, 1 confident incorrect, 1 low-confidence incorrect, 2 confident correct, and 2 low-confidence correct Sturgeon-v2 predictions).

Overall, Sturgeon-v2 predictions and Heidelberg v12.8 classifications were concordant in 19/23 samples (82.6%). Among the 17 Sturgeon-v2 control tissue predictions, 15/17 (88.2%) were also independently classified as control tissue by Heidelberg v12.8. The remaining 2/17 samples (11.8%) were both confidently classified as glioblastoma, IDH-wildtype by the Heidelberg v12.8 classifier. 3/23 Sturgeon-v2 tumor predictions (13.0%) showed low confidence (two correct and one incorrect). Of these, Heidelberg v12.8 showed matching classifications in 2/3 samples (66.7%). The remaining lowconfident incorrect sample was classified as low-confident correct by Heidelberg v12.8. The single confident incorrect Sturgeon-v2 prediction (sample 109) was correctly classified by Heidelberg v12.8 at the class level (confidence score 0.99), although incorrectly classified at the subclass level (oligosarcoma, IDH-mutant; confidence score 0.90) (Supplementary Figure 3).

Heatmap highlighting patient-specific heterogeneity within tumor groups

To examine concordance and heterogeneity across classifiers and molecular features at the patientlevel, an heatmap representation for the Dutch cohort is shown in Figure 4. Two versions of the Heidelberg classifier are shown, as a subset of samples was obtained after release of version v11b4 and could only be classified using version v12.8. Across both cohorts, most confident correct Sturgeon-v2 predictions showed the expected patterns compared to the reference diagnosis, with CNA profiles, histology and tumor purity estimates aligning within individual samples. Locally executable workflow

The diagnostic equivalence and cross-cohort reproducibility presented here support implementation of the nanopore workflow in routine diagnostic practice. To facilitate local workflow implementation, we developed a containerized pipeline called NEURON that automates methylation extraction, Sturgeon classification, CNA analysis and tumor purity estimation from nanopore sequencing data and compiles this into one molecular report. To ensure reproducibility, both source code and container are publicly available through github and Zenodo (see Data availability).

Discussion

DNA methylation profiling is nowadays a desirable and sometimes essential tool for the diagnosis of many CNS tumor entities [7, 9, 10] and incorporated into multiple diagnostic guidelines [30] [31] [32]. In this study, we demonstrated that a nanopore-based methylation sequencing workflow using Sturgeon-v2 and CNA analysis achieves diagnostic equivalence to a WHO CNS5-compliant integrated reference diagnosis for CNS tumors in adults. Together with the containerized pipeline NEURON and the publicly available Sturgeon-v2 classifier these findings support local implementation in routine post-operative neuropathology practice worldwide. No outcomes discordant with the reference diagnosis were observed in either the Dutch or the Portuguese cohort. After exclusion of samples inadequate for methylation profiling, concordance with the reference diagnosis reached 95.4% across the combined cohorts, with the remaining 4.6% requiring additional analyses. Compared with the workflow using the Sturgeon-v1 model [16], this performance reflects a shift toward increased specificity at the expense of modestly reduced sensitivity. Low-confidence predictions in otherwise adequate samples might relate to model recalibration following expansion of the training dataset [33]. In line with this, the number of confident incorrect predictions decreased from five with Sturgeon-v1 to one with Sturgeon-v2 (Supplementary Figure 4). Control tissue predictions and low-confidence calls were strongly associated with insufficient tumor signal, typically due to low tumor cell content or extensive necrosis. This was supported by complimentary microarray-based analysis using the same DNA as used for nanopore sequencing, where most Sturgeon-v2 control tissue predictions were validated by control tissue classifications using the Heidelberg v12.8 classifier (Supplementary Figure 3). These findings underscore that most discrepancies do not arise from incorrect prediction, but from biological and technical limitations such as low tumor purity or extensive tissue necrosis, observed in both the Dutch and Portuguese cohorts. Similarly, samples requiring additional analyses frequently showed only partial or absent glioblastoma-associated CNAs (+7/-10 and/or EGFR amplification), aberrations that are supportive yet do not independently establish a glioblastoma diagnosis [7, 34].

Applying an ACE-estimated tumor purity cut-off of 20% after nanopore sequencing excluded 18/22 (81.8%) control tissue predictions of Sturgeon-v2 in the retrospective setting (Supplementary Figure 5). The increased number of Sturgeon-v2 control tissue predictions than expected from the reference diagnoses likely reflects differences in selection of material from the patients following the procedures of formalin fixation versus fresh material. For routine methylation array workflows, pathologists typically select the most representative tumor tissue from multiple FFPE blocks. In some cases, a representative region within the selected tissue is then demarcated on an FFPE section prior to DNA isolation which ensures optimal tissue quality and high tumor cell percentage. In contrast, nanopore sequencing in this study was performed using a single fresh-frozen tissue sample without prior tumor region selection, making the workflow more susceptible to low tumor purity and tissue necrosis. For clinical implementation, we recommend to mitigate this limitation before nanopore sequencing through careful sampling of representative tumor regions and, where feasible, inclusion of multiple fresh tissue fragments to select the most representative tissue.

Importantly, there were still occurrences where Sturgeon-v2 prediction was either inconclusive or confidently wrong, even with sufficient tumor purity. Integrating CNA data proved crucial in resolving those cases, as exemplified by confident misclassification of Dutch sample 109 (Figure 3C). Thus, confident predictions should not be accepted uncritically, as rare but relevant misclassifications occur. Conversely, low-confidence results should not be dismissed when in line with other data, as highconfidence scores are achieved in only 50-70% of cases in real-world cohorts, indicating that lowconfidence predictions remain common but informative [13, 14, 35, 36]. From a technical perspective, multiplexing up to 20 samples (dependent on the type of flow cell) and sequencing for 48 hours provided sufficient coverage for robust classification and CNA profiling in most cases. Increasing sequencing depth by reducing multiplexing and increasing sequencing time would be expected to further improve CNA resolution and can enable more reliable detection of additional genomic features, including somatic mutations, structural variants such as translocations and MGMT promoter methylation [20, 37, 38]. However, this must be balanced against cost-efficiency in routine practice. Several limitations should be considered. While MGMT promoter methylation assessment is feasible [20, 37, 39], this remains dependent on sufficient CpG promoter coverage and was not addressed in this study. Nevertheless, NEURON is equipped to predict MGMT promoter methylation status by leveraging a previously published prediction model [40]. In addition, the requirement for fresh frozen tissue represents a logistical challenge, as FFPE material remains the standard in diagnostic pathology. On the other hand, nanopore sequencing measures methylation directly on native DNA, thereby avoiding bisulphite conversion artifacts [41]. Nevertheless, recent technological advances may enable high-quality, high-throughput analysis of FFPE-derived DNA, facilitating simultaneous genome-wide methylation profiling and whole-genome sequencing in routine pathology workflows (Kokoris et al. bioRxiv, 2026). Informative methylation profiles can also be generated from FFPE material using nanopore sequencing; however, fragmentation and chemical damage of FFPEderived DNA can increase variability in sequencing output and may impact cost and time required for robust classification [42, 43]. In parallel, nanopore whole-genome sequencing and targeted enrichment approaches such as adaptive sampling may further expand the diagnostic capabilities of the proposed workflow by improving coverage of clinically relevant genomic regions [39]. Furthermore, although our cohort included several uncommon CNS tumor entities, it was primarily powered to evaluate the most prevalent CNS tumors in adults encountered in routine diagnostics. Consequently, performance estimates for rarer entities remain limited. However, as the Sturgeon-v2 model was trained on a broad reference dataset encompassing many rare tumor entities, this limitation relates to clinical validation rather than classifier development.

In conclusion, the nanopore workflow demonstrated diagnostic equivalence to the WHO CNS5compliant integrated reference diagnosis for CNS tumors in adults, which incorporated conventional array-based classification. Integration of genome-wide CNA profiling and tumor purity assessment proved essential for robust diagnostic interpretation, particularly in low tumor purity samples. These findings provide the necessary performance evidence supporting implementation of nanopore methylation sequencing using Sturgeon-v2, as a scalable and potentially cost-effective alternative to array-based methylation profiling for routine CNS tumor diagnostics in adults. Our containerized pipeline NEURON facilitates a locally executable and reproducible workflow, enabling implementation across pathology laboratories worldwide. As methylation-based classifiers expand, this study may serve as precedent towards extending the approach to other tumor types, particularly for diagnostically challenging entities such as head and neck including salivary gland tumors, lymphomas, and sarcomas, where methylation profiling is promising [44] [45] [46].
