Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

The marginal module searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The list of terms is a subset of those listed in a blog post by Matthew Hankins. The module returns all sentences that match terms describing ‘marginally significant’ results.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. Some differences come from the ground truth: it lacks p-values with a correction name (“p Bonf = .061”), and counts partial eta squared written with its subscript after it (“η 2 p = .05”), “p = .05”, and some table notes and figure legends (“† p < .10, *p < .05”), which this module leaves out, as non-significant p-values. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Abstract

Background: Although most individuals with schizophrenia do not show aggressive behavior, rates are higher than in the general population. Identifying the neurobiological correlates of aggression may inform prevention.

We included 2260 individuals with schizophrenia spectrum disorders and 3058 healthy controls (HC) from 26 sites in the ENIGMA-Schizophrenia Working Group. The former were classified as showing at least moderate aggression (SZ+A; N = 231) or not (SZ-A; N = 2029) based on symptom rating scales. Structural covariance networks were constructed from cortical and subcortical volumes. We examined global and nodal network topology, as well as density within six large-scale brain networks and between each network and the rest of the brain. Metrics were summarized as the area under the curve (AUC) across a prespecified range of densities (K = 0.10-0.25, ΔK = 0.01). Group differences were assessed using pairwise permutation tests with Benjamini-Hochberg correction for multiple comparisons.

Results: Global and nodal network topology did not differ significantly between SZ+A and HC. Network-rest density of the salience network (SN) was higher in SZ+A than in HC, although this difference did not survive multiple-comparison correction (ΔAUC = 0.018, pBH = .083). In exploratory subgroup analyses, higher SN network-rest density was observed only in SZ+A with high delusion severity (ΔAUC = 0.021, p = .007). By contrast, SZ-A demonstrated extensive alterations in global network topology compared with SZ+A and HC, alongside selective alterations in nodal topology and large-scale network density compared with HC. Results were broadly consistent across sensitivity analyses using alternative definitions of aggression and adjusting for current antipsychotic dose.

Conclusions: SZ+A was characterized by fewer-not more-abnormalities in structural covariance network organization than SZ-A, which may reflect etiological heterogeneity. In SZ+A with high delusion severity, higher network-rest density of the SN may point to aberrant salience processing in aggressive behavior.

Introduction

Aggression among individuals with schizophrenia represents a major clinical challenge, with substantial consequences for themselves, victims, and mental healthcare systems (1, 2). Although uncommon, the risk of aggressive behavior is higher in this population than in the general population (3). Several clinical and environmental risk factors have been identified, including positive symptoms, cognitive impairment, poor illness insight, substance misuse, and social adversity (1, 4). However, the neurobiological mechanisms underlying aggression in schizophrenia remain unclear. Elucidating these mechanisms may improve risk stratification, inform preventive interventions, and identify novel therapeutic targets.

Converging evidence from animal, lesion, and neuroimaging studies implicates distributed cortical and subcortical regions in aggressive behavior, including prefrontal, cingulate, parietal, and insular cortices, as well as the hippocampus, amygdala, and striatum (5) (6) (7). Many of these regions are key nodes of the salience network (SN), central executive network (CEN), default mode network (DMN), and reward network (RN) (8, 9). Several also belong to a network involved in emotion regulation (10) (11) (12), referred to here as the affective control network (ACN). However, aggression likely emerges from coordinated interactions throughout the brain rather than from individual regions or large-scale brain networks in isolation (13), highlighting the importance of examining the brain as an interconnected system.

Schizophrenia has long been conceptualized as a disorder of dysconnectivity, in which disrupted interactions among brain regions underlie its symptoms and clinical features (14). Consistent with this conceptualization, widespread abnormalities in gray matter (GM) volume and white matter (WM) microstructural integrity have been reported (15) (16) (17), alongside structural and functional abnormalities of the SN, CEN, DMN, RN, ACN, and language network (LN) (18) (19) (20). Abnormalities in these networks have been linked to positive symptoms (21) (22) (23), cognitive impairment (24) (25) (26), and poor insight (27, 28). Together, these findings suggest overlap with networks implicated in aggression.

This overlap provides a rationale for examining brain network organization in relation to aggressive behavior in schizophrenia. Graph theory provides a framework for quantifying brain network organization (29). Brain networks can be constructed from magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) data as structural covariance networks (SCNs) based on interregional correlations in GM morphology, structural connectomes based on WM pathways, or functional connectomes based on interregional correlations in brain activity. These network types reflect partly distinct biological processes (30). In schizophrenia, a meta-analysis of global network topology found reduced segregation and integration in structural connectomes but no consistent alterations in functional connectomes (31). At the nodal level, alterations in structural and functional connectomes vary considerably in direction and anatomical distribution (32, 33). Findings from the comparatively limited number of SCN studies are mixed across global and nodal metrics (34). However, neuroimaging studies of aggression in schizophrenia have largely focused on global or regional measures of GM volume, WM integrity, and functional connectivity (35). To our knowledge, only one study has investigated brain network organization. Graph-theoretical analysis of SCNs showed higher global efficiency and shorter characteristic path length in patients with a history of serious violence than in those without such a history and healthy controls. Average nodal degree was higher in nonviolent patients (36). Interpretation is limited by the small, all-male forensic sample and retrospective assessment of violence.

To address this gap, we investigated the association between brain network organization and aggressive behavior in a large international sample of individuals with schizophrenia spectrum disorders and healthy controls. We used graph-theoretical analysis of SCNs to examine differences in global and nodal network topology, as well as the density of large-scale brain networks, among patients with (SZ+A) and without (SZ-A) clinically significant aggression and healthy controls (HC). We expected both SZ+A and SZ-A to show altered brain network organization relative to HC, consistent with the dysconnectivity hypothesis of schizophrenia. We also expected SZ+A and SZ-A to differ in nodal topology and large-scale network density, particularly in regions and networks implicated in aggressive behavior. Given inconsistent graphtheoretical findings in schizophrenia and the paucity of studies on aggression, we did not formulate directional hypotheses.

Methods

Participants

This study was conducted within the Enhancing NeuroImaging Genetics through Meta-Analysis (ENIGMA) consortium's Schizophrenia Working Group (ENIGMA-SZ), with data contributed by 26 sites across 14 countries. The pooled sample comprised 2260 individuals with schizophrenia spectrum disorders and 3058 healthy controls. Consistent with previous ENIGMA-SZ studies and for readability, the term "schizophrenia" is used throughout the manuscript. Patients were recruited from various mental healthcare settings, including psychiatric hospitals, outpatient clinics, and residential care facilities. Diagnoses were established according to Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM)-III (37), DSM-IV (38), DSM-5 (39), or International Classification of Diseases (ICD)-10 criteria (40). Ethical approval was obtained from the local ethics committee at each site, and all participants provided written informed consent. Some participants were also included in two previous ENIGMA-SZ studies examining associations between aggression and regional brain structure (41, 42).

Image acquisition and processing

All sites acquired high-resolution T1-weighted magnetic resonance imaging scans (voxel size ≤1 mm 3 ), which were processed using FreeSurfer (43) in accordance with ENIGMA-SZ protocols. Based on the Desikan-Killiany atlas (44), structural measures were extracted for 34 cortical regions (cortical thickness [CT] and surface area [SA]) and 7 subcortical regions (volume) in each hemisphere. Intracranial volume (ICV) was also extracted. Site-specific acquisition and processing details are provided in Table A1.1. Left and right hemisphere values were averaged to obtain bilateral measures. If a value for one hemisphere was unavailable, that from the contralateral hemisphere was used. Cortical volume was calculated as the product of CT and SA. Regional measures were then harmonized across sites using ComBat (45), while preserving biologically relevant variation attributable to age, sex, and study group. Finally, measures were residualized for age, sex, and ICV using linear regression models to minimize their potential confounding effects on group comparisons.

Assessment of aggression and other clinical variables

Depending on the site, aggression was assessed using item 10 ("hostility") of the Brief Psychiatric Rating Scale (BPRS) (46), item P7 ("hostility") of the Positive and Negative Syndrome Scale (PANSS) (47), or item B3 ("aggressive and agitated behavior") of the Scale for the Assessment of Positive Symptoms (SAPS) (48). The PANSS was administered at most sites (k = 20), followed by the SAPS (k = 4) and the BPRS (k = 2). To harmonize scores across instruments, we collapsed the highest two response categories of the BPRS and PANSS (scores 6 and 7). Because a score of 7 was rare (n = 4), this recoding was unlikely to materially affect the results. Scores were then recoded to a common 0-5 scale. Patients were classified as SZ+A or SZ-A based on a predefined cut-off corresponding to at least moderate aggression (harmonized score ≥ 3). This cut-off was chosen to identify clinically meaningful aggression and is consistent with those used in previous studies of other symptom domains based on the same instruments (49) (50) (51).

Positive symptoms (delusions, hallucinations, and disorganized thinking), negative symptoms (blunted affect, alogia avolition, and anhedonia/social withdrawal), poor impulse control, and lack of illness insight were assessed using corresponding items from the BPRS, PANSS, or SAPS (Table A1.2). The same harmonization procedure was applied to these symptom domains. Patients were classified as having early-course schizophrenia if they had first-episode psychosis (FEP) or a duration of illness (DOI) of ≤ 5 years. This accommodated the lack of DOI data in most FEP cohorts and was based on commonly used definitions of early-course schizophrenia (52). Patients with a DOI > 5 years were classified as having chronic schizophrenia. Definitions of FEP and DOI varied across contributing cohorts. FEP was typically defined as first presentation with psychosis, first treatment contact, first psychiatric admission, or entry into an early intervention program. DOI was calculated as the time between illness onset and assessment, with the former most commonly approximated by age at FEP or first diagnosis. Information was also available on current antipsychotic treatment status and dose. Antipsychotic doses were converted to chlorpromazine (CPZ) equivalents according to Woods et al. (53).

Structural covariance network construction

We constructed SCNs separately for each group (SZ+A, SZ-A, and HC). Pearson correlation coefficients were calculated between all pairs of regional volumes (30), resulting in 41 × 41 association matrices. Diagonal elements and negative correlations were set to zero (54). Networks were thresholded across a range of densities (K = 0.10-0.25, ΔK = 0.01), retaining the strongest correlations at each density. Proportional thresholding facilitated comparisons by maintaining equivalent network densities across groups (55). The selected range is consistent with those used in previous ENIGMA studies of SCNs (56) (57) (58) (59), including one in which it was selected based on prespecified criteria for connectedness, modularity, and small-world organization (56).

Calculation of graph-theoretical metrics

Graph-theoretical metrics were summarized as the area under the curve (AUC) across the specified density range using the trapezoidal rule (60). Summarizing metrics across density thresholds reduced dependence on any single, arbitrarily selected density threshold (61).

Global and nodal network topology

We selected a parsimonious set of graph-theoretical metrics to capture complementary aspects of network topology. For global network topology, we calculated transitivity, modularity, global efficiency, and small-worldness. These metrics quantify clustering, community segregation, network-wide integration, and the balance between segregation and integration, respectively (62). Modularity was estimated using the Louvain algorithm (63). Because the Louvain algorithm is non-deterministic and can yield different solutions across runs, it was repeated 100 times at each density threshold, with the median modularity value retained to provide a robust estimate (64). Small-worldness was calculated relative to 200 degree-preserving rewired graphs (10 × E rewiring steps) using the largest connected component (65).

For nodal network topology, we calculated degree, harmonic centrality, betweenness centrality, within-module degree (WMD), and participation coefficient (PC), which quantify nodal connectivity, proximity to other nodes, involvement in shortest paths, connectivity within communities, and connectivity across communities, respectively (62). Harmonic centrality was used instead of conventional closeness centrality, as it remains well-defined in disconnected graphs (66). To calculate WMD and PC, community partitions were first identified using Infomap at each density threshold (67). These partitions were then combined into a co-classification matrix. Each value in this matrix represented the proportion of partitions in which a pair of nodes was assigned to the same community. Values below τ = 0.50 were set to zero, and the resulting weighted matrix was repartitioned using Infomap to generate a consensus partition (68). This partition was held fixed across density thresholds, providing a consistent community reference for the calculation of WMD and PC (69).

Large-scale brain network density

We calculated within-network density and network-rest density for six large-scale brain networks: SN, CEN, DMN, LN, RN, and ACN (see Table A1.3 for definitions). These networks were selected based on their relevance to schizophrenia and aggression, as outlined in the Introduction. Within-network density was defined as the number of connections that were present among nodes within a given network relative to the total number of possible connections among those nodes. Network-rest density was defined as the number of connections that were present between nodes in a given network and nodes outside that network, relative to the total number of possible connections between these two sets of nodes. These metrics quantify the internal connectivity of a network and its connectivity with the remainder of the brain, respectively (70).

Statistical analyses

Differences in demographic and clinical characteristics between SZ+A and SZ-A patients were assessed using independent-samples t-tests, χ 2 tests, or Mann-Whitney U tests, as appropriate.

In the primary analyses, we compared graph-theoretical metrics derived from volume-based SCNs among SZ+A, SZ-A, and HC using pairwise permutation testing (10 000 permutations). For each permutation, group labels were randomly reassigned while preserving the original group sizes (71). The nominal level of statistical significance was set at 5% (two-sided). To account for multiple comparisons, false discovery rate (FDR)-corrected p-values were calculated using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure (72). Within each group comparison, FDR correction was applied to predefined families of tests: all global metrics; within-network density and network-rest density, each across the six large-scale networks; and each nodal metric across the 41 brain regions. Nonparametric bootstrapping (73) (5000 resamples) was performed to estimate 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for all metrics.

We conducted two exploratory post hoc subgroup analyses. First, because illness stage differed between SZ+A and SZ-A, and GM loss is particularly pronounced during the early course of schizophrenia (74), we examined whether illness stage contributed to the group differences in global network topology. These analyses were repeated separately in patients with early-course and chronic schizophrenia, with HC included in both. Correction for multiple testing was applied as in the primary analyses. Second, patients were stratified by delusion severity ('low' vs 'high') to examine differences in SN network-rest density. This analysis was motivated by the nominally significant difference in SN network-rest density between SZ+A and HC, together with the higher delusion severity in SZ+A than in SZ-A and the hypothesized role of aberrant salience in delusion formation (75). Delusion severity was dichotomized using the same procedure as for aggression. As only SN network-rest density was examined within each group comparison, no correction for multiple testing was applied.

Prespecified sensitivity analyses evaluated (i) a cut-off corresponding to at least mild aggression (harmonized score ≥ 2), (ii) aggression defined using PANSS item P7 alone, and (iii) additional residualization of regional volumes for antipsychotic dose, expressed as chlorpromazine (CPZ) equivalents.

Analyses were performed in R (version 4.4.1), with graph-theoretical metrics calculated using the igraph package (76). The first author (JL) used ChatGPT (OpenAI) to assist with drafting, refining, and troubleshooting R code for data analyses and figure generation. Suggestions from ChatGPT were reviewed and, where applicable, tested before being incorporated. No study data were entered into ChatGPT, and all analytical decisions were made by the authors.

Results

Sample characteristics

Across sites, the mean age was 34 years (SD = 12) in patients and 33 years (SD = 12) in HC. The proportion of male participants was higher among patients (n = 1451, 64%) than among HC (n = 1466, 48%). Most patients were receiving antipsychotic medication (n = 1794, 88%), with a median CPZ-equivalent dose of 300 mg (interquartile range [IQR] = 378). Sample characteristics by site are summarized in Table A2.1.

Aggression scores were concentrated at the lower end of the harmonized scale, with similar distributions across instruments (Table A2.2). Overall, 231 patients (11%) met criteria for at least moderate aggression. SZ+A scored significantly higher than SZ-A on positive symptoms, impulsivity, poor illness insight, and several negative symptoms. They were also more likely to be in the early course of illness and had a lower median CPZ-equivalent dose (Table 1).

Primary analyses

Global network topology SZ+A and HC did not differ significantly on any metric of global network topology (Fig. 1; Table A3.1). Compared with both other groups, SZ-A showed lower modularity and small-worldness. Transitivity was also higher and efficiency lower in SZ-A than in HC.

Nodal network topology

At the nodal level, no significant differences were found between SZ+A and either SZ-A or HC. In contrast, SZ-A differed from HC on most metrics (Fig. 2; Tables A3.2-A3.6). Degree was lower in the temporal pole, entorhinal cortex, parahippocampal gyrus, lingual gyrus, cuneus, pericalcarine cortex, caudate nucleus, and nucleus accumbens. Betweenness centrality was lower in the entorhinal and pericalcarine cortices, while harmonic centrality was lower in the temporal pole, entorhinal cortex, parahippocampal gyrus, lingual gyrus, cuneus, pericalcarine cortex, and nucleus accumbens. Participation coefficient was lower in the parahippocampal gyrus and cuneus. Degree, betweenness centrality, and participation coefficient were higher in the globus pallidus.

Large-scale brain network density

Network-rest density of the ACN was significantly higher in SZ-A than in HC (Fig. 3; Table A3.7). At the nominal significance level, network-rest density of the SN was higher in SZ+A than in HC (p = .014).

Exploratory post hoc subgroup analyses

The differences in global network topology observed for SZ-A in the primary analyses were confined to early-course patients (Table A4.1). No significant differences emerged in chronic SZ-A or between SZ+A and HC at either illness stage.

Network-rest density of the SN was higher in SZ+A with high delusion severity compared with HC (Table A4.2). No other group differences were significant.

Discussion

Overall, the findings suggest that aggression is not associated with more pronounced or widespread illness-related alterations in structural brain network organization. Despite more severe positive symptoms, greater impulsivity, and poorer illness insight, SZ+A did not significantly differ from HC in global or nodal network topology. The only indication of altered SCN organization in SZ+A was higher SN network-rest density than in HC, although this difference did not survive correction for multiple comparisons. By contrast, SZ-A showed extensive alterations in global network topology compared with SZ+A and HC, alongside selective alterations in nodal topology and large-scale network density compared with HC. These findings were robust across sensitivity analyses using alternative definitions of aggression and accounting for current antipsychotic dose.

There are several possible explanations for the limited evidence of altered SCN organization in SZ+A relative to HC. First, aggressive behavior among individuals with schizophrenia may arise through multiple pathways, including acute psychosis, impulsivity, substance misuse, and social adversity (1). The neurobiological basis of aggression may differ across these pathways, potentially obscuring group-level differences in structural covariance. Second, substance useparticularly cannabis use-may precipitate schizophrenia independently of genetic liability (77) or despite relatively preserved premorbid cognitive (78) or social functioning (79). Because substance use is also associated with aggression (80), patients following this pathway may be overrepresented in SZ+A. If they have less pronounced or distinct structural brain changes, as suggested by previous studies (81, 82), SCN organization may appear preserved. This possibility could not be examined in the present study. Third, the stability of GM morphology over time (83) may mean that SCN alterations are more strongly associated with persistent than with statedependent aggression. Our short reference period may have disproportionately captured the latter, particularly given the greater severity of concurrent positive symptoms we observed in SZ+A. Finally, aggression-related alterations in brain network organization may be more evident in structural or functional connectomes than in covariance of GM morphology. Although the relatively small SZ+A sample may have reduced statistical power, point estimates were generally similar to HC, providing little indication of substantial group differences.

The higher SN network-rest density may reflect aberrant salience processing-a mechanism implicated in the formation and maintenance of delusions (75). Consistent with this interpretation, higher SN network-rest density was observed only in SZ+A with high delusion severity in subgroup analyses. Our previous ENIGMA-SZ study similarly found that delusions partly mediated the associations of reduced global GM volume and WM integrity with aggression (41). Dysregulated salience processing could also contribute to aggression more directly through heightened attention to threatening or provocative stimuli (84) and an increased tendency to interpret ambiguous stimuli as such (85). However, the difference in SN network-rest density did not survive correction for multiple comparisons in the primary analyses, and the subgroup analyses were conducted post hoc. These findings should therefore be considered hypothesisgenerating.

In SZ-A, we observed increased transitivity and reduced modularity, efficiency, and smallworldness, indicating greater local clustering, reduced global integration, and weaker community segregation. Notably, these alterations were confined to early-stage schizophrenia, possibly reflecting the rapid and unevenly distributed GM loss that occurs during the first five years after illness onset (74, 86). Changes in the spatial distribution of GM loss over the course of illness may contribute to the absence of comparable alterations in chronic schizophrenia. This interpretation remains speculative and should be tested in longitudinal studies. At the nodal level, reduced centrality and participation in temporal, occipital, and subcortical regions may reflect a diminished role in information transfer and intermodular communication. The globus pallidus showed the opposite, with increased centrality and participation. This finding may partly relate to antipsychotic exposure, which has been associated with structural changes in the basal ganglia (87). Analysis of large-scale brain networks revealed increased network-rest density in the ACN. Together, these findings involved regions and large-scale networks associated with memory, visual processing, reward processing, and emotion regulation-domains commonly affected in schizophrenia (19, 26, 88, 89).

Strengths and limitations

To our knowledge, this is the largest study to date investigating the association between structural brain network organization and aggressive behavior in any population. Including SZ+A, SZ-A, and HC enabled us to distinguish alterations associated with aggression from those associated with schizophrenia more broadly. Additional strengths include recruitment of patients from diverse mental healthcare settings worldwide and standardized image processing protocols across sites.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the study was cross-sectional, and aggression was assessed over a relatively short period. This precluded assessment of changes in SCN organization over time and distinction between transient and persistent patterns of aggressive behavior. Second, we investigated brain network organization based on regional GM volume because it captures the multidimensional nature of cortical morphology while providing a common measure across cortical and subcortical regions. However, this approach does not permit inference about the relative contributions of CT and SA. Given their distinct developmental and genetic determinants (90), analyses of these phenotypes may provide complementary insights. Third, aggression was assessed with single items from instruments developed to assess symptom dimensions of schizophrenia. Multi-item instruments specifically developed to assess aggression would likely provide greater sensitivity. Fourth, constructing SCNs at the group level required dichotomization of aggressive behavior, potentially reducing sensitivity to variation in severity. Group-level estimation of graph-theoretical metrics also required permutation testing of group differences, which precluded covariate adjustment for potential confounders.

Nevertheless, important sources of methodological and biological variation were reduced before network construction by harmonizing regional volumes across sites and residualizing them for age, sex, and intracranial volume. Individual-level SCN methods have recently been developed, allowing dimensional modeling. However, these approaches construct SCNs from perturbations of a normative covariance network (91) or from joint variation in regional deviations from normative morphometry (56, 57). As such, they address different research questions. Fifth, although sensitivity analyses accounted for current antipsychotic dose, the influence of cumulative antipsychotic exposure-which may affect brain structure (92)-and confounding by indication cannot be fully ruled out. Finally, we lacked information on social risk factors, substance misuse, and specific cognitive deficits, which may confound or mediate associations between brain structure and aggression. Nevertheless, comparisons between SZ+A and SZ-A across a wide range of clinical characteristics and exploratory subgroup analyses provided insight into potential mechanisms.

Clinical implications and recommendations for future research

Our findings provide little evidence that group-level SCNs derived from regional GM volume can serve as markers of aggressive behavior in individuals with schizophrenia. This is in line with the wider neuroimaging literature on aggression, in which associations with brain structure have been inconsistent (7). A better understanding of aggressive behavior will likely require the integration of neurobiological, psychological, and social risk factors (1). If replicated, the finding of increased SN network-rest density in SZ+A could motivate investigation of aberrant salience processing as a mechanism contributing to aggressive behavior in schizophrenia, particularly in the presence of severe delusions. More broadly, our findings suggest that associations between brain structure and aggressive behavior may vary according to the severity of specific symptoms. Such heterogeneity may help explain the inconsistent findings reported across neuroimaging studies of aggression in schizophrenia (35) and should be investigated further.
