To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 3 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). Statcheck might still miss one-sided tests that are described in another sentence, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

If you want to extend the package to be able to download files from additional data repositories reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Parkinson's disease (PD) is a progressive neurodegenerative disorder affecting roughly one million people in the U.S. People with PD (PwPD) exhibit classic motor symptoms that limit performance on functional tasks and reduce quality of life (QOL). Despite functional ability (FA) being a critical indicator of patient well-being, objective and quantitative measurement of FA during activities of daily living (ADLs) is not a part of standard clinical assessments. To address this gap, we conducted a pilot study of PwPD (n=39) and healthy controls (n=7) performing ADLs. We attached nine-axis inertial measurement units (IMUs) to toothbrushes and hair combs to collect continuous acceleration and angular velocity data during toothbrushing and hair-combing tasks. After preprocessing, we visualized signals with kernel density estimation and continuous wavelet transforms, revealing characteristic distributional, temporal, and spectral patterns. We then segmented signals into 3-9 second non-overlapping intervals. From each interval, we extracted 30 features (13 acceleration-derived, 17 angular-velocity-derived) and trained eXtreme Gradient Boosted (XGBoost) classifiers to discriminate disease state and severity, symptom presence, and impaired FA. Notably, models trained on hair-combing data discriminated PwPD from controls (balanced accuracy 0.864) and identified patient-reported impairment in FA (0.869). Models trained on toothbrushing data achieved moderate performance on the same tasks (balanced accuracy 0.752 and 0.686, respectively). These preliminary findings suggest that short-segment IMU data from common ADL implements can quantitatively characterize disease severity and task-specific performance in PwPD, supporting their integration into routine clinical care.

[div-01]

The ability to perform activities of daily living (ADLs) without assistance is a critical determinant of quality of life (QOL) in PD. Loss of FA increases comorbidity risk and is closely associated with depression, social isolation, and reduced dignity [6] [7] [8]. Occupational therapy has emerged as an evidencebased intervention that can limit FA decline in PD, with therapists providing personalized strategies to help patients maintain mobility and ADL performance despite motor impairments [9] [10] [11].

Widely used clinical rating scales, including the Unified Parkinson's Disease Rating Scale (UPDRS) and its Movement Disorder Society-sponsored revision (MDS-UPDRS), incorporate ADL and QOL assessments. However, these scales are nonlinear, discontinuous, and often rely on subjective patient reporting, which introduces bias and limits measurement precision [12, 13]. Time and operational constraints further lead clinicians to prioritize motor examinations (e.g., UPDRS Part III) during revisits, often neglecting direct evaluation of FA despite its importance [14]. Motor examinations are also subject to intra-rater variability. Consequently, current assessments remain non-standardized, qualitative, and limited in interpretability and clinical actionability. This gap highlights the need for objective, quantitative tools to monitor FA during ADLs, which serve as proximate measures for overall independence [15]. Such tools could provide a consistent and quantitative way to measure patient outcomes across therapeutic interventions and inform the caretaking team.

Sensor-based technologies have emerged as a promising solution to this unmet need, offering noninvasive, quantitative, and continuous real-time motion measurement [16 -18]. Inertial measurement units (IMUs)-which capture triaxial acceleration, angular velocity, and orientation data-have commonly been mounted on the limbs or trunk to measure movement for a low-cost [19]. Using signal processing and machine learning (ML) techniques, IMU data have been used to identify cardinal PD symptoms, distinguish PwPDs from healthy controls, and quantify longitudinal changes in motor state [20 -23].

Critically, few comprehensive datasets capture PwPD performing ADLs, and consequently, few models can estimate FA using movement features derived from these QOL-relevant tasks [24, 25]. Such data would provide direct insight into patient QOL. Although some IMU datasets exist for specific ADLs (e.g., toothbrushing), they are often limited to healthy populations, precluding comparison with PwPD [26, 27]. Other studies do include PD participants but are restricted by small sample sizes or narrow clinical scope [28, 49, 50].

To address this, we collected movement data from PwPD and controls during two ADLsbrushing teeth and combing hair-generating a larger dataset than previously reported for these tasks. We characterized distributional differences; extracted temporal, statistical, and spectral features; and used them to identify those with impaired task performance. Our preliminary results suggest that impaired task performance and disease severity can be effectively quantified using short segments of this modality, which may support scalable, objective assessment of FA in home-based studies.

II. Methodology

II. Methodology

A. Study Design

PD patients (n = 39) and controls (n = 7) capable of performing ADLs were analyzed in this report, which includes participants enrolled through March 19 th , 2026. This cohort represents the initial cohort collected by investigators; enrollment is ongoing. Participants were recruited from multiple sites within the Johns Hopkins Medicine system under IRB-approved protocol #00442448, with additional controls recruited from the Johns Hopkins University Homewood Campus. All participants provided written informed consent. Exclusion criteria included diagnoses of other neurological or psychological conditions affecting movement or participation ability. Controls-typically care partners of enrolled patients to ensure age-matching-were defined as individuals without clinically diagnosed PD or other movement disorders.

Prior to task performance, Parts I-III of the UPDRS were administered to PwPD to assess symptom severity [29]. Clinical notes regarding motor patterns, brushing technique, dominant hand, hair type, and at-home toothbrush type (manual/electric) were documented for contextual reference. Controls were not evaluated by a neurologist and were assigned naive UPDRS subscores of 0. No protected health information (e.g., age, name, sex) was collected; however, trials were videotaped to provide a visual reference for the recorded motion data.

UPDRS Parts I-III subscores were summed to compute several composite scores reflecting distinct clinical domains: rigidity (RG), bradykinesia (BK), functional ADLs (FT), resting tremor (TR), action/postural tremor (AT), Part III total (P3) and total Part I-III UPDRS score (TOT) (Table 1). For example, subscores "3.23 Finger Taps", "3.24 Hand Movements", and "3.25 Rapid Alternating Movements of Hand" for the corresponding side used during the task were used to calculate the BK composite score. For upper body related scores, only subscores corresponding to the side used during task performance were included (Supplemental Table 1).

Motion data (i.e., acceleration and angular-velocity data) was collected using a WitMotion WT9011DCL Bluetooth 5.0 Accelerometer, a 9-axis, off-the-shelf inertial measurement unit (IMU) (manufactured by WitMotion Shenzhen Co., Ltd, ShenZhen China) containing an accelerometer, gyroscope, magnetometer, and other sensors [30]. Accelerometers were connected to mobile devices via the WitMotion application, and they were calibrated before each data collection session using a built-in "acceleration calibration" setting. Sensor "bandwidth" (sampling frequency) was set to 98 Hz. The sensor was attached to a standard toothbrush or standard comb with a 3D-printed plastic holder. Separate holders were designed to be kept secure on the toothbrush and comb with friction. The sensor was held on the holder with a rubber band near the bottom of the shaft. The position of the sensor partially obstructed the handle but largely did not affect participant arm motion as they performed the activities. (Fig. 1a, 1b).

First, participants performed either toothbrushing or hair-combing without a specified duration to approximate typical home performance times (Fig. 1c-f). Following this initial trial, participants completed two or three additional one-minute trials, with the number determined by the length of the first trial. If the participant's first trial was already longer than one-minute, only two additional trials were performed; if not, three were performed. Continuous motion data were captured throughout all trials. After data ingestion, they were preprocessed, visualized, and used for a machine learning inference pipeline (Fig. 1h).

B. Preprocessing & Feature Extraction

Signals shorter than 30 seconds were excluded to remove any trials likely to be incomplete or not focused on the ADL for an extended period. Those with sensor artifacts that rendered them unusable were excluded as well (n = 6 participants). For both angular-velocity and acceleration time-series, the x-, y-, and z-components were combined into a single magnitude signal. Gravity's contribution was removed from the acceleration magnitude using the Euclidean Norm Minus One (ENMO) method [31]. However, negative values were not clipped to zero to preserve a symmetric distribution about zero. Outliersdefined as absolute values exceeding three standard deviations from the mean magnitude-were replaced with the mean. Signals were segmented into non-overlapping intervals of 3, 5, 7, and 9 seconds [32, 33]. From each window, thirty features were extracted, and a separate machine learning analysis was performed for each interval length (Supplemental Table 2).

C. Kernel density estimation

Both the acceleration and angular velocity signals were used to construct kernel density estimator (KDE) plots using a Gaussian kernel with Silverman's rule [34]. The KDE plots were used to both visualize the distributions of signal values, generate statistical features, and check for statistically significant differences. KDE plots were generated for both brushing and combing activities separately to account for task-specific variation. These KDE plots were also used to qualitatively compare acceleration and angular velocity distributions between experimental groups, notably between PD patients and controls and between different disease severities. Unlike other analyses, signals for KDE visualization were not preprocessed with ENMO. Instead, the mean was subtracted from each signal to center its KDE at 0 g. This approach facilitated direct comparison of distributional spread across populations and protected against outlier signals that were not centered at 0.

D. Power spectral density

To assess signal features in the frequency domain, power spectral density (PSD) plots were generated from the acceleration magnitude signal using a Welch's method-inspired approach [35]. A Hanning window was applied to each full-length signal to reduce spectral leakage, with appropriate correction for the window's energy, followed by computation of the Fast Fourier Transform (FFT). A onesided PSD was derived by taking the squared magnitude of the FFT coefficients at positive frequencies, applying a scaling factor of 2 excluding the DC and Nyquist components. Finally, PSD values were normalized by the window energy, sampling rate, and number of points to yield a proper power spectral density estimate.

E. Continuous wavelet transform

To assess how frequency contribution evolved during task performance, continuous wavelet transform (CWT) plots were generated based on the first 30 seconds of each signal; signals with less than 30 seconds of data were excluded [36]. To generate the CWT, a complex Morlet wavelet ('cmor1.5-1.0') was used as the mother wavelet. The analysis was performed over a frequency range of 0.5 to 20 Hz, relevant for human movement, using 80 linearly spaced frequency points. For each time point t, the squared magnitude of the CWT coefficients across all frequencies was computed to obtain a time-varying power spectrum. The spectrum at each time point t was normalized to integrate to 1 across frequencies, converting it into a probability density function (PDF). Therefore, these plots represent the relative distribution of signal power across frequencies at each moment in time, independent of the total power.

F. Computation of complex plot CWT ellipse area

In addition to analyzing the entire time-frequency matrix generated by CWT analysis, a single compact feature (the ellipse area of the CWT complex plot) was computed from each signal's CWT representation. G. XGBoost ensemble model inference task

We framed the analysis as both a binary and multiclass classification task. The vector of features extracted from the sensor data was the model input, and a binary prediction was output for each composite score: whether the score was zero or positive. Labels were assigned at the participant level. For higherresolution classification, the TOT score was used to group participants into four classes (Class 0: TOT 0; Class 1: TOT 1-32; Class 2: TOT 33-56; Class 3: TOT 57+). All analyses were performed separately for the toothbrushing and hair combing tasks. Furthermore, to determine the optimal signal segmentation, the entire modeling pipeline was independently executed for non-overlapping window lengths of 3, 5, 7, and 9 seconds, with the goal of identifying the interval length that delivered the best overall performance across all tasks (Supplemental Table 3).

1) Classification

We use gradient-boosted decision trees as implemented in the open-source XGBoost package [38] with default hyperparameters; no tuning was performed. Data were split 80/20 into training and test sets and were grouped by participant so that no participant appeared in both. This split was not stratified by class. Features were standardized using statistics from only the training set. An XGBoost model with scaled positive-and negative-class weight (for binary tasks; class weighting was not applied in the multiclass task) was trained and then tested on the held-out set. We report balanced accuracy (BA; unweighted mean of per-class recall) due to class imbalance (Fig. 1g). We then performed stratified, participant-grouped, tenfold cross-validation. Cross-validation was repeated ten times with different seeds to yield 100 scores per model. We choose to report the average and standard deviation of the BA across the cross-validation as a robust and stable measure of performance across different folds. Ninety-five percent confidence intervals were constructed using the mean BA for each repeat, and paired t-tests (with Holm-Bonferroni p-value correction) were used to assess significant differences between the grand mean of each interval length. Extreme outliers were 95% winsorized.

2) Feature Importance

To improve explainability of the XGBoost models, we employed the Shapley Additive Explanations (SHAP) approach and its corresponding open-source package [39, 40]. For each model trained, mean absolute SHAP values were calculated on the held-out test set and used to determine the ten most relevant features for the classification task. These were displayed on beeswarm plots for visual inspection. Volcano plots were also produced to investigate significance versus effect size of all features between PwPD and controls.

H. Software

All analysis was conducted using Python 3 and standard packages such as NumPy, Pandas, Scikit-Learn, XGBoost, and SHAP. Continuous wavelet transformations were applied to the signals using the PyWavelets package [41]. Visualization was done through Matplotlib and Seaborn. Analysis was conducted using the SAFE Desktop virtual machine system provided by Johns Hopkins Research Information Technology (Windows 11, 6 CPUs, 24GB RAM, 1GB GPU).

III. Experimental Results

Kernel density estimation reveals progressive, bradykinesia-sensitive distributional differences in ADL-based movement data

PwPD classically present with bradykinesia, the presence of slowed or diminished movements [42]. We hypothesized that bradykinesia would be evident in distributional changes in accelerometry (measuring acceleration) and gyroscope (measuring angular velocity) data. To assess distributional changes between PD patients and controls, and patients of varying disease severity, we generated kernel density estimation (KDE) plots of acceleration or angular velocity data. KDE analysis smooths discrete time-series data into a continuous probability density function (PDF) by summing Gaussian kernelseach centered on an individual data point-and normalizing the result.

We predicted that patients with more severe bradykinesia would display a narrower, more peaked acceleration distribution centered near 0 g, reflecting fewer extreme changes in velocity. While brushing teeth, PwPD (𝜎 = 0.2941) exhibited a slightly diminished acceleration distribution compared to controls (𝜎 = 0.3164), consistent with more severe bradykinesia (p = 0.36) (Fig. 2a). These distributional differences were further pronounced during combing trials; PwPD (𝜎 = 0.2751) exhibited nearly half the variation in acceleration compared to controls (𝜎 = 0.4792; p = 0.001) (Fig. 2b). Closer examination revealed that brushing trials from two participants exhibited modes that deviated from their mean at 0 g, likely inflating the group-level variance and thereby attenuating distributional differences between PwPD and controls during brushing. These outlier patients, both of moderate severity, likely also contributed to moderate PwPD (𝜎 = 0.3363) exhibiting a slightly higher standard deviation in acceleration during brushing trials compared to mild PwPD (𝜎 = 0.2835; p = 0.723). This interpretation is supported by visual analysis of the KDE plots; while the moderate PwPD distribution exhibits a sharper peak at 0 g and decays more rapidly than mild PwPD in the positive x-direction, it also display a small bump inconsistent with a unimodal Gaussian distribution in the negative x-direction consistent with these outlier patients, likely inflating the calculated standard deviation (Fig. 2c). Similarly, during hair combing trials, moderate PwPD (𝜎 = 0.1980) displayed a less diffuse distribution than mild PwPD (𝜎 = 0.2949), supporting the severitydependent narrowing of movement dynamics (p= 0.053) (Fig. 2d).

We similarly anticipated that patients with more severe bradykinesia would exhibit heavily rightskewed angular velocity distributions, with high probability density near 0 °/s. This pattern was observed during tooth brushing, where the angular velocity distribution of PwPD (𝜇 = 50.1246; 𝑀 = 45.2665; 𝜎 = 51.8051) was shifted toward lower values and showed similar variance compared to controls (𝜇 = 74.5968; 𝑀 = 57.7341; 𝜎 = 59.0780) (𝑝 , = 0.077, 𝑝 * = 0.068, 𝑝 -= 0.142) (Fig. 2e). Consistent with increasing bradykinesia, moderate PwPD (𝜇 = 49.3942; 𝑀 = 34.0756; 𝜎 = 45.2559) had an even more left-shifted and less variable distribution than mild PwPD (𝜇 = 62.6003; 𝑀 = 47.7953; 𝜎 = 52.8937) (Fig. 2g). During hair combing, this effect was equally pronounced: PwPD distributions (𝜇 = 110.8401; 𝑀 = 90.5921; 𝜎 = 85.0817) were markedly left-shifted and less variable relative to controls (𝜇 = 187.7073; 𝑀 = 164.6107; 𝜎 = 118.0935) (𝑝 , = 0.007, 𝑝 * = 0.024, 𝑝 -< 0.001) (Fig. 2f). Again, moderate PwPD (𝜇 = 80.8019; 𝑀 = 62.3939; 𝜎 = 63.7316) displayed a more left-shifted and narrower distribution than mild PwPD (𝜇 = 119.6160; 𝑀 = 99.0159; 𝜎 = 89.6267) (Fig. 2h). Together, these findings reflect a severity-dependent narrowing of rotational movement in patients with bradykinesia.

To further validate and synthesize these results, we generated three-dimensional (3D) KDE plots, mapping acceleration magnitude on the x-axis, angular velocity on the y-axis, and probability density as a heatmap (Fig. 2i-l). Consistent with our earlier findings, PwPD with more severe bradykinesia exhibited tighter, less diffuse probability distributions, with the highest density concentrated near the origin (0 g, 0 °/s).

Power spectral density analysis uncovers energy-independent movement frequency differences during ADL performance

Given the observed distributional differences in acceleration between PwPD and controls, we expected corresponding differences in acceleration signal energy. During brushing tasks, PwPD exhibited lower and less variable accelerometry signal energy (𝑀 = 2.6896 * 10 $ ; 𝑄1 = 1.3810 * 10 $ ; 𝑄3 = 5.6444 * 10 $ ) compared to controls (𝑀 = 6.8953 * 10 $ ; 𝑄1 = 2.3004 * 10 $ ; 𝑄3 = 1.2968 * 10 . ) (Fig. 3a). This difference was equally evident during combing trials, where PwPD again showed reduced and less variable signal energy (𝑀 = 2.3968 * 10 $ ; 𝑄1 = 1.3976 * 10 $ ; 𝑄3 = 3.9467 * 10 $ ) relative to controls (𝑀 = 1.0316 * 10 . ; 𝑄1 = 7.8699 * 10 $ ; 𝑄3 = 1.8202 * 10 . ) (Fig. 3b).

To compare movement frequencies between groups, we computed power spectral density (PSD) normalized to signal energy over physiologically relevant frequencies (0-20 Hz). During tooth brushing, controls displayed greater spectral power in the 0-5 Hz range, with three distinct peaks, while PwPD showed only one peak around 4-5 Hz (Fig. 3c). This suggests that although both groups share a similar primary movement frequency, PwPD have more diffuse and attenuated frequency contributions outside this range, possibly reflecting difficulty in generating coordinated rotational motions. During hair combing, controls again exhibited greater spectral power in the 0-2 Hz band relative to PwPD, which may result from prolonged, rhythmic combing strokes that are harder for PwPD to sustain (Fig. 3d).

The area of continuous wavelet transform complex plots distinguishes PD patients from controls

PwPD experience a phenomenon known as the sequence effect, characterized by progressive slowing of a repetitive movement [44]. To examine how movement frequency contributions evolved during task performance, we generated continuous wavelet transforms (CWT) of accelerometry data for both PwPD and controls during tooth brushing and hair combing (Fig. 3e-h). During brushing, CWT plots corroborated PSD findings; while both groups exhibited a similar primary movement frequency, controls displayed stronger contributions at lower frequencies, whereas PwPD showed more diffuse and attenuated contributions at higher frequencies (Fig. 3e, g, i). Likewise, during combing, although PwPD and controls shared a dominant movement frequency, PwPD again demonstrated more diffuse and attenuated high-frequency activity (Fig. 3f, h, j). Contrary to expectations of gradual decline in task performance across PwPD trials, we did not observe significant changes in frequency over the course of either brushing or combing tasks.

Previous studies have shown that continuous wavelet transform (CWT) representations in the complex plane often form elliptical patterns, and the area of these ellipses has proven effective in discriminating gait patterns between PwPD and controls [37]. The area of the ellipse formed by plotting the CWT of a signal in the complex plane represents the variability and intensity of the signal. We therefore hypothesized that CWT ellipse area would quantify bradykinesia and serve as a valuable feature for classifying PwPD and control movement data from ADLs. During brushing, PwPD showed a nonsignificant decrease in ellipse area (𝑀 = 4.5001 * 10 %/ ; 𝑄1 = 1.2645 * 10 %/ ; 𝑄3 = 1.4829 * 10 %0 ) compared to controls (𝑀 = 5.2960 * 10 %/ ; 𝑄1 = 2.4665 * 10 %/ ; 𝑄3 = 1.6246 * 10 %0 ) (Fig. 3m, o). During combing, the reduction in ellipse area was statistically significant for PwPD (𝑀 = 5.0233 * 10 %/ ; 𝑄1 = 1.5605 * 10 %/ ; 𝑄3 = 1.2817 * 10 %0 ) relative to controls (𝑀 = 4.7477 * 10 %0 ; 𝑄1 = 1.5995 * 10 %0 ; 𝑄3 = 1.0790 * 10 %1 ) (Fig. 3n, p). These findings suggest that CWT ellipse area is a physiologically relevant metric for assessing movement alterations in PwPD during combing tasks.

XGBoost classification with short intervals of ADL-based movement data differentiates severity and identifies cardinal motor features in PD

Having identified key differences in ADL-based movement data between PwPD and controls, we hypothesized that these data could be used to train ML models to predict specific clinical outcomes, including the presence of cardinal motor symptoms and overall disease severity. We trained and evaluated XGBoost classification models using 30 features extracted from non-overlapping signal intervals of 3, 5, 7, and 9 seconds. For each task, model performance was measured by averaging balanced accuracies (BA) across all cross-validation (CV) folds and constructing a 95% confidence interval using the ten repeats (Fig. 4). We show this average (BA) across all classification tasks (A1-A7 and B1; Supplemental Table 3).

Models trained on both toothbrushing and hair combing data consistently outperformed chance across all fold lengths and tasks (Table 2). Notably, prediction performance was strongest for motor scores (P3), action tremor (AT), bradykinesia (BK), and functionality (FT), and weakest for rest tremor (TR) and rigidity (RG) (Fig. 4b-g). This pattern likely reflects the differential expression of these symptoms during ADLs; for instance, action tremor would be expected to manifest more prominently during ADL performance than rest tremor.

Models trained on hair combing data outperformed those trained on toothbrushing data across all tasks except RG (Fig. 4e). We found no consistent relationship between interval length and CV performance. For most tasks, no interval length significantly outperformed others, with the exception that nine-second intervals yielded significantly better performance than three-second intervals. For combing models trained to predict FT, both seven-and nine-second intervals significantly outperformed three-and five-second intervals (Fig. 4g).

Feature importance analysis reveals distinct sensor features drive predictions for brushing versus combing

To understand which features drove our models' predictions, we generated volcano plots to compare the effect size and significance between PwPD and controls for each extracted feature. For both tasks, acceleration-derived features generally exhibited larger effect sizes, whereas angular-velocityderived features reached higher significance levels, indicating that acceleration features may be more variable between groups, but less discriminative than angular velocity features (Fig. 5a, b).

From brushing data, only four angular velocity-based features and two acceleration-based features surpassed the effect-size threshold (+/-0.5 log2 Fold Change). Both acceleration features had a positive effect, while all four angular velocity features had a negative effect. From combing data, in contrast, a more features from each sensor type exceeded the effect-size threshold (ten angular-velocity features, eleven acceleration features), but angular-velocity features were markedly more significant. This indicates that angular velocity may be particularly discriminative when modeling combing movements. The most significant feature for brushing was the mode of the angular velocity distribution, and, for combing, the standard deviation & variance of the angular velocity distribution.

We additionally used SHAP analysis to corroborate our findings. We generated beeswarm plots to visualize feature importance for the XGBoost models predicting P3 (Fig. 5c, d) and TOT scores (Fig. 5e, f). These plots showed that the top predictive features for brushing were consistently dominant frequency and skew of the acceleration distribution; as well as spectral entropy of the angular velocity distribution. Additionally, certain window metrics measuring intra-signal variability ("std_of_avgs") and overall spread ("avg_of_stds") were important. In combing models, however, the most important features for both P3 and TOT predictions were consistently mean squared jerk and mean of the acceleration distribution, along with window metrics. Acceleration-based features seemed to have a higher effect on model classification.

IV. Discussion

Our preliminary findings establish the utility of ADL-based movement data for assessing PD symptoms and severity, with several potential applications for the direct evaluation of FA and QOL in PwPD. We generated KDE plots to assess distributional trends in acceleration and angular velocity data collected from PwPD of varying disease severity and controls. We found that patients with increased severity tended to have narrower distributions near the origin, which we attribute to more pronounced bradykinesia and corresponding movement decrements. We corroborated our KDE analyses by comparing signal energy; while combing their hair, PwPD had less signal energy than controls.

Using PSD and CWT analyses, we found that while both populations shared a dominant frequency across tasks, controls tended to have greater low-frequency contribution, whereas PwPD had greater, diffuse high-frequency content. Surprisingly, we did not observe the sequence effect upon CWT analysis. This may be attributable to patient overperformance of goal-oriented tasks under observation in clinic or the short 30s duration of our analyses. Building on previous findings, we identified a statistically significant difference in CWT complex plots areas between PwPD and controls while combing, which we believe is a valuable feature for future investigations.

Furthermore, we determined that XGBoost models trained on very short intervals (as low as three seconds) of brushing and combing data can feasibly identify PwPD, functional impairment, and even some cardinal symptoms. Changing input interval length did not consistently impact performance, despite our expectation that longer input intervals would yield better models. While this was true for some tasks, the relationship did not hold across ADLs within a given task, and across tasks. We note that A3 (rest tremor) and A5 (rigidity) performed the most poorly amongst all aggregate scores calculated, with balanced accuracies only slightly above chance. This mirrored our expectations; rest tremors should not be detectable during ADL performance, and rigidity is typically measured with a limp arm guided by the clinician rather than with task performance. In contrast, A7 (functional score) which is more directly relevant to ADL performance was predicted with much higher BA.

To understand which features drove model predictions, we performed SHAP analysis and generated volcano plots to identify the top ten most predictive features. Acceleration-based features seemed have the largest effect on model classification, with simple distributional features like skew, mean squared jerk, dominant frequency, and variance labeled as the highest importance features.

Our study has several important limitations. First, this is a pilot analysis with a small sample, notably of healthy controls (n = 7, compared to n = 39 PwPD). Class imbalance may limit the stability of our performance estimates. Study enrollment is ongoing, and we plan to recruit additional healthy controls and PwPD. Second, the enrolled PwPD cohort was only collected from within the Johns Hopkins system and lacked heterogeneity in disease severity, with a majority (76.9%) classified as mild (TOT: 1-32). Only those who could perform the toothbrushing and combing tasks without excessive difficulty or pain participated in the study. This limits the generalizability of our findings to individuals with moderate or advanced PD. We also note some participants experienced upper-extremity dyskinesias during their participation, as measured with the Abnormal Involuntary Movement Scale (AIMS) (n = 3/33; six PwPD could not be evaluated) [43]. These participants generally produced acceleration distributions like those of their severity-matched cohorts, but they also yielded angular velocity distributions more concentrated around the origin (Supplemental Fig. 1). Relatedly, medication status (ON/OFF) was not tracked, which may misrepresent functional ability; participants that were OFF may underperform greatly. Third, while we attempted to age-match controls by recruiting spouses and caregivers of PwPD, there remains some inherent variability within our control group. Some participants were younger, and elderly controls may have exhibited age-related motor impairments, meaning their assumed UPDRS score of zero may be too reductionistic. This could reduce the contrast between patient and control groups. Finally, the 30-second duration of our task analyses and the clinic-based observation setting may have influenced participant behavior (e.g., overperformance), potentially masking symptoms like decrement of amplitude (the sequence effect) [44].

Compared to existing sensor-based wearables and predictive systems, our approach demonstrates significant potential for providing context-specific, task-based assessments that directly reflect real-world functional ability. Many continuous monitoring systems generate a vast quantity of clinical data, much of which is useful for characterization of patient disease state; however, much of it is non-contextualized, requiring separate interpretation and integration into time-constrained clinical workflows [45]. In contrast, our method addresses this challenge by requiring only minutes of targeted, task-specific data collection, thereby enabling more focused, interpretable, and clinically actionable assessments.

Our study also contributes a valuable dataset of signals recorded during ADLs from PwPD and controls. This dataset represents a meaningful improvement over resources currently available in the literature. Existing datasets are often limited by one or more of the following: 1) inclusion of only healthy controls; 2) very small sample sizes per experimental group (𝑛 ≤ 10); 3) reliance on experimental, nonstandardized tools (e.g., self-stabilizing prototypes); or 4) limited contextual information for the recorded signals [28, 46].

One recent study reported a machine learning model for detecting wrist tremor and akinesia with accuracies of 97% and 100%, respectively, validated against MDS-UPDRS scores [47]. However, this model was trained on data from only 10 patients. In contrast, our best models across interval lengthstrained on data from a substantially larger cohort-achieved BAs of 0.669 (action tremor) using brushing data, and 0.717 using combing data. Notably, our model predicted bradykinesia from combing data with a BA of 0.703. By employing cross-validation, our results demonstrate robust performance on this larger dataset of participants.

We posit that our method could be adapted to other instrumented ADLs, such as utensil use, and potentially to more complex, unstructured tasks like dressing. Furthermore, the need for objective functional assessment extends beyond PD to other movement disorders, including atypical parkinsonisms, Essential Tremor, and limb-based dystonias. These conditions are amenable to the same analytical pipeline-kernel density estimation, continuous wavelet transforms, and feature extraction for machine learning-to derive disorder-specific motor signatures. Further validation is also needed to investigate whether our approach could feasibly track task performance longitudinally.

In summary, this work establishes a promising framework for objective monitoring of functional motor ability. By translating brief, context-specific tasks into quantifiable metrics, this approach can propel the movement disorders field closer to personalized, patient-centered care and deepen our understanding of how neurodegenerative disease impacts daily life.
