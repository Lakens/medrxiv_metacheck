Reporting p values imprecisely (e.g., p < .05) reduces transparency, reproducibility, and re-use (e.g., in p value meta-analyses). Best practice is to report exact p-values with three decimal places (e.g., p = .032) unless p values are smaller than 0.001, in which case you can use p < .001.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (e.g., “decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

P-Value Text
P ≥ 0.750 These exploratory comparisons detected no association (all P ≥ 0.750), but non-significance does not establish independence or exclude unmeasured acquisition confounding.

The APA manual states: Report exact p values (e.g., p = .031) to two or three decimal places. However, report p values less than .001 as p < .001. However, 2 decimals is too imprecise for many use-cases (e.g., a p value meta-analysis), so report p values with three digits.

American Psychological Association (2020). Publication manual of the American Psychological Association, 7 edition. American Psychological Association.

To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 2 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Metacheck does not yet analyze automatically whether sentences which include non-significant p-values are correct, but we recommend manually checking the sentences below for possible misinterpreted non-significant p values.

Text Sentence
P ≥ 0.750 These exploratory comparisons detected no association (all P ≥ 0.750), but non-significance does not establish independence or exclude unmeasured acquisition confounding.
P = 0.22 The original image-level Wilcoxon comparison for EfficientNet-B0 did not detect a difference (P = 0.22); this does not establish equivalence to random attribution or absence of class-relevant information.

Murphy S, Merz R, Reimann L, Fernández A (2025). “Nonsignificance misinterpreted as an effect’s absence in psychology: Prevalence and temporal analyses.” Royal Society Open Science, 12(3), 242167. doi:10.1098/rsos.242167.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 225 papers containing 405 instances of non-exact p-values, the module correctly detected 269 cases (true positives) and incorrectly identified 78 (false positives). It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Abstract

Objectives: To determine whether similar baseline discrimination conceals different responses to synthetic occlusion, whether loss of discrimination is accompanied by reduced confidence, and whether spatial attributions are reproducible in intraoral ultrasonography models.

Methods: We analysed 477 images from 40 healthy volunteers, using acquisition position to label superior and inferior subregions. Five architectures were evaluated across five training seeds on a subject-independent test set of 97 images from eight volunteers. Images underwent constant-intensity occlusion at boundaries spanning 30-70% of image depth and an area-matched band-occlusion control. Attribution analyses assessed variation across seeds and class-activation methods and compared attribution-guided insertion with random insertion.

Results: Seed-mean areas under the receiver operating characteristic curve ranged from 0.958 to 0.972, with rankings reversing across seeds. EfficientNet-B0 fell below 0.60 in 49 of 50 boundary-sweep conditions, compared with zero to two for the other architectures; the difference persisted with area-matched masking. Under 50% occlusion, its mean confidence on incorrect predictions rose to 0.989 and expected calibration error from 0.094 to 0.478. Between two inference runs, EfficientNet-B0's per-seed occluded AUCs differed by up to 0.161; seed means were 0.51-0.55 in both. Attribution patterns varied across seeds and methods.

Conclusions: Similar baseline discrimination concealed different occlusion responses, and loss of discrimination could coexist with high confidence. Attribution reproducibility provided a further constraint on interpretation. These findings concern the evaluated pipelines and synthetic masks; they do not establish anatomical mechanisms or clinical reliability.

Advances in knowledge: Joint evaluation of perturbation sensitivity, confidence under perturbation and attribution reproducibility distinguished model behaviours that baseline discrimination alone did not reveal in intraoral ultrasonography.

1. Introduction

Intraoral ultrasonography provides real-time imaging without ionizing radiation. It has been investigated for ultrasound-guided resection of buccal squamous cell carcinoma [1] and for preoperative measurement of tumour depth and surgical margins [2, 3]. Other applications include oral and maxillofacial assessment [4], mucosal characterization [5], salivary gland assessment [6] and imaging with dedicated intraoral probes [7]. Interpretation depends on probe placement and recognition of the imaged tissue layers. Deep learning has been applied to dental implant planning in cone-beam computed tomography [8], periapical disease detection in dental radiographs [9] and ultrasound in other specialties [10, 11]. Its use in intraoral ultrasonography remains comparatively limited, motivating evaluation of model behaviour beyond classification performance.

The buccal mucosa is a useful setting for this methodological evaluation. It provides tissue for buccinator musculomucosal flaps [12] and mucosal grafts [13]. Ultrasonographic descriptions of facial tissue layers provide a general anatomical framework for interpreting such images [14]. In the present study, the superior and inferior acquisition regions were defined by probe position relative to the underlying musculature (Section 2.2). Differences in the imaged tissue layers motivated depth-oriented perturbations, without assuming that a particular tissue boundary was the only discriminative feature.

For models with similar classification performance, a practical question is whether they also behave similarly when image information is altered. Baseline discrimination does not characterize dependence on image features or responses to altered inputs [15, 16]. Confidence and visual attribution offer additional information, but their usefulness depends on how they behave when performance deteriorates and on whether the explanations are reproducible. A Grad-CAM overlay [17] from one trained model may appear anatomically plausible without establishing either property [18] [19] [20] [21] [22]. This leaves a model-selection and interpretation problem beyond the established observation that accuracy alone is insufficient: performance, confidence and attribution may give different accounts of the same model.

We chose superior-inferior buccal subregion classification as a methodological test case. Because the operator knows the probe position, the labels are fixed at acquisition and independent of retrospective image interpretation, and the expected anatomical differences motivate depth-oriented perturbations. For the same reason, the task is not intended for immediate clinical use; it offers a controlled setting in which to establish evaluation practices that can be applied to future clinical intraoral ultrasound models. The labels do not, however, identify which image features distinguish the classes or provide a pixel-level anatomical reference. The task permits comparison of model responses to controlled image changes without treating those changes as definitive tests of anatomical understanding.

We evaluated five architectures spanning residual, dense and compound-scaled convolutional families, a modernized convolutional design (ConvNeXt-Tiny) and a vision transformer (DeiT-Tiny), each across five random seeds. We asked whether similar baseline discrimination conceals different responses to partial image occlusion; whether deterioration in discrimination is accompanied by reduced confidence or instead by confident errors; and whether attribution patterns remain reproducible across training seeds and class-activation methods. Boundary sweeps and an area-matched control examined the scope of the occlusion response, while comparison with random insertion assessed the support for attribution-based interpretation. These questions concern the evaluated training pipelines and controlled perturbations, rather than intrinsic properties of architecture families or demonstrated clinical failure.

2. Materials and Methods

>Materials and Methods

2.1 Study population and data acquisition

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Yonsei University Dental Hospital (approval no. 2-2023-0050; approved on 19 October 2023), covering both the original anatomical investigation and the secondary deep learning analysis presented herein, and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The final cohort comprised 40 healthy volunteers (11 male, 29 female), mean age 33.4 ± 12.0 years (range 20-60) at acquisition. All provided written informed consent before enrolment. The images were originally acquired for anatomical investigation of the buccal mucosa under routine clinical conditions, not under a protocol prospectively designed for deep learning. A high-frequency intraoral linear transducer was used for both superior and inferior buccal subregions within a single session per subject; every subject contributed to both classes, matching subject-level acquisition parameters between them. Of the 531 images acquired, 54 (10.2%) were excluded at quality assessment for file corruption, inadequate anatomical visualization, or colour Doppler overlay (Supplementary Methods S2). The final analytic dataset comprised 477 images (superior: 240; inferior: 237). Volunteers were recruited by public notice and imaged in July and August 2024; the parent study's sample size was determined a priori, whereas no separate sample size was prespecified for this secondary analysis, which used every image that passed quality assessment (Supplementary Methods S11). Equipment and parameter distributions are given in Supplementary Methods S1 (Supplementary Tables S1 and S3).

Measured acquisition parameters were compared between classes across the dataset, within subjects and within the test set (Supplementary Methods S3, Table S2). These exploratory comparisons detected no association (all P ≥ 0.750), but non-significance does not establish independence or exclude unmeasured acquisition confounding.

2.2 Anatomical labelling and data partitioning

Class labels were determined at the time of acquisition by the scanning position selected by the operating anatomist, who holds a doctorate in human anatomy and more than five years of experience in intraoral ultrasonography of the buccal mucosa. The superior class was defined as the upper buccal subregion overlying the zygomaticus major and levator anguli oris musculature; the inferior class was defined as the lower buccal subregion overlying the buccinator muscle belly. General ultrasonographic descriptions of the facial musculature and tissue layers provide the anatomical context for these acquisition positions [14]. In images acquired at the inferior position, a hyperechoic dermis line was typically visible in the far field. Defining labels by scanning position rather than post hoc image reading anchored class assignment to landmarks identified during scanning and reduced reliance on retrospective image interpretation.

The final dataset showed near-perfect class balance (superior: n = 240, 50.3%; inferior: n = 237, 49.7%; difference < 1%). Data were partitioned at the subject level into training/validation (32 subjects, 380 images) and held-out test (8 subjects, 97 images; superior 50.5%, inferior 49.5%) sets with no subject overlap, to prevent leakage [23]. Split records were saved before training.

2.3 Preprocessing

Prior to dataset construction, each ultrasound frame was cropped to retain only the rectangular B-mode imaging sector. All peripheral display elements -vendor identifiers, patient information, gain, depth and frequency annotations and timestamps -were excluded, so that the model input consisted exclusively of acoustic image content. The cropped images were resized to 224 × 224 pixels and converted into three-channel greyscale. Pixel values were normalized using the mean (0.316) and standard deviation (0.171) computed exclusively from the training set to prevent data leakage. The vertical image axis represents tissue depth, from the near-field buccal mucosa toward the far-field skin. Geometric augmentation, including flipping and rotation, was not used, so that the depth orientation of the images was preserved.

2.4 Deep learning architectures

Six architectures were trained: ResNet-50 [24], DenseNet-121 [25], EfficientNet-B0 and EfficientNet-B7 [26], ConvNeXt-Tiny [27] and DeiT-Tiny [28]. EfficientNet-B7 was excluded after cross-validation and before any test evaluation (Section 3.3), leaving the five architectures reported throughout. All the models were initialized with ImageNet pre-trained weights [29], and the final classification layers were replaced with two-class output layers. They span distinct design principles: additive residual propagation (ResNet-50), dense connectivity (DenseNet-121), compound scaling with depthwise-separable convolutions and squeeze-and-excitation blocks (EfficientNet [26]), a modernized convolutional design (ConvNeXt-Tiny) and a vision transformer (DeiT-Tiny). EfficientNet-B0 (4.0M parameters) and EfficientNet-B7 (63.8M) span a wide capacity range; ConvNeXt-Tiny and DeiT-Tiny test whether the findings generalize beyond residual and dense convolutional designs. All five retained architectures were evaluated across five random seeds; three were additionally carried through the detailed best-fold analyses (Supplementary Table S10). In every architecture all layers were fine-tuned; no backbone layers were frozen, and the optimizer was applied to the full parameter set. ImageNet pre-training was used to mitigate the constraints of small-sample medical image training [30]. All pipelines were implemented in Python using PyTorch on Google Colab.

2.5 Hyperparameter optimization

Hyperparameters were optimized with Optuna v4.7.0 [31] using Tree-Structured Parzen Estimator sampling, with 20 trials per architecture. For ResNet-50, DenseNet-121 and EfficientNet, Fold 1 was the tuning set; the search covered learning rates of 1 × 10 ⁵ to 1 × 10 ⁴ (log scale), batch sizes of 8, 16 or 32, Adam or SGD, and 20-50 ⁻ ⁻ maximum epochs. ConvNeXt-Tiny and DeiT-Tiny were tuned and trained on the saved Fold 2 partition. ConvNeXt-Tiny used the same learning-rate range with Adam or AdamW; DeiT-Tiny used 1 × 10 ⁶ to 5 × 10 ⁵ ⁻ ⁻ with AdamW. Batch-size and epoch ranges were unchanged; AdamW used weight decay 0.05. Early stopping used validation F1 with patience 5. These architecture-specific settings and single-fold tuning may affect comparisons [32]. Supplementary Methods S4 and Table S4 report the settings.

2.6 Performance evaluation

Five-fold subject-independent cross-validation with GroupKFold [33] was performed for ResNet-50, DenseNet-121 and EfficientNet (approximately 6-7 validation subjects per fold). For the detailed single-run analysis, the fold model with the highest validation F1 was evaluated on the held-out test set. The five-seed experiments used the saved Fold 2 training/validation partition; ConvNeXt-Tiny and DeiT-Tiny were evaluated across seeds on this partition rather than by a separate five-fold cross-validation. Seed averages therefore quantify training-run variation on a fixed partition, not variation across data splits. Held-out metrics were precision, recall, F1 and the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC). Inference time for the three best-fold models was measured over 10 repeated runs (batch size = 1, single GPU). Fold-level results are reported in Supplementary Table S5.

2.7 Explainability analysis

Grad-CAM [17] was used to visualize and quantify spatial attribution. Occlusion experiments then measured prediction sensitivity to removing specified image regions with a constant-intensity mask. These complementary analyses do not by themselves identify the anatomical features used by a model.

2.7.1 Target-layer selection

For ResNet-50, DenseNet-121 and EfficientNet-B0, target layers were selected by comparing activation patterns across candidate layers (Supplementary Figure S1). Deeper layers gave higher mean activation but spatially concentrated, anatomically non-specific maps, whereas intermediate layers preserved spatial resolution; ResNet-50 (Layer3), DenseNet-121 (DenseBlock3) and EfficientNet-B0 (features[-3]) were used. ConvNeXt-Tiny used the final feature stage (model.features[-1]). DeiT-Tiny used the first layer normalization in the final transformer block (model.blocks[-1].norm1). For the non-distilled deit_tiny_patch16_224 model, the class token was removed and the remaining 196 patch tokens were reshaped to a 14 × 14 grid, then transposed to channelfirst format before CAM calculation. The same target layers and token transformation were used for the alternative CAM methods; no layer-selection comparison is claimed for these two architectures.

2.7.2 Quantitative Grad-CAM analysis

Three complementary spatial metrics were computed from Grad-CAM heatmaps for each test image: (1) mean activation intensity, the mean pixel value of the normalized heatmap; (2) the intensity-weighted vertical centroid (ȳ-norm), normalized to image height (0 = top,1 = bottom); and (3) the upper-to-lower activation ratio (ULR), the ratio of mean activation in the upper image half to that in the lower, clipped at 10.0 to exclude instability from near-zero lower activation. Together they separate attention magnitude from spatial location. In addition, the faithfulness of Grad-CAM attributions was assessed via insertion and deletion analyses [34] with random-mask controls (the area under the curve for insertion and deletion as well as Average Drop). Average activation maps were generated by aggregating Grad-CAM heatmaps across all test images within each class to visualize global attention patterns.

2.7.3 Representative case analysis

Correctly classified and misclassified cases were selected by classification confidence and examined for sonographic features that could account for the error.

2.8 Occlusion ablation experiment

To complement the visualization-based Grad-CAM analysis with a controlled test of perturbation sensitivity, an occlusion ablation experiment was performed on the trained models. For each test image (n = 97), two occluded variants were generated by replacing the upper or the lower region of the image with a constant value equal to the training-set mean intensity, corresponding to (1) upper-region occlusion and (2) lower-region occlusion. Occlusion was applied along the depth axis, because in ultrasound anatomical content is organized by depth; the boundary itself was a fixed geometric fraction of image height (50% in the primary analysis) and was not delineated anatomically. The retained and removed regions therefore differ systematically but not exactly in tissue content: near-field mucosal and superficial muscle layers lie predominantly in the upper region, and the far-field dermis echo predominantly, though not exclusively, in the lower region. We treat this correspondence as approximate and do not attribute any observed effect to a specific anatomical landmark.

Two inference runs of the same five-seed models are reported. The original run, performed at training time, produced Table 1. A follow-up run of the saved checkpoints in a different computing environment produced the boundary-sensitivity, area-matched, calibration and subject-cluster analyses (Section 2.9; Supplementary Methods S12). Between the two runs, per-seed AUCs differed by less than 0.002 at baseline for all architectures and by less than 0.003 for the other four architectures under occlusion, but by up to 0.161 for EfficientNet-B0 under occlusion. For each model and condition, class predictions, predicted probabilities, and standard performance metrics (accuracy, F1-score and AUC) were computed. Seed-averaged classification and occlusion performance are reported in Table 1, and the stability of Grad-CAM spatial metrics across seeds and three alternative CAM methods (Grad-CAM++ [35], Score-CAM [36], Layer-CAM [37]) in Supplementary Tables S7 and S8. Eigen-CAM and gradient-integration methods, which differ in formulation from class-activation mapping, were not evaluated.

2.9 Occlusion boundary sensitivity, area-matched control and output diagnostics

The 50% occlusion boundary is an arbitrary geometric choice. Three additional analyses were therefore performed, all by inference only using the stored seed checkpoints; no model was retrained.

First, the boundary was varied. Each architecture, at each seed, was re-evaluated with the boundary at 30%, 40%, 50%, 60% and 70% of image depth, occluding either the region above or below it -50 conditions per architecture. The two directions are complementary at a given boundary, so the fraction removed ranged from 30% to 70% in each direction.

Second, position was separated from amount. With the removed area fixed at 50% of image height, a contiguous band of that size was shifted across five vertical offsets spanning the image, so that the quantity of removed information was held constant while its location varied.

Third, the predicted probabilities were examined. For each model and condition we computed their standard deviation, interquartile range and number of distinct values (to three decimal places) across the 97 test images, with the expected calibration error [38] (15 equal-width confidence bins), the Brier score and the mean confidence on incorrect predictions -to distinguish chance-level discrimination arising from uncertain output from that arising from saturated output.

An AUC below 0.60 was used as an operational collapse criterion. Rates were also summarized at thresholds of 0.55, 0.65 and 0.70. Repeatability was checked for baseline and upper 50% occlusion within one computing environment. The original and follow-up inference implementations differed in batch size (64 versus 97 images), and the follow-up used a different computing environment; execution differences are reported in Supplementary Methods S12.

2.10 Label validation via blinded expert re-classification

To assess the anatomical validity of the acquisition-position labels used for training and evaluation, a labelvalidation sub-study was performed in which images were independently re-assigned to classes by blinded experts for comparison with the unchanged acquisition-position labels. Two examiners with anatomical expertise independently classified image subsamples as superior or inferior while blinded to the acquisition position and each other's responses and rated their confidence on a 1-5 scale. The first examiner, who acquired the images, reclassified 20 unique images plus 5 duplicated images for intra-rater repeatability; because this examiner defined the original labels, that comparison is a label-grounding check rather than an independent test. A second anatomy expert, uninvolved in acquisition or labelling, independently classified 40 images, 20 of which overlapped with the first examiner's set. Agreement was quantified as described in Section 2.11; analyses were repeated for the high-confidence subset (confidence ≥ 4).

2.11 Statistical analysis

Analyses were performed in Python. Training-seed means and standard deviations describe variation among five trained models on the fixed dataset. For the follow-up saved predictions at baseline and upper/lower 50% occlusion, 95% percentile confidence intervals were calculated by paired subject-cluster bootstrap [39] with 20,000 resamples (random seed 20261004). Eight test subjects were sampled with replacement, retaining all images of each selected subject with multiplicity. The same subject draws were used across architectures, training seeds and conditions; metrics were computed for each fixed trained seed and then averaged. Training seeds were not resampled. The AUC reduction was baseline AUC minus the mean of upper-and lower-occlusion AUC. These intervals quantify test-subject sampling uncertainty conditional on the saved models and predictions, not training-set uncertainty. Supplementary Table S14 reports these follow-up estimates separately from the original execution in Table 1. The original best-fold image-bootstrap intervals (1,000 resamples) and image-level Mann-Whitney, Wilcoxon, McNemar [40] and DeLong [41] analyses are retained only as exploratory summaries. They do not account for within-subject dependence and cannot establish population-level significance or equivalence. Holm adjustment was applied to the ten DeLong comparisons within each seed; intensity and ULR comparisons also used Holm and Benjamini-Hochberg adjustment. Adjustment does not correct clustering. Collapse counts across repeated conditions are reported descriptively, without Fisher testing. Expert agreement was summarized by percentage agreement and Cohen's κ; repeat-inference rank agreement used Spearman's ρ.

3. Results

>Results

3.1 Dataset characteristics and partitioning

Of 531 images acquired, 477 from 40 subjects met the inclusion criteria; 54 (10.2%) were excluded for file corruption, inadequate anatomical visualization or colour Doppler overlay. Classes were near-balanced (superior 240, 50.3%; inferior 237, 49.7%). All 40 subjects contributed to both classes within the same session, matching acquisition parameters across classes at subject level. Subject-independent partitioning yielded 380 training/validation images from 32 subjects and 97 held-out test images from 8 subjects (superior 49; inferior 48). The flow of participants and images is shown in Supplementary Figure S6.

3.2 Acquisition labels and expert agreement

Acquisition-position labels provided the reference for model training and testing. Expert re-classification assessed whether selected images could be assigned to the acquisition-defined classes from image appearance; it did not replace the acquisition labels or establish a pixel-level anatomical reference. Labels were fixed by the position of the probe at acquisition and were therefore not derived from image appearance. Blinded expert re-classification nevertheless recovered them from the images alone (Supplementary Table S11). The acquiring examiner (an intra-rater, label-grounding check) agreed with the acquisition labels for 90.0% of 20 images (κ = 0.80) and for all high-confidence images (κ = 1.00). An independent expert, blinded to acquisition position, agreed for 85.0% of 40 images (κ = 0.70) and 96.7% of the 30 high-confidence images (κ = 0.93); on the 20 images both read, the two never disagreed with each other (κ = 1.00). Disagreement with the acquisition labels was almost entirely confined to low-confidence images, the one high-confidence exception being an image both experts independently assigned to the same opposite class. This disagreement may reflect image ambiguity or disagreement with the acquisition label; the reading study does not resolve its cause.

3.3 Classification performance

All five architectures were trained across five random seeds and evaluated on the same subject-independent heldout set (Table 1). Observed discrimination was similar across architectures: seed-mean AUC ranged from 0.958 ± 0.026 (ResNet-50) to 0.972 ± 0.008 (ConvNeXt-Tiny), and seed-mean F1 from 0.888 ± 0.029 (ResNet-50) to 0.911 ± 0.030 (DeiT-Tiny). The seed-to-seed standard deviations were of the same order as the differences between architectures. Figure 1 illustrates the separate original best-fold runs for three architectures; it does not depict the five-seed means.

EfficientNet-B7 was excluded after cross-validation and before test evaluation because it performed substantially worse and more variably (CV F1 = 0.630 ± 0.049), possibly reflecting overparameterization relative to dataset size (63.8 M parameters; 380 training images). Cross-validation F1 fell within ±0.08 of the test F1 for the three retained architectures with reported cross-validation results (Supplementary Table S5). Exploratory best-fold performance with image-bootstrap confidence intervals, precision, recall, confusion counts and inference latency (8.4-18.1 ms per image) is reported in Supplementary Table S10 and Supplementary Figure S2.

Exploratory image-level DeLong comparisons for all ten architecture pairs at five seeds are retained in Supplementary Table S13. One of 50 comparisons remained below 0.05 after within-seed Holm adjustment (ResNet-50 versus ConvNeXt-Tiny at seed 1; adjusted P = 0.025). Because images from the same subject are dependent, these P values are not used as confirmatory evidence of architecture differences.

The sign of the pairwise AUC difference reversed across seeds for all ten architecture pairs, and each architecture had the highest observed AUC at one seed. This describes variability in the ranking within this dataset; it does not establish equivalence. Subject-cluster intervals for the follow-up baseline predictions are reported in Supplementary Table S14.

3.4 Performance under synthetic occlusion

Under the original 50% occlusion evaluation, four architectures retained AUCs of 0.851-0.917, with mean reductions of 0.063-0.109, whereas EfficientNet-B0 reached 0.549 and 0.543 under upper and lower occlusion (mean reduction 0.417 ± 0.044; Table 1). Both occluded AUCs were below 0.60 in each of its five seeds. Detailed best-fold results are reported in Table 2 as exploratory single-run summaries. In the follow-up execution, EfficientNet-B0 had mean AUCs of 0.510 and 0.522; its mean reduction was 0.447 (subject-cluster 95% CI 0.396-0.495), compared with 0.063-0.109 for the other architectures (Supplementary Table S14). The numerical discrepancy and execution differences are described in Supplementary Methods S12.

Class-wise responses were largely shared. Across seeds, all four architectures that retained discrimination defaulted toward the superior class when the upper region was occluded (superior accuracy 0.931-1.000; Figure 2B). Under lower-region occlusion no consistent default emerged, and the inverted default reported for DenseNet-121 in a single best-fold run (inferior accuracy 0.979) lay at the extreme of the seed distribution (0.658 ± 0.269), so we do not interpret it as a property of dense connectivity. EfficientNet-B0 lost accuracy in both classes under both conditions and showed no coherent default at all.

Because the 50% boundary is arbitrary, each seed model was re-evaluated with the boundary at 30%, 40%, 50%, 60% and 70% of image depth, occluding the region above or below it (50 conditions per architecture; Figure 3). EfficientNet-B0 fell below an AUC of 0.60 in 49 of 50 conditions (mean 0.494; minimum 0.357), whereas ResNet-50 and ConvNeXt-Tiny did so in none, DenseNet-121 in 1 of 50 and DeiT-Tiny in 2 of 50; these three exceptions all occurred at the largest removal fraction. The four architectures showed an overall decline in discrimination as the fraction removed increased and retained an AUC of 0.917-0.956 when 30% was removed, whereas EfficientNet-B0 was already at chance at that fraction (0.520 and 0.498 for upper-and lower-region occlusion). These repeated-condition counts are descriptive; no test treating the conditions as independent observations was used. The result was also robust to the collapse criterion: across thresholds from 0.55 to 0.70, EfficientNet-B0's collapse rate ranged from 0.82 to 1.00 and no other architecture exceeded 0.12.

In the area-matched control, a band covering 50% of image height was shifted across five vertical offsets. EfficientNet-B0 had a mean AUC of 0.503 (minimum 0.404), while mean AUCs for the other architectures were 0.811-0.869. The observed sensitivity therefore extended across the tested mask positions at a fixed area. This does not exclude sensitivity to the mask itself or establish independence from mask size outside the tested range.

Among the architectures that retained discrimination, occluding the upper region degraded performance more than occluding the lower region at matched removal fractions (at 70% removal, AUC 0.610-0.720 versus 0.791-0.879). Because the boundary is geometric rather than anatomically delineated, we report this asymmetry descriptively only.

3.5 Discrimination and confidence under occlusion

The loss of discrimination in EfficientNet-B0 was accompanied by output saturation rather than a general shift toward uncertain predictions. Under 50% occlusion, its predicted probabilities took only 8-15 distinct values (to three decimal places) across the 97 test images, compared with approximately 91 when unoccluded, and their interquartile range approached the maximum possible (0.998-1.000). The spread of its outputs increased relative to baseline (SD ratio 1.34), whereas every other architecture showed the opposite (0.47-0.83) (Supplementary Table S12; Supplementary Figure S5). These near-binary predictions had markedly reduced input-specific variation. AUC may be sensitive to small differences among saturated probabilities; the contribution of numerical effects was not isolated. This was accompanied by extreme confidence. Calibration and confidence were quantified for every seed model at baseline and under occlusion (Table 3). On intact images, expected calibration error ranged from 0.061 to 0.094 across the five architectures, and mean confidence on incorrect predictions ranged from 0.644 to 0.783; EfficientNet-B0 was the least confident of the five when wrong (0.644). Occlusion reversed this ordering: its accuracy fell to 0.520, its confidence on incorrect predictions rose to 0.989 (other architectures 0.714-0.776) and its expected calibration error to 0.478 (0.095-0.199), and 92.9% of its predicted probabilities were below 0.01 or above 0.99. The lowest baseline confidence on errors did not predict the high confidence observed on errors under synthetic occlusion.

3.6 Grad-CAM attribution: reproducibility and faithfulness

Grad-CAM spatial metrics -mean activation intensity, normalized vertical centroid and the upper-to-lower activation ratio -were recomputed across the five random seeds and across three alternative class-activation methods (Grad-CAM++, Score-CAM, Layer-CAM). Neither the magnitude nor, for several architecture-metric pairs, the direction of the between-class difference was preserved; EfficientNet-B0's centroid pattern reversed under Score-CAM (Supplementary Tables S7 and S8). Single-run values are therefore reported descriptively in Table 4 and Figures 4 and 5 and are not interpreted as evidence of anatomical alignment.

Single-run class differences varied by architecture and metric (Table 4). Across all five seeds, the majority direction occurred in three of five runs for ResNet-50, DenseNet-121, EfficientNet-B0 and DeiT-Tiny, and four of five for ConvNeXt-Tiny, for each of the three spatial metrics (Supplementary Table S7). These counts use all seeds, irrespective of image-level P values. They describe limited directional consistency in this sample rather than a validated threshold for attribution stability.

In the detailed single-run insertion analysis, Grad-CAM and random insertion AUCs were 0.791 versus 0.583 for ResNet-50, 0.731 versus 0.546 for DenseNet-121, and 0.546 versus 0.532 for EfficientNet-B0 (Table 5). The original image-level Wilcoxon comparison for EfficientNet-B0 did not detect a difference (P = 0.22); this does not establish equivalence to random attribution or absence of class-relevant information. These comparisons do not account for subject clustering. ConvNeXt-Tiny and DeiT-Tiny had positive observed insertion gain at all five seeds; deletion AUC and Average Drop were not computed for them.

3.7 Misclassified cases

Representative correctly classified and misclassified cases are shown in Figure 6 and Supplementary Figure S3. Selected errors showed indistinct fat-pad-muscle boundaries or a weak or atypically positioned dermis echo. These qualitative observations suggest possible sources of difficulty but do not establish that anatomical ambiguity explains all errors or that error mechanisms are shared across architectures.

4. Discussion

>Discussion

4.1 Principal findings

This study examined how models with similar baseline discrimination differed when evaluated through perturbation responses, confidence and spatial attribution. Acquisition-position classification provided a tractable test case in intraoral ultrasonography without requiring a diagnostic claim. Expert agreement supported the interpretability of selected acquisition-defined labels, but neither the labels nor expert reading supplied a complete reference for the image features a model should use.

The central finding was a mismatch between baseline performance and behaviour under altered inputs. Five architectures achieved similar observed baseline discrimination, yet EfficientNet-B0 fell below AUC 0.60 in 49 of 50 boundary-sweep conditions and remained sensitive in the area-matched control. Its loss of discrimination was accompanied by highly confident errors rather than reduced confidence. Spatial attribution patterns also varied across seeds and methods, limiting their ability to resolve the interpretation of model behaviour. The contribution is therefore the joint characterization of these discrepancies in intraoral ultrasonography, rather than a general assertion that accuracy alone is insufficient. The experiments do not identify their anatomical or architectural mechanism.

4.2 Similar baseline discrimination and different perturbation responses

Seed-mean AUC ranged from 0.958 to 0.972 and F1 from 0.888 to 0.911. Pairwise rankings reversed across seeds, and the eight-subject test cohort leaves substantial uncertainty. Non-significant exploratory comparisons cannot demonstrate equivalence. Architecture choice based on a single run would not capture the observed variability or the differences under synthetic perturbation.

EfficientNet-B7 performed poorly in cross-validation before test evaluation. Its larger capacity is one possible explanation, but overparameterization was not tested directly. Differences between cross-validation and test rankings may also reflect the small subject counts in the folds (Section 4.7).

EfficientNet-B0 had a mid-range baseline AUC but the largest observed loss under the tested masks. Boundary sweeps showed that the response was not confined to the primary 50% boundary, and the area-matched control showed that it persisted across mask positions when the removed area was fixed. The resulting distinction between evaluated pipelines was largely absent from the baseline performance comparison. This supports considering perturbation response as an additional dimension of model comparison, while retaining the uncertainty from the small test cohort and the differences in training settings. It does not establish an intrinsic robustness ranking of architecture families.

4.3 Occlusion sensitivity and possible explanations

Attribution maps do not by themselves establish causal use of the highlighted regions [42]. Constant-intensity occlusion changes both image content and the input distribution. The retained AUC of four architectures under 50% occlusion shows discrimination under those masked inputs; the larger loss for EfficientNet-B0 shows sensitivity to the intervention. Boundary and area controls extend this observation across specified locations and removal fractions, but do not separate dependence on distributed anatomical information from susceptibility to the artificial mask.

Potential explanations include different dependence on spatially distributed image information and different susceptibility to the artificial mask. Residual propagation [24], depthwise-separable convolutions and squeezeand-excitation weighting [26] provide architectural context for generating hypotheses, but this experiment did not isolate their effects. Differences in tuning, optimization and execution also prevent attributing the observed response to a particular design component. The most defensible interpretation is a difference among the evaluated model pipelines under the specified perturbations.

Class-wise responses were largely shared (Section 3.4). The inverted default reported for DenseNet-121 in a single best-fold run lay at the extreme of the seed distribution, so we do not interpret it as a property of dense connectivity -an instance of the same single-run instability this study documents for attribution.

4.4 Loss of discrimination without reduced confidence

The confidence response gives the occlusion result a separate implication. EfficientNet-B0 had the lowest mean confidence on incorrect predictions at baseline, yet under 50% occlusion its accuracy fell to 0.520 while mean confidence on incorrect predictions rose to 0.989. It assigned 92.9% of probabilities below 0.01 or above 0.99. Thus, favourable confidence behaviour on intact images did not carry over to these altered inputs. A review strategy that flags only low-confidence predictions could miss high-confidence errors of this type, although no clinical triage rule or threshold was evaluated here.

Expected calibration error increased from 0.094 on intact images to 0.478 under occlusion. Evaluating confidence together with discrimination under the same perturbation therefore revealed information that either baseline calibration or perturbed AUC alone would miss. Probe angulation, acoustic coupling and field of view vary in clinical imaging, but constant masks do not reproduce these changes. The observed discrimination-confidence mismatch motivates testing whether confidence remains informative under realistic acquisition variation; it does not demonstrate that the same failure occurs clinically.

4.5 Attribution reproducibility as a constraint on interpretation

Attribution adds a third dimension to this evaluation, but did not provide a stable anatomical account of the observed model differences. The direction and magnitude of Grad-CAM metrics varied across seeds and methods. For EfficientNet-B0, the small observed insertion advantage over random masking was not detected as significant in the original image-level test. That result is inconclusive about equivalence and does not prove that.

the maps contain no relevant information. Instead, seed and method variation, together with the limited insertion evidence, constrain what can be inferred from a plausible single-run map. Reproducibility and faithfulness address different aspects of attribution and should not be inferred from visual plausibility alone.

Benchmarking studies elsewhere in medical imaging have reported that saliency maps localize abnormalities less accurately than expert readers [43] and can fail model-randomization and repeatability checks [44].

4.6 Misclassification patterns

Selected misclassified examples showed indistinct tissue boundaries or a weak dermis echo. Such appearances provide plausible explanations for reader and model difficulty, but a systematic causal link between ambiguity and model error was not established. These examples are descriptive and do not validate an automated referral rule.

4.7 Methodological considerations

Hyperparameter optimization used a single validation fold rather than nested cross-validation, admitting foldspecific selection bias [32]. ConvNeXt-Tiny and DeiT-Tiny also used different optimizer search spaces, and DeiT-Tiny used a lower learning-rate range. Architecture comparisons therefore reflect the evaluated training pipelines as well as model design; they do not isolate architecture as the sole cause of performance differences.

The primary analyses average five training seeds on the saved Fold 2 partition (Table 1). For ResNet-50, DenseNet-121 and EfficientNet-B0, the detailed best-fold run is reported separately (Table 2; Supplementary Tables S5 and S10); Fold 2 was selected after tuning on Fold 1. For ConvNeXt-Tiny and DeiT-Tiny, both tuning and early stopping used Fold 2. The held-out test subjects were separate from these training/validation partitions, but seed replication does not remove single-fold model-selection bias.

All images were acquired by one operator. This reduces between-operator variation within the dataset but limits generalizability and may allow operator-specific features to influence classification. The measured acquisitionparameter comparisons did not establish the absence of confounding.

Finally, the upper-to-lower activation ratio was introduced to separate spatial from amplitude-related differences in attention. Because it was not reproducible here, we offer it as a diagnostic template whose outputs must themselves be checked for stability before anatomical interpretation.

4.8 Implications for future intraoral ultrasound models

The relevance to dentomaxillofacial imaging lies in comparing models and interpreting their outputs before considering diagnostic or procedural use. Similar discrimination on intact images may coexist with different sensitivity to altered inputs, and a model may remain highly confident as discrimination deteriorates. An attribution overlay provides further evidence only to the extent that its patterns are reproducible and supported by the evaluation used. The present subregion-classification task demonstrates these evaluation issues within a limited intraoral ultrasound setting.

A useful evaluation would report discrimination and confidence together on both intact and clinically motivated altered inputs, then examine attribution reproducibility across training runs and methods and its performance against appropriate controls. Such reporting could distinguish loss of discrimination accompanied by uncertainty from loss accompanied by confident errors, while making the limits of attribution-based interpretation explicit. Stored checkpoints allow some of these checks without retraining, although realistic acquisition perturbations and independent validation require additional work. These checks complement clinical validation and do not establish safety by themselves.

The present findings apply to the sampled buccal subregion task, evaluated training pipelines and specified masks. Their value is in exposing differences between dimensions of model behaviour that would be obscured by a comparison based only on baseline discrimination.

4.9 Limitations and future directions

The dataset comprises 477 images from 40 subjects at one institution, with only eight subjects in the held-out test set. The subject-cluster bootstrap addresses within-subject dependence for the saved follow-up predictions but remains imprecise with so few clusters and conditions on the observed training data and fixed models. Seed replication does not add independent participants. Earlier image-level intervals and tests are exploratory and may be anticonservative. Generalization beyond this cohort requires evaluation with additional centres and operators.

Expert re-classification supported agreement with acquisition-position labels in selected images, not pixel-level correspondence between attribution and anatomy. Anatomical regions were not delineated. Direct comparison against expert regions, alternative perturbation methods and model-randomization controls could provide additional evidence in future work.

Constant-intensity masks are artificial inputs. Their effects may reflect loss of distributed information, distribution shift or both. Geometric boundaries do not isolate individual tissue layers. Differences between original and follow-up EfficientNet-B0 occluded AUCs also remain unexplained; changing batch size and environment are possible contributors, not demonstrated causes. Repeatability of baseline and upper-mask predictions within one environment does not resolve this between-execution discrepancy.

Finally, this study addressed binary subregion classification, not vessel detection, flap viability assessment or surgical risk stratification; its results are not evidence for surgical decision support. The possible explanations in Section 4.3 remain hypotheses. The confidence-based review implications in Section 4.4 were not evaluated as a clinical workflow.

Future work could evaluate clinically motivated acquisition changes and extend perturbation assessment to continuous anatomical measurements. A lack of output change after occlusion would need to be interpreted alongside redundant information and distribution shift rather than treated as proof that the region was unused.

5. Conclusions

In intraoral ultrasound subregion classification, similar observed baseline discrimination concealed different responses to synthetic occlusion. Across five training seeds, EfficientNet-B0 fell below AUC 0.60 in 49 of 50 boundary-sweep conditions, while its errors under 50% occlusion became highly confident. Spatial attributions were not consistently reproduced across seeds or class-activation methods, limiting anatomical interpretation from a single map. Joint reporting of perturbation sensitivity, confidence under perturbation and attribution reproducibility therefore characterized model behaviour beyond baseline discrimination.
