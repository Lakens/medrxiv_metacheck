How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

How Replication Check works

This module checks references and warns for citations of original studies for which replication or reproduction studies exist in the FLoRA database.

The Replication Check module compares the reference list against studies in the FLoRA (FORRT Library of Replication Attempts) database based on the DOI. Abstract

Background: Medical documents are a crucial resource for medical research around the world. While troves of valuable health data exist, they are largely computationally inaccessible as hard copies of unstructured text. Moreover, the persistent prevalence of fax machines in medical settings contributes to further degradation of document quality. Manual data extraction from these documents is time-consuming and resource intensive. However, large language models (LLMs) have recently shown great promise for automated digitization and information extraction (IE), greatly improving upon previous technologies in terms of accuracy.

Objective: Here we aim to survey the landscape of LLM-based IE tools with respect to their applicability to biomedical documents and to systematically benchmark them on synthetic documents with paired reference extractions. We put special focus on locally executable tools and models that suit privacy-and resource-constrained settings. Methods: We reviewed LLM-based IE tools from the literature and assessed them with respect to their suitability for use in biomedical research. We found only one of these tools (NuExtract2) to satisfy our selection criteria and compared it to LLM foundation models prompted to perform extractions. We created 200 mock molecular test reports with paired reference data using a bespoke procedural generation approach and evaluated the tools' performance across different prompting strategies, input modalities, and document qualities.

Results: We found model performance to be very sensitive to input modalities and quality. The best overall performance was observed for the proprietary GPT 4.1-mini, with an F1 score of 79.2% averaged across quality levels and modalities. The best performing open-weights model was Mistral-small 3.1 with an average F1 score of 76.2%. The NuExtract2 model was the only one able to run on regular laptop computers, but did not perform well. Surprisingly, we found the choice of one-shot over zero-shot prompts to only have a small effect size on extraction performance in most cases. Conclusions: LLMs place the automatic processing of highly complex medical documents in resource-constrained and privacy-sensitive settings within reach, but still require human oversight.

Introduction

The critical role of early data digitization

The modern healthcare ecosystem faces a paradox: physicians and researchers are data-rich yet information-poor [23]. While vast quantities of invaluable clinical data are generated daily, a substantial portion remains locked away within paper-based records and non-standardized digital formats. Despite increased efforts toward digitization of resources, many healthcare institutions still rely on physical documents and analog technology such as photocopiers and fax machines to share information, thus adding distortions and artifacts to documents that decrease their legibility. This reliance on physical documents creates a critical bottleneck, rendering rich data sources largely inaccessible to computational analysis and modern health informatics.

As a consequence, medical information extraction (IE) and digitization are accomplished predominantly through manual curation. Researchers collect data from a wide variety of sources, including scanned documents, electronic health record (EHR)s, and faxed reports. This process is slow and labor-intensive, thus struggling to scale with the ever-increasing volume of medical data. Manual curation is also prone to human error, such as transcription mistakes or subjective interpretations, which can compromise data quality.

Automation of IE has been studied for some time. Early approaches rely on optical character recognition (OCR) followed by rule-based IE methods (e.g., regular expressions), but offer only a partial solution [29]. These systems are often brittle; they fail when encountering slight variations in document formatting and lack the semantic understanding required to interpret the context of clinical information accurately.

Using large language models (LLMs) for data extraction

In recent years, transformer-based LLMs have emerged as state-of-the-art technologies capable of understanding and extracting structured information from unstructured documents with surprising accuracy [13, 2]. LLMs vastly improve the process of data extraction and processing thanks to their flexibility and greater accuracy.

The three primary types of transformer-based LLMs, Encoders, Decoders, and Encoder/Decoder hybrids, each offer unique advantages for clinical data processing tasks, positioning them as ideal candidates for addressing the complex extraction needs faced by researchers around Canada and the world. Encoder models such as the BERT family [7] create a latent semantic representation of text data, making them most commonly used for named entity recognition (NER) and IE tasks. Encoder/decoder hybrid models, such as BART [19], are often used in transformative sequence-to-sequence tasks such as translation or summarization. Pure decoder models such as OpenAI's GPT, Mistral, and Google's Gemini follow a sequence-to-sequence paradigm to generate new text based on a prompt, predicting what words are likely to follow [25, 21, 9]. Decoder models have attracted overwhelming attention due to their instruction-following capabilities and emergent zero-shot learning skills [30].

LLMs come in different sizes, typically expressed via the number of trained parameters. Larger models are usually more capable, but require increasingly expensive and power-hungry hardware to run and depend on larger amounts of training data. Therefore, the largest models are often proprietary and can only be accessed via the cloud. Much of the information fed into these commercial models is eventually used to train the next iteration of the model. While this may benefit future users with better performing technologies, it raises privacy issues.

Conversely, smaller models can be run locally without the requirement of sending data to third-party providers. However, to perform specialized task such as IE, smaller models often require fine-tuning, which depends on taskspecific training data and machine-learning expertise.

Many published IE tools use the larger proprietary models via cloud solutions, due to their convenience. But in healthcare contexts, patient privacy and information security are paramount. In the absence of a trusted and certified cloud inference provider, locally hosted, opensource extraction tools are the only viable option for these applications.

Here, we study the feasibility of implementing LLMs for IE from biomedical documents. We benchmark a range of models and tools designed for extraction tasks. Specifically, we seek to answer the following questions: What local LLM-based options currently exist for biomedical data extraction? How do these tools perform in comparison to the cloud-based LLMs (which can be expected to be perform better, but are ineligible due to privacy issues)? What is the impact of common prompting strategies on extraction performance? Can multimodal tools and models capitalize on spatial context via image input compared to OCR-derived text input? And finally, which tool presents the best option for implementation into current workflows based on privacy, ease of use, and accuracy?

Methods

A survey of automated extraction tools

We reviewed the literature for LLM-based techniques and tools for IE tasks (Figure 1A). We used the following inclusion criteria for further investigation: (i) Tools should allow users to specify the desired entities to be extracted; (iv) Tools should have sufficient context size to enable processing full-text medical reports; (v) Tools should be open-source, such that code can be inspected for safety; (vi) Tools should either support general purpose data extraction, simple prompting of entity types to be extracted, or be purpose built for biomedical extraction tasks.

A survey of the available literature revealed 17 candidate tools for further examination. Of these, only one (NuEx-tract2) fully met our criteria. Supplementary Table S1 (Supplementary File 1) shows an overview of all candidates and which criteria they met.

NERRE [6] uses a fine-tuning approach with GPT3 [25] and Llama-2 [27]. It requires custom fine-tuning via a database of field-specific training examples. The model uses material science-specific extraction examples. Adaptation to biomedical use cases would thus require curation of applicable training data. Fine-tuning also requires the availability of a high-performance computing (HPC) environment, which may be inaccessible in resource constrained settings.

ATG [35] uses a constrained decoding approach to generate graph representations of extracted entities and their relationships. Unfortunately, model weights are not provided, requiring re-training of the model for new applications.

BioGPT [20] is a custom re-training of the GPT2 architecture on 15M PubMed abstracts. Unfortunately, the GPT2 architecture does not have a sufficiently large context window to support full-text documents. Preliminary tests showed poor IE performance, likely since GPT2 is a comparatively small decoder architecture intended for generative tasks.

GIX [11] is a tool for automatic literature search and molecular interaction extraction based on BioBERT and BERN2. Unfortunately, it does not generalize to extracting medical terms.

BERN2 [26] is an IE tool for molecular biology terms that also performs automatic entity normalization. Unfortunately, it currently only recognizes 9 biomedical entity types: Gene, disease, chemical, species, mutation, cell line, cell type, DNA, and RNA.

BioBERT [18] and Bio-LinkBERT [34] are versions of the BERT encoder architecture trained on biomedical texts. They can be used as foundations for many tokenlevel tasks such as NER. However, they do require the design and training of task-specific post-processing networks.

GPT-NER [28] uses a few-shot prompting approach with examples automatically sampled from a vector database. This database of few-shot examples needs to be custom-tailored to the task domain. While GPT-NER does not work out of the box for biomedical extraction tasks we selected its prompt design for further manual testing.

Re-Rank-NER [32] uses an output re-ranking approach to improve the performance of a BART model for NER tasks. Unfortunately, the source code was not provided. The model would also require fine-tuning for biomedical document extraction.

UniversalNER [39] uses a targeted distillation approach to train custom small extraction models using larger teacher models. Unfortunately, it requires each entity type to be processed separately, which renders it not usable out of the box.

CancerBERT [38] is an encoder model trained on articles relating to cancer genomics. Like other encoder models, this would require the development and training of a post-processing neural network. In addition, neither the training data nor the model weights were provided. P-ICL [16] uses few-shot prompts with a list of examples for each entity type to be extracted. Although the model weights were not made available, we selected the prompting strategy for further testing.

LTNER [33] uses a similar approach to GPT-NER. It uses contextualized markers to tag entities directly in the text sequence. Like GPT-NER, the tool also requires a vector database to retrieve relevant few-shot examples. While this made it difficult to fully replicate, we adapted the prompting approach for testing with fixed examples.

2INER [37] uses a fine-tuned model with few-shot prompts. The self-reported accuracy showed the tool to be approximately on-par with other NER or GPT-based methods. Unfortunately, no code or model weights were available for this tool.

NSSC [8] is a NER and entity normalization system for Spanish language clinical notes. As our test data was in English, it was unfortunately incompatible with our testing setup.

GliNER [36] is a bidirectional transformer encoder/decoder for any kind of entities to be extracted and labels text with entities. As a pure NER tool, it unfortunately does not allow users to specify fields to be extracted.

NuExtract2 [3] is a multi-modal model based on Qwen2.5-VL [1] that was fine-tuned for IE from unstructured documents. It can be installed via HuggingFace Transformers [31] and can be prompted for any extraction tasks. NuEx-tract2 met all our selection criteria and was therefore selected for further testing.

Notably, the P-ICL, LTNER and GPT-NER tools prominently featured few-shot prompting techniques. We combined these techniques into a few-shot prompt template to be tested across multiple foundation models in comparison with a zero-shot prompting approach (see Supplementary File 1 for prompt texts).

In addition to the selected purpose-built extraction tool, we also evaluated four general-purpose LLMs. Three of these are open-weights and can be run locally: Mistralsmall 3.1 24B [21]; Llama 3.1 70B [27]; and Gemma 3 27B [10]. We installed these models via HuggingFace Transformers [31] and Ollama [22]. To serve as a baseline, we also tested a proprietary cloud-service LLM: GPT 4.1mini [25].

A synthetic benchmark dataset

To evaluate the selected models, we created a synthetic evaluation dataset simulating molecular test reports (Figure 1B). We started by creating JSON-formatted structured data in a three-stage process. First, we generated clinical metadata (such as test dates, lab information, clinic information, sample types, sequencing scopes, etc.) by randomly drawing from lists of allowed values. Secondly, we generated random gene panels from a set of 800 known disease genes. We then generated a set of genetic variants by drawing a Poisson-distributed random number of genes from the appropriate panel and simulating a random nucleotide change in each. We calculated the resulting amino acid changes, Human Genome Variation Society Nomenclature (HGVS) variant descriptors, associated database accessions, zygosity information, allele frequency and other variant-related information. In the final step, we simulated American College of Medical Genetics (ACMG) classifications via simulated evidence such as associated in silico variant impact predictions, functional assay outcomes, family history, etc.

Having created simulated structured data for genetic test outcomes, we then proceeded to generate simulated report documents for each dataset. This was achieved by interpolating the data into eleven different LaTeX templates corresponding to simulated testing sites. The templates were inspired by test report layouts from major testing sites and hospitals around Ontario, Canada, as well as online examples. Text sections such as full-text descriptions of the mutations and associated biological effects and interpretations in the documents were created synthetically from the generated data using procedural text generation. The interpolated LaTeX files were then compiled to PDF documents.

Finally, the documents were passed through an Im-ageMagick [15] script that simulates the effects of a fax machine or photocopier on image quality, by introducing small, random amounts of rotation, stretch and blur and applying Poisson noise. Example documents can be found in (Supplementary File 2). The R and LaTeX code used to generate the synthetic data and interpolated files can be found on GitHub [4].

In total, we generated 200 simulated clinical report datasets with reference data in JSON format and corresponding pairs of clean and image-distorted ("faxed") PDF documents.

Experimental setup and procedure

All models were run in a Python 3.14 virtual environment. We used a temperature parameter of T = 0 for all LLM calls. This parameter controls the distribution of probability density across output tokens. At T = 0 the token with the highest score receives 100% of the probability density and is thus always sampled. This serves to force consistent output, restricting the LLM's creativity and constraining the model to the most likely tokens. The Mistral-small 3.1 installation for Ollama ships with a default setting that restricts its context window. To override this restriction, we set the num_ctx parameter to 128, 000 such as to unlock its full context size. All tested models, including the large LLama 3.1 70B were hosted on a HPC platform. NuExtract2 was also run on a standard MacBook Pro with 24GB of RAM and an M4 chip.

In the first step, images of each page were extracted using Poppler [14] and then parsed into text via EasyOCR [17]. For multimodal models (featuring both vision and text input capabilities) images were directly supplied to them alongside the prompts. As a baseline control, models were also tested on the original raw document texts in Markdown format, which were compiled directly from the interpolated LaTeX sources using Pandoc.

The LLMs were tested with two types of IE prompts: A zero-shot prompt (providing no examples to the LLM) inspired by the prompt used in P-ICL [16] and a oneshot prompt inspired by those used in LTNER [33] and GPT-NER [28]. Each prompt instructed the respective model to return its output in JSON format for easy comparison. The zero-shot prompt comprised a list of data fields to extract and, where appropriate, a list of allowed terms for each field, together with a blank JSON template for the output. The one-shot prompt contained the same elements with the addition of one text example and its expected output. See Supplementary File 1 for prompt texts.

The prompts were provided to the models alongside the raw text, OCR-processed text, or raw images respectively. The NuExtract2 model required a different input format: (1) a specific JSON template that included field names, their expected type, and options within that field; (2) a separate input file for any one-shot examples; and (3) a concatenation of the page images into a single image.

Finally, the extraction results were compared to the reference data. A detailed description of the scoring procedure, including multiple examples, can be found in the Supplementary Methods (Supplementary File 1). Briefly, the relevant reference data for each report was trimmed down to the subset of fields that were compatible with the respective document template. Next, the extracted data was compared value by value for matches. Most fields required perfect matches to count as correct. However, for certain fields containing more text-like data (e.g. hospital names, testing context), partial matches were allowed. This was calculated using character-level alignments via a simplified Needleman-Wunsch algorithm [24]. This scoring approach strikes a balance between punishing technically incorrect extractions and rewarding partial correctness. Based on these full or partial matches, we then counted the number of true positives (TPs) (correct matches), false positives (FPs) (surplus or hallucinated extractions) and false negatives (FNs) (missing extractions) in each field.

We then calculated the precision, recall and F 1 score for each JSON output (with precision defined as the fraction of TPs out of all extracted entities (i.e. TP+FP) and recall defined as the fraction of TPs out of all entities that were meant to be extracted (i.e. TP+FN), and F 1 score defined as the harmonic mean of precision and recall). The F 1 score was chosen as the primary metric of comparison as it provides a balanced measure of a model's performance.

We also tracked whether outputs adhered to the JSON formatting requirement at 5 different quality levels: (1) Output only consists of valid JSON; (2) valid JSON embedded in a Markdown code block; (3) valid JSON buried in the middle of text, detectable via searching for pairs of matching braces; (4) Invalid yet repairable JSON (via removal of comments and fixing of missing or superfluous commas, brackets or braces) either buried or embedded in text as above; and (5) Unrepairable, unparseable output. Unparseable outputs were assigned precision, recall and F 1 scores of 0, respectively.

Results

Using our testing framework, we evaluated all five models on our benchmark of 200 simulated molecular test report documents across two different prompting strategies, two input modalities and two quality levels plus the raw text input baseline. Four out of five models had multimodal input capabilities and were tested on all inputs. Llama 3.1 was only able to accept text inputs. GPT 4.1-mini was accessed via a cloud API, NuExtract2 was run locally on a laptop computer via HuggingFace Transformers, and the remaining tools and models were open-weights and were run locally on an HPC cluster via an Ollama server. Out-puts were compared to their references, F 1 scores were calculated and averaged across all outputs per hyperparameter combination.

First, we examined how well tool output conformed to the required JSON format. Most models provided Markdown-embedded JSON. Only LLama 3.1 and NuExtract provided pure perfect JSON for a substantial number of tests. Gemma 3, Llama 3.1 and Mistral-small 3.1 produced small amounts of lower quality outputs, where JSON was buried in text or required repair (22/2,000 = 1.1% ; 13/1,200 = 1.1%; and 23/2,000 = 3.1%, respectively). NuExtract2 behaved unexpectedly. Image and OCR inputs for this model largely resulted in perfect JSON. However, when given the original raw text in Markdown format, NuExtract2 overwhelmingly responded with unusable output. This behavior turned out to be caused by its built-in input processing function, which combines the prompt, JSON-template, one-shot examples and text input into a fixed Markdown structure, such that providing Markdown-formatted input confused the model's ability to determine where the fixed Markdown template ends and the document starts (Figure 2A).

We then compared the mean extraction F 1 scores of different combinations of models, prompts and input modalities (Figure 2B, Supplementary Figure S1). As expected, the GPT 4.1-mini baseline model performed consistently well across all categories, at an average of 79.2% F 1 . On raw document text, GPT 4.1-mini reached a peak performance of 90.7% F 1 (using one-shot prompts). The best performing open-weights models in this category were Gemma 3 and Mistral-small 3.1, reaching peaks of 91.6% and 91.1% F 1 respectively. While statistically significant (q = 2.31 • 10 -8 and q = 4.66 • 10 -7 False discovery rate (FDR)-corrected paired Wilcoxon signed-rank test (pWSRT), respectively), the differences are very small. Their average performances across all quality levels and input modalities differed more markedly however, with Gemma 3 at 66.7% and Mistral-small 3.1 at 76.2% F 1.

Given high quality PDFs (ingestable either as OCRderived text or direct image input), GPT 4.1-mini led similarly with 88.9% F 1 (using one-shot prompts and image inputs), followed by Mistral-small 3.1 at 87.5% F 1 (using one-shot prompts and EasyOCR) with a similarly minor difference of -1.4 percentage points (q = 7.23•10 -5 , FDRcorrected pWSRT)

The distortions caused by the simulated fax machine had a strong effect on model performances across the board. Generally, direct image inputs were superior to external OCR in this scenario. The best performance was once again seen for GPT 4.1-mini at 78.4% F 1 (given oneshot prompts and direct image inputs). Here, the best open-weights model was once again Mistral-small 3.1 at 70.8% F 1 (given zero-shot and direct image input); with a -7.6 percentage point difference (q = 9.57 • 10 -15 , FDRcorrected pWSRT).

The NuExtract2 model performed poorly in all scenarios compared to the bigger models. While this could be expected due to its much smaller size at only 2B parameters, it is nonetheless disappointing given the prospect of its minimal hardware requirements.

The use of one-shot instead of zero-shot prompts showed only very minor effects in most scenarios. NuExtract2 was the only model to see substantial performance increases (+10.3 F 1 percentage points) given one-shot prompts (q = 8.38 • 10 -10 , FDR-corrected pWSRT). While changes for Mistral-small 3.1, Gemma 3, and GPT 4.1 mini were also statistically significant, the effect sizes were numerically negligible (see Figure 2C for q-values and effect sizes).

Direct image input generally appeared to perform worse than external OCR for most models (Figure 2C). GPT 4.1mini stood out as the notable exception, seeing a small +3.2 and +2.7 percentage point increase (q = 1.4 • 10 -11 and q = 9.79 • 10 -10 ; FDR-corrected pWSRT) for zeroand one-shot prompts, respectively. The greatest impact was seen for Gemma 3, showing a -21.0 and -21.9 percentage point F 1 difference given zero-shot and one-shot prompts, respectively.

However as mentioned above, the impact of input modality was highly dependent on document quality. When given fax-distorted documents, direct image inputs performed better than EasyOCR for all vision models, most noticeably so for GPT 4.1-mini (+24.9 and +24.4 percentage points for zero-and one-shot, respectively) and Mistral-small 3.1 (+19.6 and +16.7 points for zero-and one-shot, respectively).

When examining the shapes of the F 1 score distributions, more nuance emerges (Figure 2D). Fax distorted inputs do not merely shift the mean of the F 1 distribution, but greatly increase the variance of the outputs, often reaching from below 20% F 1 to above 90% F 1 . Similarly, the use of image inputs in well performing models often adds a long tail to score distributions.

We next wondered whether the different layout templates had an impact on model performance. The templates differ in format, presentation and the volume of information contained. Unsurprisingly, the synthetic templates J and K were close to the extreme ends of the observed difficulty levels, as they were designed to cover a very minimal and maximal amount of extractable fields, respectively. Template H appeared to be generally easier to process as well due to its relatively simple layout and minimal use of tables. This primarily helped OCR and direct image inputs. Conversely, template C was among the most difficult to process. It includes large redactions and contains many visually complex tables. Another outlier was seen for template G, as it emulates a single-gene test result instead of a gene panel, thus requiring only a single gene name to be extracted and thus providing less opportunity for extraction. (Figure 3).

Next, we wondered what types of information were most difficult to extract across models. We therefore examined precision and recall for each extractable field type separately (Supplementary Figure S2). Fields like the report type, analysis type and testing context, which often needed to be contextually inferred, unsurprisingly suffered the lowest recall.

For the NuExtract model, genetic information, namely gene symbols, transcript IDs, and variants appeared to be the most challenging, suffering the lowest extraction precision. In most layout templates, gene information was densely packed within tables which required models to take into account the spatial positioning of elements to interpret their relationships to each other. This may have contributed to the lower success rate on this type of data.

In addition, gene symbols and transcript IDs appear as arbitrary combinations of letters and numbers, which makes them hard for OCR to recognize and for LLMs to tokenize and process. Depending on the specific typeface used in a given report layout, characters like 0/O, 1/I and S/5 can be difficult to distinguish when not in context of natural language words. As such, a gene symbol like PIP5L1 might be mistakenly read as P1PSLI.

Surprisingly, for all other models, only very few errors related to detailed variant information. This was likely due to the prominent formatting and clarity of sections in the clinical reports related to variant location, prevalence, and related genetic information, as variant information is the primary topic of the reports.

Discussion

Here we have evaluated the performance of LLMs tools for IE from synthetic medical documents using simulated molecular testing reports. While results are based on synthetic documents, they may help guide the use of such tools as part of digitization and data extraction tasks for biomedical research. Below, we discuss insights gained from the effects of document qualities, modalities and prompts, before discussing our conclusions on the suitability of the tested models for different purposes.

Our results showed that the design of document layouts can have a meaningful impact on interpretability by extraction tools. Depending on their internal document designs, individual clinical locations may thus benefit differently from such tools. This emphasizes the importance of choosing large, legible typefaces and clear layouts for clinical documents not only for accessibility reasons, but also for improving automated extraction performance.

Beyond document complexity, the visual quality of the document also appears to have a major impact on extraction success. Image distortions introduced by the faxmachine simulator add to the stack of potential errors: they compound OCR failures, which in turn compound context misinterpretations by the LLM, which in turn compound the ability to extract the needed information and output it in the desired structured format. This is especially true for codes and symbols such as gene names, which contain numerals and letters prone to OCR misin-terpretation (e.g. confusing "0" with "O" or "1" with "I"). This often affected extraction in gene information, especially gene names. However, models with advanced contextual reasoning abilities, notably GPT 4.1-mini, can be expected to be better at rectifying such OCR-induced mistakes during extraction, for example being able to distinguish that BRCAl should actually be BRCA1. Accordingly, documents that underwent our simulated fax-machine procedure saw an average F 1 degradation of 30.0 percentage points when relying on external OCR and 9.2 points when using direct image inputs.

Document quality also had an impact on performance variability. While most models performed relatively consistently on original-quality documents (ranging approximately between 80% and 100% F 1 ), fax-distorted documents yielded wildly varying scores (ranging between approximately 10% and 90%, Figure 2D).

We were surprised to see that using one-shot prompts over zero-shot prompts did not greatly improve extraction results. However, it is conceivable that document complexity may have played a role here as well. Other prompting strategies may be worth exploring in future research.

Prompt design can nonetheless have major impact on extraction performance. An earlier version of this study had accidentally included mistakes in the prompts that while appearing minor to a human eye (such as missing commas in the JSON format description) led to major performance degradations. A detailed ablation study of the effects of such mistakes would be useful to consider in the future.

Model suitability

From a practical perspective, the most reliable model was the GPT 4.1-mini baseline. It consistently ranked among the best results, especially when provided fax-distorted image inputs. However, as a proprietary model only accessible via remote API, it is not suitable for privacy-sensitive applications such as the processing of medical documents.

Given these caveats, the Mistral-small 3.1 model appears to offer the best combination of F 1 performance (91.1% on raw text, 87.5% on clean PDFs, and 70.7% on distorted PDFs), speed and local deployability. However, human oversight is still necessary, especially when working with complex, low-quality documents.

The NuExtract2 model unfortunately came in last. Given its small size and ability to run on laptop hardware, it may still be suitable for simpler extraction tasks under strict hardware constraints. Its apparent incompatibility with Markdown input, as reflected in its poor performance on the raw document texts compared to OCR or images, was disappointing. The additional requirement of concatenating pages into single images further complicates its usage. A larger 4B version of NuExtract2 exists, which also runs on laptop hardware. We tested it on a MacBook Pro with 24GB of RAM and an M4 chip as well as on a MacBook with 16GB of RAM and an M1 chip. In preliminary tests the 4B performed better on OCR-text than its 2B counterpart, but we were unable to make it accept image inputs.

The Gemma 3 model performed admirably. However, its vision capabilities turned out to be disappointing, lagging far behind using EasyOCR input.

The Llama model performed generally in the same ballpark as Gemma and Mistral. As a text-only model it is fully dependent on the quality of the upstream OCR tool (in this case EasyOCR).

Although error rates are impressively low, even top performing models would currently require manual verification steps before deployment in biomedical research settings. While human verification is resource-and timeintensive, it would likely still present an improvement over fully manual extraction. Verification would be a necessary step to mitigate risks associated with erroneous data extractions, thereby maintaining the data integrity critical for research.

Infrastructure and accessibility

Steep hardware requirements for using frontier-class openweights LLMs in data extraction for biomedical research do little to alleviate the equity issues faced by rural and remote communities. However, using such large models may not be necessary. The smaller local models tested here can be executed on a laptop computer. Quantized versions of Gemma 3 (27B) and Mistral-small 3.1 (24B) can theoretically run on a high-end MacBook Pro (albeit monopolizing a majority of its memory). The NuExtract2 model stands out for its very small footprint, although its performance was disappointing. An alternative solution would be the use of remote services. As discussed above, proprietary model APIs such as the one we used for GPT 4.1-mini here are incompatible with patient privacy requirements. However, they should not be the only option. A certified end-to-end encrypted biomedical data extraction service could fill an important niche here.

Limitations

Our benchmark results have several limitations. First, the artificial nature of the test dataset and the limited variety of document types, formats and layouts tested might constrain the generalizability of these results. While we used a variety of eleven different layouts and structures inspired by various clinical sites across Ontario and online examples, our synthetic dataset may not fully reflect real-world scenarios and the potentially broader scope of genomic information and types of clinical reports.

We aimed to offset this limitation by introducing distortions to our synthetic documents that mimic the effects of fax or photo copier machines. The distortions appear visually realistic, but machine vision does not necessarily rely on the same features as human vision. Real world documents can also suffer from other defects that we did not cover here, such as added handwritten annotations, highlights, coffee stains, or wrinkles.

Second, we excluded many tools from our tests for requiring more complex setup. One such requirement was the use of vector databases to enable retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) workflows and few-shot example selection. Such approaches may improve accuracy and reduce the time required for extraction by enabling models to first localize relevant information within lengthy clinical reports before performing extraction. However, this requires a dedicated dataset with partitioned examples. While we consider these improvements out of scope for this benchmark, future research may incorporate these ideas.

Third, we only examined two broad prompting strategies. However, a wide range of other strategies exists, featuring a great amount of nuance which we cannot capture here. Even though our results only showed limited differences between zero-shot and one-shot prompts, other approaches may impact model performance more drastically. Future research may include additional prompting strategies. Having seen incidental evidence of performance degradation due to typos and formatting errors in prompts during experimentation furthermore suggests that a dedicated ablation study on the effects of prompt formats, formulations and errors may be warranted.

Fourth, we only tested LLM-based tools in this benchmark, forgoing older rule-based IE systems. However, we did not find any such systems conforming to our selection criteria. Most rule-based extraction systems are bespoke to specific tasks and domains and often focus on entityrelationship extractions.

Finally, due to our exclusion criteria, we only tested five different models. The LLM field is currently very active with new models being published every month. Further studies could expand to benchmarking a wider range of foundation models such as Qwen [1] or DeepSeek [12].

Future work

We may expand our benchmark in the future to include newer models. Future research may also explore more advanced architectures such as RAG to enhance extraction accuracy and efficiency. It would also be useful to expand our benchmark beyond molecular test report documents towards other types of medical documents. Finally, it would be worth exploring to what extent task-specific finetuning of the larger multimodal models such as Gemma 3 can improve their extraction performance.

Conclusion

This research aimed to review and benchmark LLM-based IE tools from the current literature on complex clinical documents, namely molecular test reports. We tested five different models and while their accuracy was impressive, they still require human oversight and manual validation. This underlines the need for further research into improving these tools.
