Reporting p values imprecisely (e.g., p < .05) reduces transparency, reproducibility, and re-use (e.g., in p value meta-analyses). Best practice is to report exact p-values with three decimal places (e.g., p = .032) unless p values are smaller than 0.001, in which case you can use p < .001.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (e.g., “decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 1 non-significant p value that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Metacheck does not yet analyze automatically whether sentences which include non-significant p-values are correct, but we recommend manually checking the sentences below for possible misinterpreted non-significant p values.

Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). Statcheck might still miss one-sided tests that are described in another sentence, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. Full text

Abstract

Echocardiographic image quality influences clinical interpretation, yet its definition remains subjective and its impact on artificial intelligence (AI) systems has not been systematically evaluated. We developed EchoQuality, a deep-learning model for automated echocardiography quality assessment, and quantified the effect of image quality on AI performance across a broad range of tasks.

A multidisciplinary co-creation process established a definition of echocardiographic image quality, which was used to annotate 6,474 videos for EchoQuality development and internal testing, and 1,000 videos for external testing. The impact of image quality on the performance of state-of-the-art AI models was subsequently assessed for the prediction of 12 measurements, and the detection of 5 moderate dysfunctions and 6 cardiac diseases across five international cohorts totalling 762,588 videos from 25,205 echocardiography studies. In addition, a quality-aware aggregation strategy was developed to mitigate the impact of low-quality images.

EchoQuality achieved a performance comparable to the inter-observer agreement, while the agreement with an expert in a prospective, blinded best-clip selection experiment was 90.4%. In downstream AI uses, high-quality studies resulted in the highest performance in 97 of 122 (80%) of evaluations. The median performance gaps between high-and lowquality videos were 10% [5-20%] in the prediction of quantitative measurements (R² ), 14% [7-20%] in the identification of moderate dysfunctions (sensitivity), 12% [3-20%] in the detection of diseases (sensitivity), 12% [9-15%] in view identification (accuracy), and 3% [2-5%] in left-ventricular segmentation (Dice score). Performance gaps were consistent across state-of-the-art models, datasets, and tasks. Finally, a quality-aware aggregation strategy improved performance in 88% (63 of 72) of the evaluations, with a median relative improvement of 5% [1-15%].

EchoQuality provides an automated assessment of echocardiographic image quality, a major determinant of AI performance. Incorporating quality-awareness may improve the robustness and clinical reliability of echocardiographic AI systems.

Introduction

Echocardiography is a pillar of clinical cardiology, being indicated throughout the spectrum of practice. Yet, the technology and manual acquisition of echocardiographic images entails high variability in image appearance and quality.

Echocardiography image quality is assessed visually to estimate the degree of uncertainty of eventual clinical findings 1,2 , and it has been shown to depend on patient body habitus, clinical condition, patient position, sonographer experience, and scanner [3] [4] [5] , among others. Importantly, limited image quality negatively impacts the accuracy of the standard quantification of left ventricular (LV) ejection fraction [6] [7] [8] , size 5 , mass 9 , strain 5, 10, 11 , and other features 12.

Artificial intelligence (AI) applications to echocardiography have been growing. Examples include the support of acquisition [13] [14] [15] , view identification 16, 17 , and the assessment of multiple descriptors and diagnoses [17] [18] [19] [20] [21] . Most of research in automatic image quality assessment in echocardiography focused on quality control for AI-guided image acquisition 13- 15,22,23 and mostly defined "quality" as the extent to which a given video corresponds to one of the standard imaging planes. Beyond applications related to AI-guided acquisition, several works presented AI models to predict echocardiographic image quality [24] [25] [26] [27] [28] [29] . Nonetheless, most of image quality definitions included a mix of view-related characteristics, such as foreshortening and the presence of the expected cardiac structures, and image appearance characteristics. Only two previous works presented models aiming at predicting image quality beyond structural and viewrelated characteristics 28 . Among them, one article presented an external validation 28 , none a prospective validation.

The impact of predicted image quality on the performance of AI models in downstream tasks was included in two works 24, 28 , both limited to left-ventricular ejection fraction assessment, and no paper reported the impact on the performance of state-of-the-art AI models for echocardiography, nor in the prediction of cardiac diseases. Thus, whether echocardiography image quality affects AI models performance remains largely unexplored.

We hypothesized that an AI model, hereinafter referred to as EchoQuality, could replicate human-based quality assessment, permitting reproducible and scalable image quality grading. We further hypothesized that image quality systematically impacts AI model performance and that incorporating awareness of image quality into AI inference can improve accuracy.

Methods

Data

Six cohorts were analyzed (Figure 1). Firstly, Hospital Universitari Vall d'Hebron (HUVH), the EchoQuality development center, contributed a clinical dataset comprising 15,063 transthoracic echocardiographic (TTE) studies from 9,841 patients acquired during routine clinical care between 2019 and 2023. The local Ethics Committee waived informed consent. This dataset was randomly split at patient level into three non-overlapping datasets: (i) HUVH-quality, comprising 8,840 videos from 463 studies from 458 patients, of which 6,474 were retained after excluding videos classified as other views, further randomly split into 5,056 training and 1,418 testing videos, the latter constituting also the HUVH-view dataset used for view identification evaluation; (ii) HUVH-testing, including 442,090 videos from 14,500 studies from 9,283 patients to test the impact of image quality in AI models performance; and (iii) HUVH-BestClip, a set of 100 studies of 100 patients to be prospectively analyzed (see below). Additionally, a dedicated research dataset (HUVH-POCUS) comprising 2,458 conventional TTE videos and 1,521 same-day point-of-care ultrasound (POCUS) videos from 100 consecutive patients, each signing informed consent, was available.

Five external datasets were used to externally validate EchoQuality predictions or to assess image quality impact in AI performance. One was the (i) MIMIC-IV-ECHO [30] [31] [32] [33] [34] , including 279,660 videos from 7,059 studies from 4,552 patients in the United States. Following 35 , measurements were extracted from videos using optical character recognition (Figure S1). The (ii) Prince of Wales Hospital (PWH, Hong Kong) provided 112,883 videos from 1,310 studies acquired in 1,238 patients during clinical practice, while the (iii) Amsterdam University Medical Center (AUMC, The Netherlands) contributed 34,310 videos from 1,004 studies acquired in 890 patients during clinical practice. The (iv) Flemish Study on Environment, Genes and Health Outcomes (FLEMENGHO, Belgium), a population-based cohort study, provided 59,431 videos from 1,332 studies and 1,332 individuals. Finally, the (v) CAMUS 36 dataset (France), contributed 1,000 apical 2-and 4-chamber videos from 500 patients.

6

EchoQuality development

Image quality definition

During a co-creation process involving cardiologists, sonographers, and researchers, the key characteristics determining the perception of image quality were discussed (Figure 1A). The discussion produced an initial discrimination between characteristics related to the imaging view and those related to image appearance. Regarding view-related characteristics, the participants agreed on the importance of (i) acquiring standard views, (ii) the presence of all structures expected in that view, and, in apical views, (iii) the correct alignment with left-ventricular (LV) long axis. Regarding image appearance, participants agreed that the key characteristic is (iv) the difference in image brightness between cardiac structures and blood pool, or, in other words, the contrast between blood and cardiac structures, which determines structural boundaries definition. As well-performing, openly-available AI algorithms for view identification can identify suboptimal views 16, 17 , subsequent model development focused on the image-appearance characteristic. This metric, hereinafter referred to as image quality, was selected as the primary quality metric to be predicted by EchoQuality and was categorized using a three-level scale as "low", "mid", and "high".

Annotations

Following co-creation, three experienced cardiologists annotated the HUVH-quality dataset. The annotation included (i) view (apical 2-, 3-, 4-and 5-chamber, parasternal long-and short-axis, and "other views"), (ii) presence of all expected cardiac structures, (iii) apical foreshortening, and (iv) image quality. A subset of 350 videos was independently annotated by two experts to assess inter-observer reproducibility. Furthermore, a cardiologist annotated all 1000 CAMUS videos, hereinafter called "in-house" annotations to distinguish them from the "original" quality annotations of CAMUS. In addition, in the HUVH-BestClip dataset, an independent clinician not involved in the co-creation sessions prospectively identified the best video per view, to be compared with EchoQuality grading in the prospective validation.

Model development and validation

EchoQuality was developed as a deep learning model to assess the quality of 2D B-mode echocardiography videos. The model was trained by fine-tuning PanEcho 19 with the HUVH-quality dataset, using view classification, the three abovementioned view-related characteristics, and image quality as video-level multitask outputs. Additional implementation details are provided in Tables S1 and S2. Model weights are available at https://github.com/CardiovascularImagingVallHebron/EchoQuality.

For clinical tasks with study-level outcome a study-level quality metric was required. Mirroring how variable quality is handled in clinical practice, video-level EchoQuality predictions were aggregated using a two-stage hierarchical rule: first, within each view, the highest-quality video defined the view-level quality grade; second, view-level grades were aggregated to study level using majority voting, with highest quality winning in case of draw.

Finally, to evaluate the effect of acquisition modalities on image quality, EchoQuality grade was computed separately for conventional and POCUS videos in the HUVH-POCUS dataset.

Downstream evaluation

To characterize the impact of image quality on AI performance, EchoQuality was deployed in a large-scale, multicenter inference spanning heterogeneous cohorts, AI models, and echocardiographic assessments. Three classes of clinical tasks and two standard AI pre-processing tasks were considered.

Clinical tasks

Two state-of-the-art AI models, PanEcho and EchoPrime 17 , were evaluated for 23 predictions, grouped in three sets of tasks.

The first set of tasks concerned the quantification of twelve measurements: LV ejection fraction (LVEF), LV end-diastolic and end-systolic volumes (LVEDV, LVESV), LV diastolic and systolic diameters, LV posterior wall and interventricular septal thickness, left atrial (LA) end-systolic volume and diameter, mitral inflow velocity ratio (E/e'), tricuspid systolic excursion (TAPSE), and aortic valve peak velocity (AV Vmax). These analyses were performed in all but CAMUS datasets, and centerand quality-specific results were reported if at least 50 observations were available.

The second set of tasks concerned the detection of five at least moderate dysfunctions: LV systolic dysfunction (defined as LVEF<40%), aortic valve stenosis (Vmax≥3 m/s), LV dilation (indexed LVEDV>90 ml/m 2 in males, >71 in females), LV hypertrophy (LV wall thickness≥14 mm in males, ≥13 in females), and LA dilation (indexed LA volume≥42 mL/m 2 ). These analyses were performed in all but CAMUS datasets, and center-and quality-specific results were reported if at least 20 positive cases were available.

The third set of tasks concerned the detection of six diseases: aortic valve regurgitation and stenosis, mitral valve regurgitation and stenosis, pericardial effusion, and tricuspid valve regurgitation. These analyses were performed in four datasets, as FLEMENGHO and CAMUS have no labels, and center-and quality-specific results were reported if at least 20 positive cases were available.

Preprocessing tasks

For basic AI-preprocessing tasks, view identification was evaluated using the Zhang model 16 and EchoPrime 17 in the HUVHview dataset. LV segmentation was evaluated using two models [37] [38] [39] trained on EchoNetDynamic 40 and CAMUS, and tested on CAMUS.

Quality-aware aggregation

To test whether image quality could be exploited to improve study-level predictions, we implemented a quality-aware strategy designed to emulate how video-level information is aggregated in echocardiographic practice, where measurements derived from suboptimal images are disregarded in favor of those obtained from high-quality images.

Accordingly, video-level predictions were aggregated using a continuous EchoQuality-weight, such that higher-quality videos contribute more to study-level predictions (Supplementary Material). This analysis was restricted to measurements and moderate dysfunctions detection using PanEcho, as it provides video-level outputs suitable for aggregation.

Statistical analysis

Balanced accuracy was used to assess inter-observer agreement and EchoQuality validation performance, the latter additionally complemented by area under the ROC curve (AUROC). In downstream assessments, performance was evaluated using R² and mean absolute error (MAE) for measurements, sensitivity and specificity for moderate dysfunctions and disease detection, accuracy for view identification, and Dice score for LV segmentation. For disease detection, cohortspecific optimal thresholds were derived via Youden index. For all analyses, 95% confidence intervals were computed using 1,000 bootstraps. For PanEcho analysis video-level predictions were aggregated to study level using only the views where the structure to be measured is visible while for the Zhang view identification model grouping of view predictions was performed (Table S3 and S4). Group comparisons were performed using the Kruskal-Wallis or Mann-Whitney tests for continuous variables and the chi-square test for categorical variables. All analyses were performed using Python 3.11.9, and a p-value≤0.050 was considered significant.

The funding sources had not role in the study design, data collection, analysis and interpretation, or writing of the report.

Results

Results are presented in three sections: first, EchoQuality validation, second, impact of image quality on downstream AI performance, and finally the impact of quality-aware predictions.

EchoQuality validation

In the internal testing set of the HUVH-quality dataset, which includes 630 (40%) low-, 663 (47%) mid-, and 125 (9%) high- quality videos, EchoQuality achieved a balanced accuracy of 63.2% [CI95%: 59.8-66.0%], comparable to the inter-observer variability 47.9% [38.3-58.5%].

The discriminative performance was good (AUROC 0.784 [0.778-0.790]), with best separation for high-quality videos (0.863 [0.852-0.875]) and lower for mid-quality videos (0.658 [0.647-0.667]), reflecting the intrinsic ambiguity of intermediate quality. In the external validation cohort, EchoQuality achieved a balanced accuracy of 50.9% [44.4-57.1%] and overall AUROC of 0.681 [0.658-0.703] against the in-house annotations, and a balanced accuracy of 52.3% [49.4-55.1%] and an AUROC of 0.651 [0.628-0.676] against original CAMUS quality grades. Of note, the agreement between in-house and original CAMUS annotations was very limited (21.0% [18.5-23.5%]), with in-house annotations systematically assigning lower quality (Figure S2).

In the prospective validation study on the HUVH-BestClip cohort, expert clip selection was strongly aligned with EchoQuality grade ranking: the expert selected a video from the highest EchoQuality grade in 90.4% (207/229) of cases.

Regarding the aggregation of video-level predictions into study-level quality grades, aggregated EchoQuality grades showed strong concordance with quality annotations from echocardiographic reports (Figure 2A). For example, 67% of studies described as "bad quality" during clinical care were classified as low-quality by EchoQuality, whereas 12% of studies described as good or excellent during care were classified as low quality by EchoQuality.

In the HUVH-POCUS dataset, EchoQuality grades were consistently higher in conventional studies (52 high, 39 mid, 8 low) compared with same-day POCUS (15 high, 43 mid, 41 low), with no patient showing lower quality in conventional echocardiography than in POCUS (Figure 2B).

Impact of image quality

A total of 762,588 videos from 25,205 echocardiography studies across five cohorts and three continents were included (Table 1).

Clinical, demographic, and temporal patterns

Age was inversely associated with EchoQuality grade in HUVH-testing, AUMC and FLEMENGHO (p < 0.05) but not in PWH (p = 0.242), where the age range was minor, while minimal differences were noted with respect to patient sex (p = 0.050, Figure 2C). BMI was inversely associated with EchoQuality grade in every cohort (all p < 0.050). Over time, the overall proportion of low-quality studies decreased from 35.3% before 2017 to 14.1% by 2023-2025, while the proportion of highquality studies increased from 12.0% to 48.7% (Figure 2D). This temporal analysis was replicated in the HUVH cohort, where the same monotonic improvement in quality distribution over time was observed (Figure S3).

AI-based assessments

Across all measurements, severity stratifications, disease detections, preprocessing tasks, and cohorts and models, highquality studies achieved the best performance in 97 of 122 evaluations (80%), compared with 16 (13%) for mid-and 9 (7%) for low-quality studies. In the HUVH-testing cohort, where study-level quality was visually graded during care, the differences in performance of AI models with respect to image quality were equivalent to those obtained with EchoQuality (Figure S4, Table S5).

Measurements

High-quality studies achieved the best performance in 42 of 56 measurements (75%), with R² difference between highand low-quality studies of 0.10 [0.05-0.20] (Figure 3). R² values across all cohorts and measurements were 0.47 [0.27-0.59], 0.36 [0.27-0.50], and 0.33 [0.15-0.46] for high-, mid-, and low-quality studies, respectively. Cohort-specific median R² in high-, mid-, and low-quality videos were: HUVH 0.54, 0.47, 0.40; MIMIC-IV-ECHO 0.50, 0.38, 0.33; PWH 0.45, 0.36, 0.33; AUMC 0.51, 0.33, 0.29; and FLEMENGHO 0.18, 0.20, 0.06. These performance differences were confirmed for MAE (Figure S4), and were consistent across the vast majority of measurements, and the two models (Table S6). The qualitydependent effect was particularly evident for LVEF, where the mean R² gap between high-and low-quality studies across cohorts was 0.26 for PanEcho and 0.23 for EchoPrime. Importantly, across measurements, cohorts, and models, predictions in low-quality studies showed a more marked tendency to regress toward the cohort-specific median values compared with predictions in mid-and high-quality studies, as illustrated for LVEF in Figure S6.

Detection of moderate dysfunctions

The detection of at least moderate dysfunctions showed large differences in AI performances comparing quality grades, with high-quality studies resulting in the best performance in 17 of 20 cases (85%), compared with 3 (15%) and 0 (0%) for mid-and low-quality studies, respectively. The median sensitivity difference between high-and low-quality studies was 0.14 [0.07-0.20]. Sensitivities across all cohorts and dysfunction types were 0.39 [0.19-0.52], 0.31 [0.14-0.35], and 0.26 [0.09-0.31] in high-, mid-, and low-quality studies, respectively. Cohort-specific sensitivities in high-, mid-, and low-quality videos were: HUVH 0.42, 0.34, 0.32; MIMIC-IV-ECHO 0.22, 0.10, 0.11; PWH 0.38, 0.32, N/A; AUMC 0.22, N/A, N/A, respectively, where N/A indicates quality strata with too few positive cases. Notably, this quality-dependent difference in sensitivity was consistently observed across cohorts, conditions, and models (Figure 4, Table S7), and was accompanied by very modest increase in specificity, which remained high across quality strata. The differences were particularly pronounced for the detection of LV systolic dysfunction, LV dilation, and LA dilation, where the mean sensitivity gap between high-and low-quality studies were 27%, 21%, and 17%, respectively. Remarkably, models more frequently failed to identify positive cases in low-quality compared with high-quality studies, favoring the majority class.

Disease detection

Across the four datasets with available diagnostic information, high-quality studies obtained the best performance in 34 of 42 evaluations (81%), compared with 3 (7%) and 5 (12%) of cases in mid-and low-quality studies, respectively. The median difference in sensitivity in high-quality compared with low-quality studies was 0.12 [0.03-0.20], with sensitivity of 0.75 [0.59-0.83], 0.60 [0.50-0.69], and 0.61 [0.50-0.70] in high-, mid-, and low-quality studies, respectively. Cohort-specific AUROC in high-, mid-, and low-quality videos were: HUVH 0.73, 0.61, 0.62; MIMIC-IV-ECHO 0.79, 0.66, 0.63; PWH 0.78, 0.58, 0.64; AUMC 0.63, 0.43, 0.49. This behavior was consistent over diseases, cohorts, and models (Figure 4, Table S8), and was accompanied by moderate negative differences of -0.06 [-0.14-0.03] in specificity.

Preprocessing tasks

In a total of 1,418 videos from the HUVH-view dataset, the accuracy of both models, i.e., Zhang and EchoPrime, in view identification was consistently associated with EchoQuality grade. In particular, the accuracy obtained by the Zhang model increased from 82.5% in low-quality to 92.3% in mid-and 95.8% in high-quality videos, while for EchoPrime accuracies were 93.6, 96.9% and 98.4%, respectively, with consistent trends across views (Figure S7). Inter-model agreement in view classification was strongly influenced by image quality: disagreement rates were highest in low-quality videos (21.9%) and much lower in mid-and high-quality videos (10.7% and 6.5%, respectively). 16 Finally, in CAMUS testing set, the Dice score for LV segmentation improved significantly with EchoQuality grade for both models. Absolute increases from low-to high-quality frames were 0.05 for the EchoNetDynamic-trained model (0.75 to 0.80) and 0.02 for the CAMUS-trained model (0.93 to 0.95).

Quality-aware aggregation

Quality-aware aggregation improved performance in 63 of 72 evaluations (88%), with a relative improvement of 5% [1-15%] (Figure 5, Figure S8, Table S9). The largest gains were observed in the detection of moderate dysfunctions, with an overall sensitivity improvement of 18% [8-23%]. Such improvement was consistent across cohorts: HUVH 18% [7-21%]; MIMIC-IV-ECHO 20% [19-38%]; PWH 14% [6-15%]; AUMC 18% [14-23%]. Similarly, the performances in quantitative measurements showed an overall median R² improvement of 4% [1-6%], which was consistent across cohorts: HUVH 3% [1-5%]; MIMIC-IV-ECHO 5% [3-9%]; PWH 3% [1-6%]; AUMC 3% [0-12%]; FLEMENGHO 1% [-10-40%]. Notably, quality-aware aggregation partially mitigated the tendency of low-quality videos to drive predictions toward cohort-specific central values or more prevalent classes, thereby improving the identification of more extreme values and detection of moderate dysfunctions (Figure S9).

Discussion

This study presents EchoQuality, a deep learning model for echocardiographic image quality assessment, and reports the impact of image quality on state-of-the-art echocardiographic AI models in the largest external evaluation ever reported.

Through large-scale evaluation across cohorts, models, and prediction tasks, EchoQuality demonstrated consistent stratification of AI performance. Results demonstrated that EchoQuality can be deployed at frame, video, and study levels and enables quality-aware handling of echocardiography videos.

EchoQuality achieved a balanced accuracy of 63% on the internal testing set, and 51 and 52% on the external testing set using in-house and original annotations, respectively, slightly above the 48% inter-observer agreement among cardiologists, underscoring that performance is bound by the inherent subjectivity of quality. This subjectivity was underlined by the limited agreement between the original CAMUS quality annotations and those performed by HUVH cardiologists, which consistently assigned lower quality grades. As harmonization of quality criteria remains an open challenge, EchoQuality provides a standardized and reproducible framework that may fill this gap.

Additional evidence supporting EchoQuality validity was observed. First, in the HUVH-view dataset, the expert selected a clip with the highest available EchoQuality grade in 90.4% of cases, demonstrating strong agreement between automated quality grading and expert best-clip selection in a prospective, independent analysis. Second, study-level EchoQuality grades were significantly associated with quality assessed during clinical practice, supporting the construct of EchoQuality as a study-level indicator. Finally, quality grades were systematically higher for conventional echocardiography than sameday POCUS, suggesting that EchoQuality has truly learned generalizable image quality features.

EchoQuality grades further demonstrated meaningful demographic and temporal associations. Higher BMI was associated with lower quality, consistent with echocardiography technical limitations 9, 41 , while the proportion of low-quality studies decreased over time, as expected by the progressive technology improvements. Interestingly, such temporal shifts may progressively redefine what constitutes low image quality, and may support EchoQuality for data drift monitoring in clinical applications.

Overall, high-quality studies resulted in superior AI model performance in 80% of evaluations. Compared with low-quality studies, the median absolute performance differences in high-quality studies were 0.10 for R² , 0.14 and 0.12 for sensitivity in dysfunction and disease detections, 0.12 for view identification accuracy, and 0.03 for Dice Score in LV segmentation. This coherent directionality across heterogeneous cohorts, assessments, and models suggests that EchoQuality grade is a consistent determinant of AI model performance. The largest performance gaps, exceeding 20% in sensitivity between high-and low-quality studies for key conditions, occurred in guideline-derived severity classifications, which directly guide clinical management. Overall, these findings highlight the clinical risk of deploying AI-based tools without explicit quality control.

A particularly important observation was that predictions from low-quality videos tended to regress toward median values and most prevalent classes. For severity classification and disease detection, this behavior was characterized by markedly lower sensitivity and slightly higher specificity in low-quality studies, consistent with a shift toward conservative predictions. This behavior suggests that low quality impacts the amount of signal available and raises substantial concerns about the readiness of these models to flag high-risk patients deserving particularly-careful human evaluation.

Importantly, a quality-aware aggregation strategy improved performance in 84% of evaluations, suggesting that safety in echocardiographic AI systems may be enhanced by quality-aware selection and weighting.

When considering the present findings, certain study limitations should be considered. First, EchoQuality was developed using exclusively transthoracic 2D cine B-mode acquisitions and was not evaluated in transesophageal echocardiography, color Doppler imaging, and 1D and 3D echocardiography. Second, the definition of image quality was established through a process performed at a single center and may therefore reflect center-specific perception. Multicenter and inclusive processes should be performed to derive a broadly shared definition of echocardiography image quality. Third, analyses could be affected by the inter-center variability in the clinical definition and computation of echocardiographic measurements. Nonetheless, as most analyses were based on within-center comparisons, this is unlikely to have materially influenced the main findings.

In conclusion, EchoQuality is an automated, openly-available framework to quantify image quality in echocardiography videos. Across multiple international cohorts and tasks, higher image quality was consistently associated with superior performance of state-of-the-art AI models, while low image quality led to systematic deterioration and regression toward population-average predictions. Incorporating awareness of image quality into AI workflows may improve robustness and clinical reliability.
