To fix Exact P-Values

What we found

Show details

Reporting p values imprecisely (e.g., p < .05) reduces transparency, reproducibility, and re-use (e.g., in p value meta-analyses). Best practice is to report exact p-values with three decimal places (e.g., p = .032) unless p values are smaller than 0.001, in which case you can use p < .001.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (e.g., “decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

The APA manual states: Report exact p values (e.g., p = .031) to two or three decimal places. However, report p values less than .001 as p < .001. However, 2 decimals is too imprecise for many use-cases (e.g., a p value meta-analysis), so report p values with three digits.

P values are never exactly zero. A p value of .000 is a rounding artifact — the actual value is simply smaller than the reported precision. Very small p values should be reported as p < .001 rather than p = .000. A p = 0 can also be a model parameter, such as an ARIMA order; check that it is a p value.

To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 2 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Metacheck does not yet analyze automatically whether sentences which include non-significant p-values are correct, but we recommend manually checking the sentences below for possible misinterpreted non-significant p values.

About the personal-information flags. These are pattern matches, not confirmed disclosures, and they are tuned to over-report rather than miss something. Expect false positives:

  • Column names are matched against identifying words in English and other European languages (email, telefoonnummer, geboortedatum, …). A column called phone_number flags whether or not it holds one, and an empty or already-anonymised column flags on its name alone.
  • Values are matched against the shape of an identifier. An IP address pattern also matches a version string or a dotted numeric code; a card-number pattern requires both a real issuer prefix and a checksum, but a long numeric ID can still coincide.
  • Free text is flagged when a column holds long, mostly-distinct prose — the shape open-ended answers take. Most such columns contain no names at all.

A flag means look at this column. Where the data is already de-identified (an ip column reading Anonymized, a name column the authors emptied), no action is needed.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 128 papers with 246 instances of power statements, 203 were correctly detected (true positives), 22 were missed (false negatives) and 21 were incorrectly detected (false positives). Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

How Marginal Significance works

The marginal module searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The list of terms is a subset of those listed in a blog post by Matthew Hankins. The module returns all sentences that match terms describing ‘marginally significant’ results.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. Some differences come from the ground truth: it lacks p-values with a correction name (“p Bonf = .061”), and counts partial eta squared written with its subscript after it (“η 2 p = .05”), “p = .05”, and some table notes and figure legends (“† p < .10, *p < .05”), which this module leaves out, as non-significant p-values. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. Abstract

Claims that an intervention is safe often rest on a nonsignificant difference, confusing absence of evidence with evidence of absence; an integrated, reproducible procedure for auditing them is lacking. We developed and evaluated one, applying it to diagnostic hysteroscopy in endometrial cancer, which published syntheses describe as oncologically safe.

The framework's eight steps verify each number, test the direction of confounding, require timeto-event analysis, bound detectability and apply one information-adjusted boundary to claims of harm and of safety (verdicts: harm established, safety established, inconclusive). The rule was fixed before a retrospective test on minimally invasive radical hysterectomy for cervical cancer, later assessed in a randomized trial, and evaluated analytically and by simulation. We audited 18 comparative hysteroscopy studies: 12 with peritoneal cytology (4,078 patients), 5 with time-toevent recurrence. With a true hazard ratio (HR) of 1.25 and the information available for hysteroscopy, inferring safety from nonsignificance gave false reassurance in 34% of analyses and the rule in 1%; with adequate information and no harm, the rule established safety in 80%.

Pre-trial evidence on minimally invasive radical hysterectomy proved inconclusive, not safe. For hysteroscopy, controlling for disease extent revealed an association with positive cytology (odds ratio 2.86, 95% confidence interval [CI] 1.24-6.62) absent from crude comparisons; recurrence data (HR 1.17, Hartung-Knapp 95% CI 0.90-1.52) held 71% of required information and were inconclusive at margins of 1.10-1.40; and cytology-mediated harm would be undetectable in low-risk disease. Limitations include development on the case to which it is applied, a single author-selected retrospective test, no independent or prospective validation, and reliance on published observational data.

What do these findings mean?

• A claim of safety should state the harm it rules out: with the two limits of a published 95% confidence interval, one table gives the verdict and its meaning within minutes, and a worksheet, a one-page committee summary, a free online calculator and open code support a full audit by clinicians, reviewers and guideline panels.

Introduction

Claims that an intervention is safe are among the most consequential statements in clinical evidence, because they remove an option from scrutiny. Such claims are often based on the absence of a statistically significant difference, yet a nonsignificant result is not evidence of no difference unless the confidence interval excludes clinically important effects and the evidence was large enough to detect them [1]. Misinterpretation of nonsignificant results remains common even among randomized trials published in high-impact journals [2], and it persists in current syntheses. A 2022 meta-analysis of uterine manipulators in minimally invasive surgery for endometrial cancer found no significant difference in recurrence (risk ratio 1.11, 95% confidence interval [CI] 0.71-1.74), concluded that manipulators do not increase recurrence and questioned whether further studies were needed [3]; yet that interval was compatible with a 74% increase in risk, and a 2026 meta-analysis of time-to-event data found shorter disease-free survival with manipulator use (hazard ratio 1.18, 95% CI 1.01-1.38) [4]. In observational evidence about harm, two further problems compound the error. Confounding by indication can bias comparisons toward no effect [5], and numbers transcribed between secondary sources can drift from what primary studies reported [6, 7]. Because systematic reviews and meta-analyses are numerous and often overlap [8], an inference that has not been checked against primary data can be carried forward into later syntheses and guidance.

Existing methods address parts of this problem. The Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) approach rates the certainty of evidence and treats confidence intervals that include appreciable harm as imprecise [9, 10]; the Risk Of Bias In Non-randomised Studies of Interventions (ROBINS-I) tool assesses bias in individual nonrandomized studies [11]; trial sequential analysis relates the information accrued to the information required [12]; procedures for statistical and clinical significance combine several of these elements for meta-analyses of randomized trials [13]; equivalence testing formalizes how the absence of an effect can be supported [14]; and E-values quantify the confounding needed to explain an association away [15]. What is missing is an integrated, reproducible procedure for one specific and common question: does the evidence establish the absence of clinically important harm, or merely fail to show harm? Such a procedure must verify the numbers before analyzing them, predict and test the direction of bias, ask whether the harm could have been detected at all, and connect the result to a decision rule that neither dismisses uncertainty nor exaggerates it.

That decision rule is the domain of the precautionary principle. In a widely cited formulation, precaution involves preventive action in the face of uncertainty, a shift of the burden of proof to the proponents of an activity, exploration of alternatives and broader participation in decisions [16]. Critics have argued that strong versions of the principle are incoherent, because inaction and alternatives also carry risks [17], and others have pointed out that precaution applies to precautionary measures themselves [18]. Steel's account answers these objections by requiring a credible scientific case for harm (knowledge condition), a threat of sufficient seriousness (harm condition) and a proportionate response; a measure is proportionate when it is not itself ruled out by the same principle (consistency) and is the least burdensome among those that are not (efficiency) [19]. These elements can be made operational, but they are rarely applied in clinical evidence synthesis. Diagnostic hysteroscopy in endometrial cancer is a suitable test case. Joint statements of gynecologic societies favor visually directed over blind endometrial sampling wherever feasible [20, 21], and cancer is present in about 9% of women with postmenopausal bleeding [22] and in 13.1% of hysteroscopic polypectomies in women aged 70 years or older who bleed [23].

Distension can carry fluid through the fallopian tubes above a threshold pressure [24], and transtubal passage of viable tumor cells has been documented after hysteroscopy in women with endometrial cancer [25]. Meta-analyses have found more positive peritoneal cytology after hysteroscopy [26, 27], and positive cytology is associated with worse outcomes, including in lowrisk disease [28, 29]. Yet published syntheses have concluded that hysteroscopy is oncologically safe, mainly because survival did not differ significantly with and without it [30] [31] [32].

We aimed to develop an explicit and reproducible framework for auditing claims of safety; to evaluate its central decision rule by exact calculation and simulation; to test it retrospectively, with the rule fixed in advance, on a question whose answer is now known; to apply it to diagnostic hysteroscopy in endometrial cancer; and to provide the data, code, a worksheet, a calculator that runs in any web browser, a table that applies the central step to any published confidence interval without software and a protocol for independent audits, so that clinicians, reviewers and guideline panels can apply it to other claims. The contribution is one of integration rather than of new statistics: a single decision rule, applied symmetrically to claims of harm and of safety against a margin set in advance, preceded by checks on the numbers, the direction of bias, the effect scale and detectability, and followed by a decision that states what evidence would change it. The rule itself is not new: it is the single-analysis boundary of trial sequential analysis, applied with equal stringency to claims of harm and of safety. What the framework adds is the order of the checks around it and a strict separation between the verdict, which the locked rule computes, and the precautions, which require clinical judgment and are reported with the assumptions on which they depend.

Methods

Study design and analysis plan

This is a methodological study with three components: development of an audit framework, evaluation of its central decision rule, and two applications, a retrospective test on a question whose answer is now known and an audit of diagnostic hysteroscopy in endometrial cancer. No prospective protocol was written or registered. The analyses were determined in the following sequence. First, the eight steps were developed iteratively while the hysteroscopy evidence was being re-analyzed. Second, before the retrospective test, the decision rule of Step 5 was specified, implemented in code and locked: the margin of 1.25, the information required for 80% power at a two-sided α of 0.05, the boundary function and the definitions of the three verdicts. Third, the locked rule was applied unchanged to the retrospective test and to the evaluation of its operating characteristics; nothing in the rule was modified after the retrospective results were seen. The rule contains no parameter estimated from data: its constants are conventional (the GRADE threshold for appreciable harm, 80% power, a two-sided α of 0.05 and an O'Brien-Fleming-type boundary), which leaves little room for tuning. Development and testing of the rule were therefore kept apart: the rule was fixed before it was applied to minimally invasive radical hysterectomy, the only empirical test of the rule reported here, whereas the hysteroscopy application illustrates the complete framework, supplied none of the rule's constants and is not offered as a test of it.

We regard the locked Step 5 rule, the evaluation of its operating characteristics and the retrospective test as prespecified analyses. Four sets of analyses were defined after the main results had been seen and are reported as exploratory: (1) the grouping of cytology studies by whether disease extent was controlled, which is based on a design feature of each comparison but was defined during the analysis; (2) sensitivity analyses for the margin, for the prognostic weight of induced positive cytology and for the pretest probability of cancer; (3) boundaries calibrated recursively for repeated updates of the same evidence; and (4) the presentation of Step 5 as an audit interval and as a table of confidence limits, both algebraically equivalent to the locked rule.

None of these exploratory analyses changed a Step 5 verdict reported for either application.

The hysteroscopy application is an illustrative audit, not a systematic review, and it was not registered. Its structured search and selection are reported according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 statement [33] (S1 Checklist); the checklist does not apply to the framework itself. Only published aggregate data were used, so ethics approval and informed consent were not required.

Step 1. Claim and margin

The claim is restated as a testable proposition that specifies population, intervention, comparator and outcome, together with a margin M of clinically important harm on the relative scale. We used M = 1.25 for risk and hazard ratios, the GRADE threshold for appreciable harm [9]. The margin should be set by clinical judgment before the evidence is examined, and conclusions should be reported for alternative margins; here they are reported from 1.10 to 1.50.

Step 2. Provenance

Every number is traced to its primary report and graded: P1, copied from the full text; P2, copied from the abstract; P3, derived arithmetically from reported percentages and denominators; P4, reconstructed statistically, for example a hazard ratio from the log-rank P value and the number of events [34, 35]; and P5, approximated, for example with events estimated from Kaplan-Meier proportions. Estimates graded P1 to P4 enter primary analyses and estimates graded P5 enter only sensitivity analyses. Discrepancies between secondary sources and primary reports are logged and resolved in favor of the primary report.

Step 3. Direction of confounding

For observational comparisons, the mechanism by which patients come to receive the intervention is used to predict the direction of confounding. The prediction is tested within studies, by comparing crude and controlled estimates of the same comparison, and between studies, by grouping comparisons according to whether the key prognostic factor was controlled by randomization, adjustment, stratification or restriction. The grouping variable must be a feature of study design, not of results. The strength of unmeasured confounding needed to mask harm at the margin is expressed as an E-value, and documented imbalances are translated into the Ding-VanderWeele bounding factor [15, 36]. If controlled estimates consistently move toward harm, crude comparisons are not accepted as evidence of safety.

Step 4. Effect scale

Outcomes that depend on time, such as recurrence and survival, are analyzed as time-toevent data. Pooled cumulative proportions, which ignore censoring and unequal follow-up [34], are not accepted as evidence of absence; hazard ratios are taken as reported or reconstructed (Step 2). Estimates are pooled with random-effects models and Hartung-Knapp-Sidik-Jonkman (HKSJ) confidence intervals [37, 38], and prediction intervals are reported [39].

Step 5. Evidence of absence

For an estimated hazard ratio HR, with standard error SE of its logarithm, two statistics are computed: z harm = ln HR / SE, against the null hypothesis of no effect, and z safe = (ln M -ln HR) / SE, against the null hypothesis that the effect equals the margin. Available information is expressed as event-equivalents, D = 4 / SE 2 , and compared with the information required to detect the margin with 80% power at a two-sided α of 0.05, D req = 4(z 0.975 + z 0.80 ) 2 / (ln M) 2 [40], which is 631 event-equivalents for M = 1.25. When the standard error is recovered from a reported interval, the interval's own critical value is used: 1.96 for a normal-based 95% confidence interval and the t quantile for an interval based on the t distribution, such as an HKSJ interval. With information fraction t = D / D req , both statistics are compared with the same O'Brien-Fleming-type boundary, b(t) = 1.96 / √min(t, 1), which for a single analysis is the Lan-DeMets boundary used in trial sequential analysis [12, 41]. Harm is established if z harm ≥ b(t); otherwise, safety (absence of harm beyond the margin) is established if z safe ≥ b(t); otherwise the evidence is inconclusive. The rule is symmetric: claims of harm and claims of safety must meet the same standard. At full information it reduces to a conventional test of no effect and to a onesided equivalence test at the margin [14]; below full information it guards against declaring either conclusion from small or early bodies of evidence.

The verdict can also be read from a single interval. The audit interval, HR × exp(±b(t)•SE), is the conventional 95% confidence interval widened on the logarithmic scale by the factor 1/√min(t, 1); it coincides with the confidence interval once information is complete and corresponds to the adjusted intervals of trial sequential analysis and to repeated confidence intervals in group sequential trials [12, 42]. Harm is established when the lower limit of the audit interval is at least 1, safety when its upper limit is at most the margin, and otherwise the evidence is inconclusive. In plain terms, the audit interval is the range of effects that the evidence cannot yet rule out once its limited size is taken into account: the less information, the wider the range, so that with a quarter of the required information it is twice as wide, on the logarithmic scale, as the confidence interval. A claim of safety is accepted only if even the upper end of this range lies below the smallest harm that matters, and a claim of harm only if even its lower end lies above no effect. Probabilities that the hazard ratio exceeds 1 and M are reported alongside the verdict.

Because the verdict depends only on the two limits of a normal-based 95% confidence interval and on the margin, it can also be read from a published interval without software. Step 6. Detectability

When harm is hypothesized to act through a measured intermediate, the effect that the intermediate could transmit to the final outcome is bounded and compared with the available information [43]. The transmitted hazard ratio depends on the baseline risk of the intermediate, the effect of the intervention on it, the prognostic weight of the intermediate, and the fraction k of intervention-induced intermediate events that carry the same prognostic weight as spontaneous ones. Because k is rarely known, it is varied over its full range. If detecting the transmitted effect would require far more events than exist, the absence of a signal in the final outcome says little about the mechanism.

Step 7. Certainty and absolute effects

Certainty is rated with GRADE, in which observational evidence starts at low certainty [10], and findings are stated in standardized wording [44]. Relative effects are converted to absolute effects at assumed baseline risks, using p1 = OR•p 0 / (1 -p 0 + OR•p 0 ) for odds ratios (ORs) and r1 = 1 -(1 -r 0 ) HR for hazard ratios [45]. When the intervention is used before the diagnosis is known, the expected harm per procedure is the absolute effect multiplied by the pretest probability of the disease. The two denominators answer different questions and are labeled throughout: per 1,000 patients who have the disease describes the harm to those affected, whereas per 1,000 procedures describes the harm across everyone examined, including the majority without the disease.

Step 8. Recommendation audit and proportionate precaution

Each relevant recommendation is examined for the role it assigns to the intervention, whether it addresses the harm in question, whether it grades that evidence, and whether it infers safety from nonsignificance.

When Step 5 has not established safety, precaution is assessed with

Steel's conditions [19]. The knowledge condition is met when there is a plausible mechanism with direct human evidence and at least one controlled association with the outcome or with an intermediate of known prognostic weight; the harm condition is met when the potential harm is serious. Candidate measures are compared for consistency, rejecting any measure whose own risks would meet the same conditions [18], and for efficiency, preferring the least burdensome measure that addresses the risk. Recommendations are stratified by pretest probability and completeness of information, so that each measure is proportionate to the absolute harm at stake estimated in Step 7, and each states the evidence that would lift or strengthen it.

Evaluation of the Step 5 rule

We compared three rules for declaring an intervention safe: rule 1, no statistically significant difference (two-sided P ≥ 0.05); rule 2, an upper 95% confidence limit below the margin, whatever the information; and rule 3, the Step 5 rule. For a single analysis, the probability of each verdict was computed exactly under normal theory for true hazard ratios of 1.0 and 1.25 and information from 15 to 4,000 event-equivalents. To represent a synthesis that is updated as studies accumulate, we simulated 200,000 bodies of evidence analyzed 10 times at equal increments of information up to the required information, using independent increments of the score statistic [41], and recorded the probability of declaring safety or harm at any update (S1 Appendix). Because the locked boundary does not adjust later analyses for earlier ones, we also calibrated boundaries recursively, by Monte Carlo simulation of 1,000,000 paths, so that the probability of first crossing at each update equals the α spent since the previous update under Lan-DeMets alpha spending [41].

Retrospective test

To examine whether the framework would have distinguished absence of evidence from evidence of absence in a case whose answer is now known, we applied Steps 2 to 5 to minimally invasive radical hysterectomy for early-stage cervical cancer. Before 2018, observational studies and meta-analyses had concluded that the minimally invasive approach was oncologically safe [46, 47]; a randomized trial then showed worse disease-free and overall survival [48, 49]. The concern, exposure of tumor during manipulation, is related to that raised by hysteroscopy. We audited a large matched cohort whose authors concluded that laparoscopy was a safe alternative [46] and a pre-trial meta-analysis that reached the same conclusion [47] and, for comparison, the trial and two later meta-analyses [50, 51]. The Step 5 rule had been locked before these data were analyzed, so the test could have failed: had the pre-trial evidence crossed the safety boundary, the framework would have endorsed a claim that the trial later contradicted. The selection of evidence is ours; the test demonstrates the behavior of the framework but cannot estimate its accuracy.

Sources and eligibility

Primary comparative studies were identified from published meta-analyses [26, 27, 30, 32, 52, 53], from tabulations in narrative reviews [54, 55], and from targeted searches of web-indexed MEDLINE records and publisher and repository pages (search terms in S1

Appendix); the search was last updated on 7 November 2026. Studies were eligible if they compared diagnostic hysteroscopy with nonhysteroscopic sampling or with no hysteroscopy in women with endometrial cancer and reported positive peritoneal cytology or recurrence.

Guidelines were the current versions issued by major oncology and gynecologic societies that address the diagnosis of endometrial cancer or hysteroscopic practice [56] [57] [58] [59], together with the published National Comprehensive Cancer Network statement on cytology [60].

Statistical analysis

For cytology, each study contributed its best-controlled estimate, in the order randomization, adjustment or stratification for prognostic factors, restriction to early stage, and crude counts. Following Step 3, studies were grouped by whether disease extent had been controlled, because confounding by indication was the bias of principal concern [5]: hysteroscopy is used more when the diagnosis is uncertain and the tumor small.

This grouping was defined by a design feature of each comparison, but during the analysis rather than in a protocol; the change from crude to controlled estimates within the same studies was therefore taken as the more direct test. ORs were pooled with random-effects models (restricted maximum likelihood) and HKSJ intervals, with the variance factor not allowed below 1 [37, 38]; heterogeneity was summarized with I² [61], and small-study effects were assessed with Egger's test [62].

For recurrence, log hazard ratios for recurrence, progression or disease-free survival were pooled in the same way; the primary set contained estimates graded P1 to P4, and sensitivity sets added approximated estimates and crude recurrence ratios.

Probabilities that the hazard ratio exceeded 1 and 1.25 were obtained from the HKSJ t distribution and from Bayesian random-effects models with a reference prior (normal, mean 0, standard deviation 1 on the log scale) or a skeptical prior (5% probability that the hazard ratio exceeds 1.5), each with a half-normal prior (scale 0.5) on heterogeneity [63, 64]. Cumulative fixed-effect z statistics were computed in chronological order for Step 5. For Step 6, the baseline risk of positive cytology was 4.4% for low-risk early-stage disease [28] and 10% for high-risk histology [65]; the effect of hysteroscopy was the extent-controlled pooled OR or, for high-risk histology, the high-risk stratum of Zerbe and colleagues [66]; the hazard ratio for positive cytology was 1.98 (95% CI 1.56-2.52) [28]; k was uniformly distributed between 0 and 1; and uncertainty was propagated with 200,000 Monte Carlo draws. For Step 7, the assumed 5-year recurrence risks without hysteroscopy were 5% and 30%, close to the control-group risks in an apparent early-stage cohort (4.7%) [67] and a type II cohort (32.6%) [65], and pretest probabilities were taken from common referral scenarios [22, 23, 68, 69]. The sensitivity of the conclusions was examined for the margin (1.10 to 1.50), for the boundary (recursive calibration for the chronological updates), for k (0, 0.5 and 1) and for the pretest probability of cancer (1.9% to 42.6%).

Results

Operating characteristics of the Step 5 rule

At the information available for hysteroscopy (449 event-equivalents), inferring safety from nonsignificance (rule 1) would have given false reassurance in 34% of analyses if the true hazard ratio were 1.25; the Step 5 rule did so in 1%. With the information of a small cohort (29 event-equivalents), the corresponding probabilities were 91% and less than 0.1%, and with 171 event-equivalents they were 69% and less than 0.1% (Fig 2A). Rule 2 held false reassurance at 2.5% for a single analysis, and the Step 5 rule never exceeded 2.5% at any level of information.

When the true hazard ratio was 1.0, rule 1 declared safety in 95% of analyses whatever the information, whereas the Step 5 rule did so in 52% at 449 event-equivalents, 80% at 631 and 94% at 1,000 (Fig 2B): safety could be established, but only with adequate information. The probability of establishing harm when the true hazard ratio was 1.25 mirrored that of establishing safety when it was 1.0 (Fig 2C). All single-analysis probabilities are exact. With 10 cumulative updates of the same evidence, repeated application of rule 2 declared safety at some update in 9.7% of simulated bodies of evidence (19,415 of 200,000) when the true (6,916) and 3.4% (6,772), and the probability of eventually reaching the correct conclusion was 82% in both scenarios (164,102 and 163,979 of 200,000). Step 3: direction of confounding

When disease extent was controlled, hysteroscopy was associated with positive cytology (6 studies; OR 2.86, 95% CI 1.24-6.62; I² 32%) (Fig 4). In crude all-stage comparisons there was no association and heterogeneity was substantial (6 studies; OR 0.90, 95% CI 0.25-3.20; I² 64%); the ratio of the two pooled ORs was 3.18 (95% CI 1.05-9.62; P = 0.04). The predicted direction was reproduced within studies: controlling for disease extent changed the OR from 2.11 to 3.88 [72], from 3.20 to 7.08 [65] and from 1.11 to 4.19 [75]. In cohorts without such control, hysteroscopy groups had less advanced disease (stage III-IV, 30.1% vs 43.8%) [77], or advanced stage and vascular invasion were more frequent after curettage [78], the pattern expected under 27 confounding by indication.

In the only cohort that reported both, adjustment also raised the hazard ratio for disease-free survival, from 1.25 to 1.38 [82]. 28

The extent-controlled estimate was robust to excluding studies with derived counts (OR 3.91, 95% CI 1.41-10.82) and to omitting any single study (2.41 to 3.95). The Bayesian posterior OR was 2.69 (95% credible interval 1.47-5.09), with a probability above 99% that the OR exceeds 1. Egger's test did not suggest small-study effects (P = 0.12; S2 Fig), and published subgroups followed a gradient with pressure and medium: OR 1.18 (0.50-2.79) below 80 mmHg and 2.18 (1.28-3.73) above it [27], and 11.2 (1.3-94.5) for saline versus carbon dioxide [88] ( S3 Fig). To mask a true hazard ratio of 1.25 for recurrence, unmeasured confounding would need an E-value of 1.81 if the observed estimate were 1.0, and of 2.19 for a pooled risk ratio of 0.88 [32]; the stage imbalance in one cohort [77], combined with a hazard ratio of 4.67 for advanced stage [78], yields a bounding factor of 1.33, enough by itself to make a true hazard ratio of 1.25 appear null.

Step 4: effect scale

Five cohorts provided time-to-event estimates (Fig 5). The pooled hazard ratio for recurrence or progression was 1.17 (95% CI 0.90-1.52), without detectable heterogeneity (I² 0%; prediction interval 0.87-1.58). Adding approximated events gave 1.11 (0.91-1.35) and adding crude recurrence ratios gave 1.10 (0.92-1.32), moving the estimate toward 1 without excluding 1.25; omitting any cohort gave 1.12 to 1.24, and the hazard ratio for overall or disease-specific survival was 1.09 (0.67-1.78). Published syntheses that pooled cumulative recurrence proportions had reported risk ratios of 0.80 and 0.88, the latter with a fixed-effect model [30, 32] (Fig 6); under Step 4, these estimates cannot serve as evidence of absence. Step 5: evidence of absence

The cohorts provided 449 event-equivalents, 71% of the information required for a margin of 1.25 and 48% of that for 1.20 (Fig 7). Information was computed from the standard error of the pooled log hazard ratio (S1 Appendix); recovering that standard error from the HKSJ interval with the normal quantile 1.96, rather than the t quantile for 4 degrees of freedom, would halve the apparent information. The cumulative z statistic was 1.65 for harm and 0.71 for safety, against a boundary of 2.32; neither crossed it, so the evidence was inconclusive, and the audit interval, 0.94-1.46, contained both 1 and the margin (Fig 3). The verdict was the same with boundaries calibrated recursively for the five chronological updates (final boundary 2.39). The probability that hysteroscopy increases the hazard was 91%, and that the increase exceeds 1.25 was 26% (Bayesian: 27% with the reference prior and 17% with the skeptical prior; S4 Fig; S5 Table). Had the true hazard ratio been 1.0, z safe would have been expected to be about 2.36 with this information, and the Step 5 rule would have established safety in about half of analyses (Fig 2B); the observed value, 0.71, was well below that expectation. The verdict was the same at every margin from 1.10 to 1.40 (Table 5). Only for a margin of 1.50 did z safe cross its boundary (2.64 against 1.96): a 50% or greater increase in the hazard of recurrence was excluded, although narrowly, because the random-effects interval reached 1.52. In clinical terms, the evidence makes a large increase in recurrence unlikely but cannot exclude the moderate increases that would matter to patients.

Step 7: certainty and absolute effects

Certainty was low for positive cytology and very low for recurrence and survival (Table 6). With disease extent controlled, hysteroscopy would add about 72 positive cytology results per 1,000 women with low-risk early-stage cancer (95% CI 10 to 190 more) and 141 per 1,000 with high-risk histology. The pooled hazard ratio corresponds to 8 more recurrences per 1,000 women at a 5-year risk of 5% (5 fewer to 25 more) and 41 more at 30% (26 fewer to 118 more). Harm transmitted through cytology corresponds to a median of 1.2 additional recurrences per 1,000 women with low-risk early-stage cancer (at most 6.0) and 15 per 1,000 with high-risk histology (up to 65). Because these effects apply only to women who have cancer, expected harm per procedure scales with the pretest probability: about 1.4 additional positive cytology results per 1,000 hysteroscopies for an asymptomatic polyp and 31 when sampling has shown atypical hyperplasia, in which concurrent carcinoma was found in 42.6% [69]. Across pretest probabilities from 1.9% to 42.6%, the expected harm per 1,000 procedures ranged from 1.4 to 31 additional positive cytology results and, if the pooled hazard ratio applied, from 0.2 to 3.5 additional recurrences (upper limits 0.5 and 11) (Table 5; S6 Table). Discussion

Principal findings

This study proposes an eight-step framework for auditing claims of safety and shows three things. First, the rule most often used in practice, inferring safety from nonsignificance, gives false reassurance in most scenarios in which a clinically important harm exists and evidence is limited (34% to 91% of analyses in the scenarios examined), whereas a symmetric, information-adjusted rule keeps this error at or below its nominal 2.5% for a single analysis and still establishes safety when information is adequate (80% of analyses at the required information when there is no harm). Second, applied with the rule locked in advance to the evidence available before the trial, the framework would have classified minimally invasive radical hysterectomy as unproven rather than safe, and the reassurance offered by the pre-trial metaanalysis would have failed at four separate steps. Third, applied to diagnostic hysteroscopy, the framework shows that the evidence neither establishes nor excludes oncologic harm: an association with positive cytology emerges once disease extent is controlled, time-to-event data lean toward harm without crossing either boundary, only a large increase in recurrence is excluded, and harm transmitted through cytology lies beyond the detection limit of any feasible cohort in low-risk disease. Taken together, the findings imply that a claim of safety should be accepted only when it states the margin of harm it excludes and rests on enough verified information to exclude it. In practice, the central check takes minutes: with the two limits of a published 95% confidence interval, Table 2 gives the Step 5 verdict and what it means for decisions.

Comparison with existing methods

Each component draws on established methods; the contribution lies in their integration around a single question and in elements that existing tools do not provide. Trial sequential analysis relates accrued to required information [12], and the eight-step procedure of Jakobsen and colleagues extends this to statistical and clinical significance in meta-analyses of randomized trials [13]; neither targets claims of absence of harm built on observational data. The audit interval of Step 5 is not new as a statistical object, because adjusted and repeated confidence intervals serve the same purpose in sequential analysis [12, 42]; what is new is its use, with a margin set in advance, as a symmetric test of claims of safety and harm.

GRADE, ROBINS-I and equivalence methods each supply part of what a claim of safety requires. GRADE rates certainty and treats as imprecise an interval that includes appreciable harm or rests on less than the optimal information size [9, 10], but it returns a certainty rating rather than a verdict on the claim and leaves to the panel how much uncertainty justifies precaution. ROBINS-I judges each nonrandomized study and can record the predicted direction of bias [11], but it neither verifies the numbers nor tests that prediction against crude and controlled estimates. Equivalence tests [14] and interval classifications such as secondgeneration P values [96] give a verdict against a prespecified margin, and at full information uses its boundary for a single analysis. But trial sequential analysis is built to test benefit or harm, and its futility boundaries, the usual way to conclude that an effect of a given size is absent, are calibrated by β-spending, so they accept a probability of false reassurance at the anticipated effect of up to the chosen β, commonly 10% or 20% [12, 97]; Step 5 holds it at or below 2.5% for a single analysis, the standard it also applies to a claim of harm. None of the three verifies the numbers against the primary reports, tests the predicted direction of confounding, rejects pooled cumulative proportions for time-dependent outcomes or asks whether the harm could have been detected; in the retrospective case each of Steps 2 to 4 found a problem that these tools do not test (Table 3). The framework therefore does not replace them. It fixes the order in which they are used, adds the checks they omit, and reduces their outputs to one prespecified verdict that is kept apart from the decision about precaution.

The pre-trial evidence on minimally invasive radical hysterectomy shows what this adds in practice (Table 9). Absence of evidence, evidence of absence and precaution

The distinction between absence of evidence and evidence of absence is long established [1], and equivalence testing gives it a formal basis [14]. The framework makes the distinction operational for bodies of observational evidence that are updated over time, and separates it from the decision that follows.

Step 5 is deliberately symmetric and presumes neither harm nor safety; 46 precaution enters only at Step 8, only when its conditions are met, and only through measures shown to be proportionate. An inconclusive verdict therefore does not dictate a measure; it leads to Steps 7 and 8, where the harm at stake if the effect is real, expressed per 1,000 patients or procedures, determines which measure, if any, is proportionate (Table 2; Fig 9). This separation answers the principal criticism of precautionary reasoning, that it can justify any restriction [17]: the evidential verdict does not depend on who applies it, and the recommendation must show that it is not ruled out by the risks it creates [18, 19].

The retrospective case illustrates the division of labor. A single truncated trial provided a strong signal that met the knowledge condition for precaution but not, by itself, an established effect size, consistent with the tendency of trials stopped early to overestimate effects [98] and with the smaller hazard ratios reported by later syntheses [50, 51].

For practice, the audit supports a stratified approach based on the pretest probability of cancer and the completeness of preoperative information, rather than a uniform rule (Fig 9) : the Step 5 verdict is the same in every setting, but the expected harm is not, and each measure is scaled to it.

When the probability of cancer is low, as for an asymptomatic or symptomatic polyp (1.9% and 5.1%) [68], hysteroscopy with directed biopsy or see-and-treat can proceed as usual, at the lowest pressure that gives an adequate view and with medium and pressure recorded; the expected harm is small per procedure (1.4 to 3.7 additional positive cytology results per 1,000 48 procedures). When cancer is likely, as in women aged 70 years or older with bleeding and a polyp (13.1%) or after sampling has shown atypical hyperplasia (42.6%) [23,69], the expected harm is larger (9.5 to 31 additional positive cytology results per 1,000 procedures), and the decision depends on preoperative information. If a recent sample is benign and concordant with imaging, hysteroscopy can follow at 80 mmHg or less and be recorded; if information is incomplete or outdated, curettage under the same anesthetic comes first, distension is avoided when the tissue looks like tumor, and hysteroscopy follows at low pressure only when it does not. When cancer has already been confirmed, the excess applies to every woman examined (72 more positive cytology results per 1,000 women with low-risk early-stage disease and 141 with high-risk histology), and diagnostic hysteroscopy adds little to staging, which relies on imaging and surgical pathology; if the sample is insufficient for grade or molecular classification, resampling by curettage avoids distension. The pressure target of 80 mmHg is expert opinion, and these measures are conditional: they are proportionate to current uncertainty and would change, in either direction, if new evidence crossed a Step 5 boundary. What changes in practice, and what does not, can therefore be stated precisely. The audit gives no reason to abandon hysteroscopy, a measure rejected at Step 8 because it would forgo the detection of focal lesions that blind sampling misses, and it does not alter the indications for hysteroscopy, staging or treatment. What changes is how the procedure is performed and recorded when cancer is possible, the order of sampling when cancer is likely and preoperative information is incomplete, and how its oncologic safety is described: as not established, with the margin that was excluded and the information on which that statement rests. A hospital protocol could state: "When endometrial cancer is likely, for example after atypical hyperplasia on sampling or with bleeding and a polyp at age 70 years or older, and preoperative information is incomplete or outdated, perform curettage first under the same anesthetic; avoid distension if the tissue looks like tumor, and otherwise perform hysteroscopy at the lowest pressure that gives an adequate view (80 mmHg or less; expert opinion). In every diagnostic hysteroscopy, record medium, pressure, duration and the interval to surgery. This protocol will be reviewed if new evidence establishes safety or harm." Each element would be withdrawn or strengthened under the conditions stated in Table 8, and S1 File provides a one-page committee summary that brings together the verdict, what changes, what does not and the conditions for revision.

Related findings support this approach without settling the question. The mechanism invoked, intrauterine pressure driving tumor cells through the tubes, also underlies concern about uterine manipulators, for which a meta-analysis estimated a hazard ratio for disease-free survival of 1.18 (1.01-1.38) [4], close to the pooled estimate for hysteroscopy. Neither the duration of hysteroscopy nor the interval to surgery has been associated with positive cytology [74, 99], no transfer of endometrial cells was observed with carbon dioxide at a controlled 80 mmHg [55], and hysteroscopic tissue removal was not associated with greater dissemination in one cohort [100]. For counseling, a plain statement is supported and can be used as written: "Hysteroscopy is accurate and usually well tolerated. When cancer is present, it may increase the chance of finding cancer cells in abdominal washings, and current studies cannot exclude a small increase in the risk of recurrence, which would matter most for aggressive tumors."

Generalizability, independent use and adoption

The questions in Table 1 can be asked of any claim that an intervention is safe, without software: what margin of harm was excluded; whether the numbers match the primary reports; which way the selection of patients would bias the comparison; whether time-dependent outcomes were analyzed over time; whether there was enough information to detect the margin; and whether the harm could have been detected at all. Because the rule's error rates depend only on the true effect, the margin and the information available, not on the clinical question (Fig 2; S1 Appendix), its protection against false reassurance carries over to other claims of safety based on ratio estimates; what must be set anew for each claim is the margin, by clinical judgment. The other steps transfer to different degrees (S1 Appendix, section H). Steps 1 to 5 and 7 apply to any claim of safety that rests on comparative estimates; Step 6 applies when the harm is thought to act through an intermediate whose prognostic weight is known, and is otherwise recorded as not applicable; and Step 8 depends on the clinical setting, the alternatives available and the values of those affected, so the precautions proposed for hysteroscopy are a result of this audit, not a template for other interventions. The steps can also be used separately: Step 5 needs only a ratio estimate, its confidence interval and a margin, and Step 2 can be applied by anyone extracting data for a synthesis. The same procedure could be applied to other claims of oncologic safety, such as those concerning uterine manipulators [4] or the de-escalation of surgical staging, and to claims about drugs and devices that rest on observational comparisons.

For the 2022 metaanalysis of uterine manipulators cited in the Introduction [3], Table 2 gives the verdict in a few seconds: the ratio of the limits, 1.74 ÷0.71 = 2.45, falls in the row for 2.50, and neither threshold is met, so the evidence was inconclusive, with about 76 event-equivalents, 12% of the information required; its risk ratio also pooled recurrence proportions, which Step 4 does not accept as evidence of absence. We do not presume the results of such audits: the framework is designed to report safety when the evidence supports it as readily as it reports uncertainty or harm.

Because the framework was developed on the hysteroscopy evidence, the risk that it was fitted to that case needs explicit attention. Three features limit this risk. The part that decides the verdict, the Step 5 rule, has no parameter estimated from data: its constants, the GRADE threshold of 1.25, 80% power, a two-sided α of 0.05 and an O'Brien-Fleming-type boundary, are conventional, and the rule was locked before it was tested. Its error rates were derived analytically and by simulation for any claim based on a ratio estimate, not estimated from the hysteroscopy data (Fig 2). And the steps that involve judgment produce explicit outputs, a provenance grade for every number, a stated direction of bias and the conditions attached to each measure, that others can check against the primary reports. The computations can also be reproduced without the developers: the version that needs no software gave the same verdict as the full calculation for each of the 9 confidence intervals audited here, the complete analysis was reproduced on a separate computer by an author who had not conducted it (AR), and the calculator reproduces the values reported here in its built-in self-check. What these features cannot exclude is that the steps were shaped by the problems this particular body of evidence presented, and two applications cannot show how often the framework reads evidence correctly; only applications by others, to claims they select, can show that.

Independent applications should therefore be designed so that their results can count against the framework. Each should record the claim and the margin, with its basis, before the evidence is examined and not change the margin afterwards; have two people extract and grade every number against the primary reports; apply the locked rule unchanged, with recursively calibrated boundaries when the same evidence has been audited before; record which entries were computed and which were judged; and deposit the completed worksheet with the report (S1 File). Three studies would then test what the present evidence cannot. First, agreement: auditors who did not develop the framework would apply it independently to the same published claims, and agreement on step-level findings and verdicts would be measured. Second, predictive performance: people other than the developers would select, by predefined criteria, claims of safety based on observational evidence that were later tested in randomized trials or large registries, and apply the locked rule to the evidence available before each test, ideally before its results are known and otherwise blind to them; this would remove the selection by the developers that limits the present retrospective test. Third, effect on decisions: after usability testing, clinicians or guideline methodologists would be randomly assigned to decide on clinical vignettes with or without the framework's outputs, and their decisions and wording compared.

The framework should be revised or restricted if auditors applying the same protocol often reach different verdicts, if claims it classifies as safety established are later shown to carry harm beyond the margin more often than its nominal error rate of 2.5% allows, or if its outputs do not improve decisions or the wording of claims.

The least demanding form of adoption does not depend on these studies, because it changes only how claims are stated. Journals, reviewers and guideline panels can ask of any claim that an intervention is safe for the margin of harm it excludes, the estimate with its 95% confidence interval and the Step 5 verdict read from Table 2, and wording that matches the verdict, so that a nonsignificant difference is not reported as evidence of safety; S1 File gives wording for instructions to authors and review templates, and a full audit can be reserved for claims that would change practice. Conclusions

Claims of safety should be judged against a stated margin, on verified numbers, on the appropriate effect scale and with adequate information. The framework presented here makes these requirements explicit and reproducible. It would not have endorsed the pre-trial claim of safety for minimally invasive radical hysterectomy, and it shows that the evidence on diagnostic hysteroscopy in endometrial cancer is a case of absence of evidence rather than evidence of absence. That verdict describes the evidence; the precautions that follow from it, documented low-pressure hysteroscopy preceded by curettage when cancer is likely and preoperative information is incomplete, are a proportionate response to the uncertainty it leaves, to be revised under the same rule as evidence accumulates. A table reads the Step 5 verdict from the two limits of any reported confidence interval in minutes, and a worksheet, a one-page committee summary and a calculator that runs in any web browser, with the data and code, let others check, update or extend the audit. Whether these outputs improve decisions has not yet been evaluated, and the framework has been applied only to the two cases reported here; until independent and prospective applications are available it should be regarded as provisional, and the protocol and evaluation studies proposed in the Discussion are designed so that others can test it on claims of their own choosing.

Implications for guidelines and research

The audit suggests that guidance on this question could be made more explicit at little cost. Guidelines could state the margin against which safety is judged, grade evidence on harm separately from evidence on diagnostic accuracy, and extend the principle of avoiding tumor spillage, already endorsed for surgery [56], to diagnostic distension, for example by recommending the lowest distension pressure that provides adequate visualization when endometrial cancer is suspected or confirmed, with medium, pressure, duration and the interval to surgery recorded in the operative note. Removing cytology from surgical staging did not remove its prognostic weight [28, 101, 102], so recording it at staging remains informative.

GRADE separates the certainty of evidence from the strength of a recommendation [103], and strong recommendations based on low-certainty evidence call for explicit justification [104]; the issue here is not the strength of current recommendations but explicitness about what has and has not been shown. Claims of safety can be worded to state what was excluded and with how much information (Table 2, panel B), and recommendations that rest on inconclusive evidence can state in advance what would change them (Table 8), so that revision follows from applying the same rule to new evidence rather than from a new debate. A randomized noninferiority trial with a margin of 1.20 would need about 944 events, roughly 9,400 women at a 10% recurrence risk, which is unlikely to be feasible. Registry-based emulation of a target trial that records pressure, medium, duration and interval to surgery is the realistic path [105], and reporting hazard ratios with confidence intervals rather than proportions would allow evidence to accumulate [34]; for syntheses that are updated repeatedly, the recursively calibrated boundaries keep the overall error at its nominal level.

Strengths and limitations

The framework is explicit, uses only published data and is fully reproducible from the files provided: every number carries a provenance grade, every step has a stated output, and one command regenerates every result. Its decision rule contains no parameter estimated from data, was locked before the retrospective test, and has operating characteristics derived analytically, independently of any data set. The same rule can report safety, harm or uncertainty, and the outputs, a provenance log, a verdict and a revisable recommendation, can be checked and repeated by others, with or without software.

The main limitation is that the framework was developed on the case to which it is applied, which risks fitting the method to the conclusion. We mitigated this by locking the Step 5 rule before the retrospective test, by using only conventional constants in it, by evaluating its operating characteristics independently of any data set, and by testing it on a second case; because no protocol was registered, independent application to other claims is the necessary next step, and S1 File provides a protocol for it. For the same reason, the hysteroscopy application illustrates the framework but provides no evidence that the rule performs well; that evidence comes from its operating characteristics and, within the limits of a single case, from the retrospective test, which used pre-trial evidence chosen by us and cannot estimate sensitivity or specificity. The usability of the materials, the agreement between auditors who apply the framework independently to the same claim, its effect on decisions and its performance on claims selected by others have not yet been evaluated; the studies needed are described above (Generalizability, independent use and adoption) and in S1 Appendix, section G. External validation is therefore absent: the framework has not been applied prospectively, by auditors independent of its developers, or to claims selected by others, so it is not known how reproducible the steps that involve judgment (Steps 2, 3, 6, 7 and 8) are between auditors, nor how often the complete procedure reads evidence correctly. The operating characteristics reported here apply to the Step 5 rule, not to the eight-step procedure as a whole. Until these evaluations are available, the framework should be regarded as a provisional proposal.
