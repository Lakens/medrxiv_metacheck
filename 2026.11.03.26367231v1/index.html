Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). Statcheck might still miss one-sided tests that are described in another sentence, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

If you want to extend the package to be able to download files from additional data repositories reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Main-text word count: 3,958 . RIGHT-HTx identifies cumulative multidomain vulnerability associated with early mortality after heart transplantation. Temporal discrimination was comparable, but absolute-risk calibration differed.

The framework may support risk recognition and mitigation, not transplant eligibility or allocation; independent multicentre validation is required before clinical implementation.

[div-01]

ABSTRACT: BACKGROUND Risk models from high-income transplant programmes may not generalise to resource-constrained settings with different recipient acuity, donor characteristics and access to mechanical circulatory support. We developed RIGHT-HTx, a parsimonious framework integrating clinical and physiological vulnerabilities, and evaluated its prognostic performance in a subsequent same-centre cohort.

METHODS

This retrospective single-centre study included 436 consecutive adult heart transplant recipients (2012-2026). Of 357 development recipients, 341 (95.5%) were score-evaluable. RIGHT-HTx incorporated three vulnerability domains: clinical acuity (INTERMACS 1-2), graded renal dysfunction (eGFR 30-59 or <30 mL/min/1.73 m²) and haemodynamic-donor mismatch (TPG >12 mmHg combined with donor-to-recipient weight ratio <1.0). A seven-point Cox-derived score underwent internal validation using 1,000 bootstrap resamples and was applied unchanged to 79 subsequent recipients. Low-, intermediate-and high-risk categories represented zero, one and ≥2 affected domains, respectively.

RESULTS

Among development recipients, 75/341 (22.0%) died within one year. Mortality increased across low-, intermediate-and high-risk categories (8.1%, 20.3% and 55.6%; log-rank P<0.001). Elevated TPG was not significantly associated with mortality individually, whereas its combination with donor undersizing was independently associated with mortality (adjusted HR 2.87, 95% CI 1.53-5.40; P=0.001). The full model improved discrimination beyond renal dysfunction alone (optimismcorrected ΔC-index 0.067), and the simplified score achieved an optimism-corrected C-index of 0.732.

The temporal cohort more often had ≥1 affected domain (75.9% versus 60.4%; P=0.010). Discrimination was comparable (C-index 0.738, 95% CI 0.643-0.833), although observed Kaplan-Meier one-year mortality exceeded original predictions (35.4% versus 25.4%; calibration slope 0.929).

In pooled analyses (n=420), mortality increased from 8.4% (13/154) with no affected domain to 57.3% (47/82) with two or more.

CONCLUSIONS

INTRODUCTION

Heart transplantation remains the definitive treatment for selected patients with end-stage heart failure. ¹ Most transplants and published risk models originate in North America and Europe. ²,³,⁴ Their transportability to programmes with different recipients, donor pools, and access to mechanical circulatory support (MCS) is uncertain. External validation and calibration assessment are frequently incomplete,⁵ and model performance may vary across populations⁶ and between scores evaluated in the same transplant cohort. ⁷ These concerns are especially pertinent to resource-constrained programmes, where recipients may rely on personal funds,⁸ durable MCS access is limited,⁹,¹⁰ and deceased donors are geographically concentrated. Critically ill patients must therefore endure long-distance travel and prolonged waiting periods without durable mechanical support. ¹¹ Heart transplantation also depends on a scarce donor resource beyond the treating team's control. Socioeconomic and geographical factors influence heartfailure and transplant outcomes,¹²,¹³,¹⁴ alongside regional differences in transplant survival. ² Nevertheless, the underlying physiological determinants of early mortality are likely to be relevant across healthcare systems, even when the resources available to address them differ.

We sought to identify clinically relevant predictors of one-year mortality following heart transplantation in a resource-constrained setting and to develop a parsimonious, clinically interpretable risk-stratification framework. Candidate predictors were examined using clinical and physiological considerations alongside survival analyses, from which the RIGHT-HTx score was derived. Its performance was subsequently evaluated in a non-overlapping, same-centre temporal cohort. The score is intended to support risk recognition and potential mitigation, not determine transplant eligibility or organ allocation.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study Design and Population

This retrospective, single-centre study included 357 consecutive adult heart transplant recipients transplanted between October 2012 and September 2024 and 79 subsequent recipients transplanted between October 2024 and June 2026. The latter constituted a non-overlapping, same-centre temporal cohort. Paediatric and combined-organ transplant recipients were excluded.

Of 357 development recipients, 341 (95.5%) were score-evaluable; five lacked estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), and 11 had indeterminate haemodynamic-donor classification. All 79 temporal recipients were evaluable, yielding 420 recipients for pooled analyses. Complete-case analysis was used for model development without multiple imputation. Mortality among score-evaluable and nonevaluable recipients was compared to assess potential selection bias.

The primary endpoint was all-cause mortality within 12 months of transplantation, analysed using time-to-event methods with administrative censoring at one year. Survival status was updated through 25 October 2026. No temporal recipient was lost to follow-up; 17 had less than 12 months of observation and were censored at their last confirmed contact. Five score-evaluable development recipients were also censored before 12 months at their last documented follow-up. Clinical, biochemical, haemodynamic, donor and transplant data were obtained from prospectively maintained institutional electronic records. The Institutional Review Board approved the study and waived individual informed consent. Reporting followed TRIPOD+AI guidance. ¹⁵ eGFR was calculated using the 2021 race-free CKD-EPI creatinine equation.

Variables and Definitions

¹⁶ MELD-XI was calculated as 5.11 × ln(bilirubin) + 11.76 × ln(creatinine) + 9.44, with values below 1 mg/dL set to 1. ¹⁷ Clinical acuity was classified by INTERMACS profile at transplantation rather than listing, reflecting deterioration while awaiting transplantation where applicable. ¹⁸ Recipients requiring extracorporeal membrane oxygenation or temporary mechanical support for critical cardiogenic shock were classified as profile 1. Durable-LVAD recipients were reclassified as profile 1 only following documented clinical deterioration. Profiles were dichotomised as 1-2 versus 3-5.

Right-heart catheterisation was performed within three months before transplantation, but the exact interval between right-heart catheterisation and transplantation was not recorded. Transpulmonary gradient (TPG) was calculated as mean pulmonary arterial pressure minus pulmonary capillary wedge pressure; pulmonary vascular resistance (PVR) as TPG divided by cardiac output. Recorded haemodynamic indices were retained when constituent measurements were unavailable. Donor undersizing was defined as donor-to-recipient body-weight ratio <1.0. Haemodynamic-donor vulnerability required both TPG >12 mmHg and donor undersizing. The composite was classified as absent in non-undersized recipients regardless of TPG availability; TPG was required for classification when donors were undersized.

RIGHT-HTx Development and Scoring

Candidate predictors were examined using univariable Cox regression. Selection prioritised clinical relevance, physiological plausibility, nonredundancy and parsimony, informed by observed associations. The RIGHT-HTx framework incorporated three vulnerability domains: INTERMACSdefined clinical acuity, renal dysfunction and combined haemodynamic-donor mismatch.

The final multivariable Cox model contained four coefficients: INTERMACS 1-2, eGFR 30-59 and <30 mL/min/1.73 m² (reference ≥60), and TPG >12 mmHg with donor undersizing. RIGHT-HTx represents Renal reserve, INTERMACS-defined acuity, Graft-recipient size compatibility and Haemodynamic load in heart Transplantation; the latter two were combined into one model term.

Regression coefficients were converted to integer points by dividing by half the smallest nonreference coefficient (0.8803/2) and rounding, following established methods for presenting multivariable models as integer point scores. ¹⁹ Points were allocated as follows: INTERMACS 1-2, 2; eGFR 30-59, 2; eGFR <30, 3; and haemodynamic-donor mismatch, 2. The maximum score was seven.

Risk categories reflected the number of affected domains: low (none; score 0), intermediate (one; scores 2-3) and high (two or more; scores 4-7). Category boundaries followed the domain structure rather than outcome-optimised cutoffs. Donor age and ischaemic time were evaluated but not retained.

Incremental Prognostic Value and Internal Validation

Incremental prognostic information was assessed by comparing renal-only, renal-plus-INTERMACS and full three-domain Cox models using nested likelihood-ratio tests and Harrell's C-index. Reverseorder comparisons examined the contribution of each additional domain.

Internal validation used 1,000 bootstrap resamples. Full-model coefficients were re-estimated within resamples, while the simplified seven-point score was evaluated with fixed integer weights and a reestimated score coefficient. Optimism-corrected discrimination and calibration slopes were calculated.

A separate paired bootstrap comparison estimated the optimism-corrected improvement in discrimination over renal dysfunction alone.

Validation was conditional on the selected predictors, thresholds, weights and risk boundaries; the complete development process was not repeated. Absolute 12-month risks were estimated using the continuous seven-point Cox model, with calibration assessed against Kaplan-Meier mortality.

Haemodynamic-Donor Interaction and Sensitivity Analyses

Recipients were classified into four groups according to TPG ≤12 or >12 mmHg and donor undersizing. An analogous analysis used PVR ≤3 or >3 Wood units. Cox models examined these groups and multiplicative interactions, adjusting for INTERMACS status and eGFR categories.

Interaction terms were evaluated using nested likelihood-ratio tests.

The TPG and PVR analyses included 312 and 313 recipients, respectively; adjusted interaction models included 309. An additional complete-case sensitivity analysis compared the incremental prognostic contributions of MELD-XI and eGFR categories using nested Cox models fitted to a common sample. These analyses were exploratory, did not constitute independent validation and were not adjusted for multiplicity.

Temporal Evaluation and Cumulative Vulnerability

The unchanged seven-point score was applied to 79 subsequent recipients. Discrimination was assessed using Harrell's C-index, and calibration by comparing frozen development-model predictions with observed Kaplan-Meier 12-month mortality. The calibration slope was estimated using the frozen linear predictor.

Exploratory recalibration retained the development score coefficient while updating the baseline cumulative hazard using temporal outcomes; these predictions were not considered independent validation.

For descriptive pooled analyses, 420 score-evaluable recipients were classified by the number of affected domains (0 to 3). Renal dysfunction counted as one domain regardless of eGFR severity.

Crude mortality proportions and Wilson confidence intervals were calculated. These pooled analyses were not independent of model development.

Long-Term and Conditional Survival

Programme-level survival was assessed in all 436 recipients using Kaplan-Meier methods. Conditional survival was evaluated among development-cohort recipients surviving and remaining under observation beyond one year, retaining their original RIGHT-HTx classifications. Between-group differences were assessed using log-rank tests and Cox regression.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables were summarised as median (interquartile range) and categorical variables as number (percentage). Cox models used the Efron method for ties, with hazard ratios and 95% confidence intervals reported. Proportional-hazards assumptions were assessed using Schoenfeld residuals. Two-sided P<0.05 was considered statistically significant for primary analyses; exploratory findings were interpreted cautiously without multiplicity adjustment. Global Schoenfeld tests showed no evidence of violation for the full model (P=0.61) or the seven-point score (P=0.64).

Sample size was determined by the available consecutive cohort without an a priori calculation. The primary model included 75 events across four coefficients (18.75 events per coefficient). Analyses used Stata 19 and R 4.6.1, including survival, survminer and ggplot2.

RESULTS

Study Population and Baseline Characteristics

Among the 341 score-evaluable development recipients, 75 (22.0%) died within one year, compared with 5 of 16 (31.3%) non-evaluable recipients (HR 1.46, 95% CI 0.59-3.60; P=0.415). The small non-evaluable group limits the precision of this comparison. Univariable Associations

INTERMACS profiles 1-2 were associated with one-year mortality (HR, 3.21; 95% CI, 2.02-5.11; P<0.001). Relative to eGFR ≥60 mL/min/1.73 m², HRs were 2.55 (95% CI, 1.55-4.19; P<0.001) for eGFR 30-59 and 6.76 (95% CI, 3.70-12.35; P<0.001) for eGFR <30. TPG >12 mmHg considered individually was not significantly associated with mortality (HR, 1.49; 95% CI, 0.88-2.51; P=0.135). Donor undersizing considered individually was associated with mortality (HR, 1.96; 95% CI, 1.26-3.04; P=0.003), whereas the conjunctive TPG >12 mmHg plus undersizing exposure had HR 3.83 (95% CI, 2.11-6.95; P<0.001). These candidate associations informed the final multivariable model (Table 1).

RIGHT-HTx Model Development and Risk Stratification

The final multivariable model incorporated recipient clinical acuity, renal dysfunction, and donorrecipient haemodynamic-size compatibility (Table 2). Adjusted HRs were 2.66 (95% CI, 1.64-4.33; P<0.001) for INTERMACS 1-2; 2.41 (95% CI, 1.46-3.99; P<0.001) for eGFR 30-59; 4.09 (95% CI, 2.10-7.95; P<0.001) for eGFR <30; and 2.87 (95% CI, 1.53-5.40; P=0.001) for the conjunctive TPGundersizing exposure.

The seven-point score allocated 2 points for INTERMACS 1-2, 2 for eGFR 30-59, 3 for eGFR <30, and 2 for combined TPG >12 mmHg with undersizing. Low risk (0 points), intermediate risk (2-3), and high risk (4-7) represented zero, one, and two or more vulnerability domains. Among 341 development recipients, 135 were low risk, 143 intermediate risk, and 63 high risk. Crude one-year mortality was 11/135 (8.1%), 29/143 (20.3%), and 35/63 (55.6%), respectively (Figure 1). Kaplan-Meier survival at one year was 91.8%, 79.7%, and 44.4%, respectively (log-rank P<0.0001; Figure 2A).

Incremental Prognostic Value

The full three-domain model improved discrimination beyond renal dysfunction alone (apparent Cindex 0.7357 versus 0.6599). In a paired bootstrap comparison using 1,000 resamples, the optimismcorrected C-index increased from 0.6594 for the renal-only model to 0.7261 for the full model (ΔC=0.0667). Adding the haemodynamic-donor domain to renal dysfunction and INTERMACS improved model fit (likelihood-ratio χ²=8.795, 1 df; P=0.003; Supplementary Table S1).

In a sensitivity analysis of 242 recipients with both measures available (58 one-year deaths), MELD-XI was associated with mortality when considered alone (HR per point 1.10; P<0.001), but not after adjustment for eGFR categories (HR 1.03; P=0.486). Adding MELD-XI to eGFR did not improve model fit (LR χ²=0.48; P=0.491), whereas adding eGFR to MELD-XI did (LR χ²=8.17; P=0.017).

Internal Validation and Calibration

The full multivariable Cox model had an apparent Harrell C-index of 0.7357 and an optimismcorrected C-index of 0.7276 after 1,000 bootstrap resamples, with an optimism-corrected calibration slope of 0.9489. The simplified seven-point RIGHT-HTx score had an apparent C-index of 0.7315 and an optimism-corrected C-index of 0.7323, with an optimism-corrected calibration slope of 1.0053.

The slightly higher optimism-corrected estimates for the simplified score reflect differences in estimated optimism, not demonstrated superiority. Mean predicted one-year mortality in the development cohort was 21.92%, compared with observed Kaplan-Meier mortality of 22.01% (Table 2; Figure 3A). Bootstrap validation held predictors, thresholds, integer score weights and risk-category boundaries fixed and did not repeat the full model-development process.

Haemodynamic-Donor Interaction and Physiological Analysis

Among 312 recipients with complete TPG and donor-recipient weight measurements, one-year mortality was 56.5% (13/23) when elevated TPG and donor undersizing coexisted, compared with 20.8% (60/289) among those with neither or only one exposure. Relative to recipients with neither exposure, the combined-exposure group had substantially higher mortality (HR 3.85, 95% CI 2.02-7.35; P<0.001; Supplementary Table S2).

In an exploratory analysis adjusted for INTERMACS status and renal dysfunction, the multiplicative interaction between elevated TPG and donor undersizing remained statistically significant (HR 3.24, 95% CI 1.03-10.22; P=0.045; Supplementary Table S3). This finding supports the physiological rationale for the conjunctive haemodynamic-donor domain used in RIGHT-HTx, although the interaction and conjunctive models are not statistically equivalent. The interaction estimate was imprecise, with only 23 recipients having both exposures.

An analogous analysis using PVR >3 Wood units demonstrated a similar pattern, with an adjusted interaction HR of 2.66 (95% CI 1.01-7.02; P=0.049; Supplementary Table S4). Because PVR incorporates TPG and cardiac output, this represents physiological consistency rather than independent confirmation.

Donor-Recipient Weight Matching

Among 355 development-era recipients with complete donor and recipient body-weight measurements, donor weight increased by only 1.53 kg (95% CI 0.91-2.14 kg) for every 10-kg increase in recipient weight. The fitted slope was significantly below that expected with proportional donor-recipient weight matching (P<0.001; R²=0.063). Donor undersizing increased progressively across recipientweight categories, from 2.1% (1/48) among recipients weighing <50 kg to 98.0% (48/49) among those weighing ≥80 kg. Median donor weight increased from 65.0 to 74.8 kg across these strata, compared with an increase in median recipient weight from 45.0 to 86.0 kg. These findings demonstrate a marked disparity between recipient and donor body weights, particularly among larger recipients (Figure 4; Supplementary Table S5).

Temporal Evaluation of RIGHT-HTx

All 79 recipients in the subsequent same-centre temporal cohort were score-evaluable. Compared with the development cohort, the temporal cohort had a significantly higher prevalence of at least one represented pretransplant vulnerability (75.9% versus 60.4%; P=0.010) and higher one-year mortality (35.4% versus 22.0%). Mortality increased across RIGHT-HTx risk categories, from 10.5% in low-risk to 34.1% in intermediate-risk and 63.2% in high-risk recipients (Table 3; Figure 2B).

The continuous seven-point RIGHT-HTx score showed discrimination comparable in magnitude to its development performance (Harrell C-index 0.738, 95% CI 0.643-0.833). The temporal calibration slope was 0.929 (95% CI 0.452-1.406).

Observed Kaplan-Meier one-year mortality exceeded predictions derived from the development model (35.44% versus 25.35%). Exploratory baseline-hazard recalibration increased mean predicted mortality to 29.56%, although underprediction remained (Figures 3B-C). Detailed calibration estimates are provided in Supplementary Table S6.

Cumulative Vulnerability in the Pooled Cohort

Among 420 score-evaluable recipients (103 one-year deaths), mortality increased progressively with the number of affected vulnerability domains, from 8.4% with none to 23.4% with one, 54.1% with two and 87.5% (7/8) with all three. Recipients with two or more affected domains constituted only 19.5% of the cohort but accounted for 45.6% of one-year deaths (Figure 5). These pooled analyses were descriptive and did not constitute independent validation.

Programme-Level and Conditional Long-Term Survival

Among 436 adult heart transplant recipients, 141 deaths occurred during a median follow-up of 4.7 years (IQR, 2.0-8.0 years), of which 108 (76.6%) occurred within the first post-transplant year.

Overall Kaplan-Meier survival was 75.1% at one year, 68.2% at five years and 57.7% at ten years (Supplementary Figure S1A) Among 341 score-evaluable development recipients, 261 survived and remained under observation beyond the one-year landmark, with 31 subsequent deaths. Conditional survival was 91.0% at five years and 76.7% at ten years after transplantation (Supplement Figure S1B). Among these one-year survivors, subsequent survival did not differ significantly across the original RIGHT-HTx risk categories. Five-year survival was 92.3%, 89.0% and 90.7% in low-, intermediateand high-risk recipients, respectively (log-rank P=0.60; C-index 0.515; Supplement Figure S1C). These findings suggest that the prognostic separation associated with RIGHT-HTx is concentrated predominantly within the first post-transplant year, although the limited number of late deaths and selection inherent in landmark analysis preclude conclusions of equivalent long-term risk.

DISCUSSION

Risk models derived from high-income registries may not transport readily to resource-constrained programmes, where recipient acuity, donor characteristics, and access to advanced bridging differ.

RIGHT-HTx organised transplant risk into three domains: clinical acuity, renal reserve, and donorrecipient haemodynamic compatibility. One-year survival in the development cohort ranged from 91.8% in low-risk to 44.4% in high-risk recipients. Risk separation persisted in subsequent recipients despite greater pretransplant vulnerability and higher mortality (C-statistic 0.738). The principal contribution of RIGHT-HTx may lie more in demonstrating the convergence of vulnerabilities than in the numerical risk score alone, although sparse phenotypes cannot establish synergy.

Clinical acuity,²⁰ renal dysfunction,²¹,²² pulmonary vascular load²³ and donor-recipient size mismatch²⁴ are established determinants of heart-transplant outcomes.

Risk factors seldom operate in isolation, and cumulative vulnerability, including comorbidity burden,²⁵ frailty and obesity,²⁶ and cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic risk,²⁷ has been associated with posttransplant mortality. Previous models have incorporated combinations of donor and recipient risk factors,²⁸,²⁹ with analogous approaches in lung³⁰ and liver³¹ transplantation. However, the cumulative and potentially interrelated effects of these physiological vulnerabilities remain incompletely characterised. We are not aware of an existing framework that integrates these three domains and evaluates their combined prognostic contribution in a subsequent temporal cohort.

The donor-recipient haemodynamic domain illustrates the relational nature of RIGHT-HTx: elevated TPG was most informative when combined with donor undersizing, consistent with previous evidence that donor and recipient characteristics may modify each other's effects. ²⁸,³² One-year mortality was 56.5% when both exposures coexisted, compared with 20.8% among recipients with neither or only one exposure. A similar pattern was observed with PVR, supporting the physiological rationale for considering pulmonary vascular load in relation to donor size rather than in isolation. ³³,³⁴ RIGHT-HTx showed similar discrimination in the subsequent temporal cohort (C-index 0.738) despite greater pretransplant vulnerability. Observed mortality exceeded predictions from the development model, highlighting the distinction between discrimination and absolute-risk calibration. ³⁵,³⁶ Exploratory baseline-hazard recalibration only partially reduced this discrepancy, underscoring the need for independent validation and potential recalibration across different populations. ³⁷ During the later period, the programme also experienced disruptions in air transport, increased retrieval costs and difficulties with timely donor-organ procurement, potentially affecting waiting times and clinical status at transplantation. These circumstances illustrate the challenges of transplantation in a resourceconstrained setting, but their contribution to the higher mortality could not be determined from the available data, as the relevant variables -waiting-list duration, retrieval disruptions and their timing -were not recorded.

Two deaths among low-risk recipients in the subsequent cohort illustrate vulnerabilities not adequately captured by RIGHT-HTx. One involved complex congenital heart disease after Fontan palliation and multiple previous operations; the other involved PVR 5.3 Wood units and TPG 14 mmHg without meeting the predefined donor-undersizing criterion (donor 70 kg; recipient 67 kg). These cases highlight an inherent limitation of parsimonious, threshold-based risk scores: a score of zero does not imply absence of clinical risk.

Donor-size availability emerged as an important systems-level constraint in our transplant programme.

Whereas previous matching studies have examined acceptable mismatch and organ utilisation,²⁴,³⁸,³⁹,⁴⁰ our data demonstrate relatively constant donor weight across increasing recipient size, with undersizing increasing from 2.1% among recipients weighing <50 kg to 98.0% among those weighing ≥80 kg. Median recipient weight increased from 45.0 to 86.0 kg across these strata, whereas median donor weight increased only from 65.0 to 74.8 kg. These observations demonstrate the practical constraints of donor-recipient matching among transplanted recipients, although they do not establish the availability of appropriately sized hearts in the underlying donor pool. Size mismatch may represent a continuous gradient rather than a discrete threshold. ⁴⁰ Predicted heart mass (PHM) may provide more precise size matching than body weight,⁴¹,⁴² although ideal body-weight ratio has been reported non-inferior to PHM. ⁴³ PHM could not be reliably calculated in most recipients because of incomplete donor anthropometric data. Importantly, neither metric directly measures donor right ventricular reserve relative to recipient pulmonary vascular load. The RIGHT-HTx weight-ratio threshold should therefore be regarded as a pragmatic operational definition rather than a physiological boundary. The healthcare-system context is important when interpreting transplant outcomes. The two principal surgical options for end-stage heart failure-heart transplantation and durable LVAD support-depend on resources constrained by different factors: organ donation rates and access to reimbursement, respectively. Registry-derived risk scores, such as IMPACT, incorporate treatment-dependent variables, including mechanical ventilation and circulatory support, whose prognostic significance may differ across healthcare systems. ⁴⁴,⁴⁵ In resource-constrained programmes, the absence of advanced support may reflect limited access or affordability rather than lesser clinical need. Such variables may therefore capture both disease severity and healthcare resources, potentially limiting the transportability of existing scores. ⁴⁶ RIGHT-HTx instead used transplant-time INTERMACS profile to characterise clinical acuity less directly dependent on the support modality received. Direct benchmarking against established scores was not undertaken because the available data did not permit reliable reconstruction of all comparator predictors and some scores target different endpoints.

The cumulative-vulnerability framework also has limitations. Severity within a domain matters, not merely the number of affected domains. Recipients with multiple vulnerabilities constituted only 19.5% of the cohort but accounted for 45.6% of one-year deaths. However, the adverse prognosis associated with severe renal dysfunction illustrates that the severity of individual vulnerabilities may be as important as their accumulation.

RIGHT-HTx was developed to identify potentially modifiable peri-transplant vulnerability rather than determine transplant eligibility or organ allocation. This emphasis on early vulnerability is consistent with its prognostic separation being concentrated predominantly within the first post-transplant year, with no significant risk-category separation among one-year survivors (log-rank P=0.60).

Its three domains suggest different opportunities for intervention. Earlier referral, before deterioration in renal function and clinical acuity, may help preserve physiological reserve. ⁴⁷,⁴⁸ Timely mechanical circulatory support may prevent progression to critical illness,⁴⁹ while recognition of elevated pulmonary vascular load may inform donor-recipient size matching. Reversible pulmonary vascular load may also be modified through durable mechanical circulatory support,⁵⁰ yet access to affordable devices remains limited in this setting, where treatment costs are largely borne by recipients. ⁸,⁹,¹⁰ These are potential strategies rather than interventions evaluated in this study, and prospective evaluation is needed to determine whether score-informed management improves outcomes. Favourable outcomes among low-risk recipients highlight the potential importance of reaching transplantation before multiple vulnerabilities accumulate, while residual mortality even at score zero emphasises that low scores do not exclude clinical risk.

LIMITATIONS

This retrospective study reflects the consecutive experience of a single transplant programme. Its findings describe early post-transplant risk within that setting and require evaluation in other populations. Score calculation was possible in 341 of 357 development recipients (95.5%). Among the 16 nonevaluable recipients, five died within one year (31.3%), compared with 75 of 341 (22.0%) evaluable recipients; their exclusion may therefore have introduced selection bias. Eleven recipients had indeterminate haemodynamic-donor classification: nine had donor undersizing with missing TPG, and two lacked donor-weight measurements. Multiple imputation was not undertaken. The primary model contained 75 deaths across four coefficients, and predictor selection, thresholds and integer weights were informed by development data. Although risk-category boundaries followed the number of affected domains, bootstrap validation was conditional on these modelling decisions and may underestimate full-development optimism.

The adjusted TPG interaction analysis included only 23 recipients with both exposures, producing an imprecise exploratory estimate. PVR provided physiological consistency rather than independent replication. Haemodynamic measurements are influenced by loading conditions and timing, while body weight is an imperfect surrogate for donor cardiac mass and right ventricular reserve. The observed donor-recipient matching pattern describes transplanted recipients and cannot establish donor-pool availability.

The 79-recipient temporal cohort provided limited precision for calibration and risk-category comparisons. Seventeen recipients had less than 12 months of observation, although none was lost to follow-up. The temporal evaluation was conducted at the same centre and does not constitute geographical external validation. Baseline-hazard recalibration used temporal outcomes and did not eliminate the discrepancy between predicted and observed mortality. Small pooled phenotypes further limit precision.

Only 31 late deaths occurred among development recipients surviving beyond one year, limiting interpretation of conditional survival comparisons. Important potential predictors-including detailed graft quality, cause-specific mortality, waiting-list deterioration, donor availability, treatment access and financial constraints-were incompletely captured. A score of zero indicates the absence of vulnerabilities represented in RIGHT-HTx, not the absence of clinical risk; complex congenital anatomy, allosensitisation and other unmeasured factors may remain important. Independent multicentre validation and prospective evaluation are required before clinical implementation.

CONCLUSIONS

RIGHT-HTx integrates recipient clinical acuity, renal dysfunction and donor-recipient haemodynamic-size compatibility into a parsimonious seven-point score for one-year mortality after adult heart transplantation. Its categories correspond to zero, one or multiple score-captured vulnerability domains. Discrimination was comparable in magnitude in a later, more vulnerable samecentre cohort, although absolute-risk calibration differed. The framework may help recognise potentially modifiable vulnerability but should not be used to exclude patients from transplantation or determine organ allocation without independent evaluation. The scaling divisor was set to half the smallest nonreference regression coefficient (0.8803/2 ≈ 0.44015), and the resulting scaled coefficients were rounded to the nearest integer to assign points.

Because the eGFR categories are mutually exclusive, achievable scores are 0, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6 and 7. Risk categories were defined by the number of affected vulnerability domains: none (low risk; score 0), one (intermediate risk; scores 2-3), or two or more (high risk; scores 4-7). These boundaries followed the predefined domain structure and were not selected or optimised using observed mortality outcomes.

The model had 75 events across four regression coefficients (18.75 events per coefficient). Bootstrap estimates condition on the selected predictors, thresholds, score weights and risk boundaries; they do not repeat the full development process. The slightly negative fixed-score bootstrap optimism is compatible with Monte Carlo variation and does not establish superiority over the full model.
