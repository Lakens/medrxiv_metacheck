To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 2 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Clear cell sarcoma (CCS) is an ultra-rare cancer defined by EWSR1-CREB gene family fusions with clinical outcomes that range from indolent to chemotherapy-refractory metastatic disease. Through multi-omic profiling of primary, recurrent and metastatic tumours and cell lines, we show that CCS is more genomically complex than previously recognised. Beyond the fusion, tumours acquire chromoplexy, chromothripsis and an intermediate aneuploidy burden, with ongoing subclonal diversification. Recurrent events include chromosome17q gain and inactivation of the MYC antagonist MGA. Enriched transcriptional signatures of chromosomal instability,17q gain, 9p21.3 loss and TERT alteration each double the hazard of death, supporting a new molecular based CCS risk stratification framework at diagnosis. Transcriptomes converge on a MYC-driven ribosome biogenesis programme, a previously unrecognised therapeutic vulnerability. These findings address a central paradox of CCS, a shared oncogenic identity with divergent clinical fate, implicating tolerated aneuploidy and chromosomal diversification as candidate determinants of progression.

Introduction

Clear cell sarcoma of soft tissue (CCS) is an ultra-rare, aggressive malignancy of deep soft tissues predominantly affecting young adults (Enzinger, 1965) (Graadt Van Roggen et al., 1998) (Chung & Enzinger, 1983) most commonly presenting in the lower extremities and shares morphologic and immunohistochemical features with melanoma. A pathognomonic fusion, EWSR1::ATF1 t(12;22)(q13;q12), is the defining hallmark of clear cell sarcoma. This involves a translocation of the 5' region of the EWSR1 gene with the 3' region of the activating transcription factor 1 (ATF1) gene on chromosome 12. ATF1 encodes an activating transcription factor that belongs to the cAMP response element-binding protein (CREB)/ATF family of basic leucine zipper (bZIP) transcription factors (Chowdhury et al., 2024). The EWSR1::ATF1 fusion hence joins the transcription activation domain of EWSR1 with the DNA-binding domain of ATF1 or rarely other members of the cAMP family (CREB1 or CREM). The fusion occupies the microphthalmia-associated transcription factor (MITF) promoter, drives MITF expression which mediates CCS cell survival and proliferation and confers the melanocytic phenotype that underlies the histological resemblance to melanoma and complicates the morphological differential diagnosis (Davis et al., 2006) (Antonescu et al., 2002). EWSR1::ATF1 binds predominantly distal regulatory elements and co-opts the melanocytic core regulatory circuit, MITF, SOX10 and TFAP2A onto fusion specific enhancers, such that fusion depletion releases these factors, induces melanosomal genes (MLANA, TYRP1, PMEL) and increases melanosome number (Möller et al., 2022).

The fusion therefore specifies melanocytic identity while restricting terminal differentiation.

Moreover, EWSR1::ATF1 is sufficient to induces CCS in mouse models (Panza et al., 2021) and the differentiation programmes that emerge on its removal implicate neural crest-derived precursors as putative cells of origin (Antonescu et al., 2002) (Davis et al., 2006) (Panza et al., 2021) (Möller et al., 2022). CCS, like other fusion-driven sarcomas, is often regarded as genomically "simple" with a balanced chromosomal and an overall diploid profile with low tumour mutational burden (J. Li et al., 2021) (Panza et al., 2021) (Rasmussen et al., 2023).

Recurrent copy number alterations (CNAs) include arm-level or whole chromosome gains of chromosomes 7, 8 and 1q, and deletions on chromosome 9 (CDKN2A) and 19 (Panza et al., 2021) (Rasmussen et al., 2023) (J. Li et al., 2021).

CCS carries a high risk of local recurrence and synchronous metastasis with rates reported as high as 84% for local recurrence (Ibrahim et al., 2018). 5-year overall survival rates are reported to be around 51% with limited treatment options (A. CCS harbours pervasive low-level aneuploidy

The predominant ploidy across samples was constrained at a near-diploid state (mean ploidy of 2.2N) corroborated by a pervasive CN1 (diploid) copy number signature across the cohort. However, most samples demonstrated a gain or loss of at least one entire chromosome or chromosome arm with significant variation in the fraction of genome altered (FGA), a measure of the extent of copy number alterations (CNAs), (median = 0.17, CV=0.68, p<0.05) detected between samples (Supplementary Figure 2A). Co-attribution of CN2 and CN9 signatures with CN1 further reflects an aneuploid state across the cohort (Figure 1C). CN2, a signature associated with tetraploidy showed a 100% attribution in 2 samples (CCS_22GP-T & CCS-E-P03_T) indicative of whole genome doubling (WGD) and confirmed via flow cytometry for CCS_22GP-T. Interestingly, CCS_22GP-T represented an outlier in terms of size of tumour (700 mm in maximum dimension) compared to diploid tumours. WGD is a poor prognostic feature in cancer (Bielski et al., 2018) and is a frequent occurrence in many other cancer types but has not been previously documented in CCS. To assess the frequency of WGD in CCS, we performed DNA ploidy flow cytometry analysis (methods, Supplementary Figure 2B) on tumour samples in the expansion cohort and identified an additional two primary samples with WGD, resulting in an overall incidence of 6.25% (4/64). The extent of aneuploidy and rare WGD samples seen in our CCS samples, are supported by previous karyotyping studies (Bridge et al., 1991) (Panagopoulos et al., 2002) from the pre-next generation sequencing era but have not previously been evaluated in the context of the mutational profile or prognosis. To investigate this phenomenon further, we compared our cohort of CCS cases to the NHS Genomics England 100,000 Genomes sarcoma WGS cohort of > 1000 sarcomas, comparing the fraction of genome altered (FGA) of known fusion driven sarcomas to canonically karyotypically complex sarcomas. This analysis revealed that CCS shows a significantly higher level of FGA compared to other fusion-driven sarcomas implying a higher level of genomic instability (Figure 2A; Supplementary Figure 2C). Since elevated FGA frequently co-occurs with TP53 mutations and/or loss of heterozygosity (LOH) (Taylor et al., 2018), we assessed the correlation between them and found samples with TP53 LOH to be significantly enriched with higher FGA (Supplementary Figure 2D) in CCS.

. While CCS showed an intermediate level of overall FGA, this measure alone cannot distinguish alterations acquired early in tumour evolution from those arising later. We, therefore, examined subclonal copy-number alterations which arise after the emergence of the most recent common ancestor, and compared them across sarcoma subtypes. CCS showed a median subclonal FGA of 14.5% which is closer to complex-karyotype sarcomas than its overall FGA would suggest, and higher than other fusion-driven subtypes (Figure 2A). However, the nature of this subclonal alteration differed markedly between CCS and complex karyotypes. CCS tumours carried a median of 20 subclonal segments, compared to multiple complex sarcomas which had a mean of per-sample median of ~82 subclonal segments, despite a broadly comparable fraction of the genome being affected. We next asked whether the alterations in CCS resembled the fragmented pattern of complexkaryotype sarcomas. Cumulative distribution of clonal and subclonal segments (Figure 2A) confirmed that in CCS, most altered length came from segments larger than 10 Mb while in complex-karyotype sarcomas, a substantial share comes from smaller segments. Other fusion-driven sarcomas had little altered genome at any segment size. Subclonal change in CCS therefore appears to involve fewer, larger events, such as arm-level gains and losses, whereas in complex sarcomas like Undifferentiated Pleomorphic Sarcoma (UPS), it is distributed across many small segments, consistent with genome "catastrophe" processes such as chromothripsis.

Recurrent chromosomal CNAs of CCS have previously been described (Rasmussen et al., 2023) (J. Li et al., 2021) (Panza et al., 2021) with frequent gains of chromosome 1q, 7, 8 and 17q, and losses of chromosome 9 which are also seen in our cohort. Loss of chromosome arm 19p were also observed less frequently (Figure 2B). These gains and losses were broad overall, encompassing entire chromosomes or arms rather than focal genomic loci (Figure 2B; Supplementary Figure 2E). The only exception was a recurrent small deletion in chr3 encompassing the tumour suppressor and fragile site FHIT, which has been associated with replication stress (Waters et al., 2014) (Simón-Carrasco et al., 2024), that was observed in 8.1% (n=5) of cases. Unsupervised hierarchical clustering of allele-informed CNAs revealed two copy number clusters namely, "loss-biased" and "gain-biased" genomic phenotypes, reflecting the balance of aneuploidy within a sample shifting either towards losses and loss of heterozygosity (LOH) or gains and amplifications. Both clusters were reproducible under bootstrap resampling (mean Jaccard similarity 0.72 and 0.81), and silhouette analysis favoured k = 2 over higher partitions (mean silhouette width 0.21), indicating stable groups.

Within the loss-biased cohort, disruption in chr9p (e.g. CDKN2A/B, MTAP) frequently cooccurred with deletions and LOH in chromosomes 10 (e.g. PTEN), 16q (e.g. CDH1, CYLD), . 17p (e.g. TP53, HIC1), and 18q (e.g. SMAD2/4, DCC) (Figure 2B). This finding combined with gene specific LOH on a patient specific basis reveals large scale tumour-suppressor disruption, disturbing key biological pathways including the cell cycle (CDKN2A), DNA damage response (TP53, CHEK2), RAS/MAPK signalling (NF1, SPRED2), and chromatin regulation (SMARCA2 and ARID1B). The gain-biased samples show strong co-occurrent gains of chromosomes 8q (e.g. MYC, RAD21), 7q (e.g. EGFR, MET) and 17q (ERBB2, BIRC5, TOP2A) (Figure 2B). These chromosomes are commonly gained or amplified across many other cancers due to the density of key oncogenes on these loci. The gained-biased cohort also had an enrichment of chr1q gain often concurrent with pericentromeric losses on chromosome 1, another genomic fragile site often deleted during replicative stress.

Interestingly amongst the loss-biased phenotype, cases show a mirrored pattern of 17q gain with 17p loss, consistent with a possible isochromosome 17q event or other whole-arm copy-number alteration affecting chromosome 17. The gain-biased cohort shows possible evidence of isochromosome 8q development in some cases, a finding seen in published CCS karyotypes (Mitelman F, 2026) (J. Wang & Laframboise, 2019) (Morales et al., 2022 ) (P Dal Cin, 1999) and other sarcomas (Cortes-Ciriano et al., 2023). Besides the gain of MYC, chromosome 8 gains are postulated to buffer replication stress by upregulating RAD21 in Ewing Sarcoma (Su et al., 2021) and TMPRSS2::ERG driven prostate cancer (Su et al., 2024), a mechanism that may be relevant in CCS given the recurrent FHIT deletion and pericentromeric chr1q loss observed here.

To test whether recurrent arm-level CNAs arise through whole-chromosome missegregation, we assessed co-occurrence of p and q arm alterations within each chromosome. We found significant co-segregation of both arms for gains of chromosomes 7, 5, 12 and 20 and for losses of chromosome 20, by DISCOVER (Canisius et al., 2016), a co-occurrence test that accounts for differences in overall alteration burden between tumours (q < 0.05) (Supplementary Table 3). Chromosome 7 gain (chr7 gain) was almost exclusively at wholechromosome levels (32/34 samples in which a gain was identified; DISCOVER q = 0.02).

Chromosome 8 gain (chr8 gain) showed strong co-segregation (28 of 39 tumours), although this did not reach statistical significance (DISCOVER q = 0.08), similar to losses of chromosomes 9 (DISCOVER q = 0.09) and 10 (DISCOVER q = 0.05). In contrast, chromosomes 1, 8, 16 and 17 showed reciprocal imbalances between their two arms, however none of the association reached statistical significance. Loss of chromosome arm 1p was accompanied by gain of chromosome arm 1q in 4/5 tumours with 1p loss, gain of . chromosome arm 16p by loss of chromosome arm 16q in 5/7 tumours, chromosome arm 17p loss with chromosome arm 17q gain in 5/7 tumours and chromosome arm 8p loss with chromosome arm 8q gain in 2/3 tumours, configurations consistent with potential isochromosome formation. Together these findings indicate that several recurrent aneuploidies arise through whole-chromosome missegregation, while others may reflect centromere-associated arm-level rearrangements.

The recurrence of chr7, chr8 and chromosome 17q (chr17q) gains across CCS prompted us to investigate their mutational timing relative to the founding fusion event. Using mutation content within gained segments to infer temporal hierarchy (Baker et al., 2024), chr8 gain was predominantly a truncal event and typically preceded the other recurrent gains, though its timing varied across the cases (Supplementary Figure 2F). Interestingly, the early initial gains on chr8 were associated with shorter survival than late initial gains in chr8 (median 1.1 versus 4.2 years; HR 6.37, 95% CI 1.97-23.3, p = 0.002; q = 0.013) an association that persisted even when using the highest confidence mutation-time calls (Supplementary Figure 2F). Chr7 initial gains showed no conserved timing across cases with clonal gains split evenly between early and late. In contrast, chr17q gain was consistently acquired late in mutation time, with many instances being subclonal consistent with ongoing chromosomal instability rather than a fixed early event. Timing of the chr7 and 17q gains was not associated with survival. The early truncal positioning of chr8 gain carrying MYC and RAD21 among its oncogenic content suggests it is incorporated into the founding genomic programme of CCS. The late and subclonal nature of chr17q gain is notable given its association with adverse clinical outcome, consistent with a model in which 17q amplification encompassing ERBB2, BIRC5 and TOP2A, genes with prognostic and therapeutic relevance in cancer, confers selective advantage during progression rather than at tumour initiation.

Aneuploidy is associated with subclonal diversification

Despite sharing a common oncogenic fusion, CCS tumours exhibit striking variability in clinical behaviour and therapeutic resistance. We hypothesised that recurrent aneuploidies may drive subclonal diversification in CCS, with distinct aneuploid clones expanding under positive selection to generate the inter-tumour heterogeneity that underlies clinical variability.

. CCS15, a patient with an exceptional long progression-free clinical course illustrates this process. This patient presented with a surgically resected and irradiated primary tumour, developed local recurrence (LR) after a prolonged disease-free interval, and subsequently progressed rapidly to lymph node and systemic metastases within two years of LR (Figure 2C). Genomic profiling across all three timepoints revealed diploid copy number profiles in both the primary and local recurrence, while the distant metastasis harboured WGD, a rapid transition that raised the possibility whether the WGD population was pre-existing at a subclonal frequency in the primary tumour.

To address this, we performed multi-region sequencing of four spatially distinct regions from the primary tumour (CCS15P_R0, CCS15P_R1, CCS15P_R2, and CCS15P_R3) alongside the LR and distant metastasis. Chr8 gains were shared across all primary tumour regions, confirming their truncal origin and early acquisition (Supplementary Figure 2G). In contrast, chr12 gain was clonal in three regions but subclonal or absent in the fourth, and gains in chr7 and chr17q were spatially confined to individual regions, consistent with a branched mode of evolution in which distinct subclonal populations occupy spatially restricted niches.

Both the local recurrence and distant metastasis harboured clonal chr7 and chr8 gains with subclonal chr17q gain. Notably, the chr7 gain in the primary tumour occurred on the alternate haplotype relative to the local recurrence and metastasis, a pattern of convergent evolution implying strong positive selection for chr7 dosage independent of the specific haplotype gained. Copy number phylogenetic reconstruction confirmed independent acquisition of chr7 gain across the primary, local recurrence and metastatic lesions, and indicated early metastatic seeding from the primary tumour (Figure 2C; Supplementary Figure 2G). A similar pattern was observed in patient CCS08, in which gains of chr7q, and chr8 gain were acquired independently by primary and metastatic tumours as evidenced by the opposite haplotype (Supplementary Figure 2H), indicating convergent evolution of these CNAs.

To determine whether subclonal diversification is a general feature of CCS rather than specific to this case, we performed subclonal reconstruction across all primary tumours using bulk WGS. Most cases harboured between two and four distinct genetic subclones, with the majority demonstrating at least two (Figure 2D). Samples with greater subclonal complexity showed higher structural variant burden, although this association did not remain significant after correction for multiple testing (Figure 2E). Similarly, the association between . subclonal complexity and FGA did not reach significance at all (Supplementary Figure 2I).

Together, these findings establish that CCS is not a clonally homogeneous disease, and that structural genomic complexity, rather than simple copy number burden is the primary correlate of subclonal diversification.

Complex structural events are a prominent feature of the SV landscape Whole-genome sequencing revealed a median of 51 SVs per tumour (range 9-313) (Supplementary Figure 3A), with deletions the most frequent class (33.6%), followed by inversions (25.5%), duplications (18.8%) and breakend junctions representing complex or interchromosomal rearrangements (21.3%). Beyond the oncogenic fusion, no recurrent SVs were identified across the cohort, though a subset of cases harboured markedly elevated SV burden (>100 SVs; n=8). Strikingly, complex structural events including chromoplexy, chromothripsis and chained open-loop rearrangements were present in 50% of cases (Figure 3A-C; Supplementary Figure 3B-D), a finding that differs from the prevailing characterisation of CCS as a genomically simple disease.

Most tumours demonstrated a conventional balanced translocation generating the EWSR1::ATF1 or EWSR1::CREB1 fusion, with a detectable reciprocal in-frame ATF1::EWSR1 transcript in all such cases. However, 3/65 tumours harboured chromoplexymediated fusions, including one case (CCS_100GP-T) in which five chromosomes participated in a single chromoplectic event (Figure 3A). Chromoplexy-mediated fusions were not accompanied by reciprocal transcripts but instead produced ATF1 fusions with alternative partner genes arising from the chromoplectic loop for example ATF1::NXPE3 in CCS_16GP-T. Notably, alternative ATF1 fusion transcripts cannot be used as a reliable proxy for chromoplexy, as cases with conventional balanced translocations also expressed non-canonical ATF1 transcripts arising from concurrent complex SVs including large deletions and inversions at the fusion locus. Compared to Ewing sarcoma, where chromoplexy-mediated fusions occur at higher frequency (Anderson et al., 2018), CCS shows a lower overall rate of fusion complexity, higher instances of reciprocal transcript expression and distinctive ATF1 transcriptional consequences when chromoplexy does occur.

Unsupervised hierarchical clustering of complex variant profiles separated tumours into two groups reflecting differences in the frequency, chromosomal distribution and composition of complex rearrangement classes (Figure 3C). High-complexity tumours (Cluster A) were . characterised by recurrent chromothripsis, chromoplexy, and compound chromothripsisopen loop events spanning multiple chromosomes. Low-complexity tumours (Cluster B) showed sparse SV profiles typically limited to one or two isolated events confined to individual chromosomes. Co-occurrence of rearrangements across the same chromosomes in Cluster A samples, particularly chromosomes 12 and 22, encompassing ATF1 and EWSR1, suggests preferential co-rearrangement of these loci in tumours with high structural complexity.

To characterise the genomic context in which these complex events arise and the signs of their repair mechanisms, we performed enrichment analysis of genome topography at chromoplectic and chromothriptic breakpoints. Chromoplectic breakpoints were significantly enriched at ALU repeats, L1 repeats, open chromatin regions and early-replicating domains (Figure 3D; Supplementary Figure 3E), consistent with replication fork collapse at transcriptionally active origins triggering template switching between proximal repetitive elements during replication stress, the likely mechanistic basis for the multi-chromosomal chained rearrangements characteristic of chromoplexy (Carvalho & Lupski, 2016) (Baca et al., 2013). Chromothriptic breakpoints were instead enriched at ALU, MIR, L2 and simple repeats (Figure 3D), implicating microhomology-mediated end joining as the predominant reassembly mechanism following chromosomal shattering, mechanistically distinct from the replication-coupled process inferred for chromoplexy (Cortés-Ciriano et al., 2020) (Umbreit et al., 2020). Chromoplectic breakpoints were conversely depleted at CpG islands.

Consistent with the generation of abundant single-stranded DNA during chromothriptic remodelling, chromothriptic samples showed significant enrichment of the APOBECassociated signature SBS13 (Fisher's exact test p=0.02; OR=8.8, 95% CI 1.08-86.9). This finding indicates that chromothripsis in CCS is not solely a source of structural rearrangement but also a trigger for localised APOBEC-mediated mutagenesis, potentially increasing sequence-level genomic diversity beyond the structural changes themselves and contributing to the subclonal diversification documented in the preceding section.

Transcriptional consequences of aneuploidy

Having established the pervasive copy number landscape of CCS including gain-biased and loss-biased genomic phenotypes and recurrent focal events at clinically relevant loci we next . asked how these specific aneuploidies shape gene expression and tumour behaviour. PCA of high-depth bulk mRNA sequencing data from the UK cohort (28/32 primary tumours) identified two dominant axes of transcriptional variation (Figure 4A). PC1 (15.1% variance) segregated samples by tumour purity and captured a continuum between immune-enriched states marked by interferon-γ response and tumour-intrinsic inflammatory programmes enriched for TNFα/NF-κB and IL-6/JAK-STAT3 signalling. PC2 (11.8% variance) was significantly correlated with FGA and was enriched for G2M checkpoint, E2F target, mitotic spindle and glycolysis gene sets, reflecting a highly proliferative and metabolically active transcriptional state. High-FGA tumours were preferentially distributed toward extreme PC2 values, indicating that aneuploidy burden in CCS promotes tumour-intrinsic proliferative and Warburg metabolic programmes rather than producing broad unbuffered expression changes across the transcriptome. Individuals presenting with synchronous metastasis or short progression-free survival clustered within regions of PC space characterised by high proliferative activity and high tumour purity, consistent with an immune-excluded, rapidly proliferating tumour state (Supplementary Figure 4A). These findings were substantially replicated in the Germany cohort, where PC1 (21.2% variance) again captured immune-enriched versus tumour-intrinsic inflammatory variation correlated with tumour purity, and PC2 (11.2% variance) was enriched for the same proliferative and metabolic gene sets (Figure 4B). In this cohort, PC2 did not reach significance for correlation with global FGA, but genes contributing to PC2 were significantly enriched within recurrent GISTIC-defined focal copy number events, specifically gains at 20q13, 17q25 and 22q13 and losses at 14q32 and 22q11, harbouring cell-cycle and mitotic regulators including AURKA and BIRC5 (Supplementary Figure 4B). This indicates that the proliferative transcriptional programme in CCS is driven by specific focal copy number events rather than global aneuploidy burden, explaining the decoupling of PC2 from FGA in this cohort. Analysis of the relationship between bulk transcriptomic profiles and genomewide DNA methylation patterns revealed a weak inverse correlation (Figure 4C), consistent with methylation-mediated silencing contributing to the transcriptional landscape but suggesting that copy number-driven expression changes predominate over epigenetic reprogramming at the cohort level.

Together, these analyses reveal that aneuploidy in CCS shapes tumour behaviour through two separate transcriptional mechanisms: a global aneuploidy burden that promotes proliferative and glycolytic reprogramming, and specific focal copy number gains that amplify discrete oncogenic programmes including mitotic dysregulation through AURKA and BIRC5.

. Both programmes are associated with aggressive clinical behaviour and operate in an immune-excluded context, providing a transcriptional basis for the clinical heterogeneity that distinguishes indolent from rapidly progressive disease.

The recurrent co-occurrence of chr8 gain encompassing MYC and MGA inactivation across CCS raises the possibility of cooperative deregulation of MYC signalling, in which increased MYC dosage is further amplified by loss of MGA-mediated transcriptional repression.

Consistent with this, HALLMARK_MYC_TARGETS_V2 emerged as the top pathway positively associated with MYC mRNA expression across the cohort (Figure 4D). Tumours with lower MYC activity showed relative enrichment of coagulation, fatty acid metabolism and angiogenesis pathways, indicating that CCS partitions into transcriptional states defined either by high biosynthetic MYC activity or by stromal and metabolic programmes a distinction with potential implications for therapeutic targeting.

To understand what drives inter-tumoural differences in MYC pathway activation, we integrated genomic and epigenomic data with transcriptomic profiles. MYC RNA expression, rather than MYC copy number gain, was the primary correlate of MYC pathway enrichment (Figure 4E), indicating that transcriptional regulation rather than genomic amplification is the dominant determinant of MYC output in CCS. Hypermethylation at a MYC regulatory hotspot (EPIC V2 probe cg08526705) correlated with lower MYC expression and reduced pathway activity, consistent with methylation-mediated silencing at regulatory elements modulating MYC transcriptional output (Barry et al., 2021) an association that was most consistent in chr8-neutral cases, where copy number amplification does not override epigenetic silencing.

Network analysis of the MYC Targets V2 gene set identified a highly interconnected module enriched for rRNA processing and ribosome biogenesis genes as the central hub of MYCassociated transcriptional activity in CCS (Figure 4F). The hub spanned multiple stages of ribosome production from early small subunit processome components through large subunit biogenesis factors to nucleolar structural regulators, suggesting that MYC-high CCS enforces not only increased translational capacity but sustained commitment to pre-rRNA processing, subunit maturation and nucleolar integrity. Stratification of tumours into MYChigh and MYC-low groups by expression quartile confirmed significant differential expression of rRNA processing hub genes, with the strongest differences observed for WDR43, NOLC1 and NOP16 (Figure 4G), factors occupying complementary positions within the ribosome biogenesis pathway at the levels of early pre-rRNA processing, rRNA production and nucleolar organisation, respectively (Wada et al., 2014 ) (C. Zhang et al., 2014) (Lafita-Navarro et al., 2018) (Khan et al., 2025).

. To determine whether this relationship extends to tractable experimental systems, we profiled CCS cell lines and found that MYC pathway activity and elevated expression of the rRNA processing hub genes including WDR43, NOLC1 and NOP16 were consistently reproduced across models (Supplementary Figure 4C). These data establish CCS cell lines as relevant systems for interrogating MYC-linked nucleolar dependencies and nominate RNA Pol I-dependent rRNA transcription as a candidate therapeutic vulnerability in MYChigh CCS (Drygin et al., 2011).

Immune Landscape, HLA Diversity and T-cell Receptor Repertoire Immune-related transcriptional programmes emerged as a primary axis of inter-tumour variation in CCS (Figure 4A-B), implicating the tumour microenvironment as a potential determinant of the clinical heterogeneity that defines this disease. To characterise the TME systematically, we performed multi-modal immune deconvolution using both DNA methylation and bulk RNA-seq data, obtaining concordant estimates of immune infiltration across modalities (Supplementary Figure 5A-B). The results were unambiguous in that the vast majority of CCS tumours were profoundly immune depleted. Direct comparison with melanoma and undifferentiated pleomorphic sarcoma (UPS), two tumour types with established immunogenicity and clinical responses to checkpoint blockade (Hugo et al., 2016) (Steele et al., 2019) (Keung et al., 2020), confirmed that CCS occupies the extreme low end (immune-desert or excluded) of the sarcoma immune spectrum, with ESTIMATE immune scores significantly lower than both comparators across both cohorts (Figure 5A). A small subset of cases, CCS_16GP-T, CCS_20EP-T, CCS_22GP-T, CCS_32GP-T, CCS_81GP-T and CCS_84GP-T showed relatively higher immune infiltration and are discussed further below (Supplementary Figure 5A). Total neoantigen burden was strikingly low across the cohort (range 1-10 predicted neoantigens per sample), well below that reported for immunotherapy-responsive tumours such as melanoma (Snyder et al., 2014) and consistent with the observed low mutational burden in CCS, providing a mechanistic basis for the failure to generate tumour-reactive Tcell responses. Samples with low immune infiltration predominantly also had low neoantigen burden; notably, CCS_16GP-T, one of the cases with relatively higher immune infiltration, . also displayed low neoantigen burden, suggesting that immune recruitment in this case may be driven by mechanisms independent of neoantigen load (Supplementary Figure 5C).

Critically, immune exclusion across the cohort is not explained by defects in the antigen presentation machinery: no somatic mutations or CNAs were identified in any HLA gene across all samples, and B2M was similarly intact. Tumour cells therefore retain the molecular capacity to present antigens but fail to generate the antigenic substrate necessary to trigger a productive adaptive immune response, a distinction with direct therapeutic implications, as it identifies antigen scarcity rather than antigen presentation failure as the primary barrier to immune engagement in CCS.

High-resolution germline HLA genotyping revealed that HLA-A*02:01 was the most prevalent allele in the cohort (21.3%), followed by HLA-A*03:01 (15.6%), HLA-B*08:01 (14.8%), HLA-A*01:01 (13.9%) and HLA-C*07:01 (13.9%), a distribution consistent with predominantly Northwestern European ancestry (Figure 5B; Supplementary Figure 5D). Germline homozygosity at one or more HLA-I loci was identified in 14/61 cases (22.9%) and was unexpectedly enriched among cases with relatively higher immune infiltration (p=0.045, Fisher's exact test). No somatic mutations in HLA-A, HLA-B, HLA-C or B2M were identified, confirming that the infiltration differences are not attributable to somatic antigen presentation defects. Somatic HLA predicted loss of function was identified in only one case CCS-E-P03 indicating that homozygosity in the remainder reflects a germline diversity constraint rather than tumour-intrinsic immune escape. TCR sequencing revealed a globally low unique CDR3 count across the CCS cohort, reflecting restricted T-cell diversity consistent with an immune-desert microenvironment (Figure 5C). Despite this low clonal richness, medium and large clones dominated the T-cell population, accounting for more than 60% of the T-cell repertoire in most cases, with hyperexpanded clones present in smaller but variable proportions. Formal comparison with UPS confirmed that CCS shows significantly lower TCR clonality, a significantly higher D50 diversity index, and a significantly lower proportion of hyperexpanded clones (Figure 5D), consistent with the absence of focused antigen-driven T-cell expansion. The sparse neoantigen profile showed a weak inverse correlation with TCR clonality, confirming that neoantigen load is insufficient to drive clonal expansion in most CCS tumours (Supplementary Figure 5C).

Against this broadly immune-desert background, two distinct outlier immune patterns were apparent in the TCR data (Figure 5C). CCS_16GP-T and CCS_18GL1-T showed high . unique CDR3 counts -2,717 and 7,371 respectively -with predominance of medium and small clones and the lowest fraction of hyperexpanded clones in the cohort (approximately 3%), suggesting recent T-cell recruitment without clonal focusing and a pattern consistent with non-specific or bystander immune activation. In contrast, CCS_09GP-T and CCS_15GP-T showed the opposite pattern: very low CDR3 diversity but a very high proportion of hyperexpanded clones exceeding 75% of the T-cell repertoire, consistent with oligoclonal antigen-driven expansion in an otherwise restricted immune context. These two patterns may reflect distinct modes of partial immune engagement in a tumour type that broadly resists productive immunity, and the oligoclonal subset in particular represents a candidate population for prospective evaluation of immunotherapeutic interventions.

The most informative immune trajectory in the cohort came from longitudinal TCR profiling of CCS15, the same patient whose multi-region genomic analysis revealed spatially restricted subclonal aneuploidies and the emergence of a WGD clone specifically at the metastatic site following a prolonged disease-free interval. TCR sequencing across the primary tumour, local recurrence and distant metastasis revealed a striking parallel evolution of the immune landscape that mirrored the genomic trajectory. T-cell diversity was lowest at the primary tumour, rose substantially at local recurrence where a unique CDR3 count of approximately 900 was observed and then collapsed at the distant metastatic site. Local recurrence in this case developed after a prolonged disease-free interval following surgical resection and postoperative radiotherapy of the primary tumour, and the restoration of T-cell diversity at this timepoint suggests that removal of the primary immune-suppressive tumour mass, combined with radiotherapy-associated immunogenic cell death, transiently restored immune surveillance.

Genetic and non-genetic determinants of poor survival CCS lacks validated prognostic biomarkers for routine clinical use (WHO Classification of Tumours Editorial Board, 2026). FNCLCC histological grading, the standard risk stratification framework applied across soft tissue sarcomas, has not demonstrated prognostic value in CCS, which are regarded as high-grade by definition. This over-simplification leaves clinicians reliant on stage and tumour size alone, with no means of further stratifying risk among individuals with localised disease. We therefore examined whether the genomic features characterised in this study could provide clinically useful prognostic information.

. Overall survival varied substantially across the cohort despite shared histological diagnosis (Figure 1B; Supplementary Figure 1A-C), and we systematically evaluated candidate prognostic parameters in primary tumour samples only (Supplementary Table 4; Supplementary Figure 6A-C) through univariate log-rank test and Cox regression analysis.

Baseline metastatic status was the strongest single predictor of overall survival, with systemic spread conferring worse prognosis than lymphatic spread (Figure 6A), consistent with prior reports in CCS and other soft tissue sarcomas (H. Y. Wang et al., 2025) (H. Liu et al., 2022). Additional parameters significantly associated with poor outcome on univariate analysis were primary tumour size, chr17q gain, deletion of 9p21.3, and presence of TERT promoter mutation or TERT promoter hypermethylation (pTERT; Figure 6B-E). Chr17q gain was validated by fluorescence in-situ hybridisation (FISH) or EPICv2 microarray profiling in cases for which sequencing data were unavailable (expansion cohort) (Figure 6D; Supplementary Table 5). Similarly, to increase statistical power, we performed univariate analysis of primary tumour size and pTERT in the combined discovery and expansion cohort where data was available. CCS cases were additionally stratified by CINSARC classification, a transcriptional chromosomal complexity index developed for risk stratification across soft tissue sarcomas (Chibon et al., 2010), using the original scoring strategy. CINSARC highrisk cases had significantly worse overall survival than low-risk cases (Figure 6F).

All candidate prognostic features were tested for multicollinearity and pairwise dependencies (Supplementary Table 6); none were collinear. Baseline metastatic status showed a significant moderate association with pTERT status (Supplementary Table 6). Given the cohort size, bootstrap-based variable-selection stability was assessed using LASSOpenalised Cox proportional hazards regression with 1,000 bootstrap resamples to identify the most robust candidate predictors for multivariable analysis. CINSARC class (96.3%), chr17q gain (79.3%), and baseline metastatic status (75.9%) emerged as the most stable predictors, each exceeding the pre-specified 70% selection threshold (Supplementary Table 7). Although pTERT was significant on univariate analysis, it did not meet the bootstrap stability threshold and was not carried forward to multivariable modelling. The proportional hazards assumption for the multivariable Cox model incorporating the three retained variables was confirmed using scaled Schoenfeld residuals (Supplementary Figure 6D). All three predictors: CINSARC class, chr17q gain, and baseline metastatic status retained independent prognostic significance for overall survival in the multivariable model (Figure 6G), together defining a genomic and clinical risk framework for CCS that has not previously been available for this diagnosis. To ensure our findings were not driven by inclusion of GI-CCS cases, we repeated univariate survival analysis after removing those cases from the . cohort (n=4). All other factors remained significantly associated with OS (Supplementary Figure 6E-I) whereas pTERT was no longer statistically significant (p = 0.12), although Kaplan-Meier curves showed a similar trend (Supplementary Figure 6J), likely reflecting reduced statistical power.

To integrate these findings, we propose an evolutionary model of CCS in which a shared truncal fusion associates with a melanocytic lineage identity while divergent post-fusion copy-number trajectories determine clinical behaviour (Figure 7). Two distinct aneuploidy phenotypes emerge: a "loss-biased" cluster characterised by deletion of CDKN2A and TP53 loss of heterozygosity, in which FGA correlates with progressive p53 pathway disruption; and a "gain-biased" cluster enriched for chr17q and chr1q copy-number gains, which associates with a transcriptional convergence on proliferative and glycolytic programmes.

Superimposed chromosomal "catastrophe" events, including chromothripsis is seen in 50% of cases where genome sequencing data was available. Chromoplexy-mediated fusion formation, and whole-genome doubling correlate with subclonal diversification and, in CCS15, the case with longitudinal profiling, with metastatic progression.

Discussion

CCS of soft tissue has long been considered a genomically uncomplicated fusion-driven sarcoma; the canonical driver EWSR1::ATF1 arises as a truncal, clonal event in virtually all cases. Despite the involvement of alternative partner genes (ATF1, CREB1, CREM), all members of the CREB subfamily of bZIP domain transcription factors and appear functionally interchangeable in engaging the MITF/SOX10 melanocytic transcriptional programme (Möller et al., 2022). The fusion defines the disease, yet it does not explain why patients who carry it follow such varied clinical courses (Ikuta et al., 2023).

By integrating whole-genome, transcriptomic, evolutionary, immune and carefully curated clinical data across a multi-institutional cohort, we propose an evolutionary model in which the fusion fixes lineage and identity, while clinical behaviour is associated with subsequent genomic alterations (Figure 7). This has two practical consequences. CCS, in which histological grade carries no prognostic value (WHO Classification of Tumours Editorial Board, 2026) (Coindre, 2006) (Deyrup & Weiss, 2006), can be stratified by a small number of features measurable at diagnosis, and the same features point to therapeutic vulnerabilities that can now be tested.

. The central feature of this model is where CCS sits on the aneuploidy spectrum. On a background of low point-mutation burden and near-diploid ploidy, CCS carries more aneuploidy than other fusion-driven sarcomas but far less than complex-karyotype sarcomas, and its altered genome is dominated by arm-and whole-chromosome-scale segments rather than the fragmented profiles of complex genome sarcomas. Clonal and subclonal change are of similar extent in CCS, whereas clonal change dominates in complex-karyotype sarcomas, indicating that CCS genomes keep diversifying after transformation, most plausibly through chromosome missegregation. This intermediate position fits models in which tumour fitness peaks within a narrow range of chromosomal instability, where moderate missegregation maximises clonal diversity while higher rates are selected against (Silk et al., 2013) (Laughney et al., 2015), and in which extreme instability is associated with improved outcome (Birkbak et al., 2011).

Sustained aneuploidy would normally engage p53-dependent checkpoints, and our data suggest that CCS reaches tolerance by two genomic routes to the same endpoint. In a smaller, "loss-biased" group, the TP53 axis is disrupted directly: 17p loss of heterozygosity associates with aneuploidy burden and co-occurs with loss of CHEK2 and of 9p21.3, the CDKN2A/ARF locus. In the larger, "gain-biased" group, TP53 wild-type tumours likely attenuate p53 through gene dosage, with 17q gains carrying PPM1D and 1q gains carrying MDM4, both known inhibitors of P53 activity (Milosevic et al., 2021) (Woodfield et al., 2021).

The usual accelerants of extreme instability are uncommon: TP53 was altered in 5 of 37 primary tumours and whole-genome doubling occurred in 2 of 37 primary tumours. We propose that this dosage-level attenuation allows aneuploidy to be tolerated without the escalation that follows a complete loss of p53 function. Together with low immune infiltration, this may leave CCS in a therapeutic no-man's land, buffered against DNA damage without being fragile, and poorly visible to T cells. This hypothesis raises the possibility that adding further incremental DNA damage would bring limited benefit, whereas the p53-intact majority may respond to strategies that remove the tolerance, such as MDM2/MDMX or PPM1D inhibition.

The order of events provides "genomic signposts" where intervention might act. Gains of chromosomes 8 and 7 are clonal trunk events in most tumours that carry them, whereas 17q gain is late in mutation time and frequently subclonal. Unlike in neuroblastoma, where 17q gain is an early event, in CCS it appears to mark progression and the emergence of . aggressive subclones rather than initiation, and independent gains of chromosome 7 across longitudinal samples (Supplementary Figure 2G and H) indicate ongoing selection. Gain of 17q also raises the dosage of BIRC5 (survivin), which is overexpressed in tumours with this gain and can be targeted directly or through HDAC inhibition, as shown in Ewing sarcoma (Koppenhafer et al., 2025). Superimposed on this background, a subset of tumours undergo genome catastrophes, including chromoplexy-mediated fusion formation and chromothripsis; to our knowledge these have not been described in CCS, although they are known in other fusion-driven cancers (Anderson et al., 2018) (Baca et al., 2013). The enrichment of chromothriptic tumours for SBS13 suggests replication stress and error-prone repair (Maciejowski et al., 2020) (S. Liu et al., 2018), making this subset a candidate for replicationstress targeting. Because late subclonal events such as 17q gain may be missed in a single biopsy, longitudinal sampling of primary and metastatic disease will be needed to establish when high-risk subclones arise and whether they can be detected earlier, including in circulating tumour DNA.

Several of these alterations converge on MYC. MGA disruption, not previously described in CCS, together with chromosome 8 gain and enriched MYC transcriptional signatures, points to an underappreciated route to MYC pathway activation, with rRNA processing and ribosome biogenesis as the dominant downstream programmes. MYC-high CCS may therefore be susceptible to inhibition of Pol I transcription or early rRNA processing (Drygin et al., 2011) (Khot et al., 2019), a dependency that could also explain the limited activity of upstream inhibitors such as crizotinib (Schöffski et al., 2017). TERT promoter alterations, associated with adverse outcome here as in other sarcomas (Cross et al., 2022) (Demicco et al., 2018 ) (Y. Zhang et al., 2021), implicate telomere maintenance in progression (Gaspar et al., 2018). Despite heterogeneous copy-number and structural change, tumours converge on a limited number of transcriptional states that upregulate metabolic and proliferation pathways, which should simplify the design of biomarker-led studies.

These findings matter most for risk assessment. Alongside metastatic disease at diagnosis and large tumour size, established prognostic factors in CCS (H. Y. Wang et al., 2025) the CINSARC high-risk class and 17q gain were the most stable independent predictors of survival, and 17q gain remained associated with outcome independently of metastatic status and CINSARC class. Both features can be measured with clinically available methods, 17q gain by fluorescence in-situ hybridisation or sequencing and CINSARC by RNA-based testing. Before they guide practice, they need confirmation in independent, prospectively collected cohorts, ideally with uniform treatment data. If confirmed, they could be used to stratify entry into clinical trials, to intensify surveillance or adjuvant therapy for patients at high risk, and to spare patients at low risk from treatment of limited benefit. The immune findings shape which therapies are potentially realistic. Most tumours show low immune infiltration with intact antigen presentation machinery, suggesting that the barrier lies in T-cell access and priming rather than antigen display. This favours approaches that supply specificity, such as T-cell receptor-based therapies, T-cell engagers or antibody-drug conjugates, over checkpoint blockade alone. In the separate companion spatial proteomics study, a proliferative tumour-cell state and an E2F-target module were associated with survival, mirroring the link between high aneuploidy and G2M and E2F programmes in our transcriptomes. Genome and proteome therefore point to a single proliferative axis, and testing whether it can be read from routine histology would bring risk assessment closer to the clinic.

Our observations in a unique multi-institutional cohort support an evolutionary model in which the EWSR1 fusion initiates CCS, while acquired secondary alterations, accumulating on a background of tolerated aneuploidy, are associated with an increasingly unfavourable clinical course. The key genomic and transcriptomic features of primary tumours are recapitulated in CCS cell lines, which now allow the central predictions to be tested: that dosage-attenuated p53 can be reactivated, that MYC-high tumours depend on ribosome biogenesis, and that pushing chromosome missegregation beyond tolerance is selectively lethal. Combined with prospective sampling of primary and metastatic disease, these models provide a foundation for drug discovery in this ultra-rare cancer.

Limitations of the study and future work

Clinical and follow-up data were incomplete for the expansion cohort, so survival analyses were restricted to patients with available data. The low indel burden of CCS limited the power to assign indel signatures, which should therefore be regarded as exploratory. Copynumber profiles capture the outcome of chromosomal instability (CIN) rather than its rate, so our inferences about ongoing instability and its restraint are indirect. The subclonal fraction of genome altered was estimated from single samples, which underestimates subclonality in all tumours, probably most in complex, genome-doubled tumours such as UPS; this could exaggerate the contrast between CCS and complex-karyotype sarcomas. Differences in sequencing depth between our cohort and the Genomics England comparator data may also affect subclonal calling. Even so, a substantial subclonal burden together with a distinct, . large-segment pattern of alteration is consistent with ongoing CIN in CCS. Prognostic 821 features were selected and modelled in the same cohort, so survival estimates are 822 exploratory, and the CINSARC high-risk class and 17q gain require validation in an 823 independent series. Future work should measure chromosome missegregation directly in 824 CCS cell lines and tumour sections and test the tolerance and therapeutic hypotheses 825 functionally.
