Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Metacheck does not yet analyze automatically whether sentences which include non-significant p-values are correct, but we recommend manually checking the sentences below for possible misinterpreted non-significant p values.

Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Full text

Abstract

Oculopalatal tremor (OPT) follows disruption of the cerebello-rubro-olivary network and is frequently associated with hypertrophic inferior-olive degeneration (HOD). HOD does not invariably produce OPT; determinants of oculo-palatal, ocular-only, palatal-only, or nonoscillatory phenotypes remain undefined. We analyzed 501 patients with HOD, of which 424 with classifiable laterality: 52.1% had unilateral and 47.9% bilateral HOD. Lesion location predicted HOD laterality (chi-square=35.65, p=3.4x10^-7; Cramer V=0.31): brainstem/pons lesions produced ipsilateral HOD in 70.0%, whereas cerebellar lesions produced contralateral HOD in 86.4%. Bilateral HOD reflected combined cerebellar/brainstem lesions, and was associated with ocular oscillations (OR 2.66, 95% CI 1.31-5.43; p=0.0067) but not convincingly palatal oscillations (OR 1.49, 95% CI 0.91-2.42; p=0.137). Phenotype differed by etiology (p=0.0024): hemorrhagic and vascular-malformation lesions preferentially produced combined OPT, ischemic lesions were enriched among ocular-only presentations, and neoplastic lesions produced the most heterogeneous phenotype and were disproportionately represented among palatal-only cases. Age, sex, and lesion laterality were not associated with phenotype. Fourclass prediction was limited (macro AUC 0.555; balanced accuracy 0.301), with little improvement after adding HOD laterality (macro AUC 0.563; balanced accuracy 0.323). Among classifiable OPT waveforms, 96.9% contained a pendular component. Median OPT latency was 6 months (IQR 4.75-24); lesion location, etiology, and HOD laterality did not predict latency. HOD is therefore an important substrate, but lesion anatomy and etiology shape group-level phenotype without enabling reliable individual prediction.

Introduction:

Oculopalatal tremor (OPT) is an acquired neurological syndrome characterized by rhythmic ocular oscillations accompanied by palatal tremor and most commonly arises after structural injury involving brainstem or cerebellar pathways 12 . Symptomatic palatal tremor is distinguished from essential palatal tremor by its association with structural lesions of the brainstem or cerebellum and with secondary degeneration of the inferior olive 34 . Progressive ataxia and palatal tremor represents an additional, clinically heterogeneous syndrome in which similar circuitry may be affected in the setting of progressive cerebellar disease 5.

The anatomical substrate of symptomatic palatal tremor and OPT is disruption of the cerebellar-rubro-olivary system, traditionally conceptualized as the Guillain-Mollaret triangle 16 . Deep cerebellar nuclei efferents ascend through the superior cerebellar peduncle and decussate in the midbrain, whereas descending fibers involving the central tegmental tract project toward the ipsilateral inferior olive 78 . Interruption of these pathways produces transsynaptic degeneration of the inferior olivary nucleus. Unlike most degenerative processes, the affected olive may enlarge, producing the characteristic radiographic appearance of hypertrophic olivary degeneration (HOD) 6, 9 . Longitudinal MRI analyses indicate that increased T2 signal can become evident approximately one month after the causative lesion, whereas frank olivary hypertrophy generally emerges later, beginning at approximately six months and potentially persisting for several years 10.

HOD and the clinical syndrome of OPT, however, are not synonymous. Patients with apparently similar lesions may develop combined ocular and palatal oscillations, palatal oscillations without ocular involvement, ocular oscillations without palate involvement, or radiographic HOD without either manifestation [11] [12] [13] . Detailed ocular motor studies similarly demonstrate that inferior olivary abnormalities do not invariably predict the complete oculopalatal phenotype. In a series of patients with OPT-spectrum disease, ocular oscillations were predominantly vertical with variable torsional and horizontal components, several patients showed dissociated oscillations, and some patients with inferior olivary signal abnormalities never developed palatal tremor 11.

The ocular oscillations of classical OPT are usually acquired pendular oscillations and are frequently vertical, torsional, or multidimensional 11, 14 . complex, irregular structure is difficult to explain by a single autonomous oscillator 14, 15 . Experimental and computational work has therefore proposed a dual-mechanism model [14] [15] [16] . Following loss of normal afferent input, inferior olivary neurons hypertrophy and develop increased electrotonic coupling, allowing the inferior olive to generate synchronized low-frequency oscillatory activity 1, 14 . That signal alone is insufficient to reproduce the large and irregular ocular oscillations observed clinically; maladaptive cerebellar learning is proposed to amplify and reshape the olivary output 1514.

This oscillator-amplifier model places OPT within a distributed olivocerebellar network rather than attributing the clinical syndrome solely to inferior olivary hypertrophy 142 . Contemporary reviews similarly emphasize that OPT emerges from interruption of the cerebello-rubro-olivary pathway followed by secondary olivary and cerebellar network reorganization 17.

Despite this mechanistic framework, several clinically important questions remain unresolved. First, the relationship between the anatomical level of the primary lesion and the side of subsequent HOD has not been quantified across a large, heterogeneous patient population. Second, it remains uncertain why bilateral or unilateral HOD leads to ocular oscillations in some patients but palatal manifestations or no oscillatory syndrome in others 1112.

Third, whether the nature of the primary lesion -hemorrhagic, ischemic, neoplastic, vascular malformation, inflammatory, traumatic, or degenerative -modifies the subsequent clinical phenotype remains incompletely established 9, 12 . Finally, OPT is recognized as a delayed neurological complication, but its typical latency and the extent to which lesion characteristics predict the timing of onset remain uncertain 7, 17, 18.

Because OPT is uncommon, individual clinical series are generally small 1114 . Conversely, the published literature contains hundreds of individually described patients in whom lesion location, lesion etiology, HOD characteristics, ocular manifestations, palatal manifestations, and associated neurological features can be reconstructed. We combined these observations to create a pooled patient-level dataset of 501 patients. The objectives of this study were to determine: (1) how primary lesion location and etiology relate to HOD laterality; (2) whether bilateral versus unilateral HOD, lesion location, and lesion etiology predict ocular oscillations, palatal oscillations, or combined OPT; (3) whether OPT, isolated ocular oscillation, and isolated palatal oscillation represent distinguishable clinicoradiological phenotypes; (4) the ocular and associated neurological phenomenology of these syndromes; and (5) the expected latency from the causative lesion to OPT. We hypothesized that anatomical extent and bilateral involvement of the olivocerebellar network would be more informative than right-versus-left HOD in determining the ultimate clinical phenotype.

Methods

Study Design and Case Ascertainment

We pooled patient records from published reports and a retrospective review of our clinical practice to examine associations among lesion characteristics, hypertrophic olivary degeneration (HOD), and ocular and palatal oscillations. The dataset comprised 501 patients, including 469 literature cases (Supplementary Methods S1) and 32 previously unpublished clinical cases. Patients were the unit of analysis. We searched PubMed from inception through June 2026 for reports of HOD, oculopalatal tremor (OPT), or related disorders of the cerebellorubro-olivary pathway. We screened reports for extractable patient-level data and reviewed potentially overlapping reports to avoid duplicate inclusion (Supplementary Methods S2). We examined the clinical series separately for potential referral effects. The University Hospitals Cleveland Medical Center Institutional Review Board approved the retrospective chart review.

Data Abstraction and Phenotype Classification

We used standardized abstraction to record demographics, lesion location and etiology, radiographic HOD and its laterality, ocular and palatal findings, associated neurologic manifestations, symptom latency, MRI timing, and treatment. Two reviewers independently assessed a random 30% sample of included publications for eligibility, patient identification, and consistency of clinical and imaging abstraction. Discrepancies were resolved by consensus (Supplementary Methods S3).

HOD classification followed the source imaging interpretation. For clearly lateralized lesions, classifiable HOD was coded as ipsilateral, contralateral, or bilateral relative to the lesion (Supplementary Methods S4-S5). When ocular and palatal oscillations were both explicitly documented as present or absent, we classified patients as having OPT, ocular oscillations only, palatal oscillations only, or neither. The complete phenotype cohort included 187 patients. Of these, 142 had classifiable lesion location, etiology, and HOD pattern for association analyses (Supplementary Methods S6). A separate predictive cohort included 159 patients with confirmed HOD and complete four-class phenotype assignment. Unreported findings were treated as missing, and denominators reflected available data for each analysis (Supplementary Methods S3). Ocular waveform, palatal phenomenology, and associated neurologic manifestations were characterized descriptively (Supplementary Methods S7-S8). Symptom latency was measured from the causative lesion to reported clinical onset. MRI timing was analyzed separately as the interval from lesion to imaging (Supplementary Methods S9).

Statistical Analysis

We summarized categorical variables as counts and percentages and continuous variables primarily as medians and interquartile ranges. We assessed associations of lesion location, etiology, and HOD pattern with phenotype using omnibus Pearson chi-square tests, with Cramér V to quantify association strength. Fisher exact tests were used for pairwise or sparse comparisons. For relevant contrasts, we estimated odds ratios with 95% confidence intervals, using the group with neither ocular nor palatal oscillations as the reference for phenotype comparisons. We compared onset latency across lesion location, etiology, and HOD laterality groups using Kruskal-Wallis tests (Supplementary Methods S10).

Given sparse outcome categories, particularly the palatal-only phenotype, we used classbalanced L2-penalized multinomial logistic regression. The primary antecedent-factor model included age, sex, primary-lesion etiology, lesion location, and lesion laterality. A secondary imaging-enhanced model added HOD pattern as bilateral, left unilateral, right unilateral, or unavailable. Performance was estimated with repeated stratified four-fold cross-validation and summarized by macro one-versus-rest area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), balanced accuracy, macro F1 score, and multiclass log loss.

Results

Study Population, Data Completeness, Clinical Phenotype Distribution

The pooled dataset contained 501 patient records, including 469 literature-derived cases and 32 novel unpublished cases from our clinical practice. Table 1 summarizes analytical populations.

Among 187 patients in whom both ocular and palatal manifestations were definitively documented, 118 (63.1%) had OPT, 21 (11.2%) had isolated ocular oscillations, 8 (4.3%) had isolated palatal oscillations, and 40 (21.4%) had neither manifestation (Table 1). The remaining 314 patients could not be assigned reliably because one or both manifestations were not explicitly documented.

Hypertrophic Olivary Degeneration (HOD)

HOD Frequency and Laterality HOD status was definitively reported in 460 patients. HOD was present in 450/460 (97.8%) and absent in 10/460 (2.2%). This high frequency reflects selection of the dataset around HOD and related syndromes and prevents meaningful inference regarding predictors. HOD laterality was classifiable in 424 patients. HOD was unilateral in 221/424 (52.1%) and bilateral in 203/424 (47.9%). Among unilateral cases with documented side, 113/212 (53.3%) were left-sided and 99/212 (46.7%) were right-sided, with no meaningful overall left-right predominance.

Primary Lesion Location Predicts HOD Laterality

Primary lesion location was strongly associated with unilateral versus bilateral HOD (chi-square=35.65, p=3.4 x 10^-7; Cramer V=0.31). Focal brainstem or cerebellar lesions predominantly produced unilateral HOD, whereas combined pathway involving multiple posterior fossa structures, and other anatomically extensive lesions were more frequently associated with bilateral HOD.

Lesion Side Predicts the Side of HOD

The relationship between lesion side and HOD became anatomically coherent after stratification by lesion level. Among 70 classifiable lateralized brainstem/pons lesions, 49 (70.0%) produced ipsilateral HOD, only 3 (4.3%) produced isolated contralateral HOD, and 18 (25.7%) produced bilateral HOD (Table 2). Conversely, among 44 lateralized cerebellar lesions, 38 (86.4%) produced contralateral HOD, 5 (11.4%) produced bilateral HOD, and only 1 (2.3%) produced isolated ipsilateral HOD. Combined pathway lesions were relatively more likely to produce bilateral HOD. This lesion-level relationship was one of the strongest anatomically coherent findings in the dataset (Table 2).

Primary Lesion Etiology and HOD Laterality

Etiology was also associated with unilateral versus bilateral HOD (chi-square=27.35, p=0.0012; Cramer V=0.27; Table 2). Hemorrhagic lesions most commonly produced unilateral HOD. Neoplastic, ischemic, traumatic, and inflammatory lesions showed more balanced unilateral-bilateral distributions. Genetic, degenerative, and idiopathic disorders were overwhelmingly bilateral, likely reflecting diffuse or bilateral pathway involvement. Etiology and anatomical extent were not independent, and this association should therefore not be interpreted as a direct biological effect of lesion cause.

Predictors of Any Ocular Oscillations

Ocular examination was explicitly documented in 230 patients. Ocular oscillations were present in 182/230 (79.1%). Among patients with classifiable HOD laterality, ocular oscillations occurred in 70/84 (83.3%) with bilateral HOD compared with 62/95 (65.3%) with unilateral HOD. Bilateral HOD was therefore associated with substantially greater odds of ocular oscillations (OR 2.66, 95% CI 1.31-5.43; Fisher exact p=0.0067).

Primary lesion location was associated with ocular oscillations (omnibus p=0.0079; Cramer V=0.26). Ocular oscillations occurred in 12/14 (85.7%) patients with combined pathway lesions, 110/135 (81.5%) with brainstem/pons lesions, 25/37 (67.6%) with cerebellar/dentate lesions, 8/12 (66.7%) with other sites, and 1/5 (20.0%) with posterior fossa/fourth ventricular lesions. Brainstem and combined pathway lesions therefore showed the highest frequency of ocular manifestations.

Etiology was also associated with ocular oscillations (omnibus p=0.0050; Cramer V=0.30). Because several etiologic strata were small, this result was interpreted primarily through the larger hemorrhagic, ischemic, and neoplastic groups rather than through extreme proportions in small, selected categories.

Predictors of Any Palatal Oscillations

Palatal examination was explicitly documented in 315 patients. Palatal oscillations were present in 197/315 (62.5%). Among patients with classifiable HOD laterality, palatal oscillations occurred in 80/125 (64.0%) with bilateral HOD and 79/145 (54.5%) with unilateral HOD. The association was weaker than that observed for ocular oscillations (OR 1.49, 95% CI 0.91-2.42; Fisher exact p=0.137). Primary lesion location was not significantly associated with palatal oscillations (p=0.250). Etiology showed a nominal association (p=0.031; Cramer V=0.24), but interpretation was limited by sparse categories.

The preceding ocular analysis compared patients with versus without any ocular oscillation, irrespective of whether palatal oscillations were also present; and vice versa for palatal analysis irrespective of ocular involvement. On the contrary complete-phenotype analysis addressed a different question and was restricted to patients in whom both ocular and palatal status were explicitly documented and classified each patient into combined OPT, ocular-only, palatal-only, or neither. This analysis determined whether lesion location, etiology, or HOD pattern distinguished the specific clinical phenotype, rather than simply predicting the presence of an ocular or palatal manifestation.

Predictors of the Complete Clinical Phenotype

The complete predictor cohort comprised 142 patients: 77 with OPT, 19 with ocular-only manifestations, 8 with palatal-only manifestations, and 38 with neither phenotype (Table 3).

HOD pattern.

When left and right unilateral HOD were combined, bilateral HOD was associated with OPT versus neither (OR 2.98, 95% CI 1.25-7.12; p=0.015), ocular only versus neither (OR 4.43, 95% CI 1.36-14.40; p=0.018), and palatal only versus neither (OR 5.37, 95% CI 1.07-27.00; p=0.044). The palatal-only estimate was based on only eight events and was therefore highly unstable. When unilateral left, unilateral right, and bilateral HOD were retained as three separate categories, the overall four-phenotype association did not reach significance (p=0.114; Cramer V=0.19). The data therefore provided no convincing evidence that left and right unilateral HOD generate different phenotypes; the principal distinction was bilateral versus unilateral involvement.

Primary Lesion Location and Phenotype.

Primary lesion location significantly differentiated the four phenotypes (chi-square=32.78, p=0.0010; Cramer V=0.28; Table 3). Combined pathway lesions had the highest proportion of OPT (72.7%), followed by brainstem/pons lesions (60.6%) and cerebellar lesions (36.0%). Posterior fossa/fourth ventricular lesions rarely produced the complete phenotype in this small subgroup.

When brainstem/pons lesions were compared with cerebellar lesions for OPT versus neither phenotype, the OR was 2.88 (95% CI 1.03-8.03), with a borderline Fisher exact p value of 0.056. The magnitude and direction of the effect, together with the significant omnibus association, supported an anatomical tendency favoring OPT after brainstem rather than isolated cerebellar lesions.

Etiology and Clinical Phenotype

Etiology differed across the four phenotypes (chi-square=34.54, p=0.0108; Cramer V=0.28). Because several expected counts were small, this omnibus association was considered exploratory (Table 3).

Hemorrhagic lesions. Among hemorrhagic cases, 59.5% had OPT, 9.5% isolated ocular oscillations, 2.4% isolated palatal oscillations, and 28.6% neither manifestation. Thus, when an oscillatory phenotype followed hemorrhage, it was most commonly the combined OPT phenotype.

Ischemic lesions. Among ischemic cases, 54.5% had OPT and 36.4% isolated ocular oscillations; none had isolated palatal oscillations. Compared with hemorrhage, ischemia was associated with substantially greater odds of ocular-only disease relative to the "neither" phenotype (OR 12.0, 95% CI 1.16-123.69; p=0.030). The wide confidence interval reflected the small ischemic subgroup.

Neoplastic lesions.

Neoplastic lesions produced the most heterogeneous phenotype: 26.9% OPT, 19.2% ocular only, 19.2% palatal only, and 34.6% neither. Compared with hemorrhage, neoplastic lesions showed directionally lower odds of OPT versus neither (OR 0.37; p=0.090). Neoplasia was disproportionately represented among isolated palatal cases (palatal only vs neither, neoplastic vs hemorrhagic: OR 6.67, 95% CI 1.09-40.74; p=0.039). This association may reflect lesion extent, progressive posterior fossa distortion, surgery, radiation, recurrence, or involvement of multiple pathway components rather than tumor biology itself.

Vascular malformations.

Among nonhemorrhagic vascular-malformation cases, 70% had OPT and 30% had neither manifestation; no completely classified ocular-only or palatal-only cases were identified. Overall, the etiologic findings suggested that hemorrhagic and vascularmalformation lesions preferentially produced combined OPT when an oscillatory phenotype occurred, ischemic lesions showed relatively greater representation of isolated ocular manifestations, and neoplastic/post-treatment lesions produced a more heterogeneous phenotype.

Predictive Modeling Among Patients with Confirmed HOD

The confirmed-HOD predictive cohort included 159 patients: 91 with OPT, 20 with ocular-only oscillations, 8 with palatal-only oscillations, and 40 with neither manifestation. This analysis treated the four mutually exclusive phenotypes as the ground-truth outcome and used only demographic and lesion characteristics that could precede the clinical phenotype. Etiology differed across outcomes (p=0.0024). Vascular malformations and other structural causes were enriched among OPT cases, ischemic lesions showed relatively greater representation of ocular-only disease, and neoplastic lesions were disproportionately represented among palatalonly cases. Lesion location showed an anatomical trend (p=0.082): OPT occurred in 75.0% of combined pathway lesions and 60.8% of brainstem/pons lesions, compared with 36.0% of cerebellar/dentate lesions. Age (p=0.556), sex (p=0.219), and primary-lesion laterality (p=0.746) did not differentiate phenotype.

The antecedent-factor model included age, sex, lesion etiology, lesion location, and lesion laterality. Cross-validated four-class discrimination was limited: macro AUC was 0.555 (SD 0.077), balanced accuracy 0.301 (SD 0.091), macro F1 score 0.262 (SD 0.070), and log loss 1.370 (SD 0.083) (Table 4). For comparison, chance-level balanced accuracy for four classes was 0.25. The strongest directional model coefficients were vascular-malformation etiology for OPT, ischemic etiology for ocular-only disease, and neoplastic etiology and atypical lesion location for palatal-only disease; these coefficients were shrinkage estimates and were not interpreted as conventional inferential odds ratios.

Adding HOD laterality produced only a small improvement in discrimination: macro AUC increased to 0.563 (SD 0.076), balanced accuracy to 0.323 (SD 0.083), and macro F1 to 0.278 (SD 0.058), while log loss decreased to 1.357 (SD 0.079). Bilateral HOD was directionally associated with an oscillatory phenotype, whereas right-versus-left unilateral HOD added little information. In a literature-only sensitivity analysis excluding the clinically referred Cleveland cases, macro AUC was 0.600 for the antecedent model and 0.598 after HOD laterality was added, confirming that the imaging variable did not materially improve discrimination (Table 4).

A hierarchical analysis did not resolve the limited prediction. For any ocular or palatal oscillation versus neither manifestation, the antecedent model yielded an AUC of 0.508 and the imaging-enhanced model an AUC of 0.556. Among symptomatic patients, discrimination of OPT from ocular-only and palatal-only phenotypes remained modest (macro AUC 0.570 without and 0.557 with HOD laterality). These results indicate that the measured factors define population-level associations but do not provide sufficient separation for an individual-level clinical prediction tool.

Ocular Phenomenology

Oculopalatal Tremor

Among 118 patients with OPT, ocular waveform was classifiable in 65. Fifty-nine had a pendular waveform, 2 had a pendular waveform with a jerk component, 2 had other explicitly pendular variants, 1 had a pure jerk waveform, and 1 had rapid jerky oscillations. The described directions included vertical, vertical-torsional, torsional, multiplanar horizontal-vertical-torsional, and dissociated or asymmetric vertical oscillations.

Isolated Ocular Oscillations

Among 21 ocular-only patients, six had explicitly pendular oscillations and one had an irregular rhythmic waveform; waveform was not reported in 14. Reported manifestations were often heterogeneous and included pendular, gaze-evoked, upbeat, horizontal, mixed verticalhorizontal, and dissociated nystagmus.

Palatal Phenomenology

Terminology used for palatal manifestations included palatal tremor, palatal myoclonus, symptomatic palatal tremor, palatal/pharyngeal tremor, and palatopharyngeal or palatolaryngeal myoclonus. In these reports, use of "tremor" versus "myoclonus" appeared to reflect nomenclature rather than demonstrably different physiology.

Associated Neurological and Movement-Disorder Phenotypes

Associated neurological manifestations were explicitly documented in 243 patients. Ataxia was the dominant associated manifestation. Among OPT patients with sufficiently documented associated findings, 52/73 (71.2%) had ataxia, 30/73 (41.1%) weakness or plegia, 19/73 (26.0%) another tremor, and 10/73 (13.7%) dystonia. Among ocular-only patients with documented associated findings, 13/16 (81.3%) had ataxia. All four palatal-only patients with documented associated manifestations had ataxia, although this denominator was too small for comparative inference. Chorea was exceptionally uncommon.

Latency From Primary Lesion to Clinical Manifestation

Overall Timing. An explicit symptom-onset interval was reported in 105 patients. Across these patients, median onset was 7 months (IQR 4-17 months), mean onset was 19.2 months, and the range extended from immediate onset to 288 months. The mean was strongly influenced by a small number of very delayed cases; medians and IQRs were therefore considered more representative. Among 45 patients in the complete-phenotype cohort with timing information, median onset was 7 months (IQR 4.5-18 months).

OPT-Specific Timing. Timing was available in 35 patients with OPT. Median onset was 6 months (IQR 4.75-24 months), mean onset was 29.3 months, and the range extended from immediate onset to 288 months.

Cumulative onset was 5.7% by 1 month, 20.0% by 3 months, 51.4% by 6 months, 65.7% by 12 months, and 77.1% by 24 months. Thus, approximately half of documented OPT cases became clinically apparent by six months, approximately two thirds by one year, and more than three quarters by two years. Delayed onset several years after the causative lesion was documented but uncommon.

Do Anatomical or Etiologic Variables Predict OPT Latency?

No convincing predictor of onset latency emerged. There were no statistically significant differences according to etiology (p=0.267), lesion location (p=0.527), or HOD laterality/side (p=0.841). MRI was performed or HOD was identified at a median of approximately 6 months (IQR 3-12 months), but MRI timing was determined by clinical practice and should not be interpreted as biological onset of HOD. Do demographics predict ocular or palatal oscillations? Age was available in 456 patients. Median age was 51 years (IQR 36.8-60.3; range 1.5-89 years). There were 280 male and 172 female patients; sex was not reported in 49. Neither age nor sex predicted ocular or palatal oscillations (age and ocular oscillations, p=0.364; age and palatal oscillations, p=0.268; sex and ocular oscillations, p=0.489; sex and palatal oscillations, p=0.904). Anatomical characteristics were therefore substantially more informative than basic demographic variables.

Discussion

This pooled patient-level analysis provides a large-scale examination of the anatomical, etiologic, and clinical determinants of OPT and related manifestations of HOD. Several findings emerged consistently across complementary analyses. First, the anatomical level of the causative lesion strongly determined the laterality of subsequent HOD: brainstem lesions produced predominantly ipsilateral HOD, whereas cerebellar (deep cerebellar nuclei) lesions produced contralateral HOD. Second, bilateral HOD was substantially more strongly associated with ocular oscillations than with palatal oscillations. Third, the complete OPT phenotype was preferentially associated with brainstem and combined pathway lesions, while isolated cerebellar/dentate lesions were less likely to produce combined OPT. Fourth, right-versus-left unilateral HOD had little apparent relationship to phenotype; bilaterality was more informative than side. Fifth, classical OPT was overwhelmingly characterized by "pendular" ocular oscillations. Finally, OPT typically became clinically apparent several months after the inciting lesion, with median onset around six months, but the available anatomical variables did not reliably predict either phenotype or latency in an individual patient.

Together, these observations support the concept that HOD represents a substrate for abnormal oscillation but does not itself determine the clinical syndrome. The anatomical extent of network injury and subsequent olivocerebellar reorganization appear to be critical determinants of whether HOD is expressed as ocular tremor, palatal tremor, combined OPT, or no clinically apparent oscillation.

Lesion level accounting for the laterality of HOD is an intuitive result. More focal lesions affecting brainstem, pons, or cerebellum leads to focused disruption of descending central tegmental tract fibers leading to unilateral HOD. Central tegmental track does not cross, leading to ipsilateral deficit, while interruption of cerebellar outflow that cross in the superior cerebellar peduncle causes contralateral deficit. Combined pathway lesions, being more extensive, produce bilateral HOD.

Inferior olivary deafferentation has been implicated in symptomatic palatal tremor 3 . The oscillator-amplifier model proposes that synchronized olivary activity provides a low-frequency signal that maladaptive cerebellar learning amplifies and reshapes into complex ocular oscillations 14, 15, [19] [20] [21] . Bilateral HOD may mark more extensive involvement of this network, increasing the likelihood that pathological activity is expressed in ocular motor circuits, favoring ocular oscillations by engaging more of the olivocerebellar network. Consistent with this possibility, ocular oscillations were more frequent with bilateral than unilateral HOD (83.3% vs 65.3%, OR 2.66). Among fully characterized cases, bilateral HOD was associated with approximately threefold higher odds of combined OPT and more than fourfold higher odds of ocular-only oscillations, each relative to neither manifestation. The same concept also explains why combined pathway lesions had the highest proportion of OPT, followed by brainstem lesions.

A pendular component was present in 96.9% of OPT cases with documented waveform, often with vertical, torsional, or mixed components and interocular asymmetry 11, 14, [20] [21] [22] . The ocular-only group also included gaze-evoked, upbeat, and horizontal nystagmus, some of which may reflect the primary lesion itself. It is noteworthy that number of cases included in this analysis offered clinical characterization. On the contrary, objective measure of oculographic waveform highlights non-pendular, coarse irregular eye oscillations; these are neither jerk nor pendular; but represent combined effects of more than one oscillators as well as mal-adaptive cerebellar learning 14, 21, 22 . Among OPT cases with documented associated features, more than 70% had ataxia and approximately 14% had dystonia. The frequency of ataxia places OPT within a broader cerebellar and brainstem syndrome, as well as maladaptive state induced by olive-cerebellar hypersynchrony suppressing normal motor learning 14, 19, 20, 23 . Dystonia is becoming increasingly recognized feature accompanying feature of the disease and it could represent manifestation of aberrant cerebellar hyperactivity transmitted to the basal ganglia, making the circuit unstable 14, 19, 20.

Hemorrhage tended to accompany combined OPT and similar pattern was seen with vascular malformation. This is an expected finding since vascular malformation often leads to hemorrhage in corresponding location. Ischemia more often accompanied ocular-only oscillations, and neoplasia was overrepresented among isolated palatal cases. Ischemia and tumor both are likely to be focal, presenting more limited involvement, and isolated presentation. In other words, these exploratory patterns may reflect differences in lesion location, extent, chronicity, or treatment, including surgery and radiation.

The timing analysis provides a clinically useful characterization of OPT evolution. Among 35 OPT patients with explicit timing, median onset was approximately six months. About half had developed OPT by six months, approximately two thirds by one year, and more than three quarters by two years. This delayed clinical course corresponds broadly to the known temporal evolution of HOD. Increased olivary T2 signal may appear approximately one month after pathway injury, while frank hypertrophy generally emerges later, around six months 10 . The similarity between the approximate timing of olivary hypertrophy and median OPT onset is biologically appealing, but the two measures should not be conflated. MRI timing in this dataset reflects when imaging was obtained, not when olivary pathology first developed.

One of the most important findings was the discrepancy between biologically coherent grouplevel associations and poor individual-level predictive performance. In the confirmed-HOD cohort, lesion etiology differentiated phenotype, and bilateral HOD was more frequent among patients with ocular manifestations. Nevertheless, the antecedent four-class model achieved a macro AUC of only 0.555 and balanced accuracy of 0.301; adding HOD laterality increased these values only to 0.563 and 0.323. Hierarchical modeling of any oscillatory phenotype versus neither also remained near chance. This is not contradictory. An odds ratio or group association indicates that a feature is more common in one phenotype than another; it does not imply sufficient sensitivity, specificity, or independence to identify an individual patient's future phenotype. Considerable anatomical overlap remained among OPT, ocular-only, palatal-only, and clinically unaffected patients. Moreover, the available predictors were coarse structural descriptors and did not capture exact tract-level lesion topography, inferior olivary subnuclei, quantitative hypertrophy, residual pathway connectivity, cerebellar adaptive gain, ocular motor integrator involvement, or longitudinal network evolution. The present analyses should therefore be regarded as predictor-association models rather than clinical prediction tools. Future predictive studies will require quantitative lesion mapping, tractography, longitudinal imaging, quantitative eye-movement recordings, and functional measures of cerebellar-olivary connectivity.

The data support several clinically useful principles. First, the side of HOD should be interpreted according to the anatomical level of the primary lesion. Brainstem lesions generally produce ipsilateral HOD, whereas cerebellar/dentate lesions generally produce contralateral HOD. Second, bilateral HOD increases the probability of an ocular manifestation and of combined OPT. Bilaterality is more clinically informative than whether unilateral HOD is right-or left-sided. Third, patients with combined brainstem-cerebellar pathway injury appear particularly likely to develop the complete OPT syndrome, whereas isolated cerebellar/dentate lesions can produce HOD without necessarily producing OPT. Fourth, OPT typically emerges over months rather than immediately after the lesion. Clinical surveillance should therefore extend beyond the acute neurological event, particularly through the first one to two years. These associations can inform clinicoradiological interpretation and patient counseling but are not sufficiently discriminative to predict an individual patient's outcome with confidence.

This study has several limitations. Ocular and palatal status were documented in 230 and 315 of 501 patients, respectively, and four-class phenotype assignment required explicit documentation of both findings. Most cases came from retrospective reports or series with variable examination and imaging protocols. Publication and referral bias, shared study-level ascertainment, and broad lesion categories limit generalizability. Primary-lesion laterality was unavailable in more than half of the confirmed-HOD predictive cohort. Only eight isolated palatal cases and 35 timed OPT cases were available, limiting phenotype-specific and latency analyses. Multiple exploratory comparisons further limit inference, particularly for small etiologic and phenotypic subgroups.

In conclusion, this pooled patient-level analysis demonstrates that the clinical expression of hypertrophic olivary degeneration is determined more strongly by the location and extent of cerebello-rubro-olivary pathway disruption than by demographic characteristics or right-versusleft olivary involvement. The side of HOD follows the known anatomy of the pathway: lateralized brainstem lesions predominantly produce ipsilateral HOD, whereas cerebellar/dentate lesions predominantly produce contralateral HOD. Bilateral HOD preferentially associates with ocular oscillations and increases the likelihood of the combined OPT phenotype, while the relationship with palatal oscillations alone is substantially weaker. Combined brainstem-cerebellar pathway lesions show the greatest propensity for complete OPT, whereas isolated cerebellar/dentate lesions are less likely to produce the full syndrome. Classical OPT is overwhelmingly characterized by pendular ocular oscillations, most often involving vertical, torsional, or mixed planes. OPT generally develops after a delay, with a median onset of approximately six months and more than three quarters of documented cases emerging within two years. These findings support a distributed network model in which inferior olivary degeneration creates the substrate for pathological oscillation but does not alone determine clinical expression. The extent of pathway injury, bilateral olivary involvement, and subsequent cerebellar network adaptation likely determine whether the resulting syndrome manifests as ocular tremor, palatal tremor, combined OPT, or no overt oscillatory phenotype. Although these anatomical and etiologic variables provide meaningful population-level associations, their limited cross-validated discrimination indicates that they cannot yet reliably predict the phenotype of an individual patient.

S4 Primary Lesion Location and Etiology

We grouped lesion locations as brainstem or pons, cerebellum or dentate nucleus, combined brainstemcerebellar or multicomponent pathway involvement, posterior fossa or fourth ventricle, and other sites. We grouped etiologies as hemorrhagic, ischemic, neoplastic, vascular malformation, traumatic, inflammatory or infectious, genetic or degenerative or idiopathic, and other causes. When hemorrhage associated with a vascular malformation was classified as the primary lesion, we assigned the case to the hemorrhagic category alone.

S5 Definition and Laterality of HOD

HOD status followed the radiographic interpretation in the source record. We classified HOD as unilateral or bilateral and recorded left or right for unilateral cases when the side was available. Analyses relating lesion side to HOD included only patients with a clearly lateralized primary lesion and classifiable HOD. We coded HOD as ipsilateral, contralateral, or bilateral relative to the causative lesion.

S6 Clinical Phenotype and Analysis Cohorts

We assigned one of four mutually exclusive phenotypes only when the source explicitly documented both ocular and palatal oscillation status. Combined oculopalatal tremor (OPT) required both to be present. The ocular-only phenotype required ocular oscillations to be present and palatal oscillations to be absent. The palatal-only phenotype required palatal oscillations to be present and ocular oscillations to be absent. The neither phenotype required both to be absent.

The complete phenotype cohort included 187 patients. Association analyses requiring classifiable HOD side or laterality, primary lesion location, and primary lesion etiology included 142 patients. For predictive modeling, we used the 159 patients with confirmed HOD and complete four-class phenotype assignment. This predictive cohort included 91 patients with OPT, 20 with ocular-only oscillations, 8 with palatal-only oscillations, and 40 with neither manifestation.

S7 Ocular and Palatal Phenomenology

We classified ocular waveform and direction using the terminology in each source report. We coded pendular, pendular-with-jerk, and other explicitly pendular waveforms as having a pendular component. We retained separate descriptions for gaze-evoked, upbeat, horizontal, mixed, dissociated, and other nonpendular patterns.

We retained the reported palatal terminology because historical use of "tremor" and "myoclonus" did not consistently distinguish underlying physiology. Phenomenologic analyses were primarily descriptive given incomplete waveform documentation.

S8 Associated Neurologic Manifestations

We grouped associated manifestations as ataxia, weakness or plegia, tremor other than palatal tremor, sensory abnormalities, dystonia, and chorea. Percentages used the denominator of patients with explicit documentation of associated neurologic manifestations.

S9 Symptom and MRI Timing

We extracted the interval from the primary lesion to clinical symptom onset when explicitly reported. We analyzed symptom timing among all patients with a documented onset interval, within the complete phenotype cohort, and within the OPT subgroup. We analyzed MRI timing separately. The interval from the causative lesion to an MRI demonstrating or evaluating HOD represented the timing of imaging observation. The date of first biological onset of HOD could not be inferred from this interval.

S10 Statistical Analysis

We summarized categorical variables using counts and percentages and continuous variables primarily using medians and interquartile ranges because several distributions, particularly symptom-onset latency, were markedly right-skewed. We used Pearson chi-square tests for multigroup categorical comparisons and calculated Cramér V to describe association strength. We used Fisher exact tests for two-group comparisons and when individual categories were sparse.

We first examined associations of lesion location, lesion etiology, and HOD pattern with the four-level phenotype using omnibus tests. We then performed focused pairwise comparisons for anatomically or clinically relevant contrasts, reporting odds ratios and 95% confidence intervals. The group with neither ocular nor palatal oscillations was the primary reference for comparisons with OPT, ocular-only, and palatal-only phenotypes.

Because some outcomes were uncommon, particularly isolated palatal oscillations, we used class-balanced L2penalized multinomial logistic regression. The primary antecedent-factor model included age, sex, primarylesion etiology, primary-lesion location, and primary-lesion laterality. Etiology was consolidated as hemorrhagic, ischemic, neoplastic, vascular malformation, or other/unclear. Lesion location was consolidated as brainstem/pons, cerebellum/dentate, combined brainstem-cerebellar pathway, or other/unclear. Lesion laterality was classified as left, right, bilateral/midline, or unknown. Age was standardized; categorical variables were dummy coded within each training fold. A secondary imaging-enhanced model added HOD pattern as bilateral, left unilateral, right unilateral, or unavailable.

Models used an L2 penalty with inverse regularization strength C=0.5 and balanced class weights. We estimated out-of-sample performance with repeated stratified four-fold cross-validation using five repetitions for the principal models. Performance measures were overall accuracy, balanced accuracy, macro F1 score, macro one-versus-rest AUC, and multiclass log loss. We emphasized balanced accuracy, macro F1, and macro AUC because the four outcomes were imbalanced. Coefficients from the penalized full-cohort model were interpreted directionally as shrinkage estimates, not as conventional inferential odds ratios.

We performed two sensitivity analyses. First, we repeated the primary and imaging-enhanced models after excluding the clinically referred Cleveland cases. Second, we used a hierarchical approach that compared any oscillatory phenotype with neither manifestation and then, among symptomatic patients, distinguished OPT, ocular-only, and palatal-only phenotypes. These analyses tested whether collapsing the outcome into clinically simpler stages improved discrimination. Analyses were performed in Python 3.12 using scikit-learn 1.8.0 and SciPy 1.17.0.

We compared onset latency across lesion etiology, lesion location, and HOD laterality groups with Kruskal-Wallis tests. All statistical tests were two-sided. Given the exploratory design and correlated comparisons, we interpreted p values in the context of effect sizes, confidence intervals, sample sizes, biological plausibility, and consistency across related analyses.

S2 Literature Search and Case Ascertainment

The pooled patient-level dataset consisted of 469 cases from a previously assembled literature extraction and 32 previously unpublished cases from our clinical practice. The patient was the unit of analysis. We treated the pooled records as a heterogeneous observational dataset because source reports varied in referral populations, clinical assessments, imaging protocols, terminology, and completeness of reporting. We pooled the clinical series with the literature cases for the principal clinicoradiologic analyses and examined the clinical series separately to assess potential referral effects. The University Hospitals Cleveland Medical Center Institutional Review Board approved the retrospective chart review of the unpublished clinical cases.

We conducted a structured PubMed search from database inception through June 2026. Search terms included "oculopalatal tremor," "oculopalatal myoclonus," "inferior olive hypertrophy," "hypertrophic inferior olivary degeneration," and "progressive ataxia palatal tremor." We screened reports for patients with hypertrophic olivary degeneration (HOD), ocular or palatal oscillations associated with lesions affecting the cerebellorubro-olivary (Guillain-Mollaret) pathway, or both. We reviewed relevant articles for extractable clinical, anatomical, imaging, etiologic, phenomenologic, and temporal data at the individual-patient level. When publications from the same author group described patients with similar clinical characteristics, lesion etiologies, imaging findings, or other identifying features, we compared the reports for potential overlap. We included patients judged to have been reported more than once only once in the pooled dataset.
