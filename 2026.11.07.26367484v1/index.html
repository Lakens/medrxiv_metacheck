What we found

We found 20 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Metacheck does not yet analyze automatically whether sentences which include non-significant p-values are correct, but we recommend manually checking the sentences below for possible misinterpreted non-significant p values.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Marginal Significance works

List all sentences that describe an effect as ‘marginally significant’.

The marginal module searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The list of terms is a subset of those listed in a blog post by Matthew Hankins. The module returns all sentences that match terms describing ‘marginally significant’ results.

Some of the terms identified might not be problematic in some contexts, and there are ways to describe ‘marginal significance’ that are not detected by the module.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Abstract

Evidence on sex-and age-specific associations between lead levels and childhood growth remain limited. We analyzed 10,324 children aged 12-59 months from the Bangladesh Multiple Indicator Cluster Survey 2025 using survey-weighted multivariable linear regression to examine sex-and age-specific associations of blood lead level (BLL) with anthropometric measures and haemoglobin levels. The geometric mean BLL was 4.43 µg/dL, and 66.3% of children had concentrations above the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) threshold (≥3.5 µg/dL). Higher BLL was associated with lower height-for-age zscore (HAZ) (β = -0.129, 95%CI: -0.182 to -0.076), weight-for-age z-score (WAZ) (β = -0.108, 95%CI: -0.156 to -0.060), and weight-for-height z-scores (WHZ) (β = -0.052, 95%CI: -0.101 to -0.002).

Higher BLL was also associated with lower HAZ and WAZ among boys (HAZ: β = -0.114, 95%CI: -0.188 to -0.040; WAZ: β = -0.113, 95%CI: -0.179 to -0.046) and girls (HAZ: β = -0.150, 95%CI: -0.225 to -0.075; WAZ: β = -0.104, 95%CI: -0.171 to -0.036), while higher BLL was associated with lower WHZ among boys (β = -0.076, 95%CI: -0.144 to -0.007). Higher BLL was associated with lower HAZ (β = -0.266, 95%CI: -0.408 to -0.125) and WAZ (β = -0.206, 95% CI: -0.326 to -0.086) among boys aged 24-35 months, while among girls aged 36-59 months, corresponding estimates were -0.211 (95%CI: -0.310 to -0.113) and -0.106 (95%CI: -0.191 to -0.022). The findings suggest that longitudinal studies are needed to clarify the temporal and developmental patterns underlying these associations.

A joint UNICEF-Pure Earth assessment estimated that about 800 million children worldwide had blood lead concentrations of at least 5 µg/dL, which is equivalent to one in every three children (UNICEF and Pure Earth, 2020). This burden is disproportionately concentrated in low-and middle-income countries (LMICs) (Ericson et al., 2021), with South Asia accounting for a substantial share of the global burden (Roy and Das, 2026). The effects of lead may continue long after the exposure has occurred. A global modelling study estimated that lead exposure was associated with 765 million lost IQ points among children younger than five years in 2019 and with approximately 5.5 million adult cardiovascular deaths (Larsen and Sánchez-Triana, 2023).

Lead exposure has been associated with adverse neurological, haematological, renal, cardiovascular, and developmental outcomes, and no blood lead concentration has been established as being without harmful effects in children (WHO, 2026). Child growth is a clinically relevant marker of health and cumulative nutritional and environmental stress. However, the association between lead exposure and anthropometric status is not consistent across settings. In a study of young Chinese children, higher blood lead levels (BLL) were associated with lower height-for-age z-score (HAZ) and weight-for-age z-score (WAZ) (Zhou et al., 2020). In rural Bangladesh, concurrent BLL was associated with a higher odds of stunting among children aged 20-40 months, whereas cord-BLL was not associated with later stunting (Gleason et al., 2016). A separate study of children living in informal settlements in Bangladesh found association with stunting and underweight but not with wasting (Raihan et al., 2018). Findings from other populations have been mixed, with associations varying according to the timing and biomarker of exposure, the growth measure examined, and the age and sex of the child (Ahmadi et al., 2022). These inconsistencies suggest that a single overall association may conceal important developmental or sex-specific patterns.

Lead may influence haemoglobin through disruption of haem biosynthesis, including inhibition of enzymes in the pathway, and this effect may be compounded by iron deficiency, which can increase gastrointestinal lead absorption (Abelsohn and Sanborn, 2010; WHO, 2026). Nevertheless, epidemiological findings concerning blood lead and haemoglobin have been inconsistent, and relatively little evidence has examined whether this relationship differs across specific developmental periods (Irawati et al., 2022; Mitra et al., 2012). Lead may also affect growth through interference with endocrine and skeletal processes. For example, higher BLL have been associated with lower circulating insulin-like growth factor-1 in children, although the direction and clinical importance of this pathway remain uncertain (Fleisch et al., 2013; Roels et al., 1976). However, these mechanisms do not necessarily imply that associations should be uniform across childhood. Exposure patterns, nutritional status, rates of growth, physiological development, and susceptibility to toxicants can change substantially during the first five years of life (Abelsohn and Sanborn, 2010; Afeiche et al., 2012).

Sex and developmental age may therefore be important sources of heterogeneity in the association between lead exposure and child health. However, previous studies have not reached a clear conclusion about whether boys or girls are more affected by lead. In a study of 1,678 young children in China, higher blood lead was associated with lower HAZ and WAZ among boys but not girls, and the authors concluded that sex-specific patterns warranted further investigation (Zhou et al., 2020). Other studies have reported different patterns of evidence (Ashley-Martin et al., 2019; Malin Igra et al., 2021). These inconsistencies suggest that sex-specific susceptibility cannot be assumed to operate in a uniform direction. Age may be equally important. During the first five years of life, rapid changes in growth, behaviour, diet, and exposure pathways can affect lead exposure and nutritional needs, particularly because of frequent hand-to-mouth behaviour, greater gastrointestinal absorption, and the transition to complementary and family foods (Hauptman et al., 2017; WHO, 2026). For example, a study in Mexico suggested that infancy may be a sensitive period for lead exposure in relation to height at 48 months (Afeiche et al., 2012). However, evidence on whether lead exposure is associated differently with physical growth and haemoglobin across developmental age groups remains limited.

Despite the biological plausibility linking lead exposure to impaired growth and haemoglobin synthesis, evidence from LMICs, including Bangladesh, remains limited. Previous studies on lead and child growth have largely focused on rural communities, urban informal settlements or specific geographic areas, often with relatively small sample sizes and varying age ranges (Gardner et al., 2013; Gleason et al., 2016; Raihan et al., 2018). Findings have also been inconsistent, and few studies have examined blood lead, haemoglobin, and multiple anthropometric indicators within the same nationally representative population. Evidence on whether these associations differ by sex or developmental age is also limited.

The Bangladesh Multiple Indicator Cluster Survey (MICS) 2025 provides an opportunity to address these gaps. Covering all 64 districts, the survey collected blood lead, haemoglobin, and standardized anthropometric measurements among children aged 12-59 months and represents the first national household survey in Bangladesh to measure blood lead and other heavy metals at this scale (BBS and UNICEF, 2026). These data allow assessment of lead exposure and child health beyond the restricted populations examined previously. However, national prevalence estimates do not indicate whether blood lead is associated with specific growth or haemoglobin outcomes or whether these associations vary by age or sex. Therefore, this study aimed to examine the associations between blood lead concentration and child growth indicators, and haemoglobin among Bangladeshi children aged 12-59 months using the nationally representative MICS 2025 dataset. Specifically, we assessed the overall associations and evaluated whether they differed by sex and developmental age group.

2. Methods

>Methods

2.1. Data source and participant selection

We used data from the Bangladesh MICS 2025, a nationally representative household survey conducted by the Bangladesh Bureau of Statistics (BBS) in collaboration with UNICEF as part of the global MICS programme. The survey was conducted from 24 February to 30 June 2025 using computer-assisted personal interviewing based on the 2022 Bangladesh Population and Housing Census. A multistage, stratified cluster sampling design was used to select households. The sampling framework comprised 67 sampling domains, including the 64 administrative districts and three separate city-corporation domains: Dhaka North, Dhaka South, and Chattogram. Within each domain, census Enumeration Areas (EAs) served as primary sampling units (PSUs) and were selected separately within urban and rural strata using probability-proportional-to-size sampling. Following household mapping and listing, 20 households were selected systematically from each sampled EA. Overall, 3,149 clusters (862 urban and 2,287 rural) and 62,980 households were selected across Bangladesh. The survey included household, women, and child questionnaires, along with a biomarker component for children aged 12-59 months and pregnant women. The present study included children aged 12-59 months with available measurements of BLL, haemoglobin, and anthropometric outcomes (BBS and UNICEF, 2026).

The Bangladesh MICS 2025 included 24,680 eligible children under five years of age. Of these, 18,913 children were aged 12-59 months and were included in the population eligible for the blood lead and other heavy-metal assessment. Under the blood collection procedure, 17,132 children were eligible for blood collection, of whom 10,634 yielded a valid laboratory result. Further, we excluded participants with invalid or out-of-range anthropometric measurements or haemoglobin levels, resulting in 10,488 participants. Children with missing covariate information were also excluded and the final analytical sample comprised 10,324 children aged 12-59 months. The participant selection process is shown in Figure S1.

2.2. Blood collection and biomarker assessment

Blood biomarkers were assessed as part of the Bangladesh MICS 2025 biomarker component. Children aged 12-59 months were eligible for blood collection. In households with eligible children, one child was selected for blood collection. However, when more than one child aged 12-59 months was present, selection was performed using the Kish grid incorporated into the computer-assisted interviewing system. Blood collection was conducted at the household level by trained phlebotomists using aseptic precautions and sterile, disposable needles and syringes. Approximately 2 mL of venous blood was collected into metal-free tubes and transported to the Immunobiology, Nutrition and Toxicology Laboratory at the International Centre for Diarrhoeal Disease Research, Bangladesh (icddr,b) in Dhaka for laboratory analysis. The biomarker component was implemented with technical support from the Institute of Epidemiology, Disease Control and Research (IEDCR) and icddr,b (BBS and UNICEF, 2026).

2.3. Blood lead exposure assessment

Blood lead concentration was determined in whole blood using inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS). Blood specimens were digested with concentrated nitric acid and analysed using calibration standards and internal standards. Blanks were used to assess background contamination and carryover, while three levels of ClinChek control materials were used for quality assurance of analytical accuracy and precision. Blood lead concentration was reported in μg/dL and was used as the biomarker of internal lead exposure (BBS and UNICEF, 2026). In the present study, blood lead concentration was analysed both continuously and categorically. Because of its right-skewed distribution, blood lead was natural-log transformed for continuous analyses. For categorical analyses, blood lead was classified as <3.5 μg/dL and ≥3.5 μg/dL, with <3.5 μg/dL as the reference category. The 3.5 μg/dL threshold corresponds to the CDC blood lead reference value for children (CDC, 2021).

2.4. Anthropometric and haemoglobin measurements

Anthropometric measurements were collected as part of the standard MICS under-five survey procedures. Children's weight and height/length were measured by trained field teams following standardized procedures. WAZ, HAZ, weight-for-height z-scores (WHZ), and body mass index-for-age z-scores (ZBMI) were derived according to the WHO Child Growth Standards (BBS and UNICEF, 2026). In the present study, WAZ, HAZ, WHZ, and ZBMI were included as continuous measures of child growth and nutritional status.

Haemoglobin was measured from whole blood using the cyanmethemoglobin method. Whole blood was mixed with Drabkin's solution for 30 minutes, followed by measurement of optical density at 540 nm using a UV-Vis spectrophotometer. Haemoglobin concentration was calculated using a three-point calibration curve prepared with cyanmethemoglobin standards, with commercial quality-control blood used to monitor measurement performance (WHO, 2011). In the present study, haemoglobin concentration (g/dL) was treated as a continuous outcome measure.

2.5. Covariates

Covariates were selected a priori based on previous literature (Ashley-Martin et al., 2019; Gardner et al., 2013; Gleason et al., 2016; Raihan et al., 2018; Zhou et al., 2020), together with the availability of relevant variables in the Bangladesh MICS 2025 dataset (BBS and UNICEF, 2026). The adjustment set included child-level characteristics, maternal characteristics, household socioeconomic and environmental characteristics, and potential lead-exposure-related factors. Child-level covariates included age (months), age group (12-23, 24-35, and 36-59 months) and sex (male/female). Maternal characteristics included maternal age (years) and educational attainment (pre-primary/no education, primary, lower secondary, secondary, higher secondary, and higher). Household socioeconomic status was measured using the MICS wealth index, a composite score derived by principal component analysis (PCA) from household ownership of durable assets, dwelling characteristics and access to basic services (Rutstein and Rojas, 2006). Households were ranked by their standardized wealth score and divided into five equal quintiles: poorest, poor, middle, rich and richest. Place of residence was categorized into urban and rural. Administrative division was included as an eight-category variable representing Bangladesh's eight divisions. Household environmental characteristics included the main source of drinking water and the type of sanitation facility, each classified as improved, unimproved, or other/unspecified following the WHO/UNICEF Joint Monitoring Programme (JMP) definitions used in MICS (WHO, 2025). Improved drinking-water sources included piped water, tubewells/boreholes, protected dug wells and springs, rainwater, packaged water, and delivered water, whereas unprotected wells and springs and surface water were classified as unimproved. Improved sanitation facilities included flush or pour-flush toilets connected to a piped sewer system, septic tank or pit latrine, ventilated improved pit latrines, pit latrines with slab, and composting toilets, while flush toilets discharging to an open drain, pit latrines without slab, bucket or hanging toilets, and open defecation were classified as unimproved. Other responses were grouped as other/unspecified. Housing characteristics included interior wall condition (no paint, paint in poor condition, or paint in good condition) and floor material (natural/rudimentary or finished). Household smoking was defined based on whether smoking was reported during the preceding three months. Two additional binary variables were constructed from the MICS lead-exposure module. Presence of a lead-related activity near the household was coded as "yes" if any of six activities reported in the survey: vehicle-battery manufacture, vehicle-battery recycling by melting, vehicle-battery repair or disassembly, jewelery manufacture or recycling, e-waste recycling, or automobile repair; otherwise, it was coded as "no". Similarly, lead-related occupational exposure among child caregiver was coded as "yes" if any of the same six activities was reported for such an individual during the preceding three months, and "no" otherwise (BBS and UNICEF, 2026).

2.6. Statistical analysis

Descriptive characteristics were summarized using means and standard deviations for continuous variables and survey-weighted percentages for categorical variables. BLLs w summarized using the geometric mean, median, and interquartile range because of their right-skewed distribution, and distributions were examined across sex and age groups. Spearman correlation coefficients among continuous predictors, BLL, and study outcomes conducted (Figure S3). The correlations were generally modest, supporting the inclusion of the predictors in multivariable models (Figure S3). Variance inflation factors (VIFs) were also examined to assess multicollinearity (VIF > 5) (Table S1).

Blood lead was primarily analyzed as a continuous exposure after natural-log transformation. Associations with HAZ, WAZ, WHZ, ZBMI, and haemoglobin concentration were estimated using survey-weighted multivariable linear regression, with β coefficients and 95% CIs reported. BLL was additionally categorized as <3.5 versus ≥3.5 µg/dL, with <3.5 µg/dL as the reference group. The 3.5 µg/dL cut-off was based on the CDC BLL reference value and was used as an analytical threshold rather than a level indicating absence of harm. Associations were subsequently examined separately by sex and age group to assess whether the relationship between BLL and the outcomes differed across developmental stages and between boys and girls. Age was categorized into three groups: 12-23, 24-35, and 36-59 months. Sex-specific models were fitted separately for boys and girls, while age-specific models were fitted separately within each age group. Additional analyses examined associations within combined age-by-sex groups to describe patterns across developmental stages and sex. Variables corresponding to the stratification factor were omitted from the corresponding stratified models; for example, sex was not included in sex-specific models and age was not included as a continuous covariate in age-specific models. Sensitivity analyses assessed robustness to alternative exposure specifications by modeling BLL on the log scale, with effect estimates interpreted per doubling of BLL. Potential nonlinear exposure-response relationships were evaluated using restricted cubic spline models (knot = 4), with non-linearity assessed using Wald tests of the spline terms. These analyses were considered sensitivity analyses to assess whether the primary linear exposure specification adequately represented the observed associations.

All models incorporated sampling weights, strata, and primary sampling units using the survey package in R (version 4.3.1) to account for the complex sampling design of the Bangladesh MICS 2025. Models were adjusted for child sex, child age as a continuous variable (months), maternal age as a continuous variable (years), maternal education, household wealth, residence, administrative division, drinking-water source, sanitation, interior wall condition, floor type, household smoking, nearby lead-related activity, and lead-related occupational exposure among child caregiver. Sex-and age-specific models were additionally fitted for boys and girls and for children aged 12-23, 24-35, and 36-59 months; the corresponding stratification variable was omitted from each stratified model. All tests were two-sided with α = 0.05, and effect estimates were reported with 95% CIs.

3.5. Non-linear associations

Restricted cubic spline analyses showed that the associations between BLL and HAZ, WAZ, WHZ, and ZBMI were broadly consistent with linearity in the overall sample, with no evidence of non-linearity for HAZ (P for non-linearity = 0.317), WAZ (P = 0.669), WHZ (P = 0.396), or ZBMI (P = 0.819). In contrast, the association between BLL and haemoglobin showed evidence of non-linearity in the overall sample (P = 0.006) (Figure 4). In sex-specific analyses, no evidence of non-linearity was observed for the growth indicators among either boys or girls, whereas haemoglobin showed evidence of non-linearity among girls (P = 0.047), but not boys (P = 0.152) (Figure S4). Age-specific analyses showed evidence of non-linearity for haemoglobin among children aged 12-23 months (P = 0.005), WHZ among those aged 36-59 months (P = 0.014), and ZBMI among those aged 36-59 months (P = 0.007); the remaining agespecific associations showed no evidence of departure from linearity (Figure S5).

4. Discussion

>Discussion

4.1. Summary of the main findings

This nationally representative study demonstrated a substantial burden of elevated BLLs among Bangladeshi children aged 12-59 months, with nearly two-thirds (66.26%) exceeding the U.S. CDC BLL reference value of 3.5 µg/dL (CDC, 2021). Against this background, higher BLLs were consistently associated with poorer physical growth, particularly lower HAZ, WAZ, WHZ. The overall pattern was broadly consistent across boys and girls for HAZ and WAZ, although some outcome-specific differences were apparent. Age-specific analyses further showed that the associations with HAZ and WAZ were more evident among children aged 24-35 and 36-59 months, whereas the association with haemoglobin was concentrated among children aged 12-23 months, particularly boys. These findings extended previous evidence on childhood lead exposure by examining multiple dimensions of growth within a nationally representative population and by considering whether the observed associations vary across developmental stages and between boys and girls.

4.2. Blood lead levels and child physical growth

Our study found higher BLL to be associated with lower HAZ, suggesting that children with greater lead exposure tended to have poorer linear growth. Consistent with our findings, a study of young Chinese children reported significantly lower HAZ associated with increasing BLLs (Zhou et al., 2020). Similarly, a study of rural Bangladeshi children aged approximately 20-40 months found increased odds of stunting associated with BLL (Gleason et al., 2016). Another study among Bangladeshi slum children reported that elevated BLL was associated with stunting (Raihan et al., 2018). While other studies observed no significant association between BLLs and HAZ (Ahmadi et al., 2022; Taylor et al., 2026).

The association with WAZ showed a similar pattern in our study. Elevated BLL was associated with lower WAZ, indicating that the relationship was not restricted to linear stature but also extended to overall weight relative to age. Previous findings on BLL and childhood weight, however, have been inconsistent.

Similarly, Raihan et al. (2018) reported an inverse association between higher BLL and WAZ among Bangladeshi slum children. However, other studies including NHANES III and Canadian MIREC cohort, reported no significant association between BLL and body weight (Ashley-Martin et al., 2019; Ballew et al., 1999).

The association with WHZ was also inverse, although weaker and less consistent than those observed for HAZ and WAZ. The weaker association observed for WHZ may therefore reflect the different growth dimension captured by this indicator, which evaluates weight relative to attained height rather than weight or height relative to age. This interpretation is compatible with findings from the Bangladeshi study of young children, which reported an association between elevated BLL and stunting but did not find a significant association with wasting. This study suggested that chronic lead exposure might be more closely related to chronic growth impairment than to the more acute manifestation represented by wasting (Raihan et al., 2018).

These findings might be partly explained by the persistent and heterogeneous nature of lead exposure among Bangladeshi children, whereby repeated exposure may affect growth even at the relatively lower BLLs. In our study, the geometric mean BLL was 4.43 µg/dL, considerably lower than levels reported in earlier Bangladeshi studies (Gleason et al., 2016; Mitra et al., 2012), yet higher BLL was associated with lower HAZ, WAZ and WHZ. Such associations at comparatively lower exposure concentrations suggest that growth-related effects may not be restricted to children with markedly elevated BLLs. Young children have tendency to chew or swallow non-food materials may further increase lead ingestion through contaminated soil, dust, and household materials (De la Burdé and Reames, 1973). Pica, reported in approximately 5.5% of children in rural Bangladesh (Khan et al., 2009), might be major potential exposure pathway. In Bangladesh, other documented sources of childhood lead exposure include industrial contamination, indigenous medicines, paints, and contaminated household materials (Boman et al., 2005; Mitra et al., 2012 Mitra et al., , 2009)).

We found no association between BLL and ZBMI. This finding differs from some previous evidence. Scinicariello et al. (2013), using NHANES data, reported lower BMI associated with higher BLLs, while other studies observed positives (Wang et al., 2015) or null associations between BLL and BMI (Ashley-Martin et al., 2019). The absence of an association with ZBMI in our study should therefore not be interpreted as evidence that BLL is unrelated to growth. Rather, it may reflect the different construct captured by BMI-for-age.

Contrasting with prior studies (Liu et al., 2012; Mitra et al., 2012), our study did not observe association with haemoglobin (Hb). The absence of an overall association in our study may therefore reflect the multifactorial determinants of haemoglobin and the relatively lower exposure distribution. Importantly, however, our spline analysis suggested a non-linear relationship between BLL and Hb, suggesting that the linear estimate may not fully capture the exposure-response pattern. This finding is consistent with the broader toxicological literature showing that dose-response relationships may be non-linear at relatively low exposure levels, although evidence from other outcomes cannot be directly extrapolated to Hb (Lanphear et al., 2005). Therefore, this finding warrants further investigation of non-linearity in the hematological effects of low BLL.

4.3. Sex-specific patterns in growth indicators and haemoglobin

Our findings suggest that BLLs and child growth associations were not consistent between boys and girls. Boys had significantly higher BLLs than girls. Despite this exposure differences, elevated BLL was associated with lower HAZ and WAZ in both sexes, whereas the association with WHZ was evident only among boys. These findings are partly consistent with (Zhou et al., 2020), who reported inverse associations of BLL with HAZ and WAZ among young children, with associations more evident among boys. In contrast, the Canadian MIREC cohort reported evidence of sex heterogeneity for the association between BLL and BMI, with higher BLL tending to be associated with lower BMI among girls (Ashley-Martin et al., 2019). The biological basis for the observed sex-specific pattern remains uncertain, but several pathways may plausibly contribute. An earlier review suggested that lead-related toxicity may differ by sex, with some evidence of greater susceptibility among boys (Llop et al., 2013 ). Vahter et al. (2007) also reported sex-related differences in the disposition and biological effects of toxic metals, and noted that boys may be more susceptible to some developmental effects of BLL. Experimental evidence suggests that male offspring may be more vulnerable to lead-related growth impairment, with developmental lead exposure producing greater suppression of somatic growth in male than female rats (Ronis et al., 1998). Lead may also interfere with endocrine pathways involved in growth, and epidemiological studies have reported sex-specific associations between BLL and thyroid-related measures, including altered thyroid hormone profiles among males (Luo and Hendryx, 2014; Meeker et al., 2009). Although these studies were conducted primarily in adults, these findings suggest that sexdependent endocrine responses may contribute to differences in growth-related associations.

4.4. Differences across developmental stages

Our age-specific findings suggest that the relationship between BLL and child physical growth may vary across developmental stages. The inverse associations with HAZ and WAZ were observed among children aged 24-35 months, and this pattern was mainly observed among boys in this age group. Among girls, the associations were more evident in the 36-59-month group. Previous findings on lead-related growth associations is inconsistent. In the Cincinnati Lead Study, higher BLL during the second and third years of life were associated with lower attained height at 33 months (Shukla et al., 1991). Evidence from Bangladesh is also mixed, with Igra et al. ( 2021) reporting inverse associations of urinary lead with HAZ and WAZ among boys but not girls at 10 years, whereas an earlier longitudinal study with same population found no significant associations between lead exposure and height or weight through age 5 (Gardner et al., 2013).

For haemoglobin, our study found an inverse association with BLL among 12-23 months aged children and this was more apparent among boys in that age group. Evidence specifically examining agedependent associations betweenand haemoglobin in children is limited. Previous studies have generally examined broader childhood age ranges and have reported inconsistent findings. Mitra et al. (2012) reported an inverse correlation between BLL and haemoglobin among Bangladeshi children younger than 16 years, but did not identify a specific early-childhood age window. Similarly, an Ecuadorian study of children with chronic lead exposure found an overall inverse association between BLL and haemoglobin but no significant age-by-sex differences for haemoglobin (Counter et al., 2000).

The biological basis for the age-specific finding in our study is unclear, but may partly relate to greater gastrointestinal absorption of lead and frequent hand-to-mouth behaviour in young children (CDC, 2025).

Children in this age have high physiological iron requirements during the transition to complementary feeding, when iron stores may be vulnerable to depletion (Osendarp et al., 2016). These factors could contribute to the variation observed across age groups, but differences in cumulative exposure, nutritional status, and developmental susceptibility cannot be separated in our cross-sectional study.

4.5. Potential mechanisms

Several biological pathways may explain the associations observed between BLL and child growth. Lead may interfere with skeletal growth by affecting bone formation, mineralization, and osteoblast activity.

Experimental studies have reported reduced longitudinal bone growth and bone strength following developmental lead exposure (Ronis et al., 2001), while epidemiological evidence has linked higher BLLs with reduced linear skeletal growth (Ignasiak et al., 2006; Li et al., 2022). Lead may also affect the growth hormone (GH)-insulin-like growth factor-1 (IGF-1) axis. In children, higher BLLs have been associated with lower circulating IGF-1 (Fleisch et al., 2013), and experimental studies suggest that lead can interfere with IGF-1 signalling and skeletal growth (Xu et al., 2014). These pathways provide biological support for the inverse associations with HAZ and WAZ observed in our study. Lead can interfere with haem synthesis, while iron deficiency may increase lead absorption, potentially increasing the susceptibility of young children to haematological effects of lead (Capitão et al., 2022; Tempowski, 2021). Lead inhibits key enzymes in the haem biosynthetic pathway, particularly δ-aminolevulinic acid dehydratase and ferrochelatase, thereby impairing haem production (Jangid et al., 2012; Piomelli et al., 1982). Evidence from children has demonstrated inhibition of ALAD with increasing BLLs (Roels et al., 1976), while other evidence supports the relationship between lead exposure and haematological outcomes in children (Rawat et al., 2021). Iron status may further influence this pathway because iron deficiency has been associated with higher BLLs in children (Farkhondeh et al., 2022).

4.6 Strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths. It used a large, nationally representative sample with objectively measured BLL, haemoglobin, and anthropometric outcomes. The detailed survey design was incorporated into all regression analyses, and the associations were examined using both continuous and categorical measures of BLL. We also assessed patterns by sex, age, and age-by-sex groups and evaluated potential non-linear associations. However, several limitations should be considered. The cross-sectional design prevents assessment of temporality and causal relationships and does not exclude residual confounding. Important factors such as detailed dietary intake and micronutrient status were not fully available in the dataset. BLL was measured at a single time point, so we could not assess the duration, timing, or cumulative burden of exposure. The anthropometric measures also reflect children's size at the time of assessment rather than changes in growth over time, while a single haemoglobin measurement cannot establish the underlying cause of low haemoglobin.

5. Conclusions

In this nationally representative samples, higher BLLs were associated with lower HAZ, WAZ, and WHZ among Bangladeshi children aged 12-59 months. The sex-specific analyses showed inverse associations of BLL with HAZ and WAZ in both boys and girls, while the association with WHZ was observed only among boys. The age-specific analyses showed associations with HAZ and WAZ among children aged 24-35 and 36-59 months, whereas the association with haemoglobin was observed in 12-23-month group. Further stratification by age within sex showed a more specific pattern, with inverse associations of BLL with HAZ and WAZ among boys aged 24-35 months and girls aged 36-59 months, and with haemoglobin among boys aged 12-23 months. Overall, these findings suggest that the relationship of BLL with child growth and haemoglobin varies across sex and developmental stage, with the most consistent associations observed for linear growth indicators, particularly HAZ and WAZ. Longitudinal studies with repeated measures of BLL, growth, haemoglobin, and nutritional status are needed to determine whether these age-and sex-specific patterns reflect differences in exposure timing, cumulative exposure, or developmental susceptibility.
