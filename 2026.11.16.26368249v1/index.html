Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). Statcheck might still miss one-sided tests that are described in another sentence, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. Some differences come from the ground truth: it lacks p-values with a correction name (“p Bonf = .061”), and counts partial eta squared written with its subscript after it (“η 2 p = .05”), “p = .05”, and some table notes and figure legends (“† p < .10, *p < .05”), which this module leaves out, as non-significant p-values. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. Full text

Abstract

Stunting among children under 2 years old remains a major public health concern in Rwanda despite the efforts to reduce its prevalence. Community-based nutrition interventions, including food supplementation, growth monitoring, screening and referral, are widely delivered, yet high coverage has not consistently translated into lower stunting prevalence. This study explores caregivers, community health workers (CHWs), and health service managers experiences' and understanding of these interventions.

A qualitative descriptive study was conducted in the areas of Bugeshi (Rubavu district) and Rugarama (Nyarugenge district) health centres, purposively selected for their contrasting district stunting trajectories. We conducted six focus group discussions with caregivers and CHWs (48 participants) and 21 in-depth interviews with facility-based nutritionists and environmental health officers, local health service managers, and national, international and private-sector programme managers and experts. Data analysis using reflexive thematic analysis showed seven emerging themes, including competing explanations for persistent stunting; experiences of receiving and using food supplements; the emotional and social dimensions of malnutrition; the central but under-resourced, and at times unsafe, role of CHWs; difficulties in reaching eligible children; governance and financial sustainability; and supply chain constraints. All groups valued food supplementation and shared an understanding of stunting. Caregivers and frontline CHWs more often described problems at the point of delivery such as eligible children being missed, supplements being shared with siblings and other household members or being unavailable, and CHWs facing hostility during periods of stock-outs. Managers and programme experts emphasised financing, procurement and distribution as essential pillar for intervention success.

Community-based nutrition interventions were widely valued, but participants described a substantial gap between programme design and day-to-day implementation. Strengthening implementation will require attention to the working conditions of CHWs, household economic realities, programme eligibility criteria, supply and financing systems, and routine use of caregiver and CHWs feedback in programme monitoring.

Introduction

Childhood stunting is a chronic form of undernutrition that typically originates in the first 1,000 days from conception to a child's second birthday. Stunting is associated with impaired cognitive development, reduced adult productivity and greater susceptibility to disease, and is widely regarded as a marker of a population's human capital [1].

Rwanda has substantially reduced childhood stunting through sustained investment under successive national development strategies [2] [3] [4]. The Second National Strategy for Transformation (NST2, 2024-2029) sets a national target of 15% by 2029 [5].

In Rwanda, among children under 5 years stunting prevalence fell from 51% in 2005 to 33% in 2019-20 [6] and to 27% in 2025 [7]. These gains nevertheless remain uneven. In the 2025 survey, prevalence ranged from less than 10% in Nyarugenge district in Kigali to more than 30% in 11 districts, most of them located in the Western Province [7]. Routine screening of children aged 2 years between 202-2024 showed a similar pattern with a national prevalence in this age group falling from 33% to 22%, but district trends diverged markedly, and the smallest relative reductions were recorded in districts of the Western Province [8], highlighting the need for understanding community and stakeholder perspectives to explain these differences.

Rwanda's response although embedded in national nutrition and health sector policies it combines child-focused nutrition services with community-based delivery [9] [10] [11]. Micronutrient powders have been distributed to children aged 6-23 months under the "Ongera" programme since 2012 [12]. Since 2017, locally produced fortified blended foods, such as "Shisha Kibondo" and "SOSOMA" (a sorghum-soy-maize flour), have been distributed monthly through health centres, alongside advocacy and counselling on their preparation and on infant and young child feeding [12, 13]. The eligibility for fortified blended food and other social protection services has historically been combined the household's socio-economic category under the national Ubudehe classification, a community-based poverty-ranking system introduction. Ubudehe originally comprised six categories, which were consolidated into four numbered categories in 2015 and revised in 2020 into five lettered categories (A-E), ranging from the highest-income households (A) to the most vulnerable (E) [14, 15]. Owing to inclusion and exclusion errors in beneficiary selection, the government discontinued the use of Ubudehe categories for targeting in 2023. In February 2024, it was replaced by the Imibereho Social Registry, which classifies households primarily on the basis of income, supplemented by asset data such as land and vehicle ownership, drawn from linked administrative databases [14, 16]. Individual programmes apply their own thresholds to registry data. For community-based health insurance, for example, households are assigned to one of five income-based contribution categories, ranging from Category I (no income, fully subsidised by the government) to Category V (monthly income above 120,000 Rwandan francs) [17] [18] [19].

Implementation of stunting reduction interventions relay mostly on Community Health Workers

(CHWs) who carry out routine growth monitoring, home visits and household counselling, run cooking demonstrations in village nutrition kitchens, and refer children with growth faltering or signs of malnutrition to the health centre. At the health centre, nutritionists or nurses assess the child and, where eligibility criteria are met, enrol the child for food supplementation or for treatment of acute malnutrition. Data from these services are recorded in health centre registers and in an electronic individual tracking system (e-Tracker) [20].

Nutritional status is also assessed in mass screening of children by CHWs during the twice-yearly national Maternal and Child Health Weeks and coordinated from village to district level [8, 9, 11].

Several donor-funded projects supported the implementation of stunting prevention interventions

such as the USAID-funded Gikuriro programme (2015-2020) which combined village nutrition schools with savings groups and water, sanitation and hygiene promotion, and trained volunteer "lead mothers" to lead peer learning and cooking demonstrations for other mothers in their village [21]. Also, the World Bank-supported Stunting Prevention and Reduction Project, approved in 2018, provided additional financing for nutrition services [22, 23]. Although the coverage of these services was high, in a national survey in 2023, despite 94% of children aged 6-23 months received food supplements, yet 1 in 5 children aged 2 years were still stunted in 2024 [8]. The coexistence of near-universal supplementation coverage and persistently high stunting prevalence suggests that coverage alone does not reflect the quality, continuity or contextual appropriateness of interventions, and highlights the need to understand how programmes are implemented and experienced at local level. Implementation depends not only on the availability of services but also on how they are delivered, accessed, understood and accepted. However, little is known, in Rwanda or in comparable programmes in the region about how these interventions are experienced and enacted by the different actors involved [24] [25] [26]. Therefore, this study is aimed at understanding the perspectives of stakeholders, workers delivering nutrition services, in particularly CHWs, facility-based nutritionists, and health service managers, together with international and private-sector programme managers to explore the barriers of interventions' effectiveness and their implications in programme implementation.

Materials and methods

Design

This qualitative descriptive study combined focus group discussions (FGDs) and in-depth interviews (IDIs), analysed using inductive reflexive thematic analysis [27] supported by elements of the Framework Method [28]. The study is reported in accordance with the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) [29] (S1 Checklist).

Study setting and site selection

Site selection was informed by a previous analysis of routine national screening data for children aged 2 years [8]. In the period 2020 to 2024, stunting prevalence in Rubavu district, which includes Bugeshi sector, fell from 41% to 35%. This decrease of 6 percentage points corresponds to a relative reduction of 14.6%, one of the 2 smallest relative reductions among Rwanda's 30 districts.

In contrast, prevalence in Nyarugenge district, which includes Rugarama sector, fell from 23% to 6%, a decrease of 17 percentage points and a relative reduction of 73.9%, one of the largest reductions nation-wide [8]. Therefore, those 2 settings were purposively selected as they provide contrasting trends rather than on baseline prevalence alone. Bugeshi is predominantly rural, agrarian and water-scarce area bordering the Democratic Republic of the Congo (DRC), while Rugarama is a mixed urban and peri-urban setting.

Participants and sampling

Participants were purposively sampled from 3 stakeholder groups. Group 1 comprised caregivers of children under 2 years who were using or had used community-based nutrition services. Group 2 comprised health workers delivering nutrition services, including CHWs, facility and district hospital nutritionists, and Community Environmental Health Officers (CEHOs). Group 3 comprised health service managers and programme experts involved in overseeing, coordinating or supporting nutrition interventions at local and national levels. Those at local level included health centre managers, Early Childhood Development (ECD) coordinators and sector and district officials. Those at national level included representatives of government agencies, United Nations agencies, an international nutrition organisation and a private-sector food supplement manufacturer, and 2 independents public health consultants.

Caregivers and CHWs perspectives were explored through FGDs, which suited the exploration of shared community experiences. At each site, 2 sex-specific caregiver FGDs (1 female, 1 male) were held. In addition, 1 FGD was organised with CHWs at each site and included both female and male CHWs. Data collection for other health workers and for local and national managers and experts was done through IDIs, with a total of 21 interviews conducted. IDI participants were sampled across facility, sector, district and national levels until additional interviews yielded no substantially new issues [30]. Caregivers and CHWs were recruited through health centre registers, and other participants through institutional contacts. All those approached participants agreed to participate and provided informed consent.

Data collection

Data were collected between May and July 2026 by trained interviewers using semi-structured topic guides (S1 Text) covering perceptions of stunting and its causes, experiences of food supplementation and nutrition services, community engagement, programme monitoring and sustainability, and equity and access. Guides were adapted to each respondent group around a common core of questions to allow comparison across stakeholder groups.

FGDs were conducted in Kinyarwanda in a private room at the health centre and lasted approximately 1 hour. Health centre staff did not take part in caregiver FGDs. Each FGD was facilitated by a moderator and a note-taker. IDIs with facility, sector and district staff took place in the 2 study districts, and IDIs with national, international and private-sector participants took place in Kigali. Sessions were audio-recorded with participants' consent.

Data analysis

Recordings were transcribed verbatim, translated into English, quality-checked and analysed in ATLAS.ti version 24. The analysis combined the stages of the Framework Method (familiarisation, coding, framework development, indexing, charting and interpretation) with Braun and Clarke's iterative, reflexive approach to theme development [27, 28]. Codes and themes were developed inductively, without a pre-existing codebook or theoretical framework.

In Bugeshi, participants described mothers spending long hours in small-scale cross-border trade with the DRC; in Rugarama, they described children left in the care of hired domestic workers. Household alcohol use and domestic conflict were also described as pathways to child undernutrition. Water scarcity and cultural feeding taboos were also mentioned, and helminth infections were raised as a concern in Bugeshi.

Caregivers in Rugarama further described children presumed to be malnourished if were found to have an underlying condition, such as tuberculosis or cleft palate, or whose mother had breast cancer. These accounts challenge a purely behavioural reading of growth faltering and show the value of clinical assessment for children identified through growth monitoring.

Experiences of receiving and using food supplements

Food supplementation was valued in all stakeholder groups and described as beneficial, and in some cases clinically effective, when used as intended. Caregivers described their children's recovery, and health workers and managers described demand for supplements from families whose children did not meet the eligibility criteria. These benefits were repeatedly interrupted by stock-outs, and rigid distribution schedules could delay access.

Participants in all groups described families sharing supplements intended for the eligible child with siblings and other household members, and occasionally selling them, and mostly linked this to household food insecurity rather than to a lack of understanding.

Some health workers reported that caregivers had stopped breastfeeding early, or avoided family planning, to keep a child eligible for supplementation. Food support ends when the child reaches the upper age limit or when the household is reclassified into a socio-economic category that is no longer eligible. Health workers reported that the end of support at the age limit was increasingly preceded by preparation of the caregiver, whereas the end of support following reclassification of the household could be abrupt. Caregivers, particularly women, described children relapsing after food support ended when household difficulties had not been resolved. Female caregivers also described the opportunity costs of attending services, such as lost working time.

The social dimensions of malnutrition: stigma, shame and solidarity

Caregivers described a diagnosis of stunting or malnutrition as emotionally significant, often involving embarrassment and fear of judgement. Health workers at both sites described CHWs collecting supplements for families who did not want to be seen receiving them and delivering them privately.

Health workers and managers independently described some wealthier households denying that their child was malnourished or avoiding services, including caregivers who rejected a diagnosis or used services "secretly". Caregivers identified gossip as a deterrent. Several participants who described these behaviours also stated that "there is no stigma" in their community. This suggests that stigma was understood narrowly as open discrimination, and that shame, concealment and avoidance were not recognised as forms of stigma.

Caregivers also described mutual support, including neighbours donating milk, savings-based cooperative schemes and families whose children had recovered serving as examples. In contrast, male caregivers in Bugeshi described resentment among some households that did not receive supplements, including reports of parents giving their own children less food. Although not widespread, these accounts point to a little-examined risk of means-tested nutrition programmes.

Community Health Workers: central actors under pressure

All 3 stakeholder groups described CHWs as the most trusted and central actors in the nutrition system, a centrality that came with a heavy workload. CHWs reported chronic shortages of anthropometric equipment, such as height boards, mid-upper arm circumference tapes and weighing scales, and continued reliance on unpaid volunteer labour despite Rwanda's performance-based financing scheme for CHWs.

CHWs in Bugeshi described threats to their personal safety. CHWs identify children at risk and mobilise caregivers to attend the health centre or village nutrition kitchen; when caregivers found no commodities available, their frustration was directed at the CHWs, the most visible local representative of the programme. CHWs described being insulted, threatened and refused entry to homes. Supply disruptions thus affected not only service availability but also the safety and working relationships of frontline workers.

Caregivers in Rugarama described CHWs deciding not to refer to the health centre children whom caregivers believed needed assessment, effectively acting as gatekeepers to services. Caregivers also alleged that some CHWs and local leaders under-reported cases of malnutrition to protect their reputation. Frontline discretion thus shaped who was identified, referred and reached.

Difficulties in reaching eligible children

Equity concerns related mainly to horizontal equity, that is, whether all children meeting the eligibility criteria were identified and served. Managers and programme experts generally expressed confidence that services reached all eligible children. This confidence was less evident among health workers and female caregivers, who were closer to day-to-day delivery. Female caregivers perceived favouritism in which children were referred and enrolled and described poor households in urban neighbourhoods being missed. In Bugeshi, when stock was insufficient for all enrolled children, households closer to the health centre were more likely to receive supplements than those farther away. Stock-outs thus translated directly into unequal access among children with the same need.

The clearest equity concern emerged in Rugarama, where health workers and caregivers independently described poor households being overlooked in wealthier urban neighbourhoods.

Outreach concentrated in visibly poor villages could miss poor households in or near wealthier neighbourhoods, and gated compounds and households employing domestic workers were described as barriers to identifying eligible children.

In Bugeshi, managers and health workers described substantial unmet need, and visible frustration, among historically marginalised communities. Inequity can therefore arise not only from formal eligibility criteria but also from how vulnerability is recognised and translated into outreach.

National and international participants described targeting based on formal vulnerability criteria, but none mentioned the exclusion of eligible children at the point of delivery described by health workers and caregivers, suggesting a gap between targeting design and its visibility in practice at national level.

At both sites, female caregiver FGDs gave more detailed and critical accounts of programme barriers, whereas male FGDs more often framed household decisions around the father's role as provider and judged the distribution of services as fairer.

Governance, monitoring and financial sustainability

Participants in all groups were concerned about dependence on external funding. They attributed the end of 2 valued donor-supported initiatives to reduced funding. National and international participants described unpredictable, short or poorly aligned funding cycles as a constraint on multi-year planning. Rather than describing domestic resources as already compensating for the loss of donor funding, they presented greater domestic financing and local production as needs for the future.

Supply chain constraints and stock-outs

Health workers and managers in both districts described recurrent, sometimes unexplained stockouts lasting several weeks. Caregivers returned home without supplies, CHWs faced hostility and limited stock was rationed. National and international participants described the upstream causes to be mostly related to procurement delays, bottlenecks in the national supply chain and constraints in last-mile distribution to health centres. Frontline respondents thus experienced the consequences of stock-outs that national participants explained, and both groups saw supply reliability as a main constraint on programme effectiveness.

Discussion

This study compared how caregivers, health workers and health service managers experience community-based nutrition interventions in 2 contrasting settings in Rwanda. Three findings stand out. First, supplementation was universally valued, but its effect was diluted by household food insecurity and by families' responses to eligibility and withdrawal rules. Second, CHWs held the system together while absorbing many of its failures, including hostility when commodities were unavailable. Third, the supply and financing constraints described by national participants explained much of what frontline respondents experienced.

Supplementation in food-insecure households

Sharing of child-targeted supplements is well documented. In southern Ethiopia, caregivers treated therapeutic food both as medicine and as a household resource that could be shared or sold [33].

In Niger, researchers concluded that consumption by the target child alone is unrealistic where other household members are also hungry [34], and a recent Rwandan study reported the same practice for Shisha Kibondo [12]. This further explains the study participants experience in sharing food supplements mainly as a response to food insecurity.

Our findings add evidence of behavioural responses to the programme's eligibility and exit rules, including early cessation of breastfeeding, avoidance of family planning and relapse after food support ended. In Ethiopia, some caregivers sought repeated admissions to prolong access to therapeutic food [33]. Because eligibility depends on the child's age and nutritional status and on the household's socio-economic category, a child-targeted benefit that becomes an important household resource creates incentives that can work against programme aims. Combining supplementation with household-level nutrition-sensitive support, such as social protection or livelihood interventions [35], and basing the end of food support on verified household circumstances, with a planned transition rather than an abrupt exit, could reduce these effects.

The competing explanations of stunting point in the same direction. Some frontline workers attributed persistent stunting to caregiver knowledge, whereas others, and national participants, emphasised land, livelihoods, flooding and time. Women's workload is a recurring reason why agricultural and income gains fail to improve child nutrition [36], and women's time constraints were the most frequently reported barriers to child health care as seen in northern Kenya [37].

Also, caregivers in South Africa linked malnutrition to poverty [25]. That income from crossborder trade or domestic work did not translate into caregiving time suggests that counselling alone is insufficient and that childcare arrangements deserve more attention in nutrition strategies.

Community Health Workers as backbone and shock absorber

The centrality of CHWs, and the pressures on them, echo earlier Rwandan evidence of respected and motivated CHWs facing an overwhelming workload [38]; our findings suggest that this workload persists, together with shortages of basic anthropometric equipment. Remuneration, supervision and a reliable supply of commodities are key determinants of CHW performance [39, 40], and the World Health Organization recommends that CHWs be remunerated in line with their workload [41]. Low pay and high turnover is common [40, 42, 43], however, were not prominent in our data, may reflect Rwanda's cooperative and performance-based model [38] or CHWs' reluctance to express dissatisfaction in discussions held near health facilities.

The safety concerns raised by CHWs in Bugeshi add a less-documented dimension, as regional research has mostly framed CHWs challenges in terms of workload and resources [26]. Because the delivery model links a CHW's credibility to commodity availability, frustration falls on the CHWs when caregivers are mobilised to a facility that has nothing to give. Supply reliability is therefore a matter of workforce protection as well as service quality.

Accounts of CHWs declining referrals and under-reporting illustrate frontline discretion. Lipsky's concept of street-level bureaucracy describes how frontline workers facing high demand and limited resources develop their own routines for rationing services [44], and such discretion has been shown to shape access to care in African health systems [45]. In Rwanda, performance targets may add a reputational motive: community members elsewhere perceived some programmes as implemented mainly to meet local leaders' performance contract ("imihigo") targets [12].

Discretion also allowed CHWs to deliver supplements privately to families who feared stigma, but it means that routine coverage indicators cannot show who was excluded at the frontline. Because declined referrals, perceived favouritism and under-reporting were reported by caregivers but are invisible in routine indicators, caregiver feedback is one of the few ways to detect them.

Who is reached and who is missed

Eligibility for food supplementation rests on the child's age and nutritional status and on the household's socio-economic category. Through a horizontal equity lens, the gaps described were therefore mostly exclusion of children who met these criteria: rationing by distance during shortages, discretionary referral and enrolment, and poor households hidden in wealthier neighbourhoods [31, 32]. Such exclusion bias is common in targeted programmes that rely on visible markers of poverty or on local administrators to identify beneficiaries [46], and misclassification and favouritism in beneficiary selection have been reported elsewhere in Rwanda [12]. Also, the exclusion of poor households in wealthier neighbourhoods in Rugarama is consistent with evidence that socio-economic inequalities in child stunting are larger within urban than within rural areas [47]. Therefore, screening of all children in a particular village, regardless of apparent wealth, can be a practical response.

Stigma may add a further barrier. In northern Kenya, caregivers of acutely malnourished children were considerably more likely than others to report shame as a barrier to care [37]. Fear of judgement also been described among caregivers of vulnerable infants in Malawi [24]. In this study, the participants described shame and concealment while denying that stigma existed fits a broader understanding of stigma as labelling and loss of status, not only overt discrimination [48].

Programmes that assess stigma only by asking about discrimination may therefore underestimate it.

Female caregivers gave more critical accounts of programme barriers, consistent with Rwandan evidence that household food allocation and spending decisions are gender biased [49]. Building on Rwanda's gender-transformative programmes delivered by CHWs [50], community feedback on nutrition programmes should include separate spaces for women, so that the barriers they face are not underrepresented in mixed or male-dominated forums.

Study strength and limitations

A strength of this study is its comparison of accounts of the same interventions across 3 stakeholder groups, from caregivers to national programme managers, and in sites selected on trends quantified in an analysis of national surveillance data, so that the findings can be interpreted in relation to documented differences in outcomes. However, several limitations should be considered.

Fieldwork was carried out in 1 health centre catchment area in each of the 2 districts, so the findings are not representative of all Rwanda. Although they may be transferable to similar settings; national interviews broadened the system perspective but do not substitute for additional sites. With 1 FGD per stakeholder group, sex and site, some issues may not have reached saturation within the FGD data, and comparisons between female and male caregivers are exploratory. Social desirability may have influenced discussions held in or near health facilities, although the substantial disagreement across groups and sites suggests that accounts were not uniformly positive.

Conclusions

Community-based nutrition programmes in Bugeshi and Rugarama were broadly valued, and their rationale was well understood by caregivers, health workers and managers alike. Beneath this shared narrative, however, participants described an uneven implementation reality, supply disruptions felt most directly by CHWs, household food insecurity undermining the child-specific use of supplements, eligible children missed in both the rural and the urban setting, and frontline discretion that could both ease and obstruct access.

The findings from this study offer plausible, but not causal, explanations for the divergent stunting trajectories in Rubavu and Nyarugenge. Livelihoods, caregiving time, water and sanitation, urban inequality, supply reliability and frontline responsiveness may all shape how national policies translate into community-level outcomes, which are difficult to capture through routine surveillance alone.

Sustained progress will therefore require more than maintaining commodity coverage. It will require support for the CHWs who deliver nutrition services, attention to the household economic and caregiving conditions that shape their use, eligibility and withdrawal rules that reflect household circumstances, reliable supply and financing, and routine use of caregiver, health worker and manager perspectives to detect gaps between reported performance and lived experience.

Supply chains and financing

Stock-outs linked many of the problems described by frontline respondents. The reported coverage falls and recovering with stock availability suggests that coverage may partly reflect the availability of supplies rather than changes in children's needs or in programme performance.

Coverage assessments in 21 countries found that most community-based malnutrition treatment programmes did not reach recommended coverage, underlining the importance of measuring who is actually reached rather than relying on administrative figures [51]. However, local production of food supplements can shorten lead times and support local economies [52].

Dependence on time-limited donor funding was nuanced at community level through the loss of valued activities [12]. National participants proposed more predictable, multi-year and increasingly domestic financing. This need is becoming more pressing: official development assistance from members of the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development fell by an estimated 23% in real terms between 2024 and 2025, with the largest reduction in assistance from the United States [53]. Sustaining community-based nutrition services in this environment will depend on the extent to which domestic resources can replace declining external funding.

Alongside the analysis of routine screening data that informed site selection [8], the study findings suggest several hypotheses for the contrasting trajectories in Rubavu and Nyarugenge.
