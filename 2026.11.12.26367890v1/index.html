How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 225 papers containing 405 instances of non-exact p-values, the module correctly detected 269 cases (true positives) and incorrectly identified 78 (false positives). It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Background. Rule-derived (weak) labels can enlarge a training set when reported labels are scarce. Their value depends on label quality, on how the extra records shift the training distribution, and on whether the comparison accounts for reported labels set aside for tuning or calibration.

We ran an exploratory label-budget experiment on Escherichia coli urine cultures from two de-identified electronic health record datasets: Stanford ARMD and UT Southwestern ARMD-UTSW. The task was to predict reported non-susceptibility to cefepime, cefoxitin and ceftazidime (Stanford) and to cefepime and ceftazidime (UT Southwestern). Reported-label budgets of 10%, 25%, 50% and 100% of development patients were drawn over five patient-disjoint outer splits. Logistic regression and histogram gradient boosting were trained with reported labels only, or with added rule-derived labels using equal weights, a tuning-selected weight, class-proportion adjustment or priorprobability correction. These were compared with an all-budget reported-only baseline that used every budgeted reported label for training. Stanford used the inferred labels released with the dataset and a feature set including resistance history. UT Southwestern used researcher-derived labels from Stanford rule text, which were not validated for that site, and an age-only primary model. Brier-score differences were assessed with paired patientcluster bootstrap intervals.

All rule-derived labels were susceptible. Equal-weight augmentation lowered mean predicted risk well below observed prevalence (for example, Stanford cefepime at the 10% budget: 0.35% versus 4.85%) and had a higher Brier score than the all-budget baseline in every drug, model and budget summary. The tuning-selected weight did not consistently improve on the all-budget baseline. Its median Brier difference was positive in 38 of 40 drug, model and budget summaries. Its paired intervals lay above zero in 100 of 296 Stanford analyses and 31 of 198 UT Southwestern analyses, and below zero in 3 and 5. The tuning set often selected a weight of zero (146 of 296 and 153 of 198 analyses). At UT Southwestern, the age-only models showed little discrimination (median AUROC 0.49 to 0.54), so those comparisons mainly reflect calibration.

Conclusions. Under these designs, rule-derived labels did not consistently improve probability prediction over a reported-only model that used the whole reported-label budget for training. Evaluations of weak supervision should include such a baseline and should report calibration as well as discrimination.

1. Introduction

Supervised models need labelled examples, and labels are often the expensive part of a machine learning project. Weak supervision replaces part of the manual labelling with cheaper sources such as heuristic rules, knowledge bases or other imperfect labellers. Snorkel made this approach practical by letting users write labelling functions and combining their noisy outputs without ground-truth labels [1]. Since then, many methods for aggregating and using weak labels have been proposed. The WRENCH benchmark was built because these methods had been evaluated on different, often private, datasets with different weak sources and evaluation protocols, which made results hard to compare [2]. Later work found that relatively simple designs can match more complex weak-supervision pipelines on standard benchmarks [3].

How the evaluation counts labelled data matters. Zhu et al. showed in natural language processing tasks that many weakly supervised methods depended on clean labelled validation data. Training directly on those clean examples largely removed the advantage of the more sophisticated methods [4]. Methods that learn example weights from a small clean validation set, such as learning-to-reweight [5], have the same property: the validation labels are part of the method's labelling cost. Our study asks a related question in a different setting. When a fixed number of reported labels is available, is it better to keep some for choosing how rule-derived labels are used, or to train a reported-only model on all of them? Zhu et al.'s findings motivate this comparison; they do not show what will happen in tabular clinical microbiology data.

Weak labels are also not uniformly useful. In an empirical study of generative models trained with weak supervision, Hernández-González and Pérez found that weakly labelled data helped when fully labelled data were limited, and that the benefit depended on how much supervision information the weak labels carried and how informative it was [6]. Rule-derived labels in our setting carry a particular kind of information: the rules we study only ever imply susceptibility, so every added label belongs to the majority class.

Adding labels from one class changes the class balance of the training data, and class balance affects predicted probabilities. In clinical risk prediction, corrections for class imbalance such as undersampling, oversampling and SMOTE did not improve discrimination and produced badly miscalibrated risks [7]. Analyses of random resampling reached similar conclusions and showed that the resulting miscalibration could largely be reversed by a correction for the changed class prior [8]. Calibration is therefore a central part of evaluating probability predictions, not a secondary check [9]. When two data sources differ only in class proportions, the label-shift assumption holds: the class-conditional feature distribution is the same and only the class marginal changes [10]. Under that assumption, predicted probabilities can be adjusted to a new prior with a simple closed-form formula [11]. Rulederived labels need not satisfy this assumption, because they are attached to cultures that were selected differently from those with reported results.

Antimicrobial susceptibility data provide a natural test case. The Stanford Antibiotic Resistance Microbiology Dataset (ARMD) releases reported susceptibility results and, separately, implied susceptibility labels inferred by linking results between related antibiotics according to Stanford microbiology laboratory protocols based on CLSI standards [12]. The UT Southwestern dataset (ARMD-UTSW) has a similar structure but no separate inferred-label file [13]. In UT Southwestern data, weak labels therefore have to be constructed by the researcher, and their validity for that laboratory cannot be assumed.

We compare reported-only training with several ways of adding rule-derived labels at fixed reported-label budgets, at both sites. The main comparator is an all-budget reported-only baseline that spends the whole budget on training. This is an empirical comparison of established techniques, not a new algorithm. The UT Southwestern analysis refits the models on UT Southwestern data with a different feature set and differently constructed labels. It is therefore an exploratory independent-site replication of the design, not an external validation of a fixed Stanford-trained model in the sense used in prediction-model research [14]. We used the TRIPOD+AI statement [15] as a guide for reporting the prediction-model components.

2. Methods

>Methods

2.1 Data and prediction task

We used two publicly released, de-identified microbiology datasets derived from electronic health records: Stanford ARMD (dataset release of 22 October 2025) [12] and ARMD-UTSW (Dryad version of 26 September 2025) [13]. From each, we used the culture cohort, demographic and prior-resistance files. At Stanford we also used the released impliedsusceptibility file.

The unit of analysis was a culture order, identified by the composite key of patient, encounter and order identifiers, all read as strings. We selected E. coli urine cultures with a complete key. At UT Southwestern, culture orders linked to more than one patient or encounter would have been excluded; none were found. The outcome for each target antibiotic was reported non-susceptibility: Resistant or Intermediate = 1, Susceptible = 0. Other reported values (for example, inconclusive) were ignored, and a culture whose valid reported results disagreed on the binary outcome was excluded. The organism is treated as known, so the task is not prediction of the organism before culture.

Target antibiotics were cefepime, cefoxitin and ceftazidime at Stanford, and cefepime and ceftazidime in the UT Southwestern modelling experiment. UT Southwestern cefoxitin was audited but not modelled: it had 56 reported cultures with 3 non-susceptible results, below the prespecified minimum of 50 cultures per class.

2.2 Rule-derived labels

Stanford. We used the released implied-susceptibility labels as provided, applying the same binary mapping. Keys with conflicting implied labels, keys that did not match an E. coli urine culture, and keys that also had a reported result for the target were excluded. All released implied labels for these targets were susceptible.

UT Southwestern.

We applied the E. coli rules from the Stanford rule file as research weaklabelling heuristics. Cefepime and ceftazidime: susceptible if susceptible to ceftriaxone, cefotaxime or cefazolin. Cefoxitin: susceptible if susceptible to cefazolin. These are Stanford laboratory reporting rules. They were not independently validated for UT Southwestern, and we do not present them as UT Southwestern labels or as rules suitable for patient care. We used a conservative version fixed before training. A culture received a weak susceptible label only if at least one source antibiotic was reported susceptible, no source antibiotic was reported non-susceptible, and no source antibiotic had conflicting results on that culture.

Cultures with any reported result for the target received no weak label. Missing results never produced a label. As a diagnostic, rule predictions were compared with reported target results on cultures that had both. These cultures were selected for testing of both drugs, so agreement on them may not carry over to the cultures that receive weak labels.

While preparing the UT Southwestern analysis, we checked whether the rule text reproduces Stanford's released labels. Applying the cefoxitin rule (susceptible to cefazolin, no reported cefoxitin result) to Stanford E. coli urine cultures gave 24,217 cultures. Of these, 18,123 had a released Stanford implied label and 6,094 did not. The released labels therefore reflect conditions beyond the rule text, and labels derived from the same text at UT Southwestern are not equivalent to them.

2.3 Predictors

Stanford primary features: age band; gender code (an unordered category; no meaning is given to the codes); ordering mode; and, for any resistance, resistance to the target antibiotic and E. coli resistance, the number of prior-resistance records, the smallest day offset and the number of records within 30, 90 and 365 days (log1p-transformed; no history encoded as 0 records and 99,999 days). Positive values of `resistant_time_to_culturetime` were read as days before the culture, and zero, negative and non-numeric offsets were excluded. Neither the direction of this offset nor when earlier results became available is independently verified.

UT Southwestern primary features: age band only. Gender was excluded because its coding is not known to match Stanford's. The UT Southwestern documentation does not describe an ordering-mode field, and it describes the history offset only as days between the culture order and the susceptibility result, which does not establish its direction or availability. A reported-only sensitivity analysis added history features under the Stanford assumption; it is reported in the exported results but not used for the main comparisons. Because the feature sets differ, absolute performance is not comparable between sites. Identifiers, the current result, label source, rule provenance and same-culture results for other antibiotics were never predictors.

2.4 Patient allocation and reported-label budgets

Figure 1 summarises the design. For each of five fixed salts, each patient was assigned by SHA-256(salt + patient identifier), using the first eight hexadecimal digits modulo 100. Values of 80 to 99 formed the outer test set; values of 0 to 79 formed the development set, with a training role (0 to 59), tuning role (60 to 69) or calibration role (70 to 79). A patient's role does not depend on the budget, and all cultures of a patient stay in that patient's role. Development patients with at least one reported culture for the drug were ordered by a second salted hash for each budget sample. A budget fraction of 10%, 25%, 50% or 100% took a prefix of this order, so budgets are nested within a sample. Three budget samples were drawn for each fraction below 100%, and one at 100%, giving 15 planned analyses per fraction below 100% and 5 at 100% for each drug and model. Rule-derived labels came only from development patients with the training role and were not limited by the budget. Tuning, calibration and test patients never contributed rule-derived records. Every method within an allocation used the same patients, and the outer test set was never resampled. The same salts and cut points were applied at both sites; because the patients differ, the partitions themselves are site-specific.

2.5 Models and compared methods

Both model classes used fixed settings without tuning: L2-penalised logistic regression (C = 1, up to 1,000 iterations) and histogram gradient boosting (100 iterations, 7 leaves, at least 40 samples per leaf, L2 regularisation 5, no early stopping), as implemented in scikit-learn 1.8.0 [16]. One-hot encoding and standardisation were fitted on the training reported cultures of each allocation. Sample weights were rescaled to a mean of 1. Table 2 lists which resources each method uses. 2.6 Evaluation

On the outer test cultures we computed AUROC; average precision for the non-susceptible class (the AUPRC summary used here); Brier score [18]; log loss; observed prevalence; and mean predicted risk. We also computed calibration-in-the-large (the intercept of a logistic model with logit p as offset; 0 ideal) and calibration slope (the coefficient of logit p; 1 ideal) [9]. Both calibration measures were fitted by unpenalised maximum likelihood and reported as missing when the fit did not converge or diverged. Training and prediction times were recorded. Stanford results were computed on a 2-CPU Linux container and UT Southwestern results in Google Colab, so runtimes are not comparable between sites.

For Brier-score differences we used a paired patient-cluster bootstrap [19] with 2,000 resamples. For each drug and outer split, one set of patient resamples was drawn and shared by every budget, sample, model and method. All cultures of a drawn patient entered with that patient's multiplicity, and the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of the replicate differences formed the interval. A positive difference means the compared method has a worse Brier score than its baseline. These intervals describe uncertainty from test patients only. They condition on the fitted models and do not include variation from budget sampling, training patients or refitting. That variation is summarised separately across outer splits (median and range of split means) and budget samples. The outer splits share patients, and the data had been explored in earlier analyses, so the splits are repeated exploratory evaluations, not independent replications. Many comparisons were made without multiplicity adjustment.

Analyses were run with Python and pinned versions of numpy (2.3.5), pandas (2.2.3) and scikit-learn (1.8.0). The Stanford results reported here come from the same notebook run on the same files as an earlier Colab run. For the methods shared by both runs, test Brier scores agreed within 1.3 × 10 -8 and AUROC within 3.1 × 10 -5.

3.3 Mean predicted risk

Adding all rule-derived labels with equal weight lowered mean predicted risk well below the observed prevalence at both sites (Figure 2), most strongly at small budgets where rulederived records dominated the training data. For logistic regression at the 10% budget at Stanford, median mean predicted risk fell from 4.47% (reported only) to 0.35% (equal weight) for cefepime, from 12.21% to 2.88% for cefoxitin, and from 7.93% to 2.60% for ceftazidime. Observed test prevalences were 4.85%, 11.92% and 8.12%. At UT Southwestern the shift was larger for ceftazidime, from 35.0% to 0.42% against an observed 37.4%, because about 17,500 susceptible weak labels were added to a budget of a few hundred reported cultures. The selected weight, class adjustment and prior correction largely removed this shift, and the all-budget baseline stayed close to the observed prevalence.

3.4 Comparison with the all-budget reported-only baseline

Table 4 and Figure 3 summarise Brier-score differences against the all-budget baseline, and Supplementary Table S2 gives the test metrics. Equal-weight augmentation had a higher median Brier score than the all-budget baseline in every drug, model and budget summary at both sites. Its intervals lay above zero in 227 of 300 Stanford analyses and 191 of 200 UT Southwestern analyses, and never below zero. At UT Southwestern ceftazidime the difference was large: a median of +0.127 at 10% for logistic regression, against a baseline Brier score of about 0.24.

The selected weight did not consistently improve on the all-budget baseline. Its median difference was positive in 38 of the 40 drug, model and budget summaries; the two exceptions were UT Southwestern ceftazidime at 10% (gradient boosting, -1.34 × 10 -3 ) and Stanford ceftazidime at 100% (logistic regression, -0.003 × 10 -3 ). At the 10% budget the medians (× 10 -3 , logistic regression / gradient boosting) were +0.19 / +1.07 for Stanford cefepime, +0.41 / +1.12 for cefoxitin and +1.39 / +1.30 for ceftazidime, and +0.11 / +0.13 for UT Southwestern cefepime and +1.10 / -1.34 for ceftazidime. Most individual intervals included zero. Across all budgets, the intervals lay above zero in 100 of 296 Stanford analyses and 31 of 198 UT Southwestern analyses, and below zero in 3 and 5. The direction varied between splits for UT Southwestern ceftazidime at 10%: for gradient boosting, 3 of 5 split means were below zero. Results there were also unstable, with single analyses reaching +0.115 for logistic regression; the tuning sets in these analyses contained about 31 cultures. Class adjustment and prior correction were also mostly worse than the all-budget baseline (Table 4). The tuning set often selected a weight of zero, which reproduces the reported-only model: in 146 of 296 Stanford analyses and 153 of 198 UT Southwestern analyses (Supplementary Table S3). For the selected weight, the comparison with the all-budget baseline then largely reflects the reported labels held out for tuning and calibration. The all-budget baseline itself had a lower median Brier score than the reported-only model in every Stanford summary. At UT Southwestern, differences between these two were small and mixed in sign.

3.5 Discrimination and recalibration

Rule-derived labels changed AUROC little relative to the reported-only and all-budget models (Figure 4). At Stanford, the median AUROC of logistic regression at the 100% budget was 0.70 for cefepime, 0.67 for cefoxitin and 0.71 for ceftazidime. At UT Southwestern, the age-only models were close to chance: median AUROC 0.49 to 0.54 for cefepime and 0.49 to 0.53 for ceftazidime across budgets and methods. The UT Southwestern comparisons are therefore informative mainly about calibration and the effect of class balance, not about ranking.

Recalibration was often unavailable at small budgets because calibration sets had too few positives. For Stanford cefepime at 10%, the recalibrated reported-only model was available in 3 of 15 analyses and the recalibrated selected-weight comparison in 2 of 15. Where both recalibrated models were available, their differences were usually exactly zero, because the tuning set had selected α = 0. Remaining differences were small and of mixed sign (exported results). Median training times per model at the 100% budget were below 0.4 s for every logistic regression method and below 0.35 s for gradient boosting methods without weight selection. The selected weight required several fits (median 1.8 s for gradient boosting at Stanford).

4. Discussion

At two sites, three antibiotics and two model classes, adding rule-derived labels did not consistently improve probability prediction over a reported-only model that used the entire reported-label budget for training. Equal-weight augmentation was clearly worse, and the tuning-selected weight was usually slightly worse or indistinguishable. These results do not show that rule-derived labels cannot help. They show that, in this design, any benefit was not large enough to offset the reported labels set aside for weight selection, and that unweighted addition of single-class labels damaged calibration.

The result resembles Zhu et al.'s observation that clean validation data are often better spent on training [4], but the settings differ: tabular features, a small number of rules, labels that only ever imply the majority class, and probability-based evaluation. Our study does not test whether their conclusions hold generally. It suggests that the comparison they recommend, against a model trained on all the clean labels, is also informative in this application. Choosing α on a tuning set is a coarse form of the validation-guided reweighting studied by Ren et al. [5]. That α = 0 was chosen in about half of the Stanford analyses and three-quarters of the UT Southwestern analyses indicates that the tuning sets often found no benefit from the rule-derived records.

The calibration findings agree with work on class-imbalance correction in risk prediction [7, 8]. Adding many susceptible labels acts like oversampling the majority class, and the resulting underestimation of risk could largely be undone by restoring the reported class proportions, either through weights or through a prior correction [11]. These corrections recovered calibration-in-the-large but not the Brier score of the all-budget baseline. A likely contributor is that rule-derived cultures differ from reported cultures in more than class proportion, violating the label-shift assumption [10]. We did not test this directly.

Weak-label quality is uncertain at both sites. Stanford's implied labels follow documented laboratory protocols [12], but we know of no independent validation of their accuracy. At UT Southwestern, the labels are our own application of Stanford rule text. Agreement with reported results was high on co-tested cultures (for example, 16 disagreements among 16,690 cefepime cultures), but those cultures were selected for testing and may not represent the cultures that received weak labels. Our check on Stanford data also showed that the rule text alone does not reproduce Stanford's released labels. WRENCH highlighted that weak-label sources differ in ways that complicate comparison [2]. The same applies here: the two sites differ in how labels were produced as well as in patients, testing practice and features.

The two-site design should not be read as external validation. Following the distinction drawn in prediction-model research [14], we did not take a model fitted at Stanford and evaluate it at UT Southwestern. We repeated the design and refitted every model at each site. The primary feature sets differ (rich at Stanford, age-only at UT Southwestern), and the UT Southwestern models had almost no discrimination. The UT Southwestern results therefore test whether the label-budget findings recur under weaker features and differently built labels, not whether a particular model transports. We also could not verify whether the two datasets share patients.

Strengths and limitations

Strengths include a design in which every method uses the same reported patients within an allocation; an explicit all-budget baseline; separate tuning and calibration patients; paired cluster bootstrap intervals; reporting of availability, including analyses that could not be run; and checks that a selected weight of zero reproduces the reported-only model exactly.

The limitations are substantial. The analysis is exploratory, and the Stanford data had been examined in earlier analyses with the same outer splits, so there is no untouched confirmatory test set. Outer splits overlap, and we made many comparisons without adjustment. Bootstrap intervals cover only test-patient sampling. Reported results may be affected by selective testing and are not assumed to be error-free; the UT Southwestern ceftazidime prevalence of 38% suggests strong selection. The timing of resistance-history records is not verified, so leakage of information unavailable at prediction time cannot be excluded at Stanford. Dates are jittered, so no temporal validation was attempted. The budgets were simulated by subsampling fully labelled data, and real low-label settings may differ in which cultures are labelled. Hyperparameters were fixed. The rules imply only susceptibility. The study does not assess clinical utility, and nothing here supports clinical use of these models or rules. A preliminary feature-matched comparison between sites was not part of the completed analyses and is not reported.

Future work

Useful next steps are a prespecified analysis on data not used during method development; feature sets that are matched across sites where meanings can be established; weak-label sources that can imply both classes; and an independent assessment of rule validity at each laboratory. Any new method for combining weak and reported labels should be compared with an all-budget reported-only baseline and evaluated on calibration as well as discrimination.

5. Conclusion

In exploratory label-budget experiments at two sites, rule-derived susceptibility labels did not consistently improve probability prediction compared with using the whole reported-label budget for training, and unweighted addition of these single-class labels seriously miscalibrated predictions. Evaluations of weak supervision should count the reported labels used for tuning and calibration, include an all-budget reported-only baseline, and report calibration alongside discrimination.
