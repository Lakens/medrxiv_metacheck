What we found

We found 8 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Using multiparametric flow cytometry and state-of-the-art mass spectrometry, we demonstrate that exercise training promotes an anti-inflammatory state at both the systemic and immune-cell proteomic levels and ameliorates dysregulated CCR4 expression on disease-associated LFA-1α high CD4 + T cells in multiple sclerosis patients.

Introduction

Multiple sclerosis (MS) is a progressive neuroinflammatory autoimmune disease that primarily affects females, is commonly diagnosed in early adulthood, and affects more than 2.8 million people worldwide (1). Disease progression is driven by localized inflammatory processes within the central nervous system (CNS), leading to continuous neurodegeneration and diverse symptom complexes depending on affected CNS structures. The main concept of disease-modifying therapies (DMTs) is the modulation of peripheral immunity to mitigate inflammation-induced neurodegeneration either by preventing immune cell invasion into the CNS, (un-)targeted cell depletion, or dampening cellular activation (2, 3).

Besides positive effects of regular exercise on patient-reported outcomes in MS(4), a growing body of evidence now supports the application of structured exercise programs to beneficially affect immune cell characteristics implicated in the immunopathology of the disease (5-7) without causing adverse effects, which are oftentimes a consequence of DMTs. These observations are consistent with exerciseinduced anti-inflammatory effects in healthy subjects and other clinical populations (8, 9) and are complemented by increasing evidence that levels of physical activity and cardiorespiratory fitness (CRF), which are known to be decreased in persons with MS (10, 11), are associated with disease progression and neurodegeneration (12, 13).

Especially high-intensity interval training (HIIT) has been shown to be more effective in improving CRF both in healthy subjects and persons with MS (14, 15), but also regarding the modulation of disease-associated cellular and molecular disease markers (16, 17).

A main goal of drug therapies in MS is to dampen immune cell trafficking across or their attraction toward the blood-brain-barrier which is achieved by targeting adhesion molecules and chemokine receptors (18, 19). Although preclinical studies report profound immunomodulatory effects of exercise on immune cell infiltration into CNS and lymphoid organs, cytokine production, and disease progression (20, 21), there is limited translational evidence for these findings (22). Furthermore, most clinical studies investigating exercise-induced effects on the immune system in MS are limited by small sample sizes, short intervention periods, or targeted analytical approaches, which restrict the exploratory potential of resulting data. Just recently, unbiased analyses of alterations within the proteome of circulating immune cells after one training session revealed profound intensity-dependent changes (23), providing implications of a significant remodeling of the cellular proteome with functional adaptions in the course of a training intervention over several months.

In this randomized controlled trial (RCT), we comprehensively investigated immediate and sustained effects of a three-month HIIT intervention on circulating immune cells in persons with MS and included a healthy control group matched for age, sex, and body mass index (BMI) for comparison. We characterized MS-relevant T cell subsets and their expression of proteins involved in vascular adhesion and chemotaxis using flow cytometry and further assessed exercise-induced changes within the proteome of peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) by mass spectrometry-based profiling of more than 7,000 proteins. Besides a pronounced increase in physical fitness, HIIT induced immediate and sustained changes in composite indices of systemic inflammation, while changes within the PBMC proteome at follow-up indicated a shift of innate and adaptive immune cells toward anti-inflammatory phenotypes and a reduced abundance of proteins linked to chronic inflammatory disease (i.e., MTHFD2).

We further identified an LFA-1α high CD4 + T-cell subset that was associated with disease biomarkers and exhibited increased C-C chemokine receptor type 4 (CCR4) expression compared with healthy controls that declined in the intervention group.

Together, these findings advance our understanding of cellular and molecular mechanisms underlying exercise-induced immunomodulation in persons with MS, thereby strengthening the mechanistic basis for current recommendations of exercise and lifestyle physical activity.

Results

From a total of 142 subjects that were assessed for eligibility, 75 persons with relapsing-remitting MS were randomized either into the intervention (n=39) or passive control group (n=36) (Fig. 1). The matched healthy subjects (n=29) did not differ from the MS cohort regarding age, BMI, sex, and peak oxygen uptake (V ̇O2peak) (Table 1), with sex-based subgroup analysis similarly revealing no statistically significant differences (Supplementary Table S1). Peak power output (PPO) was lower in the MS cohort as well as in the male subgroup. In total, seven participants were considered drop-outs (relapse: n=4; change in medication: n=2; other: n=1), and four participants were lost to follow-up as they either did not start the intervention or discontinued the training after one session (Fig. 1A). Additionally, four assessments were missed due to illness of participants (post intervention: n=1; follow-up: n=3). Mean attendance (number of successfully conducted exercise sessions) of all participants who completed the study was 88.73% (± 9.7) with one person dropping below 70% (Supplementary Fig. S1A). During the study period, one adverse event (severe cardiovascular exacerbation) was reported which did not result in drop-out. One participant in the intervention group experienced an episode of known atrioventricular nodal reentry tachycardia (AVNRT) during a training session. The session was therefore interrupted, and the tachycardia ceased spontaneously at rest.

The MS cohort exhibited a higher NLR (7.2×10 -3 , rrb = .34) compared to healthy subjects, whereas the SII was not different between both groups (Fig. 3A). HIIT restructures the proteome of circulating immune cells

We next investigated whether exercise-induced changes in cellular inflammation markers were also reflected by alterations in the PBMC proteome. This is of particular relevance since proteomic profiling provides functional insights into cellular states which cannot be captured by phenotypic characterization alone. Using state-of-the-art UHPLC-MS/MS instrumentation, a total of 9,465 proteins were identified and 8,949 proteins were quantified, respectively, across all samples, thereby representing a comprehensive data resource that exceeds results from other studies in the field of immunology and MS (30, 31).

Assessment of data robustness and potential confounders

Quantification of proteins per group and time point (ranging from 7,266 to 7,496, Supplementary Fig. S4A) as well as the inter-individual variability of all quantified proteins (ranging from 28.36% to 32.10%, Supplementary Fig. S4B) were highly consistent. After data preprocessing, the dataset comprised 7,111 proteins that were included into the final analysis. Data imputation across all time points per group showed a well-balanced and homogeneous pattern (Supplementary Fig. S4C), indicating that the imputation procedure preserved the overall data structure without introducing apparent systematic bias. Although the distribution of DMTs among both groups was comparable and remained largely consistent over time (Table 1, Supplementary Table S3), we assessed whether the PBMC proteome of DMT subgroups cluster together due to their well-known effects on circulating immune cells (2, 3) to rule out any bias arising from medication-induced proteomic alterations.

Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) clustering revealed that participants receiving the same DMT were distributed heterogeneously across the data . space and did not form distinct clusters, indicating that DMT-specific effects were not prominently reflected in the PBMC proteome (Supplementary Fig. S4D). Results from flow cytometry analysis (Supplementary Table S2) did not reveal large shifts in major immune cell populations in either group which was complemented by a group-by-group comparison of major immune cell populations per time point (Supplementary Fig. S4E), ruling out that group, time, and group × time interaction effects arise from differences within the PBMC compartment.

HIIT induces a coordinated decline in mitochondrial proteins

Using linear mixed model analysis, a total of 34 group × time interaction effects, 1 group effect, and 402 time effects were identified (Fig. 4A). Whereas exercise-induced effects on the proteome at post-intervention were scarce, profound time and interaction effects were observed with a particular focus on changes within the intervention group of 376 differentially abundant proteins. Inspection of significant interaction effects at post-intervention revealed four proteins that were significantly downregulated in the intervention group (Fig. 4B). Among them is PPM1K (p = 3.5×10 -3 , log2FC = -0.49), a protein that is predominantly located in the mitochondrial matrix and necessary for branched-chain amino acid metabolism. SNX14 (p = 3.1×10 -2 , log2FC = -0.51) is involved in lipid metabolism, mitochondrial transport and anchored to the ER, whereas SEC61G (p = 7.1×10 -3 , log2FC = -1.1) plays an important role in transporting polypeptides into the ER for protein folding and maturation. These results suggest that immediate effects of chronic exercise predominantly led to changes in mitochondrial metabolism of non-glucose-derived substrates, but also the translational machinery in immune cells of persons with MS.

At follow-up, a total of 30 interaction effects were identified of which 22 proteins were significantly downregulated in the intervention group (Fig. 4B). Although a different set of proteins was identified, a similar picture of altered mitochondrial metabolism could be drawn from the reduced abundances of NDUFA3 (p = 4.1×10 -2 , log2FC = -0.52) and COX6A1 (p = 2.7×10 -2 , log2FC = -0.74) that belong to mitochondrial respiratory complex I and IV (32), and SLC25A16 (p = 1.2×10 -2 , log2FC = -0.39) which represents a mitochondrial transporter facilitating the exchange of molecules between the cytosol and the mitochondrial matrix (33). Similarly, RIPK3 (p = 3.8×10 -2 , log2FC = -0.67) and PDCD5 (p = 2.2×10 -2 , log2FC = -0.63) affect mitochondria either by enhancing aerobic respiration and subsequent mitochondrial ROS production (34) or by activating the mitochondria-related apoptotic pathway (35). Methylenetetrahydrofolate dehydrogenase 2 (MTHFD2) (p = 2.4×10 -2 , log2FC = -0.58), located in the mitochondrion and involved in nucleotide synthesis and redox homeostasis, represents a critical protein necessary for activated CD4 + T cells to maintain de novo purine synthesis (36). Sugiura et al. (36) further showed that blockage of MTHFD2 promoted Treg and repressed TH17 differentiation, and that MTHFD2 is highly upregulated in CNS-infiltrating CD4 + T cells in a mouse model of MS which highlights this protein as a potential candidate that drives exercise-induced beneficial effects on CD4 + T cells in MS.

Anti-inflammatory adaptations of circulating immune cells are mirrored by inferred transcriptional regulation after HIIT

To evaluate the impact of HIIT on transcriptional regulation of PBMCs, we next applied a computational approach to infer transcription factor (TF) activity based on the abundance of proteins encoded by downstream TF target genes within our dataset (37). In line with the number of identified time and interaction effects on the protein level (Fig. 4A), pronounced differences in inferred TF activity were identified at followup, whereas no significant differences were revealed at post-intervention. Within the passive control group, MAZ (p = 2.4×10 -2 , log2FC = 0.092) represented the only TF whose activity was significantly upregulated (Supplementary Fig. S4F). In addition to reducing peripheral inflammation, DMTs further limit immune cell recruitment to and migration across CNS barriers by modulating chemokine receptors and adhesion molecules (2, 3, 18). Besides very late antigen-4 (VLA-4), the lymphocyte function-associated antigen 1 (LFA-1) represents another disease-associated adhesion molecule whose expression on lymphocytes is higher in persons with MS (56). Reducing or abrogating LFA-1 expression on peripheral CD4 + T cells in mice significantly hampered their CNS-invading potential and reduced neuroinflammation (57, 58), whereas blockage of the alpha chain of LFA-1, namely LFA-1α, similarly diminished transmigration of activated CD4 + T cells from persons with MS (59). Here we observed that CD4 + T cells expressing LFA-1α can be discriminated into an LFA-1α low and an LFA-1α high population (Fig. 5A). To elucidate whether both populations can be discriminated by their cumulative potential for migration and chemoattraction, we next analyzed LFA-1α low and LFA-1α high subsets for their protein expression of other adhesion molecules (i.e. PSGL-1 and CD49d) and chemokine receptors (i.e., CCR4, CCR6, CXCR3, CXCR5, CXCR6, and CX3CR1). We observed that the LFA-1α high CD4 + subset expressed a significantly higher cumulative amount of both adhesion molecules and chemokine receptors (Fig. 5B). Similar results were observed for CD8 + LFA-1α cells (Supplementary Fig. S6A). Given the central role of immune-cell recruitment and migration in MS immunopathology, we examined whether the frequencies of LFA-1α high CD4 + /CD8 + subsets were associated with disease biomarkers and clinical characteristics. Indeed, significant associations were observed with serum glial fibrillary acidic protein (sGFAP), serum neurofilament light chain (sNfL), NLR, and disease duration (Fig. 5C), thereby corroborating a potential pathogenic role of LFA-1α high T cells in MS. However, no significant exercise-induced changes in these parameters were observed (Supplementary Table S2). Based on these disease-associated characteristics of LFA-1α high T cells, we were further interested whether a dysregulated, disease-specific signature of certain chemokine receptors or adhesion molecules on these subsets exist. A detailed analysis of chemokine receptor and adhesion molecule expression within LFA-1α high subsets compared to healthy subjects was conducted and revealed significantly higher levels of CCR4 (p = 1.2×10 -4 , rrb = .49) and PSGL-1 (p = 1.9×10 -4 , rrb = .47) on LFA-1α high CD4 + cells, and CX3CR1 (p = 1.9×10 -2 , rrb = .30) on CD8 + LFA-1α high cells (Fig. 5D-F, Supplementary Fig. S6B). In regard to exercise, a significant group effect (p = 1.1×10 - 2 , ƞp2 = .09) was observed for CCR4 expression, showing a decrease from baseline to follow-up (p = 3.7×10 -3 , d = .80) and significant group differences both at postintervention (p = 4.9×10 -3 , d = .77) and follow-up (p = 5.0×10 -2 , d = .55) (Fig. 5D). These results underscore the effectiveness of HIIT in persons with MS and significantly expand the knowledge of how exercise training exerts immunomodulatory effects in MS with further implications for future studies.

The observed improvements in V ̇O2peak are in line with other studies in the field (14) and indicate, together with the low drop-out rate and high training attendance, that our training program was well-accepted and effective. We further showed that the increase in V ̇O2peak was sustained at least for four weeks, although the participants' reported weekly leisure-time activity returned to baseline levels at follow-up. This rules out a potential bias arising from a continuation of exercise after the intervention period. The profound increase in PPO should be emphasized, since this measure is not reported frequently (14) but constitutes a relevant feature in MS due to the disease-related decline in motor function especially in the lower limbs (24). Although not significant, a trend (p = .074) for a decline in PPO from post-intervention to follow-up was observed, whereas the V ̇O2peak remained unchanged, suggesting more sustainable adaptations of the cardiorespiratory system compared to the musculoskeletal system. So far, only one pilot study assessed the effects of exercise training on skeletal muscle morphology and metabolism in MS without including a follow-up time point (62), so that future studies may elaborate on these observations.

The diminishing effects of HIIT on the SII remained stable until follow-up which confirms and extends previous findings from another RCT involving persons with MS (17). On the subset level, we showed that the Treg-to-TH17.1 ratio was significantly higher in the intervention group at post-intervention which did not sustain until followup, indicating a beneficial but temporary effect that necessitates continuous exercise to persist. Although regular exercise has been shown to be a promising strategy to modulate TH17-related outcomes (6,7), we were the first to assess exercise-induced changes on TH17.1 cells which are increasingly discussed in MS immunopathology (27, 28). Using flow cytometry, we further identified an LFA-1α high T cell subset that exhibited a significantly higher cumulative expression of other adhesion molecules and chemokine receptors compared to the LFA-1α low subset and is associated with disease biomarkers and clinical measures of MS. LFA-1α represents the larger subunit of the heterodimer LFA-1, is almost exclusively expressed in its dimerized form on the cell membrane . (67), and is the primary target for modulating the biological function of LFA-1 (59, 68, 69). Findings that attenuation and abrogation of LFA-1 expression on CD4⁺ T cells dampened neuroinflammation by reducing transmigration into the CNS (57, 58) increase the interest to target LFA-1-expressing cells therapeutically. We found that CCR4 expression on disease-associated LFA-1α high CD4 + cells was higher in persons with MS compared to matched healthy subjects and that HIIT significantly diminished CCR4 expression. Indeed, CCR4 is increasingly implicated in inflammatory diseases (70), has been identified as a susceptibility gene for MS (71), and is constitutively expressed on TH17 and TH2 cells. It was shown that CCR4 + cells are readily able to enter the CNS (72) and that CCL22, a cognate ligand for CCR4, is increased in cerebrospinal fluid of persons with MS (73, 74), leading to an enhanced attraction of peripheral CCR4 + cells. In the context of experimental MS, evidence supports the role of CCL17, another cognate ligand of CCR4, in attracting TH17 cells to the CNS (61), while inhibition of CCR4 signaling hampered TH17 and TH1 polarization and ameliorated disease progression (75). However, no previous study has specifically investigated CCR4⁺ CD4⁺ T cells expressing LFA-1, particularly the LFA-1α high subset, in MS. Our results highlight regular exercise as a strategy to dampen dysregulated CCR4 on a disease-associated cell type expressing high levels of LFA-1α.

Some limitations of this study should be addressed. Based on the exploratory nature of including high dimensional data from untargeted proteomics and multiparametric flow cytometry, the majority of the results reported here were derived from exploratory analyses and should therefore be interpreted accordingly. In addition, the generalizability of the present findings may be limited by the characteristics of the study cohort. Although HIIT over three months was well tolerated and effective in our study cohort, this exercise regimen might not be equally applicable to persons with greater disease severity (EDSS > 4) due to more pronounced motor impairments. Further, our access to biological samples was constrained to the peripheral blood compartment which limits clinically meaningful interpretation in this neuroinflammatory disease.

Future studies in this field should therefore acquire cerebrospinal fluid to better translate exercise-induced effects observed in the periphery to potential changes within the CNS. Additionally, we did not perform functional assays in this study (e.g., transmigration assay across human brain endothelial cells, in-vitro stimulation assays using MS-specific autoantigens) which would provide mechanistic evidence for our observed findings. Although we assessed exercise-induced changes of thousands of proteins within circulating PBMCs, they are composed of a heterogeneous mixture of adaptive and innate immune cells which mask the identification of cell type-specific alterations. This limitation should be addressed in future studies by sorting cell types of interest such as CD4 + T cells and monocytes, thereby validating and extending our findings. Another limitation that needs to be addressed is the heterogeneous use of MS-specific medications within the study cohort, which, although largely stable between groups over the study period, potentially introduced confounding effects.

In conclusion, we substantiate existing evidence on the beneficial effects of HIIT on physical fitness and systemic inflammation in persons with MS and significantly expand this knowledge by our findings on its ability to reshape cellular immunity toward antiinflammation on the phenotypic and proteomic level. The finding that the strongest changes within the PBMC proteome emerged at follow-up is original and opens an interesting and overlooked perspective on delayed immunometabolic adaptations to training. The observation of an exercise-induced decline in dysregulated CCR4 expression on disease-associated LFA-1α high CD4 + T cells proposes another potential mechanism of how exercise exerts immunomodulatory effects in MS. Overall, our results strengthen national and international recommendations on regular exercise for persons with MS by providing biological underpinnings of exercise-induced immunomodulatory effects within the cellular immune system without causing any adverse effects.

Materials and methods

Subjects

A total of 75 persons with relapsing-remitting MS participated in this RCT. Subjects were included if they were between 18 and 60 years of age, had approved relapsingremitting MS, an EDSS between 0 and 4, and approved sports suitability assessed by a medical doctor via an electrocardiogram (ECG) at rest and during exercise (custo diagnostic, custo med GmbH, Ottobrunn, Germany). Study inclusion was further only considered if no adjustments in DMT were made within three months before study commencement. Subjects were excluded if they had any previous history of internal, cardiovascular or orthopedic disease that prohibit study participation, pregnancy or breastfeeding, and if they experienced a relapse or significant worsening of MS-related symptoms during the study period. If a change in DMT was made during the study period, respective time points were not considered for follow-up analysis.

The study was reviewed and approved by the ethics committee of the Leibniz Institute the first visit, written informed consent and disease-related data were obtained which was followed by baseline testing. Each visit took place between 7 a.m. and 11 a.m. at the sports medical laboratory at TU Dortmund University. Participants were asked to arrive fasted and well hydrated, to avoid strenuous exercise (at least 48 hours) and to not drink alcohol, coffee or to take dietary supplements (at least 24 hours) prior to the visit. Questionnaires were filled out online using a link that was provided to the participants 24 hours before each testing day at the TU Dortmund University.

Each visit included the recording of anthropometric data, a blood draw, and cardiopulmonary exercise testing (CPET) until voluntary exhaustion which was conducted on a cycle ergometer (Cortex Bike M) using the MetaLyzer 3B-R² (CORTEX Biophysik, Leipzig, Germany) during which heart rate was continuously measured

Randomization

Using concealed allocation, study participants were randomly assigned into the intervention or passive control group immediately after baseline testing using stratified permuted block randomization with a 1:1 allocation ratio and randomly varying block sizes of four and six. The random allocation sequence was computer-generated in Microsoft Excel by an investigator who was not involved in participant enrollment or assessments. To ensure a balanced group allocation into each study arm, stratification factors were used and dichotomized as follows: age (18-34 vs. 35-60 years), EDSS (0-2.0 vs. 2.5-4.0), and BMI (< 25 vs. ≥ 25 kg/m 2 ).

Blinding

Due to the nature of the trial, participants could not be blinded to group allocation.

However, assessors taking outcome measures after the intervention phase and at follow-up were blinded to group allocation by avoiding any contact between participants and assessors throughout the study period (except for assessments).

Intervention

The exercise intervention was conducted at the training facility at the TU Dortmund University. Participants allocated to the HIIT group exercised three times per week over a three-month intervention period on a bicycle ergometer (ergoselect5, ergoline GmbH, Germany). This intervention period was split into four blocks of three weeks with progressing intensity from block one to four. The exercise intensity was determined by peak power output (PPO) in watts (W) that was achieved during the baseline assessment. Each training session started with a five min warm-up, comprised a total of seven intervals of 90 seconds that were interspersed by active recovery periods of 3 min between each interval, and ended with a five min cool-down, resulting in a total training duration of 38,5 min. Whereas the intensity during the active recovery phases remained constant over the entire study period (20-25% PPO), the exercise intensity progressively increased from block one to four (block 1: 70-75% PPO; block 2: 80-85% PPO; block 3: 85-90% PPO; block 4: >90% PPO). Since the body adapts to the exercise stimulus over time, another CPET was conducted in intervention week six in order to update the PPO that was used for defining exercise intensity. A detailed overview of the training intervention is provided in Supplementary Table S4. During each training session, the heart rate was continuously measured (ers2 ECG Transmitter, ergoline GmbH, Germany) and the RPE (76) was quantified at training start, after the 4 th and the 7 th interval, respectively. Severe adverse events, defined as i) severe cardiovascular, neurological, orthopedic, or pulmonary diseases (e.g. renal failure, hepatic dysfunction, cardiovascular disease) or ii) severe cardiovascular exacerbations, were recorded during the study period. After finishing the intervention phase, a four-week follow-up period was included into this study in which participants were asked to continue their daily life.

For ethical reasons and to provide all participants the opportunity to benefit from the exercise program, the participants in the passive control group were included in a waiting list to be offered the same exercise program after the study period.

Furthermore, all participants were asked not to change their behavior in daily life routines such as exercise, nutrition, or sleep. Participants attending less than 25 training sessions (corresponding to 70% of overall attendance) were considered non-compliant to the study protocol and were therefore excluded from follow-up analysis (6). The CPET in week six was considered equivalent to a training session, since the conduction of three training sessions and a CPET within one week was not feasible.

Healthy control group

Healthy subjects were recruited through public announcements and carefully selected to match participants from the MS cohort for age, BMI and sex. Subjects were included if they were between 18 and 60 years of age, had no internal, neurological or orthopedic diseases, no chronic intake of immunoregulatory or anti-inflammatory drugs, and no illness within four weeks before study participation. The participants were asked to attend one visit at the sports medicine lab which included, similar to the assessment of the MS subjects, the collection of anthropometric data, a blood draw, and a CPET. The ramp protocol was chosen according to a recently published study by Wenzel et al. (78). Questionnaires were filled out online using a link that was provided to the participants 24 hours before the testing day at the TU Dortmund University. Written informed consent was obtained prior to participation.

Blood draw and sample preparation

Blood sampling was performed at each visit by vein puncture after the BIA when participants rested for ten min in a lying position. PBMC purification was done immediately after blood draw from 27mL whole blood, collected in lithium heparin S-Monovettes ® (Sarstedt), by density gradient centrifugation at RT using SepMate™ PBMC Isolation Tubes (STEMCELL Technologies). Purified PBMCs were resuspended in 1mL freezing medium (90% FBS, 10% DMSO), stored overnight in a cell freezing container at -80°C, and transferred to -150°C on the next day. For serum isolation, Serum-Gel S-Monovettes ® (Sarstedt) were kept in upright position at 4-8°C for 30 min.

After clotting, tubes were centrifuged for 10 min at 2500xg at 4°C, aliquoted, and stored at -80°C until analysis.

The same sample preparation procedures were carried out for blood derived from the healthy control subjects.

Flow cytometry

Haemogram

Within 10 min after blood draw into lithium heparin S-Monovettes ® (Sarstedt), the absolute cell numbers of major blood cell populations were quantified on a Hematology Analyzer (SYSMEX XP3000, Norderstedt, Germany). The SII was calculated as (platelets × neutrophils) × lymphocytes -1 and the NLR as neutrophils × lymphocytes -1.

Serum neurofilament light chain (sNfL) and serum glial fibrillary acidic protein (sGFAP) analysis

Serum was used to measure both sNfL and sGFAP as RUO immunoassays (ECLIA, Electrochemiluminescence immunoassay, two-incubation step sandwich assay) according to the manufacturer's instructions using the Roche Cobas 8000 (Roche Elecsys ® module). All laboratory technicians performing the analyses were blinded to the clinical data and group allocation. Using Generalized Additive Models for Location, Scale and Shape (GAMLSS) from large reference databases (79, 80), z-scores from the quantified concentration of both parameters by adjusting for age and BMI (sNfL) or for age, BMI, and sex (sGFAP), respectively.

UHPLC-MS/MS-based untargeted proteomics of PBMCs

Sample preparation

In brief, cryopreserved PBMCs were gently thawed in a water bath at 37°C, washed twice with PBS containing 1mM EDTA, and lysed in a commercial RIPA buffer containing 0.1% SDS (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), 10mM sodium fluoride (Sigma-Aldrich, Merck, Germany), 1mM sodium orthovanadate (Sigma-Aldrich, Merck, Germany), cOmplete™ Protease Inhibitor Cocktail, PhosSTOP™ (both Roche Diagnostics GmbH, Germany), 250 U ml -1 benzonase (LubioScience GmbH, Switzerland), and 10 U ml -1 DNase I (Roche Diagnostics GmbH, Germany). Samples were incubated at 4°C for 1 hour and then centrifuged at 13,000xg at 4 °C for 15min.

The supernatant was transferred to a protein LoBind tube (Eppendorf SE, Germany) and the protein concentration was quantified using a commercial bicinchoninic acid assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). An amount of 5μg of protein per sample was digested (Trypsin, Promega Corporation, USA) using an AssayMAP Bravo liquid handling system (Agilent Technologies, USA) running the autoSP3 protocol according to Müller et al. (81). After sample preparation, the remaining peptides were vacuum dried and stored at -20 °C until LC-MS/MS analysis.

MS method Orbitrap Astral Zoom

Samples were processed and measured in a block randomized order to prevent any technical bias that might occur during sample preparation or LC-MS/MS measurement (82).

Dried peptides were reconstituted (97.4% water, 2.5% hexafluoro-2-propanol and 0.1% trifluoroacetic acid) and 200ng were analysed using a Vanquish Neo UPLC system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) directly connected to an Astral Zoom mass spectrometer for a total of 30 min per sample. Peptides were online desalted on a trapping cartridge (Acclaim PepMap300 C18, 5µm, 300Å wide pore; Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) with a loading volume of 60µL using a 30 µL/min flow of 0.1% trifluoroacetic acid in water. The analytical multistep gradient (400 nL/min) was performed with an Aurora Ultimate XT analytical column (1.7 µm, 75 µm x 250 mm, IonOptics, Australia) using solvent A (0.1% formic acid in water) and solvent B (0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile). For 24.5 min the concentration of B linearly ramped from 2% to 30%, followed by a quick ramp to 80%.

Eluting peptides were analyzed in the mass spectrometer using data independent acquisition (DIA) mode. A full scan at 240k resolution (380-980 m/z, 500% AGC target, 5 ms maxIT) was followed by 300 DIA windows. The DIA acquisition covered a mass range of 380-980 m/z using windows of fixed width. Windows overlap by 2 m/z, the AGC target was 100% with a maxIT of 3 ms and spectra are recorded using Astral analyser. All laboratory technicians performing the measurement were blinded to the clinical data and group allocation.

Data analysis

Data processing

The normalized data derived from Spectronaut was screened for gene name duplicates. Only the event with the highest number of identified precursors across all samples was kept. The dataset was then filtered for proteins that were quantified in ≥70% of the samples in at least one group × time point combination. Following log2 transformation, missing values were imputed separately within each group-by-timepoint combination using the missForest package (84).

Assessment of physical activity behavior

For subjective measurement of participants' physical activity behavior, the Global Physical Activity Questionnaire (GPAQ) and the Godin Leisure-Time Exercise Questionnaire (GLTEQ) were used, respectively (77, 85). The GLTEQ asks for weekly frequencies of strenuous (9 points), moderate (5 points), and mild (3 points) activities with all points being multiplied by the number of weekly occurrences. Reference score units are ≥ 24 (≙ active), 14 to 23 (≙ moderately active) and < 14 (≙ insufficiently active). The German GPAQ consists of 16 short-answer questions on sedentary behavior and physical activity participation in the three domains (1) activity at work, (2) travel to and from places, and (3) recreational activities and are categorized as moderate-or vigorous-intensity.

Statistics

Data from a total of 75 persons with MS and 29 matched healthy subjects were available for statistical analyses. According to the flow diagram in Fig. 1, a total of 226 of the 254 possible samples were available for flow cytometry analysis. For three PBMC samples, no protein could be extracted, resulting in n = 223 for final proteomics analysis. Similarly, isolation of serum was not possible for three samples, resulting in n = 223 for sGFAP and n = 222 (one sample measurement failed) for sNfL analysis.

An overview of the sample size per group × time point, including V ̇O2peak, PPO, SII, and NLR, is given in Supplementary Table 5. Statistical analysis and visualization were performed in R (version 4.5.1). If not otherwise indicated, data wrangling and processing was mainly done using the dplyr (86) and tidyr (87) packages followed by visualization with ggplot2 (88).

Baseline differences between persons with MS and healthy subjects.

Data distribution was assessed for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test and checked for linearity via quantile-quantile plots and histograms. In case of normal distribution, group differences were assessed using two-sided unpaired Student´s t-tests, otherwise the Wilcoxon-Mann-Whitney test was applied. The level of significance was set at p ≤ 0.05. Effect sizes were calculated using the effectsize package (89), with Cohen's d (d) reported for parametric comparisons and the rank-biserial correlation (rrb) for nonparametric comparisons.

Correlation analyses.

Pearson's correlation coefficient was used to assess associations between continuous variables, whereas Spearman's rank correlation coefficient was used when at least one variable was ordinal (i.e., EDSS). Correlation coefficients are reported together with the corresponding two-sided p-values. For the exploratory correlation matrix comprising multiple pairwise comparisons (Fig. 5C), pvalues were adjusted for multiple testing using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure to control the false discovery rate (FDR). No adjustment for multiple testing was applied to the predefined correlation analyses presented as individual scatterplots (Supplementary Fig. S1C, S2).

Effects of HIIT on physical fitness, immune cells, and targeted protein abundance. Group (intervention, passive control), time point (baseline, postintervention, follow-up), and their interaction (group × time) were included as fixed effects, while subject ID was included as a random intercept and the baseline value as a fixed covariate (90). Linear mixed-effects models were fitted using the lmer function from the lmerTest package (91). The significance of the fixed effects (group, time point, and interaction) was then assessed using analysis of variance (ANOVA) via the built-in anova function from R stats. Results from targeted protein abundances derived from the proteomics dataset were adjusted for multiple testing using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure (92) to control the FDR, while results derived from flow cytometry and physical fitness analyses were not corrected for multiple testing. Partial eta squared (ƞp 2 ) effect sizes were reported for the main effects using the eta_squared function from the effectsize package (89). In case of significant results, pairwise comparisons of estimated marginal means between time points and/or groups were performed by applying the emmeans function from the emmeans package (93) and adjusted for multiple testing using the Bonferroni correction. Statistical significance was defined as an adjusted two-sided p-value of ≤ 0.05. Effect sizes (Cohen's d) for significant pairwise comparisons (group and time) were calculated from the estimated marginal means using the residual standard deviation of the mixed model with the eff_size function from the emmeans package (93).

Exercise-induced changes on the PBMC proteome.

To identify proteins that are significantly altered between the intervention and passive control group, linear models were fitted to log2-transformed, normalized, and imputed protein intensities using the limma R package (94). To account for repeated measurements, within-subject correlation was estimated using the duplicateCorrelation function with subject ID as the blocking factor. The model included the group (intervention, passive control), the time point (baseline, post-intervention, follow-up), and their interaction (group × time) as fixed factors. Moderated t-statistics (95) were computed for each predefined contrast using the empirical Bayes approach implemented in the eBayes function with mean-variance trend correction and robust variance estimation. Resulting p-values were adjusted for multiple testing using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure (92) to control the FDR, and statistical significance was defined as an adjusted two-sided pvalue of ≤ 0.05.

Transcription factor activity analysis. Transcription factor (TF) activities were inferred from the proteomics data using the DoRothEA human regulon (confidence levels A-C) (37) and the multivariate linear model implemented in the decoupleR package (96). Only regulons represented by at least 20 target proteins in the dataset were included in the analysis. TF activity scores were subsequently analyzed using linear models implemented in the limma R package (94). To account for repeated measurements, within-subject correlation was estimated using the duplicateCorrelation function with subject ID as the blocking factor. The model included the time point, group, and their interaction as fixed factors. Empirical Bayes moderation was applied using the eBayes function, and predefined contrasts were used to assess within-group temporal changes and group × time interactions. Resulting p-values were adjusted for multiple testing using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure (92) to control the FDR, and statistical significance was defined as an adjusted two-sided p-value of ≤ 0.05.

Exercise-induced effects on cellular inflammation indices and T cell activity of several TFs was modulated in the intervention group over time with the strongest repressive effect observed for STAT1 activity (p = 2.0×10 -2 , log2FC = -0.170) (Fig.4C), thereby contrasting the results observed in the passive control group.

An exercise-induced modulatory effect on CD4 + T cell differentiation was suggested by a decrease in GATA3 activity (p = 4.4×10 -2 , log2FC = -0.071), the master transcription factor of TH2 cells, or an increase in NR2F6 activity (p = 2.0×10 -2 , log2FC = 0.096) which has been shown to counteract TFH cell differentiation(40). The increased activity of JunB (p = 2.0×10 -2 , log2FC = 0.115) can be linked to enhanced Treg differentiation(41, 42), although experimental evidence from other studies indicate TH17-promoting effects (43), suggesting context-dependent effects that determine downstream JunB signaling. Similarly, an increased ARNT activity (p = 2.0×10 -2 , log2FC = 0.132) is indicative of an enhanced CD4 + regulatory cell type-or TH17-signature depending on whether the complexing partner of ARNT is the aryl hydrocarbon receptor(44, 45) or hypoxia-inducible factor 1-α (46). Regarding effects on myeloid cells, increased MEIS1 activity (p = 2.0×10 -2 , log2FC = 0.144) indicates an enhanced differentiation of anti-inflammatory M2 macrophages (47) or monocytic myeloid-derived suppressor cells(48). The increases in KLF9 (p = 2.0×10 -2 , log2FC = 0.113) and MAFB activity (p = 2.4×10 -2 , log2FC = 0.114) were consistent with the aforementioned observations as KLF9 inhibits STAT1 transcription and enhances M2 macrophage polarization (49), while MAFB contributes to an antiinflammatory profile of monocyte-derived macrophages (50). On the other hand, an increase in RELA activity (p = 2.4×10 -2 , log2FC = 0.105), a canonical NF-κB subunit and important for TH17 polarization and cytokine production in MS (51), implies an adverse effect on immune cell signaling in response to exercise. Targeted analysis of proteins involved in the NF-κB signaling pathways showed that the expression of RELA itself and other relevant proteins (i.e., NFKB1, NFKB2) remained mainly unchanged over time and between groups (Fig.4D, Supplementary Fig.S5A). In contrast, the abundance of STAT1 significantly decreased from pre-intervention to follow-up in the intervention group and was complemented by similar kinetics for STAT2, STAT6, and other related proteins (Fig.4D, Supplementary Fig.S5B) which underlines the finding of a decrease in inferred STAT1 activity. Interestingly, CLOCK activity was significantly reduced at follow-up in the intervention group compared to the passive control group (p = 2.6×10 -2 , log2FC = -0.164) (Fig.4C). Besides being a core component of the transcriptional circadian clock machinery, CLOCK is implicated in the activation of NF-κB (52) and the NLRP3 inflammasome (53) which contrasts the finding of increased RELA activity (p = 3.7×10 -2 , log2FC = 0.156) in the intervention group. Although enhanced NR2F6 (p = 2.6×10 -2 , log2FC = 0.137) and decreased MAZ (p = 2.1×10 -2 , log2FC = -0.122) activities in the intervention group compared to the passive control group indicate a reduced STAT1 signaling and an attenuated induction of TFH differentiation(38, 40), a decreased BACH2 activity (p = 2.1×10 -2 , log2FC = -0.141) implicates a diminished ability to stabilize Treg function and to repress TH17-mediated inflammatory signaling(54, 55).

Using state-of-the-art UHPLC-MS/MS-based analysis of the PBMC proteome, we identified considerably more differentially abundant proteins at follow-up compared to post-intervention with most of them being decreased in the intervention group. This suggests that a recovery period with reduced cardiovascular and musculoskeletal strain may be necessary for (delayed) proteomic and immunometabolic adaptations to emerge in circulating immune cells in response to training. Our findings point to an altered mitochondrial metabolism indicative of a diminished mitochondrial respiration or a reduced synthesis of macromolecules, lipids, and nucleotides that leads to an attenuated proliferation of immune cells(63), a mode of action of some DMTs (e.g., teriflunomide or cladribine). The reduced abundance of MTHFD2 in the intervention group is particularly relevant in the context of MS, as MTHFD2 downregulation limited the proliferation and migration of autoreactive CD4 + T cells while promoting Treg differentiation(36). Indeed, reducing MTHFD2 activity has just recently been proposed as a therapeutic strategy to suppress sustained T-cell-mediated inflammation in rheumatoid arthritis(64). Therefore, future studies in this field may focus on MTHFD2 as a potential candidate of exercise-induced effects on CD4 + T cell metabolism and differentiation.In line with the observations on the protein level, the majority of alterations in inferred TF activity point toward gene regulation that favored an anti-inflammatory state which is primarily supported by a decrease in inferred STAT1 activity and increases in NR2F6, MAFB, KLF9, and MEIS1 activity, respectively. While STAT1 transcription is necessary for TH1 differentiation (39), an increased activity of NR2F6 is capable of inhibiting TFH cell differentiation(40), cell types that are implicated in the immunopathology of MS (2). Additionally, the inferred increase in MEIS1, MAFB, and KLF9 activity indicate an enhanced polarization of monocytes and macrophages toward an anti-inflammatory state(47) (48) (49). Monocytes are largely overlooked in the context of exercise-induced effects in MS despite a growing attention in recent years(65), so that future studies may focus on the effects of regular exercise on circulating monocytes. The interpretation of the inferred increases in both JunB and ARNT activity is less clear, since experimental evidence exists for their induction and stabilization of the Treg(41, 42, 44, 45) or TH17 lineage(43, 46), respectively. Although exercise-induced activation of the aryl hydrocarbon receptor in PBMCs has been reported(66), future studies should characterize both upstream regulators and downstream signaling of JunB and ARNT in more detail. Results from the observed interaction effects of inferred TF activities support an increase in NR2F6 and a decrease in MAZ in the intervention group complemented by a decrease in CLOCK activity, indicating decreased inflammatory signaling and a diminished potential of TFH cell differentiation(38, 40, 52, 53). However, the altered activities of RELA and BACH2 contrast these findings by their involvement in NF-κB signaling (RELA) and immunoregulatory and Treg-promoting characteristics in CD4 + T cells (BACH2)(51, 54, 55). Taken together, most changes in the PBMC proteome indicate a shift toward an anti-inflammatory state both on the protein and inferred TF activity level, although some results suggest a more complex or partially divergent response. Since the results on transcriptional regulation were based on inferential TF activity modelling and reflect changes in the abundance of proteins encoded by downstream TF target genes, future studies should elaborate on these findings by validating an activation or inhibition of highlighted TF as well as associated signaling pathways.
