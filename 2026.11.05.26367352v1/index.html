To fix Exact P-Values

Show details

Reporting p values imprecisely (e.g., p < .05) reduces transparency, reproducibility, and re-use (e.g., in p value meta-analyses). Best practice is to report exact p-values with three decimal places (e.g., p = .032) unless p values are smaller than 0.001, in which case you can use p < .001.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (e.g., “decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Schizophrenia (SCZ) is a highly heritable psychiatric disorder characterised by substantial clinical heterogeneity. While global polygenic scores (gPGS) capture genetic liability to SCZ, their ability to explain clinical variability remains limited.

Pathway-stratified polygenic scores (sPGS) offer a biologically informed approach to patient stratification.

In this study, common genetic variants were annotated across five neurotransmitter (NT) signalling pathways -acetylcholine (ACh), dopamine, γ-aminobutyric acid (GABA), glutamate (Glu), and serotonin. sPGS adjusted for sex and ancestry principal components, were calculated in two independent cohorts with different available clinical data (N=3,044 and N=645). By clustering top-decile individuals across the five sPGS dimensions, we classified patients into five mutually exclusive patient profiles, which were then evaluated for their associations with Positive and Negative Syndrome Scale (PANSS) symptom dimensions, age at disease onset (AAO), and three-year clinical remission and treatment response.

Although gPGS showed no significant association with clinical outcomes, some sPGSderived profiles captured clinically relevant heterogeneity across cohorts. Patients assigned to the Ach profile exhibited higher overall symptom severity, particularly negative symptoms and general psychopathology. Patients within the Glu profile were characterised by more severe negative symptoms, earlier AAO, reduced three-year remission, and poorer treatment response. Similarly, the GABA risk profile was also associated with three-year remission rates. These findings suggest that NT pathway-specific polygenic profiling may capture clinically relevant variation overlooked by genome-wide approaches. By identifying genetic profiles associated with symptom severity, disease onset, and longitudinal outcomes, this framework could contribute to biologically informed patient stratification and inform future precision psychiatry approaches in SCZ.

Introduction

Schizophrenia (SCZ) is a complex neurodevelopmental disorder affecting 1% of worldwide population, that presents a public health challenge, in part, due to its variable clinical trajectory and highly heterogeneous treatment outcomes. SCZ has a substantial genetic component with twin-based heritability between 60% to 80%, while landmark genome-wide association studies (GWAS) have mapped hundreds of common-variant loci, establishing an overall SNP-based heritability of 20% [1].

However, a major hurdle for clinical translation remains: while global polygenic scores (gPGS) capture part of the genetic liability to distinguish cases from healthy controls (HC), their ability to explain clinical variability among affected individuals remains limited [2] [3] [4] [5]. This predictive disconnection presents a challenge to clinical psychiatry, as broad diagnostic categories group divergent biological trajectories under a single clinical label [6].

Recent research has increasingly focused on dimensional phenotypes and longitudinal clinical outcomes rather than binary diagnostic classifications [7, 8]. Genetic liability to SCZ has been associated with cognitive traits, including general cognitive performance, working memory, and processing speed, in patients with SCZ, clinically at-risk individuals, and even in individuals without a diagnosis [9, 10]. However, gPGS generally explain only a small proportion of the variance in these quantitative phenotypes, providing limited information on the clinical heterogeneity observed.

Among patients with SCZ, clinical characteristics such as age at onset (AAO), symptom severity, treatment response, and long-term remission vary considerably between individuals, potentially reflecting differences in the biological processes contributing to disease expression and progression [2, 6].

Genetic evidence consistently implicates neuronal and synaptic biology in SCZ.

Common risk variants are predominantly enriched in genes expressed in mature cortical and subcortical neurons [11] [12] [13], converging on biological processes such as neuronal development, ion channel activity, and synaptic function [1, 14]. This synaptic convergence is further reinforced by rare copy-number variants and rare damaging coding mutations, which disproportionately disrupt synaptic genes, including neurotransmitter (NT) receptors and signalling molecules [15, 16]. Together, these findings place synaptic and NT signalling among the major biological substrates of SCZ genetic liability.

Accordingly, altered neurotransmission has long been considered a central mechanism underlying SCZ pathophysiology and remains the primary target of current . psychopharmacological interventions [17] [18] [19]. Although hyperdopaminergic signalling has historically dominated neurobiological models and first-generation drug development, converging evidence from genetic, neuroimaging, and pharmacological studies supports the involvement of multiple NT systems, including acetylcholine (ACh), γ-aminobutyric acid (GABA), glutamate (Glu), serotonin (5-HT), in addition to dopamine (DA) [20] [21] [22] [23] [24]. Localised dysfunction within these signalling cascades maps to specific clinical manifestations of the disorder, including symptom severity, cognitive impairment, AAO, and treatment response [22, [25] [26] [27] [28]. This multi-system involvement is reflected pharmacologically, as second-, and third-generation antipsychotics exert therapeutic effects through complex multi-receptor binding profiles rather than a single signalling pathway [29, 30]. These observations raise the possibility that aggregating genetic liability into a single genome-wide PGS may obscure biologically distinct components of SCZ risk. Decomposing polygenic liability according to specific NT systems could therefore provide a means to identify genetic profiles associated with distinct clinical trajectories [2, 31].

Our study systematically deconstructed overall common-variant liability into five independent, pathway-specific PGS targeting the ACh, DA, GABA, Glu, and 5-HT signalling cascades. We mapped these neurochemical risk profiles against granular clinical outcomes, evaluating Positive and Negative Syndrome Scale (PANSS) subscales, AAO, 3-year treatment resistance, and clinical remission. We hypothesised that pathway-specific genetic loads drive distinct clinical trajectories, providing a mechanistic framework to stratify patients and identify individuals at high risk for early onset, clinical severity or primary treatment failure.

Methods

Sample

Clinical and genomic data were derived from two independent, well-characterised cohorts, comprising HC and patients diagnosed with SCZ-spectrum disorders according to the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fourth Edition (DSM-IV) criteria [32]. Inclusion required a primary diagnosis of SCZ, or related psychotic spectrum disorders.

The Biomedical Research Network in Mental Health (CIBERSAM) cohort provided the primary large-scale dataset, comprising 3,856 individuals (2,145 patients from psychiatric in-patient units across seven medical centres and 1,711 HC) recruited in Spain [33]. This cohort was used to establish genomic associations with core clinical endophenotypes, specifically AAO and baseline symptom severity. Symptom severity was evaluated at study intake by certified research psychiatrists using the Positive and Negative Syndrome Scale (PANSS), capturing positive, negative, and general psychopathology subscales. AAO was defined as the age at which the first psychotic symptoms emerged, determined using a structured consensus protocol integrating reports from the treating psychiatrist, patient, and first-degree relatives. In cases where this information was not available, the date of the patient's first psychiatric evaluation for psychotic symptoms was used [4]. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants (or legally authorised representatives) prior to enrolment, and research protocols were approved by the Clinical Research Ethics Committees of each participating hospital.

To evaluate the impact of genetic liability on long-term disease trajectories and treatment outcomes, we analysed the Cantabria Program for Early Intervention in Psychosis (PAFIP) cohort. PAFIP is a prospective longitudinal cohort that enrolled 739 individuals (552 patients and 187 HC) including patients presenting with a FEP between 2001 and 2018 [34]. FEP patients were assigned to three consecutive clinical trial phases (PAFIP I, II, and III), encompassing randomised, flexible-dose, and openlabel clinical trials with a standardised 3-year prospective follow-up period. Antipsychotic dosing was adjusted at the treating psychiatrist's discretion to maintain the minimum effective therapeutic dose, with medication switching permitted based on clinical efficacy and patient tolerability. Patients underwent longitudinal prospective evaluation at 3 years post-enrolment to assess i) treatment response and resistance using the Treatment Resistance and Response in Psychosis (TRRIP) consensus criteria [35]; and ii) long-term clinical remission of symptoms was defined according to the standardised criteria [36]. Sociodemographic characteristics were recorded at baseline. The research protocol was approved by the Clinical Research Ethics Committee of Cantabria, and all participants provided written informed consent.

Genotyping, quality control, and imputation

Genome-wide array genotyping was conducted independently across both cohorts.

The CIBERSAM cohort was array-genotyped using the Illumina Infinium PsychArray platform at the Broad Institute of MIT and Harvard as part of the Psychiatric Genomics Consortium (PGC) wave 3 SCZ GWAS meta-analysis [1]. The PAFIP cohort was arraygenotyped using the Illumina Infinium Global Screening Array (GSA) v3.0. Quality control (QC) protocols were applied independently for each cohort prior to genotype imputation using PLINK v1.90b7.2 [37]. Briefly, QC included the sequential exclusion of samples and variants based on individual genotype missingness (>2%), sex discrepancies (reported males with F < 0.8; reported females with F > 0.2), minor allele frequency (MAF < 0.01), deviation from Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium (for HC P < 1e -6 ; for cases P < 1e -10 ), heterozygosity (mean (x ) ± 3 SD) and relatedness (pi-hat > 0.125). Finally, genotypes from each cohort were projected onto the 1000 Genomes Project Phase 3 reference panel (IGSR) using Principal Component Analysis (PCA).

Genetically non-European individuals, defined as those exceeding 3 SD from the European population centroid along principal components 1 (PC1) and PC2, were excluded from downstream analyses. After QC, 3,044 individuals (1,755 patients and 1,289 HC) from the CIBERSAM cohort were included, of whom 1,544 (88%) patients had available AAO data and 440 (25%) had PANSS data. In the PAFIP cohort, 645 individuals (472 patients and 173 HC) were included in the analyses; among patients, 363 (77%) had 3-year follow-up data on symptom remission and 361 (76%) had treatment response data (Table 1).

Genotype imputation was performed using the Michigan Imputation Server v2.0 platform [38]; accessed in November 2025) using the Haplotype Reference Consortium reference panel (HRC-r1.1). Genotypes were phased using EAGLE2 and subsequently imputed with minimac4. Post-imputation QC steps included removing variants with low imputation quality scores (r 2 < 0.9 or INFO < 0.9), and low minor allele frequency (MAF < 0.001).

Neurotransmitter pathway gene set curation and genomic annotation

To define genetic components of SCZ liability specific to individual NT pathways we systematically curated gene sets for five primary NT signalling networks: ACh, DA, GABA, Glu, and 5-HT. Biological pathways and ontologies were extracted from four comprehensive databases (accessed in May-June 2025): GeneOntology (GO) using AmiGO2, KEGG using KEGGREST (v1.48.0), Reactome using ReactomePA (v1.52.0), and WikiPathways using rWikiPathways (v1.28.1). Systematic query parameters were established for each NT system using specific inclusion and exclusion strings (ACh: inclusion of ach, acetylcholine, cholinergic, muscarinic, and nicotinic; exclusion of chromaffin, and gastric; DA: inclusion of dopa, and exclusion of dopachrome; GABA: inclusion of gaba, and gamma-aminobutyric acid; Glu: inclusion of glutamate, and glutamic acid; exclusion of taste, and tRNA; 5-HT: inclusion of serotonin, serotonergic, and 5-hydroxytryptamine). Genes were retained if captured in at least one database, yielding a comprehensive set of 1,478 non-redundant NT-associated genes: 302 for ACh, 322 for DA, 301 for GABA, 993 for Glu, and 210 for 5-HT (Figures 1 and S1; Table S1). Because broad glutamatergic ontologies capture ubiquitous metabolic processes distinct from central neurotransmission, the global Glu annotation inherently included pleiotropic genes involved in general cellular energy and amino acid metabolism. To isolate central synaptic signalling, we created a functionally refined Glu subset (Glu Lite ; n = 912 genes). This refined set was confined to synaptic transmission, ionotropic/metabotropic receptor signalling, and vesicular transport machinery for parallel sensitivity analyses (see Supplementary Methods; Figure S1; Table S1).

Genomic coordinates for all curated target genes were mapped using the Ensembl database (GRCh37/hg19 assembly). To capture both proximal coding variation and distal cis-regulatory elements (enhancers/promoters), a 1Mb flanking genomic window was applied around gene boundaries. These intervals were intersected with SCZ GWAS summary statistics [1], yielding an initial pool of 4,273,261 SNPs. These SNPs were subsequently functionally annotated using Functional Mapping and Annotation of GWAS (FUMA v1.6.0; [39]. Variants were assigned to NT genes based on positional mapping, expression quantitative trait loci (eQTLs), and 3D chromatin interactions using a linkage disequilibrium (LD) independence threshold of r 2 0.8.

This procedure yielded pathway-specific variant sets comprising 246,609 SNPs for ACh, 274,463 for DA, 301,221 for GABA, 864,587 for Glu (814,236 for Glu Lite ), and 170,366 for 5-HT. Finally, to guarantee pathway independence and eliminate collinearity across polygenic scoring vectors, any SNP overlapping multiple systems within the set of 777,473 unique SNPs was excluded from the pathway-specific architectures. Thus, the final mutually exclusive pathway-specific variant sets comprised 56,104 SNPs for ACh, 80,364 for DA, 75,616 for GABA, 536,152 for Glu, and 29,237 for 5-HT (Figure 1; Table S2). Independence for each of the pathwayspecific SNP sets was verified using LD (r2 ≥ 0.8), confirming their exclusivity (100% independence for ACh, 99.97% for DA, 99.98% for GABA, 99.98% for Glu, and 100% for 5-HT).

Polygenic score (PGS) generation

We computed a gPGS for SCZ (gPGS SCZ ) and pathway-specific sPGSs (sPGS ACh , sPGS DA , sPGS GABA , sPGS Glu , and sPGS 5-HT ) using PRSice-2 v2.3.5 [40], based on the European-ancestry SCZ GWAS summary statistics [1]. To prevent sample overlap, leave-one-out summary statistics excluding the CIBERSAM cohort were used.

Following the framework established by [41], PGS were generated using a clumpingand-thresholding (C+T) approach across a grid of 100 parameter combinations. This matrix comprised 10 P-value thresholds (1, 0.5, 0.1, 5x10 -2 , 5x10 -3 , 5x10 -4 , 5x10 -5 , 5x10 -6 , 5x10 -7 , and 5x10 -8 ) and 10 LD r 2 clumping thresholds (1, 0.9, 0.8, 0.7, 0.6, 0.5, 0.4, 0.3, 0.2, and 0.1). To avoid the overfitting inherent in selecting a single optimal threshold, we performed PCA across the resulting 100-score matrix. For each participant, we kept the PC1, capturing the dominant shared variance across all combinations. In the CIBERSAM cohort, PC1 accounted for 80% of the variance for the gPGS and 61%, 69%, 66%, 68%, and 73% across the ACh, DA, GABA, Glu, and 5-HT sPGSs, respectively. Corresponding values in the PAFIP cohort were 85%, 57%, 72%, 64%, 67%, and 63%. Similar results for the PC1 variance were observed in the Glu Lite sensitivity analysis (see Supplementary Methods). The resulting PC1 were regressed on sex and the first 10 ancestry PCs, and the resulting residuals were retained as the final adjusted PGS for all downstream analysis.

Individual stratification and profiling

Individuals in the CIBERSAM cohort (both SCZ cases and HC) were stratified into deciles based on their gPGS SCZ values to evaluate their case-control discriminatory capacity. Logistic regression models were used to estimate the relative odds of SCZ diagnosis across deciles. The 5 th decile was used as the reference for calculating Odds Ratios (ORs) with 95% Confidence Intervals (CIs). Global polygenic explanatory power was quantified in the continuous liability scale (r 2 liability ) using the transformation proposed in [42], assuming a population prevalence (K) of 1% for SCZ spectrum disorders (Table S4).

To translate continuous polygenic distributions into categorical strata, participants (both patients and HC) were classified according to their NT-specific sPGS. Individuals with sPGS values in the highest decile (10 th decile) of a given NT pathway, were defined as a High-Risk group for that specific pathway. Subsequently, each individual's top-decile status was mapped across all five NT systems. The profiles were named according to the corresponding NT-risk as ACh HR , DA HR , GABA HR , Glu HR , and 5-HT HR . Individuals in the top decile for more than one NT pathway were excluded from the analyses.

Individuals who did not rank in the top decile for any of the five evaluated NT-pathways constituted the Reference baseline group (Figure 2B and S2).

High-risk status was defined a priori as an sPGS on the 10 th decile to capture individuals at the extreme tail while retaining sufficient sample size for downstream comparisons. To assess whether the observed associations depended on this specific cutoff, complementary sensitivity analyses were performed employing less stringent decile thresholds to classify High-Risk and Reference individuals (see Supplementary

Methods).

Although decile thresholds were established using the full cohort, downstream analyses were restricted to patients. Sex distribution across pathway risk profiles was evaluated using chi-square (χ²) tests, with statistical significance defined as P < 0.05.

Continuous phenotypes -specifically AAO and PANSS subscale scores in the CIBERSAM cohort -were evaluated across genetic profiles using two-sided Wilcoxon rank-sum tests. Binary longitudinal outcomes, specifically 3-year treatment response and remission status in the PAFIP cohort, were evaluated using Fisher's exact test. To control the type I error rate across multi-pathway hypothesis testing, all P-values across each studied phenotype were adjusted using the Benjamini-Hochberg False Discovery Rate (FDR) procedure. Statistical significance was defined as FDR < 0.05 and reported additionally to nominal P-values.

Results

Neurotransmitter-specific polygenic scores and participant stratification

We defined five NT-specific genetic architectures for ACh, DA, GABA, Glu, and 5-HT using curated gene sets and functional variant annotation. Variants shared across pathways were removed to generate mutually exclusive variant sets for downstream sPGS analyses (see Methods; Figure 1 and S1; Table S1).

To isolate system-specific signals from global liability, we generated a genome-wide polygenic score for SCZ (gPGS SCZ ) and five pathway-stratified scores (sPGS) restricted exclusively to variants mapped within each curated NT gene set. Scores were residualised against biological sex and the top 10 genomic principal components (PCs) to control for population stratification in subsequent analyses.

The gPGS SCZ robustly discriminated cases from HC (P = 2e -16 ), explaining 6.4% of the variance in SCZ liability. Among the NT-specific scores, sPGS Glu showed the largest proportion of variance explained on the liability-scale (1.5%, P = 2.8e -14 ). This was followed by sPGS DA (0.4%, P = 5.6e -05 ), sPGS Ach (0.3%, P = 3.9e -04 ), sPGS GABA (0.3%, P = 1.2e -03 ) and sPGS 5-HT (0.3%, P = 7.3e -04 ). Because these scores were derived from non-overlapping variant sets, they may capture independent components of the overall SCZ polygenic liability (Table S4).

Stratification of individuals by gPGS deciles revealed a clear increase in the proportion of SCZ patients with increasing polygenic burden (Figure 2A and S2). Individuals in the highest gPGS SCZ decile had approximately 4-fold higher odds of SCZ diagnosis than those in the 5 th decile (OR = 3.99, 95% CI = [2.76, 5.84], P = 4.24e -10 ; Table S5).

NT-specific stratification identifies high-risk sub-architectures

To translate continuous polygenic variation into distinct genetic risk profiles, participants were stratified according to their sPGS values across the five NT pathways (see Methods). Application of this profiling framework to the CIBERSAM cohort (n = 3,044) revealed substantial heterogeneity in individual genetic profiles (Figure 2B; Table S6).

Approximately one third of the cohort (33.2%, n = 1,010) was assigned to a single High-Risk profile, with proportions ranging from 6.4% to 6.7% across the five NT systems (n = 195 ACh, 204 DA, 204 GABA, 204 Glu, and 203 5-HT). An additional group of 237 individuals (7.8%) reached the top decile for two or more systems and were not considered for further analysis. The majority of the cohort (59%, n = 1,797) did not reach the top decile for any pathway and constituted the Reference group.

Notably, these proportions (33.2%, 7.8%, 59.0%) closely matched those expected for five independent events at a 10% frequency (32.8%, 8.1%, and 59.0%, respectively).

A similar distribution of individuals was observed in the PAFIP cohort (n = 645; Table S6). Despite differences in clinical ascertainment and genotyping platform, the stratification proportions closely resembled those observed in CIBERSAM cohort. That is: 31.1% (n = 201) mapped to single-pathway High-Risk profiles, 8.4% (n = 54) to >1

High-Risk NT profile and excluded, and 60.5% (n = 390) to the Reference group. Reprofiling using the Glu Lite definition produced similar category proportions: 91.6% of the individuals were retained their original profile (Table S6).

Discussion

Understanding the profound clinical and biological heterogeneity of SCZ remains one of the challenges in modern psychiatric genomics [43]. Although GWAS have established the highly polygenic architecture of SCZ [1], standard gPGS SCZ reduce information from thousands of loci into a single value. While effective for populationlevel diagnostic case-control discrimination, they have limited ability to resolve variation in clinical phenotypes among affected individuals. In this study, we partitioned SCZ genetic liability into system-specific sPGS spanning five primary NT pathways (ACh, 5-HT, DA, GABA, and Glu) in two independent cohorts. This revealed distinct associations with AAO, symptom dimensions, and longitudinal treatment outcomes, supporting the use of biologically informed genetic stratification as a potential framework for dissecting clinical heterogeneity in SCZ.

Regarding clinical symptomatology, the ACh HR profile was associated with greater overall and general psychopathology, while both ACh HR and Glu HR showed nominal associations with greater negative symptom burden. This is clinically relevant because negative symptoms are among the most difficult symptom domains to treat effectively with current antipsychotic therapies [48, 49]. The association between ACh HR risk and psychopathology is consistent with the central role of muscarinic and nicotinic signalling in the cognitive and negative symptom domains of SCZ [21]. Disruption of cholinergic transmission can impair auditory sensory gating, attentional filtering, and motivational processing [50, 51]. This genetic architecture is also consistent with the therapeutic efficacy of dual M 1 /M 4 muscarinic receptor agonists, such as xanomelinetrospium (KarXT), which have been shown to significantly reduce PANSS symptom scores without inducing extrapyramidal motor symptoms or metabolic disruption [52].

Regarding Glu, in FEP patients, levels of Glu are correlated with PANSS scores [53], and have been reported to induce both positive symptoms and cognitive deficits [24]. In contrast, positive psychotic symptoms showed no significant differences across NTspecific profiles in our study. This finding supports the hypothesis that hallucinations and delusions may represent convergent downstream manifestations of network dysfunction arising from upstream genetic mechanisms [17, 54].

Standard first-line antipsychotics primarily exert their therapeutic efficacy primarily through dopamine D2 receptor antagonism [55]. It has consequently been hypothesised that the ~30% of SCZ patients who are treatment-resistant represent a distinct "normodopaminergic" subtype driven by upstream cortical dysfunctions rather than primary striatal dopamine hyperactivity [54, 56]. Our findings are consistent with this hypothesis, suggesting that both GABAergic and glutamatergic genetic profiles are linked to an increased likelihood of persistent symptoms over the 3-year follow-up period. While global polygenic liability was not associated with 3-year treatment outcomes, the Glu HR profile was associated with an increased likelihood of nonremission. There were also additional nominal associations between Glu HR and treatment non-response, as well as between GABA HR liability and non-remission.

Consistent with this hypothesis, DA HR showed no association with treatment resistance.

This suggests that, when disease liability is driven by lesions in the canonical dopaminergic pathway, the resulting clinical phenotype may be highly responsive to standard D2 blockade. However, when the aetiology is rooted in Glu or GABA signalling networks, downstream D2 antagonism is pharmacologically insufficient, manifesting clinically as treatment-resistant SCZ. Our findings, also align with neuroimaging data demonstrating that treatment-resistant patients lack the elevated striatal dopamine synthesis capacity observed in treatment-responsive patients [18, 57]. Recent studies highlight disruptions in glutamatergic neurotransmissionparticularly N-methyl-D-aspartate (NMDA) receptor hypofunction-as well as GABAergic interneuron signalling [1, 58]. Additionally, rare variant studies have implicated genes involved in synaptic pruning and NMDA receptor subunits function in cognitive impairment and treatment resistance [16, 59, 60].

AAO demonstrates substantial additive heritability [4]. However, studies have either failed to detect robust associations or have only detected small variances in global polygenic burden, possibly due to signal dilution through non-developmental pathways [4, 44, 45]. By shifting the analytic focus, our results revealed that genetic liability predisposition concentrated within the glutamatergic pathway (Glu HR ) significantly accelerates disease manifestation, bringing forward the onset of first psychotic symptoms by more than two years. Glutamatergic signalling is essential for cortical maturation, synaptic plasticity, and synaptic pruning during adolescent neurodevelopment [46, 47]. Consequently, a high genetic burden within these excitatory networks likely disrupts normal synaptic refinement, potentially contributing to earlier disease onset. This pathway-specific association is consistent with recent large-scale and functional genomic analyses demonstrating that SCZ risk variants are highly enriched in excitatory pyramidal neurons active during adolescent remodelling, [1, 11].

There are several methodological limitations that warrant further consideration. First, to analyse the specific effects of NT and avoid multicollinearity, we excluded pleiotropic SNPs shared by multiple NT systems. While necessary to isolate pathway-specific main effects, it inherently discards 'hub' genes that are likely to contribute to complex psychiatric phenotypes. Second, as the NT-specific risk architectures were derived from GWAS summary statistics of European ancestry, the trans-ethnic portability of these genetic architectures is unknown. Third, our models lack granular data on antipsychotic medication, dosage, and cumulative exposure. As these treatments can cause significant changes to NT networks, there is a potential risk of confounding factors. This highlights the need for well-characterised cohorts with detailed medication exposure data in future investigations. Finally, due to sample size constraints, we were unable to perform fully powered sex-stratified analyses. Given well-established sex differences in SCZ age at onset, clinical presentation, and disease trajectories, larger multi-site cohorts will be required to determine whether pathway-specific risk architectures exhibit sex-dependent differential prognostic utility.

In conclusion, this study supports the potential value of biologically informed polygenic stratification for investigating clinical heterogeneity in SCZ. While global polygenic burden fails to differentiate symptom profiles, stratifying this monolithic risk into NTspecific sub-architectures reveals association with certain outcomes in SCZ.

Specifically, genetic liability within the glutamatergic, GABAergic and cholinergic pathways is associated with an earlier disease onset, greater psychopathology, and an increased risk of treatment non-remission. NT-specific polygenic scores may be particularly valuable in the early stages of psychosis by identifying distinct genetic risk profiles associated with symptom severity and long-term clinical outcomes, potentially enabling earlier risk stratification and more personalised therapeutic approaches. 776
