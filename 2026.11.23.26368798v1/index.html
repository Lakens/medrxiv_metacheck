Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. Abstract

Background. Linguistic barriers between patients and providers cause diagnostic error, poor adherence, and adverse events, and disproportionately affect speakers of low-resource languages. Kinyarwanda, spoken by more than 20 million people, has a small digital footprint, and the quality of off-the-shelf machine translation (MT) for its clinical dialogue is poorly documented.

We evaluated six MT systems (GPT-5, GPT-4o-mini, Gemini 2.5 Flash, Azure Translate, Google Translate, and NLLB-200) for Kinyarwanda-to-English patient-provider conversations. Using a professional reference corpus of 600 conversations, stratified across three pandemic periods and collected in Rwanda via the WelTel platform, we assessed automated metrics (semantic similarity, BLEU, chrF, and translation edit rate [TER]) and 0-100 quality ratings from three professional Kinyarwanda-English translators, with reliability quantified by intraclass correlation (ICC). De-identified secondary data were approved by UBC (H20-01031) and the Rwanda National Ethics Committee (100/RNEC/2020). Findings. On the full 600-conversation cohort, GPT-4o-mini, GPT-5, and Gemini 2.5 Flash had closely clustered semantic similarity (0.877, 0.874, 0.873), well above Azure Translate (0.809) and NLLB-200 (0.754); Gemini 2.5 Flash led on BLEU (43.13) and the lowest TER (68.15). Translators ranked GPT-5 and Gemini 2.5 Flash highest (means 81.55 and 81.51), then GPT-4o-mini (72.67), Azure Translate (55.16), and NLLB-200 (39.05); averagerater reliability was moderate (ICC 0.594, rising to 0.701 on a balanced 85-conversation subset). Automated ngram metrics understated the quality gap human raters perceived between large language models and conventional systems. Interpretation. GPT-5 and Gemini 2.5 Flash are the strongest off-the-shelf systems for Kinyarwanda-English clinical translation and primary choices for human-in-the-loop use, with GPT-4o-mini a cost-efficient alternative. NLLB-200 underperformed, showing that models built for low-resource languages are not automatically suited to specialised clinical domains without fine-tuning. Human evaluation should be prioritised over automated metrics alone when comparing LLM-based translation.

1 Introduction

Communication is a cornerstone of medicine; the accuracy and nuance of information exchanged between a patient and a healthcare provider directly impacts diagnosis, treatment adherence, and overall patient outcomes [1]. Ensuring communication is effective across linguistically diverse settings is therefore fundamental to achieving equitable patient outcomes. Similarly, data collected for research interviews or survey inputs should include diversity of languages to ensure study populations are well represented. However, linguistic barriers often obstruct this vital exchange for millions of people worldwide, leading to bias in data collection, health disparities and adverse medical events [2].

To address these challenges, many organizations utilize professional translation services to translate clinical and research tools and data. In the United States, for example, the Department of Health and Human Services (HHS) and National Council on Interpreting in Health Care (NCIHC) developed national standards that encourage use of professional human translators [3]. However, researchers or healthcare providers serving populations that speak low-resource languages often face a dilemma: rely on the limited availability of human interpreters or turn to technology. Machine Translation (MT) systems have become increasingly accessible and powerful, offering a seemingly instantaneous solution. Yet, the quality of these systems can vary dramatically, and an inaccurate translation in a clinical setting can have severe consequences, ranging from misdiagnosis to incorrect medication administration [4].

With most Natural Language Processing (NLP) research today focusing on only about 20 of the world's over 7,000 languages [5], a substantial technological gap remains. Kinyarwanda, a Bantu language spoken by over 20 million people primarily in Central African countries of Rwanda, Uganda, Democratic Republic of Congo and Burundi, is considered a low-resource language in the digital domain. This means it has a smaller data footprint for training natural language processing models compared to languages like English or Spanish, a challenge common to many African languages [6].

Despite the critical need, there is a lack of published research evaluating and comparing state-of-theart MT models for languages with a small digital footprint, especially within the specialized domain of healthcare. In this context, accurate translation is essential for patient-provider communication, medical records, research questionnaires, interview transcripts, patient feedback, and beyond. This study aims to fill that gap by addressing the following research question: How do leading, publicly available machine translation models perform on the task of translating Kinyarwanda-English patientprovider conversations, and which model is most suitable for this context?

To answer this question, we conduct a quantitative evaluation of six models: Google Translate, Microsoft Azure Translate, Facebook/Meta's No Language Left Behind (NLLB), Google's Gemini 2.5 Flash, GPT5, and GPT-4o-mini. By comparing their output against a professionally translated reference set, we provide actionable insights for practitioners and a baseline for future research in this important area.

2 Methodology

Our evaluation framework is based on a parallel corpus of Kinyarwanda text message conversations between healthcare providers and patients professionally translated to English, a set of MT models accessed programmatically, and a suite of evaluation metrics calculated using standard libraries. The experiments were conducted within a Python 3 environment using Jupyter Notebooks, leveraging libraries such as Pandas for data manipulation and PyTorch for model inference.

2.1 Dataset

The basis for our evaluation is a parallel corpus comprising 600 conversations between patients and healthcare providers, which were collected in Rwanda during the COVID-19 pandemic via the bidirectional text messaging platform WelTel [7]. Each conversation is defined as any interactive text exchange that includes at least one patient message of three words or more and at least one message from a public health clinician or automated system. The corpus consists of source texts in Kinyarwanda and corresponding English reference translations. The translations were produced and then crosschecked by two professional human translators with expertise in both languages and the healthcare context. This professionally curated English text serves as the high-quality "gold standard" for all subsequent metric evaluations.

To capture the dynamic evolution of conversational topics and medical concerns over the course of the COVID-19 pandemic, the conversation database was divided into three chronological periods corresponding to the initial acute symptom-reporting phase, the peak-phase of healthcare strain, and the later vaccine rollout and preventive care period. A random sample without replacement of 200 conversations from each of the three temporal periods was then drawn, yielding a stratified main cohort of 600 conversations that is representative of the full range of pandemic-era exchanges.

Google Translate was evaluated on an initial pilot cohort of 400 conversations sampled chronologically prior to the adoption of the stratified design. Following the redesign of the sampling framework, rerunning Google Translate on the full stratified cohort was not feasible due to resource constraints, and human evaluation had already been completed. Comparisons involving Google Translate are therefore restricted to the 85 conversations common to both cohorts.

2.2 Machine Translation Models and Implementation

We selected six widely used and technologically advanced MT systems. To ensure experimental consistency and minimize methodological bias, all models were applied to the same source corpus using a zero-shot approach (i.e., no task-specific fine-tuning or in-context examples). Where possible, batch translation was used to improve efficiency; individual request workflows were used where batch APIs were not available. Each model was accessed programmatically to translate the Kinyarwanda source text.

• NLLB-200 (distilled-600M): We utilized Meta AI's "No Language Left Behind" model [8] via the Hugging Face transformers library [9]. Specifically, we loaded the facebook/ nllb-200-distilled-600M model and its corresponding tokenizer (AutoTokenizer). Translations were generated in batches of 8 on an NVIDIA RTX 4080 GPU, with the source language set to kin_Latn (Kinyarwanda) and the target language to eng_Latn (English).

• Gemini 2.5 Flash: We evaluated the translation capabilities of a general-purpose Large Language Model (LLM), specifically Google's Gemini 2.5 Flash [10], by adapting it for the Kinyarwanda-to-English task via prompt engineering. This approach, conducted in a Pythonbased Google Colab environment using the google-genai SDK, relied on a carefully constructed zero-shot prompt to guide the model's behavior. For each text segment, the model was given the following instructions:

You are a machine translation service. You will be given text in Kinyarwanda. Translate it to English. Do not add any extra commentary, notes, or apologies. Only provide the raw translated text. Kinyarwanda Text: "{text}" English Translation:

While the Gemini API supports batch processing, our implementation performed the translation process sequentially for simplicity, processing one text segment at a time. The resulting English hypotheses were then saved to a new CSV file.

• Azure Translate: We used Microsoft's Azure Translator service, a cloud-based Neural Machine Translation (NMT) system, to translate our Kinyarwanda test corpus into English. The process was automated with a Python script in a Google Colab environment, using the official azure-aitranslation-text SDK and the pandas library. After authenticating to the canadaeast service region with an API key, we implemented a batch processing workflow to manage request volume efficiently. Source texts from our dataset were sent in batches of 30 segments, with each API call explicitly defining Kinyarwanda (rw) as the source and English (en) as the target language. A crucial step was including a 90-second pause between each batch request to adhere to the service's rate limits, ensuring the successful processing of the entire dataset. The resulting English translations were then systematically collected and saved to a new CSV file for subsequent metric evaluation.

• Google Translate: To establish another baseline, we employed the Google Cloud Translation API, a dedicated Neural Machine Translation (NMT) service. The implementation utilized the official google-cloud-translate Python library for the v3 version of the API, authenticated through a designated Google Cloud project. Our workflow processed the Kinyarwanda corpus sequentially, iterating through each text segment and sending an individual translation request to the API. Each request explicitly specified the source language as Kinyarwanda (rw) and the target language as English (en). Unlike other methods tested, no manual rate-limiting delays were implemented between requests; the process relied on the default API usage quotas. The resulting English translations were collected and saved to an output CSV file.

• GPT-4o-mini: OpenAI's GPT-4o-mini was evaluated by accessing it programmatically via the OpenAI API using the openai Python SDK. The model was applied using a zero-shot translation prompt structurally identical to that used for Gemini, instructing the model to translate the provided Kinyarwanda text to English without additional commentary. Translations were performed sequentially, with each text segment submitted in an individual API request. The resulting English translations were collected and saved to a CSV file for metric evaluation.

• GPT-5: OpenAI's GPT-5 was evaluated using the same OpenAI API workflow and zero-shot prompt as GPT-4o-mini, with the sole difference being the model identifier specified in the API request. This ensured that any performance differences between the two OpenAI models reflected model capability rather than methodological variation.

2.3 Evaluation Metrics and Implementation

We employed a combination of metrics to provide a holistic view of translation quality. Each metric is explained below with a non-technical description followed by its specific implementation details.

Metric Selection Rationale: We employed a combination of metrics to provide a holistic view of translation quality, focusing on semantic accuracy, fluency, and practical edit-distance. This multifaceted approach ensures a robust evaluation. To maintain scientific validity, we excluded metrics like COMET, as its available pre-trained models do not officially support Kinyarwanda, which would produce unreliable scores. Our evaluation is therefore based on the following well established metrics.

• Semantic Similarity What it means: This metric evaluates how well the machine translation preserves the core meaning of the human reference translation, even when different vocabulary or phrasing is used. It focuses on whether the two sentences convey the same essential message, which is paramount in a medical context where semantic errors can lead to dangerous clinical misinterpretations. The score ranges from 0 to 1, where 1 indicates an identical meaning and a score closer to 0 signifies that the meaning has been lost or distorted. Technical Implementation: Our semantic similarity calculation was implemented in Python using the sentence-transformers library [11]. We first loaded the paraphrase-multilingualmpnet-base-v2 model. [12]. The reference and hypothesis texts were then passed to the model.encode() function to convert them into tensor-based vector embeddings. Using these embeddings, we calculated the pairwise scores with the cos_sim function from the library's util module. The final reported score is the mean of the diagonal of the resulting similarity matrix, which corresponds to the average similarity score for all translation pairs.

• BLEU (Bilingual Evaluation Understudy) What it means: BLEU measures how much the machine's translation overlaps with the professional human translation and works by counting matching individual words and short phrases [13]. A higher BLEU score suggests the translation is structurally similar to the reference and uses much of the same vocabulary. Following standard convention for reporting BLEU scores, the resulting score, which ranges from 0 to 1, is presented on a 0-100 scale for improved readability where a higher score is better.

Technical Implementation:

The BLEU score was calculated using the sacrebleu library [14], which provides a standardized, corpus-level implementation.

• CHRF (Character n-gram F-score) What it means: Popović introduced CHRF, a simple, language-independent automatic machine translation evaluation metric based on character n-gram F-scores, which shows a strong correlation with human judgments [15]. Similar to BLEU, CHRF also measures overlap, but it works at the character level instead of the word level. Following the same convention as BLEU, the resulting score, which ranges from 0 to 1, is presented on a 0-100 scale for improved readability, where a higher score is better.

Technical Implementation:

The CHRF score was calculated using the sacrebleu library [14].

• TER (Translation Edit Rate) What it means: It calculates the number of edits (additions, deletions, or substitutions) a human would need to make to fix the machine translation and make it perfect [16]. The scoring scale starts at 0 for a perfect translation and is unbounded, meaning scores can exceed 100. For this metric, a lower score is better, as it indicates the translation is closer to the human reference and requires less correction.

Technical Implementation:

The TER score was calculated using the standardized implementation in the sacrebleu library [14].

The performance of each model across the suite of metrics was calculated by comparing their English outputs against the human reference translations.

2.4 Translation Quality Assessment by Humans

Three professional Kinyarwanda-English translators independently evaluated the translation quality of each model. Five models (GPT-5, Gemini 2.5 Flash, GPT-4o-mini, Azure Translate, and NLLB-200) were evaluated across the full 600-conversation main cohort. Google Translate was evaluated on the 85 overlapping conversations common to both the pilot and main cohorts, as described in Section 2.1. The translators were asked to assess the overall quality of each translation, with specific focus on the preservation of medical meaning, clarity and fluency, and consistency across conversation turns. They were also asked to flag translations that may be clinically misleading. A score between 0 and 100 was provided for each translation with reference to a standardized scoring scale (Table 1), which also served as the complete rating guidance provided to the translators. Inter-rater reliability was assessed using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) for consistency, reported with 95% confidence intervals, with both single-rater and average-rater estimates computed.

Statistical Analysis of Evaluator Reliability

To evaluate the reliability of the human expert panel, we calculated the Intraclass Correlation Coefficient (ICC) using a two-way mixed-effects consistency model, reporting both single-rater ICC(3,1) and average-rater ICC(3,k) metrics. Unlike standard correlation coefficients, the ICC consistency framework evaluates whether raters rank the machine translation models in the same relative order, successfully isolating true translation quality from individual rater harshness or leniency. Values were interpreted using standard benchmarks [17], where scores less than 0.5 indicate poor reliability, between 0.50 and 0.75 indicate moderate reliability, between 0.75 and 0.90 indicate good reliability, and scores greater than 0.90 indicate excellent reliability. All ICC metrics are reported with their corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CI) and p-values.

Probability values (p-values) are reported for all ICC metrics, with a significance threshold of p < 0.05. A p-value below this threshold indicates that the observed rater agreement is unlikely to have occurred by chance alone.

2.6 Interpreting Evaluation Metrics

Before analyzing individual model performance, a framework for interpreting evaluation metrics is important. Different metrics measure distinct aspects of translation quality and can sometimes conflict (e.g., high semantic similarity but low lexical overlap with a reference). In the critical healthcare domain, we argue that not all metrics are equally important and propose a clear hierarchy.

Semantic Similarity is the primary metric. It addresses the key question: "Was the clinical meaning preserved?" A high score here is necessary for a successful translation, as semantic errors can lead to dangerous clinical misinterpretations.

Secondary to meaning is the quality and efficiency of the communication, best measured by TER (Translation Edit Rate). TER quantifies this effort by calculating the number of edits needed to perfect a translation. A semantically correct translation with a low TER is ideal, as it is both accurate and easy to understand, requiring minimal "correction effort" from the reader.

Tertiary metrics like BLEU and CHRF serve as indicators of fluency and lexical style. While valuable, a low score on these metrics is not necessarily a failure if the meaning is correct. It may simply indicate that the model used different, yet still valid, phrasing than our human reference. These metrics are most useful for assessing the naturalness of the output and for breaking ties between models with similar semantic performance.

A comparison of the automated and human rankings on this subset show a divergence. On automated metrics, GPT-4o-mini and Google Translate led on semantic similarity (0.8528 and 0.8526 respectively), while GPT-5 recorded the lowest semantic similarity among the top models (0.8283). This reversal across both embedding and n-gram metrics suggests that advanced LLMs generate more natural, conversationally fluent translations that diverge lexically from the reference text, causing automated metrics to systematically underestimate their quality relative to human judgment. Researchers evaluating LLM-based translation should therefore prioritise human evaluation over automated metrics alone when comparing LLMs against traditional NMT systems.

4.1

GPT-5 and Gemini 2.5 Flash Lead the Metrics: Across both the full 600-conversation cohort and the 85-conversation overlapping subset, GPT-5 and Gemini 2.5 Flash emerged as the consistently top-performing models, demonstrating a level of robustness not observed in the other systems evaluated. On the full cohort, both models achieved near-identical consolidated human evaluation scores (GPT-5: 81.55; Gemini 2.5 Flash: 81.51), and this ranking was maintained on the overlapping subset (GPT-5: 84.95; Gemini 2.5 Flash: 83.34). Gemini 2.5 Flash additionally recorded the lowest TER score on the full cohort (68.15), indicating that its translations required the least post-editing effort relative to the reference. These results show that GPT-5 and Gemini 2.5 Flash are the primary recommendations for high-stakes clinical translation tasks where accuracy and fluency are important.

4.2 N-gram Metrics alone failed to accurately capture LLM Translation Quality

We find that traditional automated metrics, specifically BLEU, CHRF, and TER, are poorly suited to evaluating the output of advanced LLMs when compared against translations produced by conventional neural MT systems. On the 85-conversation overlapping subset, automated metrics rated Gemini 2.5 Flash (BLEU: 36.76), Google Translate (BLEU: 36.65), and GPT-4o-mini (BLEU: 35.54) as nearly equivalent. Human evaluators, however, rated Gemini 2.5 Flash 11.27 points higher than Google Translate (83.34 vs. 72.07), and GPT-5 12.88 points higher (84.95 vs. 72.07), despite Google Translate achieving a higher BLEU score than GPT-5 (36.65 vs. 34.87), and despite GPT-5 recording the lowest semantic similarity among the top models on this subset (0.8283), below Google Translate (0.8526) and GPT-4o-mini (0.8528). LLMs generate translations that are more natural and conversationally fluent, paraphrasing instead of direct word-for-word output. Because BLEU and CHRF measure lexical overlap with a single reference translation, they penalise this variance even when the meaning is superior.

4.3 Azure Translate showed moderate but Inconsistent performance:

Azure Translate occupies a middle tier in this evaluation, but its cross-subset variability warrants careful consideration. On the full 600-conversation cohort, Azure Translate received a consolidated human evaluation score of 55.16, placing it substantially below GPT-4o-mini (72.67). On the 85conversation overlapping subset, however, its score increased to 71.45, placing it statistically on par with both GPT-4o-mini (72.43) and Google Translate (72.07). This 16-point swing suggests that Azure Translate's performance is sensitive to the specific types of dialogue present in the evaluation set, and that certain conversational structures or vocabulary present in the wider corpus expose translation weaknesses not evident in the overlap subset. Practitioners should therefore exercise caution when interpreting Azure Translate evaluations conducted on small or non-representative samples: while it may appear competitive on a narrow subset, its performance can degrade significantly across a broader and more diverse corpus. For applications requiring consistent quality across varied patientprovider exchanges, a more robust system such as GPT-5 or Gemini 2.5 Flash is preferable..

4.4 Google Translate showed speed and convenience at a Measured Quality Cost

Google Translate was evaluated exclusively on the 85 overlapping conversations common to both cohorts, achieving a human evaluation score of 72.07, placing it in the middle tier alongside GPT-4omini and Azure Translate on this subset. Its automated metrics were competitive: semantic similarity (0.8526), BLEU (36.65), and CHRF (58.16) were all broadly comparable to the top LLMs on the same subset, though this comparability reflects the limitation of n-gram metrics discussed in Section 4.2 rather than equivalent fluency or naturalness. Google Translate's primary advantage remains its speed and ease of access: it processed our corpus in approximately 5 minutes with no manual rate-limiting. Its widespread availability and easily accessible web application make it a convenient tool for quick translations, particularly when dealing with a smaller number of text segments. However, it should be noted that human evaluators placed it approximately 12 points below GPT-5 and 11 points below Gemini 2.5 Flash on the same conversations, and that its outputs may require more manual review in high-stakes clinical documentation contexts.

4.5 NLLB-200 Underperformed

The NLLB-200 distilled model (600M parameters) performed poorly across all evaluation dimensions. On the full 600-conversation cohort, it recorded the lowest semantic similarity (0.7543), the lowest BLEU score (14.09), and the highest TER (129.11), indicating that its translations were frequently inaccurate and would require substantial post-editing to be usable. Human evaluators awarded it a mean score of 39.05 on the full cohort and 29.25 on the overlapping subset. Its performance on the balanced subset was notably worse than on the full cohort, further confirming that this model struggles with the specific vocabulary and conversational register present in Kinyarwanda patientprovider exchanges. This underperformance is likely attributable to a combination of the model's smaller parameter count (the distilled 600M variant was used rather than the full 1.3B or 3.3B models) and the absence of in-domain medical training data. As an open-source system, NLLB-200 does retain the potential for domain adaptation, and fine-tuning on a dedicated Kinyarwanda medical corpus remains a promising avenue for future work.

4 Limitations and Future Work

First, our implementation choices, such as using a sequential process for Gemini, influenced the processing times; future work could explore the speed implications of fully optimized batch processing for all APIs.

Second, future work could also involve a blind review of the translations by medical professionals and Kinyarwanda speakers to assess their practical usability and safety. Furthermore, investigating the potential of fine-tuning models like NLLB on a dedicated Kinyarwanda medical corpus could be a promising avenue for developing a truly specialized and high-quality translation tool.

Third, Google Translate was evaluated on an earlier pilot cohort and could not be re-run on the full stratified sample due to resource constraints, limiting its direct comparability with the other five models.

5 Conclusion

In response to our research question, this study establishes a performance hierarchy among six offthe-shelf MT systems for Kinyarwanda-English healthcare translation, combining automated metrics with independent human expert evaluation to provide a multi-dimensional assessment.

GPT-5 and Gemini 2.5 Flash are the top-performing systems across both automated and human evaluation criteria, achieving the highest human quality scores on the full cohort (81.55 and 81.51, respectively) and maintaining those rankings on the overlapping subset. For practitioners where translation quality is the highest priority, either system represents a well-supported primary choice. Gemini 2.5 Flash additionally offers the lowest post-editing effort (TER: 68.15) among the full-cohort models, making it particularly attractive for workflows involving human review.

GPT-4o-mini achieved the highest automated semantic similarity score on the full cohort (0.8770) and a human evaluation score of 72.67, placing it as a capable and cost-efficient alternative where budget constraints preclude the use of higher-tier models.

Azure Translate and Google Translate occupy a middle tier on human evaluation, with competitive automated metrics but lower fluency ratings from professional translators. Azure Translate's performance variability across evaluation subsets (55.16 on the full cohort vs. 71.45 on the overlapping subset) suggests it should be validated on a representative sample before broad deployment. Google Translate remains the fastest and most accessible option for near-instantaneous use cases.

NLLB-200 underperformed across all metrics, underscoring that models designed for low-resource languages are not automatically suited to specialised clinical domains without fine-tuning.

Ultimately, this evaluation serves a data-driven guide for healthcare providers, NGOs, global health, health equity researchers, technology implementers etc. By choosing the right tool and ensuring validation, they can dramatically speed up and reduce the cost of translation, effectively bridge the communication gap and ensuring that Kinyarwanda-speaking patients receive safer and higher-quality care.
