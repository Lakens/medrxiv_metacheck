Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 225 papers containing 405 instances of non-exact p-values, the module correctly detected 269 cases (true positives) and incorrectly identified 78 (false positives). It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. Introduction

Background

Conversational agents, commonly called chatbots, have moved rapidly from novelty to routine components of digital health delivery. A scoping review of the health care literature identified 47 reports describing conversational agents used for various roles in health care (e.g., treatment and monitoring, health service support, patient education) concluded with a call for rigorous evaluation of their acceptability, safety, and effectiveness (Tudor Car et al., 2020). Other systematic reviews in specific domains have reached similar conclusions: chatbot interventions for physical activity, diet, and weight management show promise but rest on a small and methodologically uneven Evaluation of Chatbot Acceptability Scale, Page 3 evidence base (Oh et al., 2021), and reviews of conversational agents for chronic and mental health conditions describe a field still in an early, prototype-dominated stage (Berube et al., 2021; Schachner et al., 2020). Interest in these tools is driven by their scalability, their low threshold for access, and their capacity to deliver behavior-change support outside of scheduled clinical contact.

Middle-aged and older adults are a target population for this work, both because chronic disease burden concentrates in later life and because brain health and dementia risk reduction are increasingly framed as lifestyle interventions requiring sustained engagement. However, the determinants of technology acceptance in this group may differ from those in younger people. The Almere model, developed specifically to explain older adults' acceptance of assistive social agents, showed that variables relating to social interaction contributed explanatory power beyond perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use (Heerink et al., 2010). A meta-analysis of 35 studies confirmed that perceived usefulness, perceived ease of use, and social influence predict behavioral intention among older adults, while also showing that individuals' acceptance of unfamiliar health technologies may depend on different factors from more familiar consumer devices (Ma et al., 2021). More recent chatbotspecific work has extended these models further, adding technology anxiety, selfefficacy, perceived physical condition, and self-actualization needs (Wu et al., 2025; Yu & Chen, 2024). In addition, one study of community-dwelling older adults found that stronger anthropomorphism of a social chatbot predicted lower intention to use it (Sou et al., 2026). Taken together, this literature establishes that the determinants of Evaluation of Chatbot Acceptability Scale, Page 4 technology acceptance in younger or general-population samples cannot be assumed to be the same in older populations.

Overall, it appears that the term acceptability lacks a rigorous definition. In an overview of 43 systematic reviews of health care interventions, Sekhon and colleagues found that none explicitly defined or theorized acceptability, and that most studies inferred it indirectly from behavior such as consent rates, adherence, or dropout (Sekhon et al., 2017). The authors subsequently characterized acceptability as illdefined, under-theorized, and poorly assessed, and proposed the Theoretical Framework of Acceptability as a multi-construct alternative (Sekhon et al., 2018), later operationalized as a generic questionnaire (Sekhon et al., 2022). A parallel review of the mobile health literature reported that terms such as acceptability, acceptance, and adoption are used inconsistently. Additionally, the authors noted that existing models are rarely adapted to populations with particular needs, and that custom, unvalidated surveys are the predominant measurement approach (Nadal et al., 2020).

These problems are especially acute in chatbot research. In a scoping review of 32 studies evaluating conversational agents for mental health, 203 distinct outcome measurement instruments were identified (Jabir et al., 2023). Nearly three quarters of these instruments appeared in only a single study, and no validity evidence was cited for more than half. Of those, the large majority had been created or adapted specifically for the study in which they appeared. Independent reviews of usability assessment in health conversational agents (Denecke & May, 2022), of voice-based agents for chronic and mental health conditions (Berube et al., 2021), and of chatbots delivering cancer genetic risk assessment (Scalia et al., 2026) have each reported the same pattern of Evaluation of Chatbot Acceptability Scale, Page 5 non-comparable, ad hoc measurement. The absence of shared metrics was sufficiently pressing that a Delphi study was convened specifically to build consensus on an evaluation set for health chatbots (Denecke et al., 2021).

Instruments with established psychometric properties exist, but they do not address this issue. The Chatbot Usability Scale (Borsci et al., 2022; Borsci et al., 2023) and the Chatbot Usability Questionnaire (Holmes et al., 2019) are chatbot-specific and psychometrically evaluated; however, both measure usability and satisfaction with interaction rather than acceptability, and both were developed outside of health care contexts. Also, the Artificial Social Agent Questionnaire (Fitrianie et al., 2025) similarly targets interaction experience. Several chatbot acceptance scales have been published recently, but they were developed and validated in higher-education samples of students and teachers (Ibrahim & Nemt-allah, 2026; Polat & Renner, 2026). On the health side, the Artificial Intelligence in Mental Health Scale is brief and psychometrically sound but measures general attitudes toward AI chatbots as a category of mental health support rather than acceptability of a specific deployed intervention (Katsiroumpa et al., 2025), and the Digital Health Acceptability Questionnaire (Haydon et al., 2023), though grounded in the Theoretical Framework of Acceptability (Sekhon et al., 2017), was developed and validated for telephone and videoconference telehealth. The most widely cited study of health chatbot acceptability used a purpose-built survey that does not have readily available psychometric data (Nadarzynski et al., 2019).

In summary, the result is a specific and consequential gap. No brief, theorygrounded, psychometrically evaluated measure is available for assessing the acceptability of health-focused conversational agents among middle-aged and older adults. This is important since this is the population in which acceptance determinants are known to diverge and the population targeted by a growing number of chatbotdelivered health interventions. Investigators developing such interventions are left choosing between long usability instruments that measure something adjacent, acceptance scales validated in students, and locally written items with unknown properties.

This Study

We report the preliminary psychometric evaluation of a brief measure of chatbot acceptability developed and administered within a development study of Smart Learning and Engagement for Effective Personalized Sleep Using Artificial Intelligence (SLEEP-AI), a behavioral intervention incorporating a conversational agent that delivers tailored sleep-health information to people with sleep difficulties. The measure comprises 10 items drawn from constructs central to the Technology Acceptance Model (TAM) (Davis, 1989) and the Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB) (Ajzen, 1991), selected for brevity so that it could be embedded in a development protocol without adding excessive participant burden. We drew items from TAM and TPB rather than from the Theoretical Framework of Acceptability (Sekhon et al., 2017) because the target construct was intention to use a specific conversational agent rather than the acceptability of a broader care process. The aims of these analyses were to ( 1) describe item-level performance and distributional characteristics of the 10-item measure in a development sample of 20 participants; (2) estimate internal consistency; (3) examine the measure's latent structure using exploratory factor analysis; and (4) provide an initial test of convergent validity against an established usability instrument. Consistent with the developmental stage of both the intervention and the instrument, these analyses are preliminary. The sample was determined by the requirements of the development study rather than by a target for scale validation. The exploratory results reported are intended to establish whether the measure performs well enough to warrant formal validation in a larger, independent sample, not to establish the measure as a validated tool.

Ten candidate items were developed to represent perceived information quality and credibility (drawing on TAM's perceived usefulness) and acceptance constructs from the TPB (attitude, subjective norm, perceived behavioral control, and behavioral intention). The SUS is a widely used 10-item usability instrument scored 0-100, with higher scores indicating better perceived usability (Brooke, 2013). Standard scoring, including reversal of negatively worded items, was applied. The SUS served as the external anchor for convergent validity for which scores near or above the established average of approximately 68 indicate good perceived usability (Bangor et al., 2008). the mean of their constituent items, preserving the 1-5 response metric; the total score was computed as the sum of all 10 items (possible range 10-50). The SUS was scored using standard procedures (Brooke, 2013). Item discrimination was indexed by corrected item-total correlations (the correlation of each item with the sum of the remaining items on its subscale), with values of .30 or greater conventionally considered adequate.

Reliability.

Internal consistency was estimated with Cronbach's alpha for the total scale and for each subscale, with 95% confidence intervals computed by Feldt's method (Feldt et al., 1987), along with alpha-if-item-deleted to identify items whose removal would raise reliability. Alpha values of .70 or greater were considered adequate for research use (Boateng et al., 2018). Because alpha increases with inter-item correlation, values above approximately 0.95 were interpreted as possible item redundancy rather than as evidence of superior measurement.

Dimensionality was examined with exploratory factor analysis using principal axis factoring. Factorability was evaluated with the Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) measure of sampling adequacy and Bartlett's test of sphericity. Both orthogonal (varimax) and oblique (direct oblimin) rotations were examined. Since the theory does not require the factors to be uncorrelated, the oblique solution is reported as primary and the interfactor correlation is presented. Individual response patterns were inspected for atypical cases, whose influence on the solution was examined by repeating the analysis with them excluded.

Convergent validity

was assessed with Pearson correlations between the full scale and derived subscale scores (computed as item means) and the SUS total.

Phase comparisons.

Because the intervention was revised between Phases 2 and 3, total and subscale acceptance scores and SUS totals were compared between phases using independent-samples t tests with Welch's correction for unequal variances. Given the small group sizes and the negatively skewed distributions, Mann-Whitney U tests were run as a sensitivity check. Effect sizes are reported as Hedges' g (positive values indicate higher scores in Phase 3). SUS scores, which were also collected in Phase 1, were compared across all three phases with one-way ANOVA and the Kruskal-Wallis test. To determine whether phase differences in acceptance and usability moved together, and whether pooling across phases inflated the convergent validity estimates, we compared the acceptance-SUS correlations within each phase with the pooled correlations and computed partial correlations between acceptance and SUS controlling for phase.

Interpretability of a small-sample factor analysis.

The adequacy of a sample for factor analysis depends less on the raw case-to-item ratio than on the level of communality and the degree to which factors are overdetermined (i.e., defined by several strongly loading items). Under conditions of high communality and welloverdetermined factors, factor solutions recover population structure well even at small sample sizes (MacCallum et al., 1999), and a factor defined by four or more loadings above .60 may be stable largely independent of sample size (Guadagnoli & Velicer, 1988). These conditions are used below to assess the interpretability of the present solution.

Within phases, correlations between the total score and the SUS were r = .81 (p = .008) in Phase 2 and r = .36 (p = .27) in Phase 3. The weaker Phase 3 association is consistent with the restricted range of both measures in that phase, with most participants near the ceiling on each.

Discussion

This pilot study developed a brief 10-item measure of health chatbot acceptance grounded in the TAM and TPB frameworks and examined its psychometric properties in a small sample of predominantly middle-aged and older adults. The measure showed a coherent two-factor structure (i.e., Information Quality, Acceptance) that was invariant across rotation methods, provided high internal consistency, and produced convergent validity against an established usability instrument. The moderate correlation between the two factors indicates that perceptions of information quality and behavioral acceptance are related but separable, consistent with the theoretical distinction between the credibility and usefulness of delivered information and the disposition to adopt and continue using the agent.

The observed structure is theoretically interpretable. Factor 1 aligns with the TAM's dimension of perceived usefulness, extended to include the comprehension, credibility, and trustworthiness of health information. These dimensions are especially salient when an automated agent delivers health content, and when users are older adults for whom trust and comprehension are recurrent themes in the acceptance literature (Heerink et al., 2010; Ma et al., 2021; Sou et al., 2026). Factor 2 collapses several TPB constructs (i.e., attitude, subjective norm, perceived behavioral control, behavioral intention) into a single acceptance dimension. That these TPB constructs did not separate empirically is unsurprising given the use of a single indicator per construct.

The subjective-norm item ("friends/family would approve") was the most complex, showing the largest secondary loading, consistent with subjective norm being the TPB construct least redundant with attitude and intention. Although we drew items from TAM and TPB, the two emergent factors map broadly onto constructs in the Theoretical Framework of Acceptability (Sekhon et al., 2017). These included Information Quality onto perceived effectiveness and intervention coherence, and Acceptance onto affective attitude and self-efficacy.

The between-phase comparison helps explain the wide spread of scores in this small sample. Participants in Phases 2 and 3 rated different versions of the study intervention, and both acceptance and usability were substantially higher, and less variable, in Phase 3, with effect sizes above one standard deviation. Part of the variability in the pooled sample therefore reflects changes in the intervention rather than individual differences in disposition toward chatbots. This has two implications. First, it is encouraging for the measure's intended use in formative evaluation in that the scale appears sensitive to changes in the intervention participants were rating. Further, those changes paralleled independently assessed usability on the SUS. The items that changed most (relevance, understanding, and subjective norm) are plausibly those most affected by changes in the intervention, whereas belief and trust in the information were high in both phases and changed least.

Limitations

The principal limitation of this exploratory study is the small sample (N = 20). The conditions under which previous reports have shown that analyses using small samples may recover factor structure were met, with high item communalities and factors determined by five items each with loadings greater than 0.60 (Guadagnoli & Velicer, 1988; MacCallum et al., 1999). Given the small sample, however, these results remain preliminary. The very high inter-item correlations produced a near-singular correlation matrix, reflecting redundancy among the most similar items, belief and trust. As a result, this suggests that near-duplicate items should be consolidated in a revised version of the scale. The separation between the two factors was sensitive to a single respondent with an atypical response pattern. With this case excluded, the subscale intercorrelation rose from r = .47 to r = .80. The factor structure should therefore be retested in an independent sample. Finally, the sample was drawn from a single sleep application pilot study and was demographically specific. Although the measure is intended for middle-aged and older adults, the sample included three participants younger than 40 and eight in their 40s, and was too small to test whether the measure functions equivalently across age groups. Generalizability to other chatbots, populations, and health domains is unknown.

Future Directions

Confirmatory factor analysis was not appropriate for these data since fitting a two-factor confirmatory model to the same sample that generated the structure would be circular, and the model's parameter count exceeds the available cases (Wolf et al., 2013). The necessary next step is replication in an independent, adequately powered sample, in which the pre-specified two-factor structure can be tested to evaluate the solution found in this pilot. A revised item set that consolidates redundant Information Quality items and strengthens the underrepresented subjective-norm content with additional items would improve the measure. Following best-practice scale-development guidance (Boateng et al., 2018), subsequent work should add test-retest reliability, discriminant validity, and evaluation of measurement invariance across age. An a priori power analysis for the confirmatory model, for example by Monte Carlo simulation using the present solution as the population model, may be useful in determining the sample size required for that next stage.

Conclusion

A brief, theory-based measure of health chatbot acceptance suggested a coherent two-factor structure, preliminary reliability, and convergent validity against an established usability scale in a pilot sample predominantly including middle-aged and older adults. These provisional findings support the measure as a candidate instrument for formative evaluation of health chatbots in this population and provide a basis for confirmatory testing in future, adequately powered research.
