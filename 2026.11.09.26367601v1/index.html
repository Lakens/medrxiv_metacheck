Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). Statcheck might still miss one-sided tests that are described in another sentence, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Here, we report a multi-ancestry genome-wide association study of 1,955 serum and urine metabolites in 4,431 European American and African American participants from the Atherosclerosis Risk in Communities study. Introduction

Genome-wide association studies of the metabolome (mGWAS) connect genetic variation to small-molecule levels, and thereby to the enzymes and transporters that produce, convert and clear them. Targeted nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) panels such as Nightingale quantify about 250 predominantly lipoprotein, lipid, fatty-acid, amino-acid and glycolysisrelated traits in up to 619,372 individuals. 1, 2 Untargeted mass-spectrometry panels such as Metabolon resolve more than a thousand molecules, including amino acids, lipids, nucleotides, xenobiotics, and unnamed compounds. [3] [4] [5] [6] [7] The strongest signals typically fall in genes whose products act directly on the associated metabolite, 8, 9 such as the desaturase FADS1 for polyunsaturated phospholipids, and the transporter SLC16A9 for carnitine and urate 10 . Because such loci often also associate with disease, metabolites can point to candidate causal genes at disease loci and pose a mechanistic hypothesis for experimental validation.

Joint profiling of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) and plasma proteomes has led to major advances 11 in neurological disease, and CSF metabolomics has proven similarly complementary to blood. 12 For metabolomics, most efforts have focused on serum or plasma, 1, 8, 13 leaving the urine metabolome, which reflects renal handling and xenobiotic clearance, underinvestigated despite a growing number of efforts. 7, 14 Paired plasma and urine mGWAS have revealed enzymatic and transport processes at their interface, motivating joint modeling. 14 Moreover, the metabolome has been mapped in only a few multi-ancestry mGWAS. 1, 15, 16 Multi-ancestry designs exploit differences in allele frequency and linkage disequilibrium (LD) to increase power and finemapping resolution, as the Million Veteran Program (MVP) showed for both variant discovery and credible set resolution. 17 Participants of African American as well as African ancestry remain severely underrepresented in GWAS and mGWAS efforts to date with few exceptions [18] [19] [20] , despite the greater genetic diversity and shorter LD blocks on average in African American ancestry populations, and no Metabolon-based urine mGWAS in African American participants has been reported. Ancestrally diverse reference panels such as TOPMed have improved imputation accuracy in these populations. 21, 22 They also recover small insertions and deletions (indels), a variant class that earlier panels captured poorly 23 and that has therefore been largely absent from previous mGWAS. Here, we mapped the genetic regulation of 1,955 serum and urine metabolites in European American (EA) and African American (AA) participants of the Atherosclerosis Risk in Communities (ARIC) study 24, 25 , using.

Results

A catalog of 969 mQTLs across two biofluids and two ancestries

We conducted a multi-ancestry mGWAS of the serum and urine metabolome in participants of EA and AA ancestry with available genotype data and metabolomic profiling from visit 5 (2011-2013) from the ARIC study (serum: 3,607 EA / 818 AA; urine: 797 EA / 794 AA; both biofluids: 1,585; Supplementary Table 1; Extended Data Fig. 1). The participants were on average 76 years old, with a mean estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) of 72 mL/min/1.73m² (serum) and 66 mL/min/1.73m² (urine), and clinical characteristics were comparable across ancestry-by-biofluid strata (Supplementary Table 1). After stringent quality control, 1,316 serum and 1,374 urine metabolites, harmonized to 1,955 unique analytes, were analyzed within each stratum (Methods; Supplementary Table 2). Genomic inflation factors were well-controlled across all GWAS (λ range: 0.98-1.02; median = 1.001; mean = 1.001) and within each ancestry-by-biofluid stratum (EA serum: mean λ = 1.005; EA urine: 1.002; AA serum: 1.000; AA urine: 0.999; Supplementary Table 2), arguing against residual population stratification.

A total of 969 unique metabolite quantitative trait loci (mQTLs) were identified across all analyses (P < 5 × 10⁻⁸ / 1,955; Supplementary Tables 3 4, Extended Data Fig. 2). Each mQTL is a metabolite-by-locus association, with up to four mQTLs per metabolite within an ancestrybiofluid stratum (Fig. 1; Methods); because the 1 Mb window merges rather than splits nearby signals, this definition is conservative with respect to locus counts. The 969 mQTLs involve 553 unique metabolites, fall into 287 non-overlapping genomic regions and were assigned to 247 most likely underlying genes (Supplementary Table 5). Among EA participants, we identified 620 serum mQTLs for 470 metabolites and 138 urine mQTLs for 130 metabolites, and among AA participants 102 serum mQTLs for 97 metabolites and 109 urine mQTLs for 101 metabolites. Effect directions were consistent in the other biofluid for 88% (EA) and 90% (AA) of mQTLs measured in both (Supplementary Tables 3 4). Notably, 58 (6%) of the index variants were small insertions and deletions, reflecting the enhanced variant resolution of TOPMed-based imputation. The index variant explained a median of 10.7% of metabolite variance (Q1-Q3: 6.6-15.7%). Higher values in urine than in serum (median 14.8% vs. 8.8%) and in AA than in EA (14.4% vs. 9.2%) reflected winner's curse in the smaller strata rather than a difference in architecture: in AA, where serum and urine sample sizes are closely matched, the urine-versus-serum difference largely disappeared (14.5% vs. 14.2%; Methods).

Serum and urine capture complementary genetic regulation

Of the 1,955 unique metabolites 581 were only detected in serum, 639 only in urine, and 735 in both biofluids with overall aligned effect sizes across biofluids in both ancestries (Fig. 2a,b).

In the AA cohort, where we have comparable serum and urine sample sizes (Nserum = 818, Nurine = 794), 211 mQTLs were detected for 159 unique metabolites of which 58 (36%) only showed associations with their levels in serum, 62 (39%) only in urine and 39 (25%) showed associations in both biofluids (Fig. 3a, Supplementary Tables 3 4; EA: 67 metabolites with mQTLs in both biofluids, Extended Data Fig. 3). Among the metabolites measured in both biofluids, 41 exhibited biofluid-specific mQTLs, which would have been missed if only one biofluid had been analyzed. This aligns with the proportions previously reported for an EA cohort, 7 highlighting the specific value of urinary metabolite quantification and, more broadly, the importance of conducting multi-biofluid molecular GWAS. 14 The increased statistical power from larger serum sample size in EA shifted proportions accordingly (76% serum only, 12% urine only, 13% both; Extended Data Fig. 3).

To test whether this shift reflects power, we repeated the EA serum mGWAS in the 797 EA participants with paired urine measurements (Supplementary Tables 6-7; Methods). At matched sample size, the serum-specific proportion fell from 76% to 35%, urine-specific rose from 12% to 45%, and shared rose from 13% to 21%; the serum:urine mQTL ratio fell from 4.5:1 to 0.8:1, i.e. urine yielded more mQTLs than serum at equal N (Extended Data Fig. 4, Supplementary Table 8). All 115 power-matched mQTLs were recovered within 1 Mb of the original full-sample serum signals (median distance 4.4 kb), supporting that down-sampling reduces power without introducing spurious loci. These results indicate that the apparent predominance of serum-specific signals in the EA stratum reflects statistical power rather than biofluid-specific biology, and that at equal sample size urine is at least as informative as serum.

Prioritized genes point to hepatic and renal enzymes and transporters

For each mQTL, we prioritized a single candidate causal gene using a ProGeM-based framework integrating proximity, LD overlap, Variant Effect Predictor (VEP) consequences, cis-eQTL and cis-pQTL colocalization, and Polygenic Priority Score (PoPS) (Methods). Integrating European American and African American Ancestry Data to Enhance Genetic Discovery

Effect sizes replicated well between EA and AA in cross-lookups of the mQTLs (Fig. 2c,d).

Power to detect these shared effects, however, differed substantially between ancestries because of differences in allele frequency. Of the 7.0 million genetic variants shared between the EA and AA analysis, 637,000 shared variants differ in MAF by ≥0.2 between ancestries (e.g., MAFEA of 5% and MAFAA of 25% or vice versa). For a sample size of 800 and effect size of 0.4, increasing the MAF from 0.05 to 0.25 would increase the power to detect this effect size from 2.5% to 93% (37-fold gain in power; Fig. 4a). In addition, there were 1.5 million and 7.9 million variants unique to the EA and AA analysis, respectively (Fig. 4a). At index variants of EA-discovered mQTLs, minor allele frequencies were systematically higher in AA than in EA (serum: P = 0.006; urine: P = 2.2 × 10⁻⁴). The same pattern held for index variants of AAdiscovered mQTLs (serum: P = 7.7×10⁻⁵; urine: P = 1.1×10⁻⁹; Fig. 4c). In other words, mQTLs . identified in either ancestry tended to sit at variants that are more common in AA, consistent with statistical power rather than ancestry-specific biology driving ancestry-specificity.

A notable example of findings in which MAFs were greater among the EA population was a clustering of urine mQTLs with MAFEA>0.4 and MAFAA<0.15 annotated to SLC17A1-3(Fig. 4c) and attributed to the power-related candidate-ancestry-specific mQTL. These mQTLs represent genetic regulation of several glutamine derivatives at a locus well-known for its pathogen-driven selection. 30 Moreover, power to detect urinary mQTLs might also be influenced by the urine concentration. We observed higher osmolality and PQ factors for the AA sample (PPQ-factors = 3.3 × 10 -7 ; Fig. 4b; Methods). Across both biofluids, 632 mQTLs were candidate ancestry-specific (590 EA-specific, 42 AAspecific; Supplementary Tables 3 4), including 95 of the 247 urine mQTLs (62 EA-specific, 33 AA-specific) despite comparable sample sizes of the 159 implicated metabolites across ancestries (range sample size difference between ancestries = [-7.6%, 5.9%]). To evaluate whether these differences may be attributable to statistical effects of unequal variant availability or power, we applied a three-step classification based on variant availability, power in the other ancestry and effect-size heterogeneity (Methods; Extended Data Fig. 2).

Of the 632 candidates, 609 (96%) were attributed to variant unavailability or insufficient power in the other ancestry. Only 23 (4%) showed significant heterogeneity irrespective of sample size and MAF and are termed ancestry-specific (Supplementary Table 20); three of these (UNC93A, LCOR and SLC36A2) are also fine-mapped ancestry-specific signals in MESuSiE.

An African American-enriched splice variant in SLC28A1 implicates renal nucleoside reabsorption

In AA, we identified an mQTL with the index variant chr15:84886788 (G>A; β = 0.64, P = 2.82 × 10⁻¹³, N = 772, MAF=0.09) strongly associated with urinary 2'-O-methyluridine levels as well as urinary uridine (β = 0.62, P = 9.72 × 10⁻¹³, Fig. 4d), with negligible frequency in EA (gnomAD v4.1: AFR MAF = 8.5%, EUR MAF ≈ 0%, Fig. 4d), so that this candidate ancestry-specific mQTL falls into the AF-driven class above, while MESuSiE assigns it an AA-specific credible set (finemapped ancestry-specific). This variant was independently confirmed as a liver-specific splice quantitative trait locus (splice-QTL) in the GTEx Project (P = 1.2 × 10⁻²⁸). The variant disrupts the canonical 5' splice donor motif immediately following exon 2, predicted to result in exon 2 skipping and consequent alteration of the 5' untranslated region. 31 Together, this genetic . and functional evidence indicates that the SLC28A1 locus variant results in reduced expression of the concentrative nucleoside transporter CNT1 encoded by the gene (Fig. 4e). Consistent with the canonical CNT1 substrate spectrum, the effect allele was additionally associated with reduced serum cytidine in African American participants, paralleling the pyrimidine nucleoside excretion phenotype observed in Slc28a1⁻/⁻ mice 32 and in two siblings with biallelic loss-offunction SLC28A1 variants (p.Ser546Pro), who showed elevated urinary uridine, cytidine, deoxycytidine, and 5-methylcytidine. 33 The concordant increase in urinary uridine and 2'-Omethyluridine in our study extends these observations, as methylated pyrimidine derivatives including 5'-methyldeoxycytidine, 3-methylcytidine, and N4-acetylcytidine were likewise elevated in the urine of CNT1-deficient mice 32.

Allele-frequency differences rather than selection underlie most ancestry differentiation

Even in serum, where the EA sample was 4.4-fold larger, we identified nine candidate AAspecific mQTLs (Supplementary Table 4, Extended Data Fig. 7). Six of these index variants were not available in EA, including those of three fatty acid dicarboxylates associated with ADH1B (MAF 22% in AA). Of the three available in EA, two showed large MAF differences: 3beta,7alpha-dihydroxy-5-cholestenoate at HSD3B7 (31% in AA vs 1% in EA) and 3methoxytyrosine at DDC (15% in AA vs 1% in EA). The remaining variant, associated with N6methyllysine at LCOR, had a comparable MAF in both ancestries (16% in AA vs 19% in EA).

A notable example illustrating the impact of ancestry differences in allele frequency on mQTL discovery is the missense variant rs37369 (chr5:35037010:C:T; NM_031900.4:c.418G>A, p.Val140Ile) in AGXT2, encoding alanine-glyoxylate aminotransferase 2. This variant was associated with 3-aminoisobutyrate levels across both biofluids in EA, where both associations were classified as shared between ancestries because of an AA mQTL within the 1 Mb window.

In total, 3-aminoisobutyrate had three serum mQTLs (1 EA-AA shared, 1 candidate EA-specific) and two urine mQTLs (1 AA-EA shared) of which only the candidate EA-specific was not annotated to AGXT2 but rather to SLC6A13. The effect allele (T, derived allele) frequency of rs37369 differs markedly between populations, at approximately 8% in EA and 55% in AA, with substantial global variation reported across diverse populations. To our knowledge, no published genome-wide scans have reported evidence of positive selection at this locus (Fst = 0.062, 76th genome-wide percentile, empirical P = 0.24), and the observed frequency difference most likely reflects drift rather than adaptive divergence. This frequency disparity . has direct consequences for statistical power: while the variant reached genome-wide significance for 3-aminoisobutyrate in EA in both biofluids, the weaker serum effect failed to reach significance in AA, and the urine association was only marginally genome-wide significant (P ≈ 1 × 10⁻⁹). The variant replicated in GCKD, where the effect allele frequency (~8%) closely mirrors that of ARIC EA, consistent with the European ancestry of that cohort.

In the locus-level classification, the 3-aminoisobutyrate mQTLs at AGXT2 are shared between ancestries in both biofluids (Supplementary Tables 3 4), so the frequency difference modulates power at a shared locus rather than marking metabolite handling that works differently between ancestries.

A contrasting case is the UNC93A locus (chr6:167294271:C:T), one of the 23 loci that remain ancestry-specific after the classification above and also a fine-mapped ancestry-specific signal.

UNC93A was recently nominated, by a machine-learning screen of orphan solute carriers, as a candidate transporter for acetylglucosamine; cells overexpressing UNC93A showed markedly increased cellular acetylglucosamine uptake, functionally supporting this candidacy, and its genetic association with circulating acetylglucosamine had previously been reported in European-ancestry cohorts. 34 In our data, the effect allele is common in both ancestries (AA: 17.6%; EA: 10.7%) and shows a strong, GCKD-replicated association with serum Nacetylglucosamine/N-acetylgalactosamine in EA (β = 0.455, P = 4.84 × 10⁻³⁵), yet no association at all in AA (β = -0.008, P = 0.89, adequately powered at N = 818). Finemapping resolves the locus to a single variant (PIP > 0.999), making differential linkage disequilibrium an unlikely explanation for this heterogeneity; a genuine gene-by-ancestry or gene-by-environment interaction is more plausible, though distinguishing between these would require direct functional follow-up. Dietary or microbiome-driven differences in circulating acetylglucosamine are one possible explanation that warrants further investigation. UNC93A thus illustrates how population-specific genetic regulation can be layered onto a transporter whose basic substrate biology has only just been established.

We next asked whether the frequency differentials extend across the mQTL catalog, using Fst between reference populations as a measure of allele-frequency differentiation (Methods).

Fst was available for 609 of 969 index variants (63%, Supplementary Tables 3 4); for each, we report its percentile in the genome-wide Fst distribution, i.e., the share of all variants that are less differentiated between CEU and YRI. Differentiation is only modestly elevated overall (mean percentile 57.9; 9.0% of variants in the top 5% of the genome-wide distribution, against . 5% expected). Index variants of mQTLs identified in African American participants sit at a higher mean percentile than those identified in European American participants (66.1 vs. 55.9), driven mainly by signals shared across ancestries (69.2) rather than those specific to African American participants (54.7). Because allele-frequency differentiation can arise from drift as well as from selection, these data indicate at most a weak, nonpervasive contribution of population-differentiating selection to the frequency differentials described above.

Multi-ancestry finemapping resolves candidate causal variants and genes

We utilized MESuSiE 35 to finemap 992 independent signals underlying 627 unique nonoverlapping region-metabolite combinations implicated by serum mQTLs as well as 238 independent signals underlying 167 regions in urine (Supplementary Tables 21 22 23 24, Supplementary Data 1). The multi-ancestry analysis improved the ability to detect Among the 759 unique finemapped variants with high posterior inclusion probability (PIP) or part of small credible sets in either biofluid (credible set size ≤5 or PIP ≥0.5; Supplementary Tables 22 and 24), 713 were SNVs and 46 were indels. Compared to a random background set of 10,000 common variants, finemapped variants showed a markedly different consequence profile (Fig. 6a). VEP annotated the majority as MODIFIER impact (87.2%), consistent with their predominantly non-coding genomic context. Notably, 9.4% of finemapped SNVs were classified as missense, and 0.8% of all variants carried HIGH impact consequences, including splice donor variants. The proportion of HIGH impact variants was highest among indels (4.3%), followed by SNVs (0.4%), with a background rate of 0.0%, highlighting the potential of multi-ancestry-based statistical finemapping to prioritize functionally consequential variants.

To complement categorical annotation with quantitative predictions of regulatory impact, we scored all finemapped variants using AlphaGenome, 36 a deep learning model that predicts molecular effects across chromatin accessibility, transcription factor binding, histone . modification, and gene expression tracks in hundreds of cell types (Supplementary Tables 22 and 24). As with other in-silico predictions used for gene and variant prioritization throughout this study, AlphaGenome scores are hypothesis-generating and were not experimentally validated here. Of the 759 variants, 746 (98.3%) showed at least one high-confidence predicted effect (|quantile score| > 0.99; Methods; Supplementary Note 1). A total of 251 variants (33.1%) showed predicted disruption of at least one transcription factor binding site in any tissue. Several established regulators of hepatic and renal gene expression were among the most frequently disrupted transcription factors: SP1 (53 variants, 21.1% of TF-hit variants), CEBPB (43, 17.1%), RXRA and FOXA1 (35 each, 13.9%), FOXA2 (32, 12.7%), and HNF4A (25, 10.0%). Of these, HNF4A, FOXA1, FOXA2, and RXRA are liver-enriched transcription factors that cooperatively regulate metabolic gene programs in liver and kidney 37, 38 , while SP1 and CEBPB are broadly expressed transcription factors with known roles in hepatic gene regulation 39, 40 . When restricting to metabolically relevant tissues (Methods), kidney was the most frequent top organ (228 of 687 annotated variants, 33.2%), followed by liver (176, 25.6%), consistent with the renal and hepatic origin of many circulating and urinary metabolites.

Among the 251 unique high-PIP variants with predicted transcription factor binding disruption, 25 (10.0%) were predicted to affect HNF4A binding in primary liver tissue or HepG2. HNF4A is of particular biological interest given its established role as a master regulator of hepatic lipid and fatty acid metabolism. One such variant, chr11:61820833:A:G (rs174564), annotated by VEP as an upstream gene variant of FADS1 on chromosome 11, disrupts a predicted HNF4A binding site in liver and appeared in credible sets for 17 serum metabolites spanning phosphatidylcholines, plasmalogens, diacylglycerols, and free arachidonate -all substrates or products of the FADS1/2 desaturation pathway. For ten of these metabolites, the variant was the sole member of the credible set (PIP > 0.96, Supplementary Table 22), making it one of the most confidently finemapped multi-metabolite variants in our study. Incorporated annotation resources indicate an involvement of FADS1, FADS2, and FEN1 in the region with multiple colocalization signals with gene expression of all three genes. For EA mQTLs associations were mapped to FEN1 for 9 metabolites and FADS1 for 7, while the AA gene assignment, available for 4 metabolites (3 shared, 1 AA-specific), consistently pointed to FADS1, including one AA-specific singleton credible set for 1-stearoyl-2-arachidonoyl-GPC (18:0/20:4), a phosphatidylcholine containing arachidonic acid (PIP = . 1.00). This locus illustrates directly how multi-ancestry finemapping improves resolution: for this metabolite, the EA association is exceptionally strong (P = 6.98 × 10⁻¹³⁰) but falls within a broad region of high linkage disequilibrium, with several correlated variants reaching comparably high significance and no single candidate resolved when EA is considered alone.

The AA association, while considerably weaker (P = 1.67 × 10⁻¹⁴), shows a more localized pattern around the same variant; jointly modeling both ancestries with MESuSiE collapses the credible set to this single variant (PIP > 0.999), despite AA's lower power (Fig. 6b).

AlphaGenome predicted that the alternate allele disrupts binding of multiple hepatic transcription factors at this site, most strongly HNF4A and RXRA in liver (Fig. 6c, Supplementary Table 22). Experimental support for HNF4A binding at this site is provided by ChIP-seq data curated in UniBind 41, 42 , which identifies robust, quality-controlled HNF4A and RXRA binding sites overlapping rs174564 in primary liver tissue, with additional HNF4A occupancy observed in HepG2 cells. AlphaGenome predicted reduced DNase accessibility across liver, kidney, and HepG2, consistent with chromatin closure at the variant position.

Notably, the CAGE transcription initiation tracks revealed a strand-dependent effect: on the positive strand (corresponding to FADS2), predicted CAGE signal was reduced in both liver and kidney, whereas on the negative strand (corresponding to FADS1), the reduction was concentrated at a distinct upstream promoter element (Fig. 6c). This strand-specific CAGE pattern suggests that the variant may simultaneously affect the transcriptional regulation of FADS1 and FADS2 on their respective strands, consistent with the known divergent transcription of this head-to-head gene pair and the established role of HNF4A in regulating hepatic fatty acid desaturase expression. The predictions also suggest additional regulatory effects at the FEN1 transcription start site (full 1 Mb prediction window in Extended Data Fig. 9). This locus also showed the broadest disease colocalization in our catalog (see below).

The ALDH7A1 locus on chromosome 5 illustrates how combining multi-ancestry finemapping with deep learning-based functional prediction can strengthen the case for follow-up of individual mQTL variants. Two mQTL regions were identified for serum levels of 6oxopiperidine-2-carboxylate, a key intermediate in lysine catabolism, which accumulates in the plasma and urine of patients with ALDH7A1 deficiency: a shared signal at the OPLAH locus and an EA-specific signal at ALDH7A1 on chromosome 5, encoding aldehyde dehydrogenase 7 family member A1. At the ALDH7A1 locus, the index variant was not represented in the 1000 Genomes CEU/YRI reference panel and therefore lacks an Fst estimate, consistent with its . classification as a candidate ancestry-specific signal driven by variant unavailability in EA. The EA association was strong (β = 0.54, P = 6.45 x 10 -19 , N = 3,595) whereas no significant signal was detected in AA (β = 0.28, P = 0.31, N = 811), consistent with a pronounced allele frequency difference (EAFEA = 0.04 vs. EAFAA = 0.01). Finemapping identified a single credible set of five variants with a cumulative coverage probability of 0.97. The top variant chr5:126595258:C:T carried a PIP of 0.52. This variant is also the EA index variant for the locus (Extended Data Fig 10a). Finemapping classified the credible set as EA-specific, underscoring how the multiancestry framework helps delineate ancestry-dependent genetic architecture even when one stratum contributes minimal information for finemapping above LD patterns. VEP annotated the variant as an upstream gene variant (CADD PHRED score 18.8). The variant is classified as benign/likely benign in ClinVar with respect to Mendelian disease, consistent with its role as a common regulatory variant rather than a disease-causing allele. AlphaGenome predicted tissue-differential regulatory disruption across multiple functional modalities. In the ChIP-TF output, the alternate allele was associated with reduced predicted binding of RNA Polymerase II (POLR2A) across 42 biosamples, as well as diminished binding of liver-relevant transcription factors including SP1, FOXA1, FOXA2, and RXRA (Supplementary Table 22). Notably, the predicted effect on the H3K4me3 active promoter mark was tissue-dependent: in K562, the variant was associated with a pronounced loss of H3K4me3 at the ALDH7A1 promoter, whereas in liver and kidney -the primary tissues of ALDH7A1 expression and lysine catabolism -H3K4me3 was predicted to increase (Extended Data Fig. 10b). By contrast, AlphaGenome predicted uniformly reduced DNase-seq accessibility at the variant site across all three tissues, suggesting that the variant alters chromatin accessibility broadly but exerts tissue-specific effects on promoter histone modifications. The H3K4me3 gain in liver and kidney suggests that the variant may enhance promoter activity at ALDH7A1 specifically in tissues central to lysine catabolism, consistent with the serum-specific mQTL association observed for its downstream product 6-oxopiperidine-2-carboxylate. Taken together, the convergence of a moderate-PIP finemapping signal with consistent, tissue-specific epigenomic disruption predicted by AlphaGenome supports chr5:126595258:C:T (ALDH7A1:c.-60G>T, a 5 prime UTR variant) as a high-priority candidate for experimental follow-up to understand the metabolic block in lysine catabolism, and illustrates how deep learning-based variant effect prediction can add functional confidence to statistically ambiguous finemapping results.

Colocalization links the catalog to cardiometabolic, kidney and liver disease

To link our mQTL catalog to diseases and clinical outcomes, we conducted colocalization analysis to test whether each locus shares a causal variant with 3,037 trait-ancestry outcomes from the Million Veteran Program (MVP) phenome-wide GWAS (Methods). 17 At a posterior probability of a shared causal variant (PP.H4) > 0.8, we detected colocalizations for 521 metabolite-stratum pairs (381 unique metabolites) across 125 genes and 217 MVP trait families spanning 12 disease categories (Supplementary Table 25). Quantitative traits dominated, led by serum triglycerides (182 metabolite-stratum pairs, 28 genes) and eGFR (181 metabolite-stratum pairs, 23 genes). Among binary outcomes, the strongest metabolite-level signals were gout (70 metabolite-stratum pairs, 12 genes), hyperlipidemia (73, 11), chronic kidney disease (74, 4), liver-function abnormalities (63, 6) and hypercholesterolemia (57, 14).

The implicated diseases matched the known physiology of the prioritized genes. Genes involved in lipid transport and metabolism colocalized with triglycerides, cholesterol and hyperlipidemia (e.g. ALDH1A2, UGT1A1); kidney-expressed transporters and enzymes with creatinine, eGFR and chronic kidney disease (e.g. NAT8); and urate-handling loci with gout (e.g. ABCG2, SLC22A5, GCKR). The most broadly connected genes were FADS1, a fatty-acid desaturase (31 metabolite-stratum pairs, 63 trait families spanning lipid, cardiovascular and inflammatory traits), whose signal multi-ancestry finemapping resolved to a single candidate variant (see above); SLCO1B1, a hepatic transporter of bile acids and statins (45 metabolitestratum pairs, 20 trait families including liver and lipid phenotypes); and CPS1, the rate-limiting enzyme of the urea cycle (26 metabolite-stratum pairs, 13 families centered on kidney and nitrogen-handling traits). That the diseases recovered for each gene consistently match its established function gives independent support to our gene prioritization, beyond metabolitelevel evidence alone.

A locus at ENPEP (aminopeptidase A, chr4:110.42-110.57 Mb), which cleaves angiotensin II in the renin-angiotensin system, colocalized with fibrinopeptide A degradation fragments (thrombin-cleavage products of fibrinogen, distinct from fibrinogen itself) and with blood pressure, hypertension and serum potassium traits (PP.H4 up to 1.00). The lead variant, a rare stop-gain allele in ENPEP (rs33966350), has independently been shown to reduce kidney ENPEP expression and raise hypertension risk, 43 and an association between this same variant and fibrinopeptide A fragments has been reported in an Amish cohort 44 (Extended Data Fig. 11).

. We investigated this locus further with complementary medication-based observational analyses in ARIC (Methods, Supplementary Note 2, Extended Data Fig 11). These indicate that genetically or pharmacologically reduced ACE activity specifically alters fibrinopeptide fragment levels. ACE-inhibitor use (but not angiotensin-receptor-blocker use) reproduces this pattern (e.g. FPB(1-13), P = 2 × 10⁻²⁹⁶) and in a mechanistically coherent direction, while clinical coagulation markers (fibrinogen, APTT, von Willebrand factor), available only from earlier study visits, did not differ between later ACE-inhibitor users and untreated participants in the same cohort. A plausible explanation is that ACE continues to act on the fibrinopeptide substrate pool generated during clot formation within the serum sample tube, similar in principle to a synthetic-substrate ACE activity assay, so that ACE genotype and ACE-inhibitor status at the time of blood draw are reflected in these measurements alongside any physiological signal.

A second locus, at SLC16A9 (MCT9, a renal carnitine/urate transporter, chr10:59.67-59.71 Mb), colocalized with serum acylcarnitines, urate-related traits (gout, PP.H4 up to 0.998) and coronary atherosclerosis (PP.H4 up to 0.997) simultaneously (Extended Data Fig. 12). A carnitine-mediated mechanism was biologically plausible a priori: MCT9 mediates carnitine efflux in a context-dependent manner, 45 and altered cellular carnitine handling could affect mitochondrial fatty-acid oxidation and acylcarnitine accumulation, both previously linked to cardiac lipotoxicity. Mendelian randomization (Methods, Supplementary Note 3) supported a causal effect of urate on gout and, less robustly, on coronary artery disease (significant with the inverse-variance-weighted and weighted-median estimators but not with MR-Egger), mirroring previous Mendelian randomization analyses of urate and coronary heart disease. 46 At this locus, the carnitine-and urate-transport signals were not statistically separable, so the coronary colocalization cannot be attributed to carnitine (see Discussion). A genome-wide gout instrument was similarly associated with coronary artery disease, but showed directional pleiotropy (MR-Egger intercept test), likely reflecting that a case-control gout diagnosis captures broader inflammatory/comorbidity-driven biology beyond urate handling, rather than a clean urate-specific signal.

Discussion

Our multi-ancestry genome-wide association study of paired serum and urine metabolomes in the ARIC cohort represents, to our knowledge, the first systematic investigation combining . ancestrally diverse populations with multi-biofluid metabolite profiling. We identified 969 mQTLs for 553 metabolites, replicated them in GCKD and, for urine mQTLs in the African American stratum, de novo in CRIC, and linked 521 metabolite-stratum pairs to clinical traits through colocalization with the MVP phenome. Three findings stand out. Serum and urine capture complementary genetic regulation that is not explained by sample size. Most ancestry-differentiated signals reflect allele frequency and power rather than ancestryspecific metabolite handling, yet frequency differences expose functional variants such as the SLC28A1 splice variant. Multi-ancestry finemapping and colocalization together prioritize enzymes and transporters whose disease links match their physiology.

The contribution of this catalog does not rest on locus counts alone. Looking at biofluid-and ancestry-matched studies using Metabolon, 30% of our mQTLs are not reported before, rising to 100% in the urine African American stratum, where no comparable prior resource exists, and 9% remain unreported even when a report in any biofluid or ancestry or platform is counted as prior knowledge. What cannot be obtained by combining the existing literature is the design itself: paired serum and urine metabolomes measured in the same individuals, a combination only GCKD also provides, and only in a chronic-kidney-disease population, together with a substantial African American component in both biofluids, for which essentially no prior Metabolon urine resource exists.

The complementary nature of serum and urine metabolome profiling was a central finding of our study. In the African American cohort, where sample sizes were balanced across biofluids, we observed that 36% of metabolites with mQTLs had associations detectable only in serum, 39% only in urine, and 25% in both biofluids. This distribution aligns with proportions previously reported in the GCKD study, which demonstrated that urine metabolomics can reveal genetic associations invisible to studies focusing solely on circulating metabolites. 14 The apparent serum predominance in European Americans disappeared at matched sample size, and other studies recovered our mQTLs far more often within than across biofluids (urine: 85% versus 53%; serum: 91% versus 31%), directly quantifying how much biofluid-specific effects a single-biofluid design would miss. These differences follow from physiology. Circulating levels integrate hepatic synthesis, conjugation and uptake, whereas urinary levels additionally reflect glomerular filtration and tubular secretion and reabsorption. Consistent with this, mQTL genes were enriched for liver and kidney expression; serum-only pathways centred on xenobiotic processing, lipid and fatty . acid metabolism and bile secretion; and glutathione and butanoate metabolism were enriched only in urine, while glyoxylate and dicarboxylate metabolism showed its strongest enrichment in urine, pointing to renal tubular metabolism. A variant in a tubular transporter can therefore leave a large urinary signal with a smaller change in blood, as at SLC28A1, where the effect allele raised urinary uridine and 2'-O-methyluridine but lowered serum cytidine. The AA-specific enrichment of uracil-containing pyrimidine metabolites, upstream of beta-alanine, points to further regulation that single-ancestry studies would miss.

A key motivation for our multi-ancestry design was to leverage the genetic diversity present in African American populations to improve finemapping precision. African American populations exhibit shorter linkage disequilibrium (LD) blocks and greater genetic diversity compared to European populations, features that have been shown to substantially improve the resolution of credible sets in finemapping analyses. 35 The Million Veteran Program has demonstrated that multi-ancestry approaches can identify population-specific causal variants and narrow credible sets relative to European-only analyses. 17 Consistent with these expectations, our multi-ancestry MESuSiE finemapping detected more independent signals than single-ancestry SuSiE, and the African American-specific signals yielded the most specific credible sets. Even in serum, where European American sample sizes were 4.4-fold larger, incorporating African American data improved credible set resolution, as at FADS1, where the joint model resolved a signal that European-ancestry data alone could not.

Ancestry-differentiated discovery was largely a matter of allele frequency. Of 632 candidate ancestry-specific mQTLs, only 23 (4%) showed heterogeneous effects once variant availability and power were accounted for, and index variants discovered in either ancestry were systematically more common in African Americans. Population differentiation (Fst) at index variants was only modestly elevated and higher at ancestry-shared than at candidate ancestry-specific loci among African American, arguing against widespread selection. Diverse cohorts therefore reveal biology that is shared across ancestries but detectable only where the relevant allele is common, as at SLC28A1, or that modulate power at shared loci such as AGXT2. Genuine heterogeneity, as at UNC93A, was rare and may reflect gene-by-environment interactions, for example through diet or the microbiome.

Among loci shared across ancestries, the AGXT2/rs37369 region exemplifies how multiancestry mQTL mapping can illuminate population-specific variants with clinical relevance.

The variant rs37369 has previously been identified as the genetic basis of hyper-beta-. aminoisobutyric aciduria in a previous urine metabolomics GWAS, 9 and subsequent work has confirmed that AGXT2 polymorphisms, including this variant, have considerable impact on both methylarginine and beta-aminoisobutyrate metabolism. Beyond metabolomics, the variant and the AGXT2 locus have been implicated in blood pressure regulation, 47 coronary artery disease risk, and chronic heart failure 48 , with several of these findings originating from East Asian populations where the effect allele frequency more closely resembles that observed in AA than in EA. The pleiotropic involvement of AGXT2 in both metabolite handling and cardiorenal phenotypes, combined with the pronounced population-level allele frequency variation at this locus, underscores the importance of multi-ancestry study designs for comprehensive mQTL characterization.

The SLC28A1 locus exemplifies the power of multi-ancestry approaches to identify functional variants with population-specific effects. We detected an African Amercian-enriched splicedisrupting variant associated with urinary 2'-O-methyluridine levels -a signal that would have been missed entirely in European-ancestry-only studies due to the near-absence of this allele in European populations. SLC28A1 encodes the concentrative nucleoside transporter CNT1, a sodium-dependent pyrimidine nucleoside transporter highly expressed in kidney tubular epithelium. 49 Our genetic findings provide human evidence for the potential role of SLC28A1 in methylated nucleoside transport, validating and extending observations from Slc28a1knockout mice. 32 The conserved metabolic phenotype across species underlines its physiological importance.

The functional consequences of SLC28A1 loss have been characterized in both human disease and mouse models. Biallelic loss-of-function mutations in SLC28A1 cause uridine-cytidinuria, an inborn error of metabolism characterized by elevated urinary excretion of pyrimidine nucleosides. 33 Supporting our findings, metabolomic profiling of Slc28a1-knockout mice demonstrated elevated urinary excretion of both canonical pyrimidines and methylated pyrimidine derivatives, suggesting that modified nucleosides derived from tRNA and rRNA degradation are also low-affinity substrates of this transporter. 32 Our observation that the African American-specific splice variant was associated with both increased urinary 2'-Omethyluridine, urinary uridine and reduced serum cytidine levels is consistent with impaired tubular reabsorption of pyrimidine nucleosides. Whether methylated nucleosides are direct CNT1 substrates (consistent with CNT1's established capacity to transport structurally modified nucleoside analogues) or accumulate secondarily to elevated parental nucleoside . pools and downstream modification cannot be distinguished from association data alone. The supporting evidence from a liver-specific splice-QTL in GTEx 50 strengthens the case for this variant's functional impact. This finding may have broader implications for pharmacogenomics, as CNT1 is involved in the disposition of nucleoside analog drugs, including gemcitabine and other antineoplastic and antiviral agents. 51 Population-specific functional variants in drug transporters may contribute to inter-individual variability in therapeutic response and toxicity, highlighting the importance of including diverse populations in both basic science and translational research.

Our use of TOPMed-based imputation contributed importantly to our results. 52 The TOPMed reference panel, with its substantial inclusion of African and Hispanic/Latino individuals, provides superior imputation quality for non-European populations compared to earlier European-centric panels. Notably, 58 (6%) of our mQTL index variants were insertions or deletions (indels), a class of variants that earlier imputation panels often failed to capture adequately. Indels can have substantial functional consequences but have been underrepresented in GWAS catalogs; their detection in our study reflects both improved imputation and the use of metabolomics as a sensitive intermediate phenotype.

Colocalization with the MVP phenome extends the catalog from genetic loci to candidate disease mechanisms. The implicated diseases consistently matched gene function: lipidhandling genes with dyslipidaemia, kidney-expressed transporters and enzymes with eGFR and chronic kidney disease, and urate-handling loci with gout. Pleiotropic hubs such as FADS1, SLCO1B1 and CPS1 connect dozens of metabolites to many trait families, consistent with disease effects that act through the pathways these genes control. Colocalization indicates a shared causal variant, however, not proof of a causal chain. SLC16A9 illustrates this caveat.

The locus links acylcarnitines to gout and coronary atherosclerosis, and a carnitine-mediated effect on cardiac lipotoxicity was plausible a priori. Mendelian randomization could not separate the carnitine-and urate-transport roles of SLC16A9, as expected for a single transporter carrying both substrates. Our estimate for urate on coronary artery disease resembles earlier Mendelian randomization work: robust with conventional estimators but attenuated under MR-Egger. 46 Randomized trials of urate-lowering therapy have not reduced cardiovascular events in patients with ischemic heart disease 53 or improved outcomes in heart failure. 54 Nor did they slow atherosclerosis, measured either as carotid intima-media thickness 55 or as coronary plaque volume. 56 These trials started treatment late in life or after . disease onset and ran for months to a few years, whereas genetic variants act lifelong.

Additionally, these trials largely enrolled people without gout. In gout, reaching a serum urate target below 6 mg/dL was associated with fewer major adverse cardiovascular events 57 , but this setting cannot distinguish an effect of urate from suppression of gout-related inflammation, and the exposure was defined by treatment response rather than assignment. These results therefore do not exclude a contribution of urate to atherogenesis, but they argue against urate lowering as a cardiovascular intervention at least in the general population. We therefore do not interpret the coronary colocalization at SLC16A9 as evidence of a modifiable urate-or carnitine-driven pathway. Genetically predicted carnitine has been associated with higher coronary artery disease risk 58 , but those instruments include pleiotropic transporter loci, so a carnitine-mediated pathway remains a hypothesis. We regard the locus as a robust statistical association whose precise causal pathway remains to be established.

ENPEP illustrates a different kind of insight. Fibrinopeptides have been proposed as a marker of disseminated intravascular coagulation 59, 60 , but this has not become part of routine clinical practice, which instead typically measures fibrinogen or D-dimer directly. Fibrinopeptides have also been explored as a marker of other thrombotic conditions 61 , raising the possibility of broader future diagnostic use. Our results indicate that measured levels of these fragments are shaped not only by a patient's coagulation state but also by their ACE-inhibitor treatment status and ENPEP/ACE genotype: ACE continues to act on the fibrinopeptide substrate pool generated during clot formation in the sample tube, so a patient's ACE activity at the time of blood draw is captured alongside, and potentially confounded with, any coagulation-related signal. Should fibrinopeptide-based testing see wider clinical use, this would be a relevant consideration -and, conversely, these fragments could in principle serve as a biomarker of ACE-inhibitor activity itself. The findings more broadly connect the renin-angiotensin and coagulation systems.

Cross-study replication in metabolomics is bounded by platform comparability. NMR panels such as Nightingale, used in the largest mGWAS including UK Biobank, quantify about 250 predominantly lipoprotein, lipid, amino-acid and glycolysis-related measures, whereas our catalog rests on 1,955 untargeted Metabolon features that include xenobiotics, nucleotides, peptides and unnamed compounds, and UK Biobank has no urine metabolomics. Consistent with this, the NMR-based study supported only 4 of our 969 mQTLs and the untargeted MS . study 73, together changing the classification of just 2 loci relative to the nine Metabolon studies when counting any biofluid and any ancestry as reported. Replication of urine and non-lipid signals therefore requires cohorts with the same platform and biofluid, which is why we combined GCKD with de-novo replication in CRIC.

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting our results. First, European American serum and urine sample sizes were unbalanced; the power-matched analysis shows that the European American biofluid-specificity proportions reflect power, and we therefore base quantitative biofluid comparisons on the balanced African American sample. Second, ARIC participants were older adults sampled at a single visit, ancestry groups were defined by self-report, and the cohort does not capture the full spectrum of human genetic diversity.

Third, urine mQTLs among African Americans were replicated in CRIC, a chronic kidney disease cohort, whereas replication of African American serum signals relied on the Europeanancestry GCKD study. Fourth, colocalization and Mendelian randomization support shared genetic effects rather than causality, and candidate causal genes and AlphaGenome predictions require experimental validation.

Our study demonstrates that multi-ancestry, multi-biofluid metabolomics GWAS designs substantially enhance genetic discovery and mechanistic interpretation. The paired analysis of serum and urine metabolomes revealed complementary genetic architecture, with approximately 75% of metabolites showing biofluid-specific associations in our balanced African American sample. Trans-ancestry finemapping improved resolution for both shared and population-specific signals, and colocalization with a clinical phenome connected prioritized enzymes and transporters to disease. The identification of an African Americanenriched splice variant in SLC28A1 illustrates how ancestral diversity can reveal functional variants with potential pharmacogenomic relevance.

As large-scale biobanks and metabolomics platforms continue to expand, integrating data across populations and biofluids will be essential for building a comprehensive atlas of human metabolic genetics. Our findings provide a template for such integrative approaches and highlight the scientific and equity imperatives for diversifying genomic research.

Methods

Study design and participants

The Atherosclerosis Risk in Communities (ARIC) study is a prospective population-based cohort study with enrollment in 1987-1989, recruiting participants aged 45-64 years from four U.S. communities: Forsyth County, North Carolina; Jackson, Mississippi; suburbs of Minneapolis, Minnesota; and Washington County, Maryland. 24, 25 The study was designed to investigate the etiology and natural history of atherosclerosis and cardiovascular disease.

Participants have undergone serial clinical examinations, with the collection of biological specimens at multiple time points.

The present study included ARIC participants with available genotype data and metabolomic profiling from visit 5 (2011-2013). Serum metabolomic profiling was available for all participants with available blood samples as well as consent while urine profiling was done in in a subsample that did not have previous kidney failure. This included all African Americans as well as European Americans sampled at the same sample size with oversampling of participants with eGFR<60 mL/min/1.73m 2 . Participants were stratified by self-reported race/ethnicity into European American (EA) and African American (AA) ancestry groups. After quality control, the final analytic sample comprised 4,431 participants with metabolite data: EA serum (Nserum = 3,607), EA urine (Nurine = 797), AA serum (Nserum = 818), and AA urine (Nurine = 794). The study was approved by the institutional review boards at each participating center, and all participants provided written informed consent (IRB00311861).

Genotyping and imputation

Genotyping was performed using the Affymetrix Genome-Wide Human SNP Array 6.0 (Affymetrix, Santa Clara, CA). Standard quality control procedures were applied, including exclusion of variants if they had call rate <95%, minor allele frequency (MAF) <1%, or significant deviation from Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium (P < 1 × 10 -6 ). Genotype imputation was performed using the TOPMed Imputation Server (https://imputation.biodatacatalyst.nhlbi.nih.gov/) with the TOPMed r2 reference panel. 52 Phasing was performed with Eagle v2.4 and imputation with Minimac4. Post-imputation, variants were filtered to retain those with imputation quality (R² ) >0.3 and MAF >1% within each ancestry stratum. The TOPMed 21 reference panel includes substantial representation of . African individuals, enabling improved imputation accuracy and capture of small insertions and deletions (indels) that were poorly represented in earlier reference panels.

Metabolite identification and quantification

Serum and urine metabolites were quantified using the Metabolon HD4 global metabolomics platform (Metabolon, Inc., Durham, NC). 62 This untargeted approach employs ultrahighperformance liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (UPLC-MS/MS) across four complementary ionization methods: two reverse-phase methods using positive ion mode electrospray ionization, one reverse-phase method using negative ion mode electrospray ionization, and one hydrophilic interaction liquid chromatography (HILIC) method using negative ion mode electrospray ionization.

Metabolites were identified by comparison of retention time, mass-to-charge ratio (m/z), and MS/MS fragmentation patterns to Metabolon's proprietary library of authenticated chemical standards. Each metabolite was assigned a unique identifier and classified into superpathways (e.g., amino acid, lipid, xenobiotics) and sub-pathways.

Raw metabolite abundance values were normalized to account for inter-day variation by rescaling each compound to set the median equal to 1 across run days. For serum metabolites, values were rank-based inverse normal transformed. For urinary metabolites, we applied probabilistic quotient (PQ) normalization to account for differences in urine concentration. 63 Briefly, PQ factors were calculated as the median of the ratios of each metabolite's abundance to a reference spectrum (median of all samples) across 372 endogenous metabolites with <1% missingness. Urinary metabolite values were divided by the sample-specific PQ factor, then rank-based inverse normal transformed. Metabolites with >50% missing values were excluded from analysis. Metabolites were retained for genome-wide association analysis if they had measurements in ≥300 participants within an ancestry-biofluid stratum.

The platform quantified a total of 1,316 metabolites in serum and 1,374 metabolites in urine after quality control. Metabolite annotations were harmonized across biofluids to reflect the newer (=urine) Metabolite annotation, yielding 1,955 unique analytes (581 serum-only, 639 urine-only, 735 detected in both biofluids).

Definition of covariates

Covariates for GWAS were age, sex and the first 10 principal components of ancestry. Age was calculated as of the date of visit 5 examination. Sex was self-reported. Genetic principal components (PCs) 1-10 were derived from genome-wide genotype data using PLINK v1.9 64 and calculated separately within each ancestry group.

Genome-wide association analysis

Genome-wide association testing was performed separately within each ancestry-biofluid stratum (EA serum, EA urine, AA serum, AA urine) using REGENIE v3.1. 65 a two-step whole-genome regression approach that accounts for relatedness and population structure while maintaining computational efficiency for biobank-scale data.

REGENIE implements

In step 1, a whole-genome regression model was fit using a subset of high-quality genotyped variants (MAF >1%, minor allele count > 100, genotyping rate >90%, Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium P > 1 × 10 -15 ). In step 2, association testing was performed for all imputed variants using a linear regression model with the step 1 predictions as an offset. All association tests were two-sided.

The association model included the following covariates: age, sex, and the first 10 ancestryspecific principal components. Association testing was restricted to variants with MAF >1% and imputation R² ≥0.3 within each stratum. Genomic inflation factors (λ) were calculated for each metabolite GWAS to assess residual population stratification or technical artifacts.

African American participants of ARIC show moderate, well-characterized European admixture (median proportion of European ancestry approximately 15%). 66 Association testing within each ancestry stratum, adjustment for ten ancestry-specific genetic principal components and REGENIE's leave-one-chromosome-out whole-genome regression, which captures relatedness and population structure, 65 together account for the global-ancestry variation that admixture generates. Study-wide significance was defined as P < 2.56 × 10 -11 (5 × 10 -8 / 1,955 metabolites), corresponding to a Bonferroni correction for the number of unique metabolites tested.

Metabolite quantitative trait loci (mQTLs) were defined by merging significant variants within ±500 kb windows within each ancestry and merging any overlapping windows (Extended Data Fig. 2).

Variance explained by index variants was compared across strata descriptively. Smaller discovery samples detect only larger-effect associations.

. Shared mQTLs were defined based on genomic proximity using a 1 Mb window. Specifically, a locus was considered shared across ancestries if the index variants for the same metabolite and biofluid in both the EA and AA analyses were located within 1 Mb of each other. Analogously, a locus was considered shared across biofluids if the index variants for the same metabolite in serum and urine were located within 1 Mb of each other. Per-stratum counts of mQTLs, distinct genomic regions, and metabolites with at least one genome-wide-significant association are provided in Supplementary Table 5. Loci that did not meet this proximity criterion were classified as biofluid-or candidate ancestry-specific, respectively. Each of the 632 candidate ancestry-specific mQTLs (590 EA-specific, 42 AA-specific; Supplementary Tables 3 4) was evaluated using a three-step rule to distinguish genuine ancestry-specific effects from those that are more parsimoniously explained as statistically driven, e.g. by imputation availability or power (Extended Data Fig. 2). First, availability: if the index variant, or a proxy at LD r² ≥ 0.8, was not available or did not pass quality control in the other ancestry, the locus was excluded as imputation-driven. Second, power: for loci passing the availability check, we calculated the power to detect the discovery effect size at the other ancestry's observed sample size and standard error; loci with <80% power were excluded as powerdriven. Third, heterogeneity: for the remaining loci, we tested for effect-size heterogeneity between ancestries using a two-sided z-test, applying a Bonferroni-corrected significance threshold (P < 0.05/632 = 7.9 × 10⁻⁵) to account for the number of loci tested; loci without significant heterogeneity were likewise excluded as statistically driven, and only those with significant heterogeneity were retained and classified as ancestry-specific. Full per-locus statistics (effect sizes and standard errors in both ancestries, power, and heterogeneity Pvalue) for the retained ancestry-specific loci are provided in Supplementary Table 20. At the level of statistical fine-mapping, a credible set is termed a fine-mapped ancestry-specific signal if MESuSiE assigns it to one ancestry (PIP > 0.5, with PIP_shared ≤ 0.5) in Supplementary Tables 21 and 23; this applies to every credible set in the dataset, independent of the locuslevel classification above. Where an mQTL is independently classified as ancestry-specific at both levels, i.e., it passes the locus-level rule above and carries a fine-mapped ancestryspecific signal for the same ancestry, we refer to it as a fine-mapped ancestry-specific mQTL.

.

Gene assignment

We prioritized candidate causal genes for each mQTL based on the ProGeM framework 67 with additional extensions (https://github.com/genepi-freiburg/GWASannotation) 68 . The pipeline integrates gene proximity, LD-based gene overlap, functional variant consequences annotated with Ensembl VEP v101, 69 genetic colocalization with cis-eQTL and cis-pQTL datasets, and polygenic priority scores (PoPS). 70 For European American mQTLs, colocalization was performed against cis-eQTLs from GTEx v8 50 and eQTLGen, 71 and against cis-pQTLs from ARIC EA participants. 72 For mQTLs among African Americans, cis-eQTL colocalization used the multi-ancestry Kachuri et al. 73 eQTL resource, and cis-pQTL colocalization used cis-pQTLs from ARIC in AA participants. 72 Colocalization was performed using the coloc.abf function from the coloc R package. 74 We retained signals with evidence for a shared causal variant (PPH4 > 0.8) and suggestive cis-eQTL or cis-pQTL associations (-log₁₀(P) > 5).

Gene prioritization relied on a weighted scoring scheme. Proximity to the lead variant was scored as 1.00, 0.99, and 0.98 for the first, second, and third nearest genes, respectively.

Genes overlapping the LD interval received 1.00, and VEP annotations indicating moderate or high impact contributed 1.02. Cis-eQTL association without evidence of colocalization contributed 0.5. Colocalization with cis-eQTLs in tissues of interest for metabolism (defined as tissues enriched in paired plasma and urine mGWAS findings 14 , GTEx v8 50 : adrenal gland, heart left ventricle, kidney cortex, liver, pancreas, small intestine terminal ileum, colon transverse, stomach; kidney-specific datasets: tubule and glomeruli) was scored at 1.04, while general cis-eQTL colocalization was scored at 1.03. For AA mQTLs, tissues of interest were not defined separately; colocalization with the Kachuri cis-eQTL resource was scored at 1.03 (general colocalization). Plasma cis-pQTL colocalization (ARIC PWAS 72 ) was weighted at 1.05. PoPS scores were computed per locus and ancestry stratum; the gene with the highest PoPS score per locus received an additive weight of 0.5 and the second-highest 0.25.

Covariate selection for multi-phenotype studies

To evaluate whether reported associations could reflect confounding by other correlated metabolites, we applied a method for covariate selection in multiphenotype studies (CMS) 75 using the authors' reference implementation (CMS_v1.0. R, function MC()), unmodified. For each of the 247 prioritized genes in each ancestry-by-biofluid stratum, we took the single most . strongly associated (target metabolite, index variant) pair (366 gene-by-stratum combinations) and offered as candidate covariates the 50 metabolites most correlated with the target (|r| ≤ 0.8; measured in ≥90% of subjects with the target measured; remaining missingness mean-imputed). We derived an equivalent sample-size increase (SSincr) as the ratio of CMS-adjusted to marginal -log₁₀P (Supplementary Table 12).

Statistical finemapping

Multi-ancestry finemapping was performed using MESuSiE (Multi-ancestry Sum of Single Effects), 35 which extends the Sum of Single Effects (SuSiE) 76 model to jointly analyze GWAS summary statistics from multiple ancestries. MESuSiE explicitly models both shared and ancestry-specific causal signals using a mixture of bivariate normal distributions, allowing identification of variants with effects in one or both ancestries. For each mQTL region, we provided ancestry-specific GWAS summary statistics (effect estimates and standard errors) and LD matrices calculated from the ARIC genotype data.

For comparison, single-ancestry finemapping was performed using SuSiE 76 separately within EA and AA strata based on the same set of shared variants. SuSiE decomposes the genetic signal into a sum of single-effect vectors, each representing one causal variant, and outputs posterior inclusion probabilities (PIPs) for each variant and 95% credible sets for each independent signal. Finemapping was performed for the genomic windows harboring mQTLs in serum and urine separately based on a harmonized cross-ancestry SNP set with joint variants. For shared loci, the window with the lowest P value across EA and AA was selected for joint multi-ancestry finemapping with MESuSiE. Multi-ancestry fine-mapping with MESuSiE was run on all 800 unique region-metabolite pairs (629 serum, 171 urine). For 6 of these (2 serum, 4 urine), MESuSiE returned no credible set, likely reflecting the absence of a remaining genome-wide significant variant (P < 5 × 10⁻⁸/1955 = 2.56 × 10⁻¹¹) within the harmonized cross-ancestry SNP set. Credible sets were therefore obtained for 794 regionmetabolite pairs (627 serum, 167 urine). Single-ancestry finemapping with SuSiE was performed on the original per-ancestry windows, but based on the harmonized cross-ancestry SNP set to be comparable to MESuSiE. Multi-allelic variants were excluded from finemapping analyses to ensure consistent LD estimation.

Credible sets were classified as ancestry-shared (PIPshared > 0.5), EA-specific (PIPEA > 0.5 and PIPshared ≤ 0.5), or AA-specific (PIPAA > 0.5 and PIPshared ≤ 0.5). Variants were considered high-. predicted effect relative to common variants in gnomAD v3 (MAF > 1%). Variant-level summaries were generated by retaining the strongest predicted effect across all tracks, as well as separately for metabolically relevant tissues (defined as tissues enriched in paired plasma and urine mGWAS findings 14 : kidney, liver, adrenal gland, heart left ventricle, pancreas, small intestine terminal ileum, colon transverse, stomach). Variants with quantile scores exceeding 0.99 were considered to have reliably predicted functional effects and are reported as highconfidence, consistent with the model's reported concordance with observed eQTL effect sizes at this threshold (Spearman r ≈ 0.73). In addition to the collapsed annotation in the supplementary tables, all individual annotations are provided in Supplementary Data 2.

AlphaGenome annotation was performed using Python 3.12.3 and alphagenome v0.6.1.

AlphaGenome predictions are in-silico, hypothesis-generating annotations of potential regulatory consequence; they were not experimentally validated in this study and are reported as supporting evidence for gene and variant prioritization.

Population differentiation (Fst)

Genetic differentiation between European (CEU) and West African (YRI) reference populations was quantified using Fst, obtained from PopHuman's 79 precomputed Fst_CEU2YRI_10kb track, which reports values in non-overlapping 10-kb windows (hg19). Index variant positions were converted from GRCh38 to GRCh37/hg19. For 609 of the 969 (63%) mQTL index variants, the Fst value of the overlapping window was extracted using the rtracklayer R package, and expressed as a percentile relative to the genome-wide Fst distribution together with a onesided empirical P-value (the proportion of genome-wide Fst values greater than or equal to the observed value). Index variants falling in a gap between windows have no Fst value, a property of the non-contiguous window track that was not further resolved; this applied to 37% of mQTLs and reflects window coverage rather than a systematic bias by locus type.

Power-matched sensitivity analysis

To test whether apparent biofluid-specific biology in the European-American (EA) stratum reflects the larger serum sample size (N = 3,607) relative to urine (N = 797), we repeated the complete EA serum mGWAS restricted to the 797 EA participants who also contributed urine measurements. Gene set, pathway, and tissue enrichment analyses (GO, KEGG, GTEx tissue, . and Metabolon super-/sub-pathway) were repeated on this power-matched locus set using the identical pipeline as the primary enrichment analysis.

Pathway and tissue enrichment analyses

Gene set enrichment analysis was performed using the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway database. 80 Enrichment was assessed using one-sided Fisher's exact tests comparing the proportion of mQTL-implicated genes in each pathway to the genomic background. Pathways with fewer than 3 mapped genes were excluded. Fold enrichment was defined as the ratio of the fraction of mQTL-implicated genes in a pathway to the fraction of background genes in that pathway.

To identify tissue-specific gene sets for enrichment analyses, we used pre-compiled lists of the top 10% of specifically expressed genes across GTEx V8 tissues and human and murine kidney cell types, derived as described previously. 7 Briefly, tissue-specific expression was assessed by ranking genes by t-statistics from a linear model comparing expression in each tissue or cell type to all others, following the approach of Finucane et al. 81 The resulting top 10% gene lists per tissue were used as the basis for enrichment testing against our identified metaboliteassociated genes. Gene set overrepresentation was tested analogously to KEGG. Of the 220 and 81 genes associated in serum and urine (overlap: 54), respectively, we were able to map 220 and 80 to the KEGG annotated genes. KEGG enrichment was performed using clusterprofiler and KEGG.db v3.2.4.

Pathway enrichment regarding annotated sub-and super-pathways as annotated by Metabolon Inc., was performed using Fisher's exact tests.

In all enrichment analyses, statistical significance was determined by a Bonferroni-corrected significance threshold accounting for the number of pathways/gene sets in the background, respectively.

Replication and cross-study comparisons

We assessed novelty of our 969 mQTLs against eleven published metabolite GWAS: nine Metabolon-annotated studies (Schlosser et al. (GCKD) 14 , Chen et al. 6 , Long et al. 4 , Yin et al. 20 , Hysi et al. 26 , Lotta et al. 27 , Surendran et al. 5 , Shin et al. 3 , Luo et al. 16 ) and, as secondary-tier evidence, two studies on other platforms (Tambets et al. 2 , Nuclear Magnetic Resonance; Tahir et al. 18 , untargeted Mass Spectrometry) treated as a secondary tier. A locus was called known . if the comparison study reported its own genome-wide-significant (P ≤ 5 × 10⁻⁸) index variant for the same metabolite within ±500 kb of the ARIC index variant, on the same chromosome, and all reported summary statistics are the comparison study's own values for its own variant. Metabolite identity was matched using, for the primary tier, any concordant match among global Metabolon chemical ID, biofluid-specific chemical ID, or normalized biochemical name; for the secondary tier, normalized name only, supplemented for Tahir by an external-identifier bridge (PubChem, then KEGG, then HMDB; unambiguous 1:1 mappings only). Looking at biofluid-and ancestry-matched Metabolon-studies served as our primary novelty measure; counting a report in any biofluid and ancestry in all 11 studies as known served as a conservative bound. Full per-locus results across both definitions are provided in Supplementary Table 9. As a targeted replication analysis, we additionally evaluated the validation rate of our mQTLs against GCKD specifically -the only other study with a paired serum-and-urine design. For index variants not found directly in GCKD's summary statistics, we searched for an LD proxy (r² ≥ 0.8, 1000 Genomes European reference panel: CEU, TSI, FIN, GBR, IBS) using LDlinkR, with allele phasing resolved via LDproxy's correlated-alleles output, and used the proxy variant's association statistics in its place. We then tested for concordant direction of effect at Bonferroni-corrected significance (P < 0.05/# lookups per biofluid) among all directly or proxy-matched pairs. Cross-ancestry and cross-biofluid consistency was assessed by comparing effect estimates between strata. Pearson correlation coefficients were calculated for effect sizes of shared mQTLs. For visualization, a regression slope was computed as the unweighted mean of the two per-direction regression slopes (regressing y on x and x on y), correcting for discovery-related winner's curse.

As no published Metabolon urine resource exists for African American participants, we obtained de-novo replication in the Chronic Renal Insufficiency Cohort (CRIC), a prospective cohort of Black participants with mild-to-moderate chronic kidney disease recruited across seven U.S. clinical centers. 28, 29 Of 1,514 Black participants with baseline urinary metabolomics, 1,323 additionally had complete covariates and genome-wide genotyping and were included in this replication.

Urinary metabolites were profiled from baseline 24-hour urine samples on the same Metabolon UPLC-MS/MS platform as ARIC and quantified by area-under-the-curve against a library of authenticated standards. Metabolites were normalized using probabilistic quotient normalization: a reference set of metabolites with <50% missingness, excluding xenobiotics . and unnamed compounds, was used to compute a per-sample scaling factor, followed by median-scaling across samples; each target metabolite was then rank-based inverse-normal transformed.

Genotyping was performed in 1,447 participants using the Illumina HumanOmni1-Quad BeadChip and imputed via the Michigan Imputation Server using the TOPMed r2 reference panel (GRCh38).

Covariates were age, sex, study site, and 10 genetic principal components; a sensitivity model additionally adjusted for eGFR (2021 CKD-EPI creatinine equation without race, 82 logtransformed).

Of the 109 AA-urine mQTLs identified in ARIC (77 unique index variants, 101 unique metabolites), all 77 index variants were available in CRIC's imputed genotype data at high quality (minimum Minimac R² = 0.950); 94 of 101 metabolites were measured in CRIC, yielding 102 testable variant-metabolite pairs. Association testing used REGENIE v3.2 65 in two steps: a whole-genome regression model fitted on quality-controlled, LD-pruned genome-wide variants to generate leave-one-chromosome-out predictions for each metabolite, followed by extraction of the 77 index variants with PLINK 2 and single-variant association testing conditional on the step-1 predictions, under the primary and eGFR-adjusted models described above.

Disease and complex-trait colocalization

We colocalized each of the four ancestry-by-biofluid mGWAS strata against the Million Veteran Program (MVP) phenome-wide GWAS 17 , testing European-ancestry ARIC strata against MVP European-ancestry summary statistics and African American ARIC strata against MVP African American summary statistics. We used the genepicoloc R package (https://github.com/genepi-freiburg/genepicoloc), which implements the colocalization approach from the coloc R package (coloc.abf 74 with default priors). Regions in which colocalization was performed were defined as a ±500 kilobase window around the index variant (the variant with the lowest P-value in mGWAS, required to reach P < 5x10⁻⁸). We tested 1,669 traits (1,470 binary, 199 quantitative) from the European-ancestry MVP data and 1,368 traits (1,187 binary, 181 quantitative) from the African-ancestry MVP data; 3,037 traitancestry analyses in total. Colocalization was declared at a posterior probability of a shared . causal variant (PP.H4) > 0.8; results were de-duplicated to one result per gene-metabolitetrait-family combination. Full per-hit results are provided in Supplementary Table 25.

Medication-based observational analyses

To test whether pharmacologically reduced ACE activity alters fibrinopeptide fragment levels, we performed two individual-level observational analyses in ARIC. RASi medication use (ACE inhibitor, angiotensin-receptor blocker, and/or aldosterone antagonist in the past 4 weeks) was defined at Visit 5, versus no antihypertensive medication as the comparator; sensitivity analyses isolated each drug class individually and added a third, non-RASi-medication comparator group. Models were adjusted for age, sex, race, BMI, eGFR (CKD-EPI creatinine equation), diabetes medication use, smoking, and statin use, in the combined EA+AA sample (primary) and each ancestry separately. The first analysis tested seven clinically measured coagulation markers at Visit 1 (N = 4,122-4,209 per marker), with factor VII and fibrinogen also available at Visit 3 (N = 306); Visit 5 itself had no directly measured coagulation markers. These markers were therefore measured about two decades before medication ascertainment. The second analysis tested the same fibrinopeptide fragments plus aspartate, glycylvaline, aspartylaspartate, and serum potassium, all measured contemporaneously at Visit 5 (metabolite values rank-based inverse-normal transformed; potassium on its native scale). Associations were tested by multivariable linear regression.

Mendelian randomization at SLC16A9

To determine whether SLC16A9's colocalization with urate-related traits and coronary atherosclerosis (CAD) reflects one or two separable pathways, we performed two-sample MR for urate (CKDGen Round 4 83 , European ancestry) and five SLC16A9-linked acylcarnitines (carnitine, acetylcarnitine, propionylcarnitine, phenylacetylcarnitine, butyrylcarnitine; ARIC and Shin et al. 3 ) against gout (CKDGen) and CAD (CARDIoGRAMplusC4D 84 ). To test gout itself, rather than continuous urate, as a cause of CAD, we performed an additional MR using a casecontrol gout diagnosis GWAS (CKDGen R4) against CAD outcome with CARDIoGRAMplusC4D.

For all analyses above, instrument SNPs were clumped using ieugwasr::ld_clump() against ancestry-matched 1000 Genomes Phase 3 reference panels 85 (r² < 0.001, 10 Mb window), and causal effects were estimated using inverse-variance-weighted regression, MR-Egger . regression, and the weighted median estimator (or the Wald ratio for single-SNP instruments) via the TwoSampleMR R package. 86, 87

Expression and splicing QTL lookup
