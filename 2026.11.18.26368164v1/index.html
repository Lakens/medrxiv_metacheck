To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 2 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Metacheck does not yet analyze automatically whether sentences which include non-significant p-values are correct, but we recommend manually checking the sentences below for possible misinterpreted non-significant p values.

p-value > 0.05 Probes with p-value > 0.05 were masked.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). Statcheck might still miss one-sided tests that are described in another sentence, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Abstract

Blood DNA methylation offers a dynamic readout of inherited and acquired influences on health, creating an opportunity for risk prediction beyond polygenic scores, which are fixed at conception and have yet to demonstrate broad clinical utility. As such, we aimed to develop methylation risk scores for 21 common diseases in 12,785 adults from Generation Scotland and the Mass General Brigham Biobank, combining technically reproducible probes, null-calibrated feature selection and ten-year survival models, with evaluation in a single holdout that was not used for feature selection, model fitting or tuning. The methylation risk scores for eight diseases reached a holdout C-index of at least 0.80: chronic kidney disease, peripheral vascular disease, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, heart failure and type 2 diabetes with at least 50 events each, and interstitial lung disease, dementia and fatty liver disease with fewer. For the five scores with at least 50 events, adjusted hazard ratios per standard deviation ranged from 2.6 to 4.1, with the highest risk tertile containing 73 to 90% of the ten-year events. Panel CpGs were enriched for cytokine signalling, innate immune sensing and sterol metabolism. Against reported values, the eight top-tier methylation risk scores exceed published polygenic and methylation scores alone, and exceed published polygenic and methylation scores that include clinical covariates for seven of the eight diseases (the exception is type 2 diabetes, where our AUC of 0.865 without clinical risk factors is close to a methylation-plus-risk-factor model at 0.872, and our C-index of 0.809 is lower than the C-index for a polygenic-plus-risk-factor at 0.85). These results indicate that a single blood methylation profile can rank ten-year risk for several common diseases at a level that compares favourably with published genetic and methylation scores.

INTRODUCTION

The accurate identification of individuals at risk for disease is central to prevention. Molecular risk markers are usually studied in one of two designs: i) prevalent (cross-sectional) analyses compare people who already have a disease with people who do not, challenging our ability to separate markers of risk from consequences of the disease or its treatment, or ii) incident analyses, which follow people who are initially unaffected until some develop the disease, predicting future disease risk. The second is what is valuable in answering the clinical question, yet most risk scores have been evaluated with the first.

Most common chronic diseases are polygenic, and genome-wide association studies have identified thousands of variants that each confer a small shift in risk. 1 Combined into a polygenic risk score (PRS), these variants stratify populations for coronary artery disease (CAD), type 2 diabetes (T2DM), breast cancer and other conditions, and for a few per cent of individuals a high PRS carries risk comparable to a monogenic mutation. 2, 3 Several limitations follow from how PRS are built. PRS capture inherited susceptibility, which is fixed at conception. This is an advantage for early risk assessment, but it also means that a PRS alone cannot register the lifestyle, environmental, metabolic and treatment exposures that determine when, or whether, inherited susceptibility becomes disease, nor change after an intervention. A systematic review of 591 studies found strong evidence of clinical validity for PRS in 22 but no study that demonstrated clinical utility, that is, improved outcomes from acting on the score. 4, 5 Many PRS have also been developed and evaluated in prevalent case-control studies, so their discrimination reflects lifetime susceptibility rather than risk over a defined horizon, 6 which limits their value for modifiable, environmentally influenced diseases. Their discrimination in the general population is modest: standalone PRS for most common diseases reach an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.58-0.71 in UK Biobank, and most of the predictive value of a clinical PRS model comes from the age, sex and clinical covariates it is combined with. 6, 7 As with most risk scores, they transfer less well across ancestries, as discovery cohorts are predominantly European. 8 A complementary, dynamic measure of acquired risk could therefore add information that inherited risk cannot. DNA methylation measured in blood records both inherited and acquired influences on health, and unlike genotype, it changes over time. Methylation at specific CpG sites tracks smoking exposure and its cessation, 9 body mass, alcohol use and other behaviours, 10 circulating protein levels and cell composition, 11, 12 and methylation-based measures of biological ageing respond to randomised interventions such as caloric restriction. 13 Predictors built on methylation therefore report on an individual's current biological state rather than on a fixed liability. This is what gives a methylation risk score (MRS) its potential for clinical utility: the same assay that stratifies risk can be repeated to show whether risk has changed, and the CpGs and pathways a score relies on point to the biology that drives the risk. Metabolomic and proteomic scores are also dynamic and predict many incident diseases, 14, 15 however, the advantage of methylation is practical, whereby a single array on a small blood sample yields many predictors, 12, 16 which have already been used to estimate exposures and biomarkers that are costly or impractical to measure directly, 10, 12 to classify rare genetic disease, 17 and to predict mortality 18 and incident disease. 19, 20 Methylation-based predictors have developed along two lines. They were first trained on outcomes, such as chronological age or mortality, and then asked whether the score was associated with disease. Epigenetic clocks of this kind are associated with many conditions, but because they capture what diseases share rather than what distinguishes them, adding little disease-specific prediction. For example, in a test of 14 clocks against 174 incident diseases in Generation Scotland (GS), adding a clock to age, sex and lifestyle improved the 10-year AUC by more than 0.01 for only 32 of 176 significant clock-disease associations. 21 The second line trained scores directly on a single disease. These disease-specific MRS have shown that blood methylation carries a usable signal. An example is a methylation score that raised the 10-year AUC for T2DM from 0.839 to 0.872 over clinical risk factors in GS and replicated in KORA; 22 CpG panels improved lung cancer prediction over smoking status, and improved breast cancer prediction over self-reported risk factors and a PRS alone. 23, 24 Methylation scores for cardiovascular disease (CVD) have been built in several cohorts, including most recently, in the Million Veteran Program (MVP). 19, 20, 25 The one study to build methylation scores for many phenotypes at once did so cross-sectionally in 831 patients and found that they outperformed PRS for most medication, laboratory and diagnosis codes. 26 Those studies also show where the field has been limited. Each disease-specific MRS was built for one disease in one development cohort, with its own feature-selection and evaluation choices, so performance cannot be compared across diseases, and few studies have tested transfer to a second cohort. 19, 22 Sample sizes of a few hundred to a few thousand have meant that feature selection and evaluation often used the same participants, which inflates apparent accuracy, 27 and that held-out evaluation with enough events to estimate discrimination with precision has rarely been possible. Most evaluations have used prevalent case-control status, as for PRS, rather than time-to-event outcomes, so the horizon over which a score predicts, which determines its clinical use, is usually unknown.

Modelling choices add a further limitation. Most methylation predictors consider marginal CpG effects or assume linear, additive effects between CpGs. Feature selection and modeling that capture interactions and non-linear effects could represent more complex relationships between CpGs and may improve discrimination.

Here we ask whether a single blood methylation profile can predict ten-year risk across many common chronic diseases when every score is built and tested in the same way. We developed methylation risk scores for 21 common diseases in 12,785 adults from two cohorts with different settings and outcome ascertainment, GS and the Mass General Brigham (MGB) Biobank, using one pipeline for probe selection, survival modelling and evaluation on a holdout set aside before any analysis. To our knowledge, this is the first uniform development and evaluation of methylation risk scores across this many diseases with incident ten-year outcomes. We report every score, including the weak ones, set the strongest against published methylation and polygenic scores, and examine the CpGs and pathways on which they rely.

DISCUSSION

We trained ten-year methylation risk scores for 21 common diseases on a single development set drawn from two cohorts and evaluated all of them in a single holdout set aside before any analysis. Eight scores reached a C-index of at least 0.80, with the strongest signal in cardiometabolic and respiratory disease: CKD, PVD, COPD, CHF and T2DM each had at least 50 holdout events, age-and sex-adjusted hazard ratios of 2.6 to 4.1 per standard deviation, and a highest risk tertile that contained 73 to 90% of ten-year events. The same . evaluation also showed where blood methylation carries little disease-specific signal at this sample size, in allergic and immune-mediated disease, colorectal cancer and alcohol-related liver disease. Because every score was built and tested the same way, these differences more plausibly reflect the disease pathophysiologies and their representations rather than the methods, which earlier single-disease studies could not establish. 19, 20, 22, 49 Set beside published values, the holdout performance compares well. For every top-tier disease with a published polygenic score alone (seven of the eight), our scores outperformed it, despite using no clinical risk factors. They also outperformed polygenic or methylation scores combined with age, sex or clinical factors for seven of the eight diseases. 6, 39, 51, 52, [54] [55] [56] [58] [59] [60] The exception is T2DM, where polygenic 5, 35 and methylation 22 models that add clinical risk factors had higher values. Published methylation scores with the same endpoint and an incident design exist for only three of these diseases (COPD, T2DM and NAFLD), and ours were higher except for those T2DM models. 22, 33, 49 For the other five top-tier diseases (CKD, PVD, CHF, ILD and dementia; 14 of the 21 diseases overall) we found no published incident methylation score with an AUC or C-index for the same endpoint. The T2DM comparison is the closest, because it uses the same cohort. Our score, without clinical risk factors, was marginally below the published model that combines clinical risk factors with methylation in GS, 22 and our AUC (0.865) exceeded the best published polygenic AUC (0.794). 53 These comparisons are of reported values across different cohorts, horizons and case definitions, not head-to-head analyses in the same participants. Most published PRS discrimination comes from prevalent or mixed case-control designs, 60, 61 largely in UK Biobank, 6 whereas ours is ten-year incident risk in a holdout. The direction, that methylation discriminates better than genotype for exposure-and metabolism-driven diseases, is nonetheless consistent with the one earlier direct comparison, in which methylation scores outperformed polygenic scores for most phenotypes in 831 patients, 26 and with the view that the two carry complementary information. 62 The CpGs the models rely on are consistent with known biology and suggest sources of the signal. The T2DM score draws on TXNIP and ABCG1, the most replicated loci in blood methylation studies of the disease, and its panel is enriched for TNF-α signalling and sterol metabolism; the COPD panel is enriched for cytokine signalling and contains CpGs from the smoking axis (AHRR, F2RL3, PRSS23); the heart failure panel is enriched for innate immune sensing. Age and sex adjustment changed the hazard ratios for the cardiometabolic and respiratory scores modestly, reduced them by about half or more for myocardial infarction and dementia, and removed the association for colorectal cancer. Moreover, 79% of the dementia panel consists of CpGs associated with age in published studies. Two conclusions follow. For the strongest scores, the signal largely persists after adjustment for age and sex and is plausibly a readout of inflammatory, metabolic and exposure state. For dementia, and to a lesser extent the other tier 1b diseases, part of the discrimination is age, and the adjusted estimates should be read as the relevant ones. For prostate and breast cancer, which occur in one sex, the mixed-sex values partly reflect sex-associated CpGs, so the within-sex values are the relevant ones. SHAP values describe how a model uses a CpG and not the direction or causality of its association.

The clinical argument for these scores rests on the breadth of information available from a single methylation profile. The 21 scores are computed from a single whole-blood assay; the same assay which supports multiple methylation-based measures that have been developed over the past decade. These include epigenetic clocks of biological age and pace of ageing, [63] [64] [65] scores that estimate circulating protein and metabolite levels, 12, 16 . predictors of smoking, alcohol and body mass, 10 and reference-based deconvolution of twelve immune subtypes. 11 Platforms that compute many of these measures from one array already exist. 66 A clinician ordering one test could therefore receive ten-year risk ranks for several diseases, alongside the biological age, immune and biomarker context needed to interpret them, akin to a metabolic panel read as a whole rather than as isolated values. Polygenic risk scores cannot provide this, because they are fixed at conception, report lifetime liability rather than current state, and have shown clinical validity but not clinical utility. 2, 4, 7 A methylation profile changes with exposure and intervention, 9,13 so the same test can, in principle, be repeated to show whether risk has moved. We have not tested that here, and longitudinal evaluation of these scores is the most important next step. Moreover, accessibility matters as much as accuracy for a test meant to be repeated. Methylation arrays run on dried blood spots give profiles highly concordant with venous blood on the 450K array from the same individuals, 67 the same BeadChip technology as the EPIC array used here, so a card collected at home or in a primary-care clinic could in principle be mailed at ambient temperature and run on the same platform. This could widen the population that can be stratified and followed to people for whom phlebotomy, specialist referral or genotyping is impractical, which is where ten-year risk stratification for chronic disease may have the most to offer.

This study has limitations. The two cohorts differ in setting, age, outcome ascertainment and follow-up.

Although the partitions were stratified by cohort, feature selection and the null threshold were pooled across the two cohorts rather than derived in each cohort separately, so we did not test whether cohort-specific null models, which could differ with demographics and outcome ascertainment, would select different CpGs, and transfer of each score from one cohort to the other has not been tested. GS is a family-based cohort and partitions were assigned at the individual level, so relatives may be divided between the development and holdout sets, which could inflate holdout performance. Scores were fitted on class-balanced training folds and are not population-calibrated, so they rank individuals but do not yet give absolute risks; recalibration of the baseline hazard is required before any absolute-risk use. Moreover, the reported model for each disease was the best of three by holdout C-index, so the holdout estimates are somewhat optimistic. Several diseases, including all of tier 1b, had fewer than 50 holdout events, and their confidence intervals are wide. Cell-type composition was not modelled, but when we intersected every panel with blood cell-type marker CpGs, only the asthma panel was enriched for eosinophil markers. This is consistent with asthma biology, as eosinophilic type 2 airway inflammation is a major asthma endotype. Blood EWAS of asthma has also reported eosinophil-linked CpGs. 68 Both cohorts are predominantly of European ancestry and all samples were measured on one array version, so performance in other ancestries and on newer platforms is unknown. The comparisons with published scores are of reported values rather than head-to-head analyses, as noted above. In particular, we did not benchmark the scores against age, sex and established clinical risk models in the same participants, so their incremental value over routinely available predictors remains to be established. Prevalent disease was excluded on the basis of recorded diagnoses, so undiagnosed disease at sampling may contribute to the prediction of early events. The 2,233 GS participants assigned a fixed censoring time are event-free at ten years by construction. Age at collection was an input to 15 of the 21 scores, including six of the eight top-tier diseases (CKD, PVD, COPD, CHF, T2DM, and dementia), and sex was also an input for dementia and prostate cancer, breast cancer, MI and depression, so these scores are not methylation-only. The within-sex values for breast and prostate cancer are not from models retrained within sex, and may reflect reduced performance because of this. Finally, methylation-based scores for CVD have shown horizon dependence, with . discrimination highest in the first years after sampling, 20 and the ten-year horizon reported here should not be assumed to hold at longer follow-up.

Taken together, the results show that a single blood methylation profile can rank ten-year risk for several common diseases at a level that compares favourably with published genetic and methylation scores, and that it does so on the same assay that yields biological age, immune composition and surrogate biomarker measures. We report all 21 scores with confidence intervals and event counts so that the weak scores are visible alongside the strong. Further work is needed to expand the cohorts and training datasets, calibrate the scores for absolute risk, compare them directly with polygenic scores in the same participants, and test longitudinally whether they change with treatment, the property that could give them a place in care that genotype cannot match.

METHODS

Study populations

We developed methylation risk scores (MRS) for 21 diseases in 12,785 whole-blood DNA methylation samples measured on the Illumina Infinium MethylationEPIC v1 array from two cohorts. Generation Scotland (GS; n = 10,156) 28, 29, 69 is a population-based cohort that recruited participants from 2006 -2011 and sampled blood at the baseline clinic visit, with outcomes from linkage to national health records. Mass General Brigham (MGB; n = 2,629) 30 is a hospital-based cohort that recruits participants through clinical care, and outcomes come from the electronic health record. Each individual contributed one sample. Mean age at collection was 49.3 years (SD 15.4) in GS and 57.2 (SD 16.0) in MGB; 56.1% of GS and 64.1% of MGB participants were female (Table 1).

Samples were split once into 70% training (n = 8,945), 15% validation (n = 1,920) and 15% holdout (n = 1,920) partitions; training and validation were pooled as the development set (n = 10,865) and the holdout was used only for final evaluation (see Data partitioning). The partitions were similar in composition: GS made up 79.5%, 79.3% and 79.4% of the training, validation and holdout partitions, mean age was 50.8-51.0 years and 57.7-57.8% were female. Control follow-up is the range across the 21 diseases, from observed follow-up only (Table 2).

DNA methylation preprocessing

Raw IDAT files were read with the SeSAMe Bioconductor package (v1.18+) 70 and processed per sample with a custom pipeline. To detect color channel switching (CCS) probes, signal intensities were first dye-bias-adjusted with dyeBiasNL; this adjustment was used only to compute the in-band to out-of-band ratio and was not carried forward. Type I probes whose in-band to out-of-band signal ratio was below 1.5 were flagged as CCS probes.

Second, a definitive non-linear dye-bias correction (dyeBiasNL) was fitted excluding the identified CCS probes and the SeSAMe-recommended EPICv1 masked probes, and then applied to all probes. Third, detection p-values were computed with pOOBAH, using out-of-band and negative-control signal as background and excluding CCS and recommended-masked probes from the background estimate. Probes with p-value > 0.05 were masked. Fourth, background subtraction was performed with noob 71 , with the same probe exclusions applied to the background estimate. Finally, beta values were extracted for all probes. CCS probes and probes with pOOBAH p-value > 0.05 were set to NA in the final beta matrix. EPICv1 probe identifiers are unique, so no . replicate-probe collapsing was applied. Masked values were imputed with the probe median for feature ranking (see below).

Sample quality control

Samples were excluded using a two-stage filter. In the first stage (QC1), samples were removed if total intensity was ≤ 5,000, detection success was ≤ 0.85, or the proportion of bad CpGs was ≥ 0.05. In the second stage (QC2), the distribution of beta values across ten bins of width 0.1 (0-0.1, …, 0.9-1.0) was computed for each sample. Samples were excluded if the proportion of probes in any bin fell outside the mean ± 4 SD of the samples that passed QC1. In total, 538 of 23,269 samples (2.31%) were excluded: 263 at QC1 and 275 at QC2.

Outcomes and follow-up

Twenty-one diseases were modelled (Table 2). Diseases were retained if more than 200 incident cases within 10 years of sample collection were available in the full GS and MGB pool. Disease definitions were harmonised across GS record-linkage codes and MGB electronic health record definitions (Supplementary Table 1). The modelling set (n = 12,785) comprised all participants with a diagnosis of at least one of the 21 diseases, plus the 2,233 GS participants flagged as healthy, who have no health record activity in the linked data. Participants with none of the 21 diagnoses and no healthy flag were not used, because cases limit class-balanced training and controls beyond four times the number of cases are subsampled in every training fold. Prevalent cases at sample collection were excluded from each disease-specific analysis. Controls were all remaining participants without a recorded diagnosis of the disease during follow-up, irrespective of other conditions; they were not restricted to disease-free individuals, so that the comparator matches the population in which a score would be applied. The time scale was years from sample collection to first diagnosis (cases) or to the end of follow-up (controls). Control follow-up differed between cohorts: median 15.1-15.2 years in GS (observed linkage follow-up) and 6.6-6.8 years in MGB (Table 1). Because the healthy-flagged participants have no linked records, they were assigned a censoring time of 12.0 years, just under the longest observed time to diagnosis among cases (about 13 years), which exceeds the 10-year evaluation horizon. For 24 MGB T2DM cases, event times were set to the recorded diagnosis dates. Outcomes were taken from linked health records (GS) and the electronic health record (MGB), recorded independently of and without knowledge of the methylation data, so outcome assessment was blind to the predictors.

Data partitioning

Samples were assigned once to training (n = 8,945), validation (n = 1,920) and holdout (n = 1,920) partitions (70/15/15) at the sample level, before any feature selection, so that the holdout could be used for an internal evaluation. Cohort mix, age and sex were balanced across the training, validation and holdout partitions.

Training and validation samples were pooled as a development set (n = 10,865 before exclusion of prevalent cases) for feature selection and model fitting, with five-fold cross-validation within it. The holdout was used only to evaluate final models. Hyper-parameters of individual model members were tuned within cross-validation folds (see Survival models).

Probe selection

Within-array probe reproducibility was quantified using EPICv1 within-beadchip paired technical replicates (n = 29). Intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC) were computed using two-way random effects models with absolute agreement, ICC(2,1) 72 , with an inclusion threshold of ICC (2,1) > 0.7, implemented via the irr R package. 73 Probes on chromosomes X and Y were also excluded. A variance filter retained probes with a standard deviation of beta values above 0.02, leaving 440,283 probes in the final pool.

Feature ranking and panel construction

Feature selection was based on comparing feature importances to null features using randomly instantiated tree-based classification models. Tree models were randomly instantiated 40 times, with a random 80/20 train-test split within each iteration. During each iteration, 20 random features were shuffled to remove any possible association with disease and create the null feature set. Feature importances for the null feature set and our real features were then computed using absolute TreeSHAP values on the test group. 74 Features were retained only if they demonstrated a median TreeSHAP value >= 10 of the null feature sets median TreeSHAP values. Panels contained 119 (allergic rhinitis) to 962 (coronary artery disease) features, including age at collection where selected. Feature selection and the null threshold were pooled across cohorts.

Survival models

Models were fitted within the development set using five-fold cross-validation. In each fold, about 80% of the development samples formed the training fold and the remaining 20% the validation fold, which was used to tune model penalties. The holdout was not used at any stage of fitting or tuning.

Within each training fold, the classes were balanced: controls were undersampled to at most four times the number of cases, and cases were then oversampled with replacement to the number of controls (1:1).

Validation folds and the holdout were not resampled. The scores therefore rank individuals by risk, but their absolute values are not population-calibrated.

For each disease, three survival models with a 10-year horizon were fitted: an elastic-net penalised Cox regression 75 , an ensemble of penalised Cox regressions (CoxPHEnsemble) and extra survival trees. 76 The CoxPHEnsemble comprised 500 members (100 per fold). Each member was trained on a bootstrap resample of the balanced training fold, using a random subset of the disease's panel of about one feature per 15 events (minimum five), with its penalty strength tuned on the validation fold. Predicted 10-year risks were averaged across members to give the risk score.

Evaluation

Each score was evaluated on the holdout at a 10-year horizon. Discrimination was measured with the inverse-probability-of-censoring-weighted concordance index (C-index) 77 resamples of the holdout. For each disease, the reported model was the one of the three with the highest holdout C-index.

To present the results by strength of evidence (Table 3), scores were grouped into four descriptive tiers by the unrounded holdout C-index and the number of holdout events within 10 years: tier 1, C-index of at least 0.80 with at least 50 events; tier 1b, C-index of at least 0.80 with fewer than 50 events; tier 2, C-index from 0.70 to below 0.80; and tier 3, C-index below 0.70. The tiers describe point estimates and are not significance thresholds. All 21 scores are reported with confidence intervals and event counts irrespective of tier.

Hazard ratios

The 10-year risk score was standardised and a Cox proportional hazards model was fitted in the holdout over full follow-up, giving HRs per standard deviation of the score, adjusted for age and sex, and unadjusted (Table 3). Benjamini-Hochberg q-values were computed across fits. Because the score is a bounded probability, the unit is one standard deviation of that probability. For prostate and breast cancer, which occur in one sex, all metrics were calculated within the at-risk sex and hazard ratios were adjusted for age alone.

Feature attribution

SHAP values, which quantify how much each feature contributes to an individual's predicted risk, were computed on the holdout for the CoxPHEnsemble of each disease. Because each ensemble member is a linear Cox model, its SHAP value for a feature is exact and equals the feature's coefficient multiplied by the individual's standardised deviation from the training mean. Values were averaged across the 500 members on the log-partial-hazard scale (zero where a member did not use the feature). As each feature is sampled by only a subset of members, features were ranked by the mean absolute SHAP value conditional on inclusion, that is, divided by the proportion of members that sampled the feature, among features sampled by at least 5 of 500 members. SHAP values show how much the model relies on a feature and do not indicate the direction or causality of an association. For dementia, the selected model was a Cox proportional hazards regression, for which SHAP values were computed in closed form and features with non-zero SHAP values in at least one cross-validation fold were retained. For CKD, the selected model was extra survival trees, for which interventional TreeSHAP values were computed on the 10-year predicted-probability scale, so their magnitudes are not comparable with those of other diseases.

Pathway and trait enrichment

Enrichment analyses were run on the panel of each of the 21 diseases, using the cg probes sampled by at least 5 of the 500 ensemble members, mapped to genes with the Illumina EPICv1 hg19 annotation. The background was the 439,530-probe variance-filtered pool from which the panels were selected (background probe panel of cg probes only). Over-representation of panel CpGs was tested in Gene Ontology biological process, KEGG, MSigDB Hallmark 78 , Reactome and WikiPathways gene sets with the missMethyl package 79 , which corrects for the number of probes per gene. P values were adjusted with the Benjamini-Hochberg method within each disease and gene-set collection (adjusted P < 0.05), without adjustment across diseases. 80 Robustness was assessed by repeating the analysis on the top 25% and 50% of each panel by conditional SHAP value and with all EPICv1 probes as background. Panels were also compared with published whole-blood EWAS associations . from the EWAS Atlas (hypergeometric test) and, as a supplementary analysis, tested by preranked gene set enrichment on the panel's own genes. For the eight tier 1 and 1b diseases, panels were also compared with CpGs reported for the same disease in 21 published blood-based epigenome-wide association and methylation-risk-score studies (hypergeometric test). Panel CpGs were also compared with the CpGs that define 12 blood cell types in the EPIC reference library of the EpiDISH package 11, 81 (hypergeometric test against the same background). Genes were ranked by the largest conditional SHAP value among their panel CpGs, using the same hg19 annotation; CpGs without an hg19 gene were not ranked. Panel CpGs were classed as associated with age, sex or smoking if the CpG was reported for that trait in blood in the EWAS Atlas (P < 1e-7). 31

Benchmarking against published scores

We compared the holdout performance of the 21 scores with published methylation risk scores (MRS) and polygenic risk scores (PRS) for the same diseases. A published score was eligible for inclusion if it was a multi-CpG methylation score or a multi-variant polygenic score for one of the 21 diseases and reported an AUC, C-index, odds ratio or hazard ratio for that disease. The following were excluded: Epigenetic clocks and other aging measures, standalone protein EpiScores and multi-omic scores, single-CpG or single-gene markers, epigenome-wide association studies without a score, cell-free DNA and non-blood tissue, and clinical-only models. Models that combined a multi-CpG methylation score with other predictors, such as clinical risk factors or a protein EpiScore, were retained, and the additional components are stated in Supplementary Tables 2 and 3.

Each eligible value was classified as closest, context or not comparable. A closest comparison required the same disease (or, for polygenic scores, its main subtype, for example idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis for interstitial lung disease and Alzheimer's disease for dementia) and an AUC or C-index; for methylation scores it also required an incident design, with blood collected before diagnosis and prevalent cases excluded. For polygenic scores, prevalent, cross-sectional and case-control designs were accepted because genotype is fixed at birth. Context comparisons were informative but not like for like, for example a different endpoint or horizon, a composite endpoint, a population restricted to people with another disease, a preprint, a study that reported only odds or hazard ratios, or an estimate that the original authors described as circular, in-sample or possibly overfitted. Not comparable values were prevalent or cross-sectional methylation scores and non-blood or cell-free samples. Some published estimates may have been derived in the data used to select the score, CpGs, weights or tuning parameter (for example, a tuning parameter chosen by C-index in the evaluation cohort, or CpGs screened before a train-test split) and are therefore likely to be optimistic. Circularity was judged from the published methods, and overlap between evaluation and selection cohorts was assessed where reported. Where such an estimate otherwise met the closest criteria, it was retained as closest and marked as an exception, so that comparisons with our scores are conservative (ten of the 49 studies with a closest comparison; Supplementary Tables 2 and 3, and † in Table 4). Values from one study could fall into different classes.

Values were taken as published from the primary paper. The C-index was preferred and the AUC reported alongside it. Our scores include age and sex only where the model selected them, so published values that include covariates make the comparison conservative. Whether the training or validation cohort of a published . score overlapped GS or the MGB was noted where reported. The difference between our value and the best closest published value was computed separately for methylation and polygenic scores, for score-alone and covariate-adjusted published values, using the same metric.
