What we found

We found 7 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Magnetic Resonance Spectroscopy is a powerful, non-invasive technique for investigating the metabolic composition of biological tissues, particularly useful in the classification of brain tumours. However,its inclusion into machine learning pipelines is limited by the scarcity of large, labelled datasets. This work evaluates whether synthetic MR spectra generated using convex Non-negative Matrix Factorisation and probabilistic modelling of the resulting weight distributions can improve brain tumour classification. MR spectra from the INTERPRET database were decomposed into interpretable source and weight matrices, both separately for each of six diagnostic tasks and jointly across five tissue classes, at short and long echo time. Class-specific weight distributions were modelled using Gaussian Mixture Models and Kernel Density Estimation across a range of hyperparameter settings, and new weight vectors were sampled and recombined with the fixed source matrix to produce synthetic spectra; hyperparameters were selected by the mean squared error to the real class means. The diagnostic utility of the synthetic spectra was assessed with Linear Discriminant Analysis under three internal and four external evaluation strategies, the latter using an independent multi-centre cohort. Synthetic spectra were readily classified by models trained on real data (mean accuracy 0.87), but models trained on synthetic spectra alone generalised poorly, both to real INTERPRET spectra (0.55) and to the external cohort (0.47, against 0.84 for training on real data; p ≤ 0.003). Augmenting real training data with synthetic spectra neither improved nor significantly harmed external accuracy (mean paired differences between -0.04 and +0.01; all p ≥ 0.027 uncorrected, none significant after correction), and class-balanced augmentation did not help, and taskspecific and joint decompositions performed comparably. The selected generative models were low in complexity and produced spectra closer to the class means than the real data are, suggesting that selection by reconstruction fidelity is poorly aligned with the goal of augmentation, and that internal validation alone would have overstated the value of the synthetic data.

1 Introduction

Clinical context and motivation.

Primary brain and other central nervous system (CNS) tumours are, in absolute terms, an uncommon disease group. Across the 40 countries covered by the most recent European Cancer Information System estimates, brain and CNS tumours accounted for an estimated 67,100 new diagnoses in 2020, corresponding to an agestandardised incidence rate of 8.9 per 100,000 population; within the EU-27 specifically, the corresponding figures were 43,600 new cases and an age-standardised rate of 9.4 per 100,000 [1]. This means that brain and CNS tumours are far from the most common types of cancer, yet their impact is disproportionate to their rate of incidence; histological diversity is high, prognosis varies greatly between tumour types and grades, and treatment decisions depend on accurate, early characterisation of the mass.

Tissue biopsy remains the diagnostic gold standard but is invasive, carries surgical risk, and is not always feasible depending on tumour location -especially true in the case of brain tumours. This has motivated interest in non-invasive diagnostic techniques capable of discriminating tumour type without surgical intervention, and proton magnetic resonance spectroscopy ( 1 H-MRS) is one such technique, providing a biochemical fingerprint of tissue metabolism that complements conventional structural imaging. Metabolite patterns derived from MRS have been repeatedly linked to tumour type and grade. Reliable automatic classification from MRS data therefore has direct clinical value, potentially shortening the diagnostic pathway and reducing reliance on invasive procedures.

The data scarcity problem in MRS.

Realising the clinical potential of machine learning for MRS is limited by the availability of large and welllabelled training data. Compared with structural Magnetic resonance Imaging (MRI), MRS acquisition is technically demanding: spectra are more sensitive to field inhomogeneity, require careful voxel placement and longer acquisition times. They are also more susceptible to motion, baseline artefacts, and low signal-to-noise ratios. As a result, MRS is acquired far less routinely in clinical practice and is often reserved for cases where conventional imaging is inconclusive.

The spectra that are acquired must also undergo expert quality control before they can be used for model development [2]. Poor shimming, lipid contamination, and other acquisition artefacts frequently leave us with spectra unsuitable for analysis, further reducing the number of usable samples. In addition, while brain tumours at large are uncommon, many diagnostic subtypes are rarer still. Building datasets of sufficient size therefore requires pooling data from multiple clinical centres, introducing additional variability due to differences in scanners, acquisition protocols, and patient populations.

Consequently, even the largest collaborative MRS datasets contain few high-quality spectra per diagnostic class. The INTERPRET [3] and eTumour [4] datasets used here (Sections 2.1.1, 2.1.2) provide only a few dozen samples per class once quality control and echo time are accounted for. Models trained on datasets of this size overfit and often fail to generalise to unseen centres; an early evaluation of INTERPRET-trained classifiers on eTumour spectra was the first of its kind, motivated by the absence of cross-centre validation in the field [5]. Improving cross-centre generalisation, rather than maximising internal accuracy, is therefore the key challenge and motivates the external validation used here.

Synthetic Spectral Augmentation: Promise and Open Questions.

Synthetic data generation is one of several strategies that have been proposed to address data scarcity in settings where acquiring further real, labelled examples is impractical or prohibitively expensive. Rather than acquiring more spectra, the aim is to model the underlying distribution of existing real data closely enough that additional, plausible samples can be drawn from it and used to augment a limited training set, either to increase effective sample size, to rebalance under-represented classes, or both. Applied naively, this strategy carries an obvious risk: if the generative model captures the real data distribution poorly, or captures it too narrowly, synthetic samples may fail to add genuine variability, may introduce artefacts of their own, or may simply reproduce the biases already present in the limited real training set, in which case augmentation would be expected to help internal validation metrics without conferring any real benefit -or even causing harmwhen the resulting classifier is evaluated on genuinely independent data.

Whether or not synthetic augmentation boosts external generalisation, as opposed to merely increasing internal cross-validation performance, is therefore an empirical question that must be evaluated directly against held-out data acquired under different conditions from the training set. A small number of prior studies have explored generative approaches specifically for MRS data of brain tumours [6] [7] [8] [9] [10] [11] [12], though, to our knowledge, none has evaluated a convex-Non-negative Matrix Factorisation (cNMF)-based generation strategy of the sort proposed within this study -the closest methodological precedent, cNMF-based decomposition of MRS data for source extraction and dimensionality-reduction-driven classification [13] [14] [15], uses the fitted decomposition directly for classification rather than to generate new synthetic training samples -nor, to our knowledge, benchmarked synthetic augmentation against a fully independent, multi-centre external cohort of the kind used in this study.

Purpose of the study.

The purpose of this study is to determine whether synthetic MRS spectra, generated via cNMF decomposition and weight-space resampling, can improve the external generalisation of brain tumour classifiers beyond what is achievable using internal real training data alone. Specifically, we (i) decompose real INTERPRET spectra into class-specific source and weight matrices via convex NMF, both independently per experiment and jointly across all five diagnostic classes; (ii) model the resulting weight distributions using Gaussian Mixture Models (GMM) and Kernel Density Estimation (KDE), generating synthetic spectra across a range of hyperparameter settings and selecting the best-performing configuration by reconstruction fidelity; (iii) evaluate the internal validity of the resulting synthetic data across three complementary classification strategies, assessing whether synthetic spectra maintain compatible distributions with real data and whether they can substitute for or augment it; and (iv) assess, across six diagnostic experiments and two echo times, whether augmenting real training data with synthetic spectra improves classification performance on a fully independent, multi-centre external test cohort relative to a real-data-only baseline. In doing so, this study aims to provide direct evidence on whether cNMF-based synthetic augmentation is a viable route to improving the clinical generalisability of MRS-based brain tumour classifiers, rather than a strategy that merely improves apparent performance under internal validation.

2 Methods

2.1 Datasets

2.1.1 INTERPRET Dataset

The primary dataset used for model training and internal validation was sourced from the INTERPRET project (2000-2002) [3], a multi-centre collaboration that acquired 1 H-MRS and associated clinical data from brain tumour patients. The dataset originates from a subset of cases that passed all quality controls and consists of 304 patients with single-voxel (SV) spectra acquired under standardised protocols across multiple institutions, at short echo time (STE, TE=20-32 ms) and/or long echo time (LTE, TE=135-144 ms), and has been used in multiple studies [5, [16] [17] [18] [19] [20] [21]. The dataset encompasses five diagnostic categories relevant to this study: astrocytoma grade II (A2), glioblastoma (GL), metastasis (ME), meningioma (MM), and normal brain tissue (NO). Class sizes ranged from 15 to 86 spectra per class per echo time, reflecting the inherent scarcity of labelled clinical MRS data. A full breakdown of sample counts per class and echo time is provided in Table 1. Table 2 summarises the acquisition parameters according to the MRSinMRS reporting standards [22].

2.1.2 External Test Set

To assess the generalisation of classifiers trained on synthetic data to an entirely unseen cohort, a complementary multi-centre SV MRS tumour dataset, acquired under comparable protocols at STE and LTE was used.

Tumour spectra were acquired as part of the EU-funded eTumour project [4, 14, 23], at three clinical centres in the Barcelona metropolitan area: CETIR-CDP (Centre Diagnòstic Pedralbes, Unitat Esplugues, Esplugues del Llobregat, Spain), CRC-Corporació Sanitària-IAT (Institut d'Alta Tecnologia, Barcelona, Spain), and IDI-Badalona (Institut de Diagnòstic per la Imatge, Unitat Badalona, Badalona, Spain). These data had previously been used as an independent test set in two studies [17, 23] Additionally, the normal brain test data had been acquired at Institut de Diagnòstic per la Imatge, Unitat Bellvitge, L'Hospitalet de Llobregat, Spain, and were also previously reported [24]. None of the test set spectra were used at any stage of model training, feature selection, or hyperparameter optimisation, ensuring a fully independent evaluation. The same preprocessing and normalisation procedures applied to the INTERPRET data were applied identically to the external spectra prior to evaluation. Class sizes for the external test set after label harmonisation are provided in Table 1. Acquisition parameters are also detailed in Table 2.

2.1.3 Preprocessing and Spectral Representation

Raw data had been previously processed according to the INTERPRET protocol [23, 25, 26], which, in brief, includes: Fourier transform; division of the suppressed water by the unsuppressed water spectrum; line broadening (1Hz) water suppression with the HLSVD algorithm (10 components in the x y interval); residual water removal setting spectral points between 4.2 and 5.1 ppm to zero; linear interpolation to 512 points over a 1000Hz of BW, resulting in 512-point vectors spanning -2.7 to 7.1 ppm, followed by L2-normalisation. For this work, spectra were further cropped to a fixed-length vector of 235 frequency bins subsequently L2normalised, such that the Euclidean norm of each spectrum equalled one [27]. This normalisation was applied independently to each spectrum and consistently across real, reconstructed, and synthetic samples.

2.1.4 Experimental Configurations

Because the value of synthetic augmentation is likely to depend on the difficulty and structure of the classification task, we did not evaluate it on a single problem but across six binary and multi-class tasks spanning a range of clinical difficulty, from a high-separability baseline to overlapping tumour-only comparisons: • A2 vs NO -binary discrimination between low-grade astrocytoma and normal brain tissue, serving as a high-separability baseline.

• MM vs ME -binary classification between meningioma and metastasis, representing a more challenging tumour-only comparison with overlapping metabolic profiles.

• MM vs ME vs A2 -three-class tumour-only classification combining two spectrally distinct low-grade tumour types, meningioma and low-grade astrocytoma and metastasis, which is high grade.

• MM vs ME vs NO -three-class task introducing healthy tissue alongside two tumour types, one low grade and the other high grade.

• MM vs GL vs A2 -three-class tumour-only classification combining two spectrally distinct low-grade tumour types, meningioma and low-grade astrocytoma and glioblastoma, a high-grade tumour with a population-diverse pattern.

• MM vs GL vs NO -three-class experiment combining low-grade beingn tumour, high-grade class-diverse tumour, and normal brain tissue.

Each configuration was evaluated independently for both STE and LTE acquisitions, yielding twelve distinct classification settings in total. This design allows us to test not only whether augmentation helps on average, but whether any benefit is consistent across task difficulty and echo time, or confined to particular regimes.

2.2 Representing Class Structure via cNMF

The first requirement for generating synthetic spectra is a representation in which each class's variability can be modelled directly. A 235-dimensional spectrum is too high-dimensional to model class-conditionally from only a few dozen examples; we therefore seek a low-dimensional representation that (i) captures the dominant spectral variation within each class, (ii) remains physically interpretable as MRS signal, and (iii) permits exact reconstruction back to spectrum space so that samples drawn in the low-dimensional space can be turned back into spectra. cNMF [28] satisfies all three requirements, which motivates its use here.

2.2.1 Convex NMF

cNMF was applied independently to each experimental subset to decompose the spectral data matrix V ∈ R m×n , where m = 235 denotes the number of frequency bins and n the number of spectra, into a product of source and weight matrices: where W ∈ R m×k contains k basis spectra and H ∈ R k×n encodes the per-sample contribution of each source component.

Unlike standard NMF, cNMF constrains W to lie within the convex hull of the original data, such that W = VA for some non-negative matrix A ≥ 0 with columns summing to one [13, 29]. This constraint encourages basis vectors to resemble convex combinations of real spectral profiles rather than abstract mathematical constructs, securing our requirement (ii) above, and improving the interpretability of the decomposition and ensuring that extracted sources remain physically plausible as MRS spectra.

2.2.2 Number of Components and Initialisation

The number of components k was set equal to the number of diagnostic classes (k = 2 for binary and k = 3 for ternary tasks) to encourage a one-to-one correspondence between sources and tissue types; clear class clustering was observed in weight space, with some overlap for spectrally similar classes (Figure 1). cNMF was run for at most 5000 iterations per initialisation, minimising the Frobenius norm ∥V -WH∥ F , and repeated over multiple random initialisations to mitigate local optima. One run per (experiment, echo time) was retained, selected once by visual inspection of the sources for correspondence to known tumour biochemistry, because the representation must support physically meaningful spectra rather than merely minimise numerical residual.

2.2.3 Joint Decomposition

As an alternative to these task-specific decompositions, cNMF was also applied jointly to all five classes for each echo time (k = 5), and the weight subset for each of the same six experiments was extracted from the joint weight matrix, allowing a direct comparison under an otherwise identical pipeline (Section 2.4).

2.2.4 Role of the Weight Matrix

The weight matrix H produced by cNMF serves as the central representation from which synthetic data are generated. Each column of H is a k-dimensional weight vector describing how strongly each source component contributes to a given spectrum. Because the source matrix W is held fixed after decomposition, any new weight vector ĥ sampled from a distribution fitted to H can be recombined with W to produce a novel synthetic spectrum. The quality and diversity of the resulting synthetic spectra therefore depend directly on how faithfully the generative models capture the true distribution of weights within each class. 2.3 Generating Plausible Spectra from the Representation

Given the weight representation from Stage 1, generating a synthetic spectrum means drawing a plausible new weight vector for a class and reconstructing it through W. This stage therefore has three parts: cleaning the weight distributions of artefacts that would corrupt a fitted model (Section 2.3.1), fitting generative models to those distributions (Section 2.3.2), and sampling and reconstructing spectra from the fitted models (Section 2.3.3). A final part selects, for each task, the generative configuration that reconstructs the real data most faithfully (Section 2.3.4).

2.3.1 Heuristic Outlier Filtering

Prior to distribution modelling, each per-class weight distribution was screened for outliers. Samples whose weight values deviated more than three standard deviations from the class-component mean (|z| > 3) were removed. The class distributions in weight space overlap, so a few extreme cases lie well inside the region occupied by another class and would pull a fitted generative model towards it; the weight-space scatter plots show such cases for A2 (Figure 1), and outlying real cases are also visible in the decision-space projection (Figures 7). Removing them is a simple, reproducible way of preventing these observations from biasing the fitted models, at the cost of discarding a small number of atypical but genuine spectra. The number of cases removed per experiment is summarised in Table 3.

2.3.2 Generative Modelling of Weight Distributions

The filtered weight matrix H, segmented by class label, was used to fit generative models independently for each class, for both the split and joint decompositions. Two complementary modelling strategies were employed and retained in parallel through the remainder of the pipeline (i.e. synthetic data generated by each was carried forward to classification separately, rather than one being selected over the other).

Gaussian Mixture Models

GMMs represent the underlying distribution as a weighted sum of K Gaussian components, each parametrised by its own mean vector, covariance matrix, and mixture weight. Models were fitted using the Expectation-Maximisation (EM) algorithm with full covariance matrices, as implemented in scikit-learn [27]. The number of mixture components K was treated as a hyperparameter and varied from 1 (reducing to a single Gaussian fit) to 10, enabling assessment of how model complexity affects synthetic data quality.

Kernel Density Estimation

KDE is a non-parametric approach that estimates the underlying probability density function by placing a kernel over each observed data point. Two kernel types were evaluated: Gaussian and tophat. The bandwidth parameter h, which governs the smoothness of the estimated density, was varied on a logarithmic scale from 10 -4 to 1. KDE was implemented using the KernelDensity estimator from scikit-learn [27].

2.3.3 Synthetic Weight Sampling and Spectrum Reconstruction

For each class, synthetic weight vectors were sampled from the fitted generative model, generating twice the number of original (post-filtering) samples for that class (synthetic-to-real ratio of 2:1). Because the cNMF weight space is non-negative, sampling was performed by rejection: samples with any negative component were discarded, up to a maximum of 20 sampling attempts. Rejection depends only on the sign of the sampled weights: no signal-to-noise-based criterion is applied to synthetic spectra. The proportion of rejected samples and the number of times the attempt limit was reached were recorded for every class and hyperparameter setting (Table 5). 2.3.4 Selecting the Generative Configuration by Reconstruction Fidelity

For each experiment, echo time and model family, the hyperparameter (GMM components, or KDE bandwidth and kernel) was chosen by minimising the mean squared error (MSE) between the synthetic spectra and a reference class mean, with n frequency bins, synthetic intensity x i and real class mean y i :

The class mean was computed once per class from all L2-normalised real spectra pooled across split experiments, so that one reference was used throughout. The MSE was averaged across classes, synthetic spectra were then reconstructed only at the optimum, and GMM and KDE settings were retained independently, with no selection between the two families. As a reference level, the same MSE was computed for the cNMF reconstruction of the cleaned real weights; this baseline is not a lower bound, since a generator concentrated near the class centre can score lower than the real weights. For the joint decomposition the sweep was run once for all five classes and the hyperparameter was re-selected by the same criterion over only the classes of each experiment. For the joint decomposition, synthetic weights were generated once for all five classes and the hyperparameter grid was swept once. When the resulting synthetic data were used for a given two-or three-class experiment, the hyperparameter was re-selected by the same MSE criterion averaged over only the classes of that experiment, so that a model complexity chosen to fit unrelated classes was not imposed on a task with a smaller per-class sample. In one configuration (joint decomposition, GMM, A2 vs NO at STE) the number of GMM components was instead fixed manually to K = 1 rather than selected automatically. This is a deviation from the otherwise automatic selection procedure and is noted as a limitation in the Discussion.

2.4 Classification Pipeline

The final stage tests the study's central hypothesis. The key methodological commitment here is that internal performance alone cannot answer it: a generative model that merely memorises the training distribution can inflate cross-validated accuracy while offering no benefit, or even harm, on data from an unseen centre. Our evaluation is therefore built in two tiers, an internal tier that validates the generative model's behaviour, and an external tier, against a fully independent cohort, that constitutes the actual test of clinical value. Figure 2 summarises the pipeline.

2.4.1 Internal Validation Strategies

Three complementary internal evaluation strategies were applied to each experimental configuration: • Real2Syn -the classifier is trained on real spectra and evaluated on synthetic spectra, assessing whether synthetic data occupies a compatible decision space.

• Syn2Real -the classifier is trained exclusively on synthetic spectra and evaluated on real spectra, testing whether synthetic data can substitute for real training data.

• Mixed2Real -the classifier is trained on real spectra combined with all synthetic spectra for that experiment, and evaluated on withheld real spectra, simulating a data augmentation scenario.

Real2Syn and Syn2Real involve training and test sets drawn from inherently separate populations (real vs. synthetic) and were therefore each evaluated as a single run, with the standard deviation estimated by bootstrap resampling (1000 resamples) of the test predictions. Mixed2Real was evaluated using 5-fold stratified cross-validation on the real data: within each fold, the held-out real partition served as the test set, while the training set comprised the remaining real folds plus the full synthetic set for that experiment; results were averaged across the five folds. The synthetic spectra used in Mixed2Real, and in Real2Syn and Syn2Real, were generated from all real spectra of the experiment, including those that constitute the heldout real partition in a given fold; the internal tier therefore does not provide a leakage-free estimate of the benefit of augmentation, which is the reason the external tier is treated as the primary test (see Discussion).

2.4.2 External Validation Strategies

The external tier tests generalisation against the independent cohort of Section 2.1.2, and is the primary measure of clinical applicability. Four strategies isolate the contribution of synthetic data against the realdata-only baseline: • Real2Ext -trained on internal real spectra only (baseline).

• Syn2Ext -trained on synthetic spectra only.

• Real Syn2Ext -trained on internal real spectra plus the full synthetic set.

• Real Syn Bal2Ext -trained on real spectra augmented with synthetic spectra sampled to bring each minority class up to the size of the largest real class (class-balancing).

Real2Ext, Syn2Ext and Real Syn2Ext, involve no random sampling and were each evaluated as a single deterministic run, with the external test set bootstrapped to obtain the standard deviation. Real Syn Bal2Ext depends on a random draw of synthetic spectra for class-balancing and was therefore averaged over 10 random seeds; feature selection for this strategy was fixed using a single reference seed to keep the selected feature set consistent across the 10 evaluation runs.

2.4.3 Feature Selection

Feature selection was performed using the CfsSubsetEval criterion in Weka [30], coupled with GreedyStepwise search operating in backwards elimination mode, approximating Sequential Floating Forward Selection (SFFS). This approach identifies a minimal, non-redundant subset of spectral indices with high predictive utility. A maximum of ten features were retained. This limit was chosen as a reasonable number based on the ranges used in previous MRS classification studies. Features were selected on the training data of the strategy being evaluated (real, synthetic, or their union): within each training partition for Mixed2Real, on the real training set for Real2Syn, and once on the full training set for Real2Ext, Real Syn2Ext, Syn2Ext and Real Syn Bal2Ext (the latter using the reference seed). The exception is Syn2Real, whose features were selected on the real spectra, which are also its test set. This real-basis choice lets the test data influence the feature set and makes the Syn2Real estimate optimistic; we therefore also report the estimate obtained when features are selected on the synthetic training set. When CFS retained fewer than two features, the first two spectral bins were used so that a classifier could be fitted. This occurred twice, both in the split KDE decomposition of A2 vs NO at STE (Syn2Ext, and one Mixed2Real fold); in both cases these bins were constant in the synthetic spectra and the classifier could not be fitted, so the two corresponding results are missing.

2.4.4 Classifier

Classification was performed using Linear Discriminant Analysis (LDA), which identifies a linear projection of the feature space that maximises the ratio of between-class to within-class scatter. LDA was selected for its established performance in MRS-based tumour classification [25], its robustness in small-sample settings, and its interpretability, properties well suited to the goal of evaluating synthetic data contributions rather than maximising predictive performance through model complexity.

Performance Metrics

Performance was quantified by accuracy and the Balanced Error Rate (BER), BER = 1 )︁ (equivalently one minus balanced accuracy), which is invariant to class imbalance.

Accuracy is the value of a single fitted model for Real2Syn, Syn2Real, Real2Ext, Real+Syn2Ext and Syn2Ext, the mean over five folds for Mixed2Real, and the mean over ten balancing seeds for Real+Syn Bal.2Ext. The accompanying standard deviation therefore has a different meaning for each: cross-validation variability for Mixed2Real, bootstrap variability over the test set (B = 1000, model fitted once) for the single-fit strategies, and seed variability for Real+Syn Bal.2Ext. These are not directly comparable.

Statistical Comparison of Augmentation Strategies

To assess whether synthetic data augmentation improved external generalisation relative to the Real2Ext baseline, paired differences in accuracy were computed across the twelve (experiment × echo time) settings for each augmentation strategy, decomposition mode (split/joint), and generative model family (GMM/KDE). Statistical significance of the mean paired difference was assessed using a two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test, with zero differences discarded. Effect size was quantified using the rank-biserial correlation r, and 95% confidence intervals on the mean difference were obtained by bootstrap resampling (10,000 resamples). The same procedure was used to compare the joint with the split decomposition. No correction for multiple comparisons was applied to the reported p-values; where relevant we state which results survive a Bonferroni correction.

3.4 Joint versus Split Decomposition

Table 10 compares the joint (single five-class) decomposition with the task-specific (split) decomposition on external accuracy. No comparison was significant. The mean joint-minus-split difference was +0.003 for the Real2Ext baseline, between -0.035 and -0.007 for Real+Syn2Ext, between -0.010 and +0.057 for Syn2Ext, and between -0.012 and +0.026 for Real+Syn Bal.2Ext (all p ≥ 0.147). The joint decomposition therefore matched the task-specific decomposition within the resolution of this design. Differences within individual settings were nonetheless sometimes large and of either sign. Even the Real2Ext baseline, which depends on the decomposition only through which cases survive outlier removal, differed by up to +0.105 (MM vs ME, LTE) and -0.083 (MM vs ME vs NO, both echo times), and Syn2Ext differed between decompositions by as much as +0.42 and -0.54 (split GMM versus joint GMM, MM vs GL vs NO at LTE and MM vs ME vs NO at LTE respectively). We therefore interpret the absence of a systematic difference as evidence of comparable average performance rather than of stability.

3.5 Statistical Comparison of Augmentation Strategies

Paired differences in external accuracy between each augmentation strategy and the Real2Ext baseline were assessed with a two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test over the twelve (experiment × echo time) settings, separately for each decomposition mode and generative model (Table 9), with effect sizes given as the rankbiserial correlation r and 95% confidence intervals on the mean difference from 10,000 bootstrap resamples. Neither augmentation strategy that retained real data (Real+Syn2Ext, Real+Syn Bal.2Ext) produced a significant improvement in any of the eight comparisons. Real+Syn2Ext showed no significant change in any of its four comparisons, whereas Real+Syn Bal.2Ext was nominally worse in one (split decomposition, KDE: -0.042, p = 0.027), a difference that did not survive correction for multiple comparisons. The 95% confidence intervals on the mean difference had half-widths of about 0.03-0.04, and their upper limits were at most +0.053 in all eight comparisons, so there is no evidence that augmentation caused harm in general, but also no evidence of benefit. The only nominally significant result for these strategies was a decrease for Real+Syn Bal.2Ext with split KDE (p = 0.027, uncorrected). Syn2Ext was significantly worse than the baseline in all four decomposition-model combinations, with very large negative effect sizes (r ≤ -0.94).

The confidence intervals for the class-balanced strategy were narrower than those for Real+Syn2Ext in three of the four decomposition-model combinations (widths 0.058-0.073 against 0.069-0.083), consistent with its more stable accuracy across settings, and the one nominally significant result for these strategies was a decrease.

4 Discussion

4.1 Principal Findings

This study asked whether synthetic MR spectra, generated by cNMF decomposition and resampling of the resulting weight distributions, can improve the external generalisation of brain tumour classifiers. Across six diagnostic tasks, two echo times, two decomposition strategies and two generative model families, the answer was no. Augmenting real training data with synthetic spectra did not significantly change accuracy on an independent multi-centre cohort (Real+Syn2Ext: mean paired differences between -0.03 and +0.01; all p ≥ 0.38). Class-balanced augmentation did not help either; it was nominally worse in one of four comparisons (-0.042, p = 0.027, not significant after correction) and indistinguishable from the baseline in the other three. The lack of harm is itself informative: synthetic spectra can be added to real training data at no measurable cost, even though they bring no measurable benefit. Training on synthetic spectra alone was significantly worse than training on real data (mean external accuracy 0.474 against 0.838; p ≤ 0.003 in every decomposition-model combination). Internally, the same synthetic spectra looked considerably more successful: they were classified correctly by real-trained models (Real2Syn, mean accuracy 0.874), and adding them to the training set gave a high cross-validated accuracy (Mixed2Real, 0.903). Only the external tier, and the Syn2Real strategy (mean 0.553, itself an optimistic figure; see Limitations), exposed that the synthetic data carried little information beyond what the real data already contained.

4.2 Synthetic Spectra Are Classifiable but Not Substitutable

The asymmetry between Real2Syn (0.874) and Syn2Real (0.553; 0.500 when features are selected on the synthetic training set rather than on the real test spectra) was present in 45 of the 48 settings and was not confined to a particular task, echo time, generative model or decomposition. A real-trained classifier can label synthetic spectra correctly because those spectra fall inside the decision regions it learned from the real data. The converse fails because the synthetic spectra do not span the real distribution: a classifier trained on them learns boundaries that are placed too tightly around the synthetic class centres and misclassifies the more variable real spectra. This is the pattern expected from a generator that is accurate about where each class is centred but too narrow about how far real spectra stray from that centre. It is not the pattern expected from an inaccurate generator, which would degrade Real2Syn as well.

Three observations from the generative pipeline support this interpretation. First, the selection criterion favoured very simple generators: a single-component GMM (one Gaussian per class) was chosen in 7 of the 12 task-specific configurations, and small KDE bandwidths were chosen throughout (Section 3.1). Second, at the selected GMM setting the synthetic spectra lay at least as close to the real class means as the cNMF reconstruction of the real weights did, in every configuration, and at only about half to two-thirds of the distance at LTE for five of the six tasks (e.g. MM vs ME, LTE: 3.6×10 -4 against 7. 8×10 -4 ). This comparison also shows that the real-weight reconstruction is not a floor for the MSE: a generator can score better than the real data it was fitted to precisely by being less variable than it. A third observation concerns the sampler itself: more than half of the sampled weight vectors were rejected for having a non-positive component (Table 5), so the synthetic weights come from a truncated version of the fitted distribution, which further concentrates them. Synthetic spectra that are closer to the class mean than real spectra are less variable than the data they are meant to represent. This is a methodological point worth foregrounding: fidelity to the class mean and usefulness as training data are not the same objective, and a criterion that optimises the former can actively select against the latter.

4.3 Why Augmentation Did Not Improve External Generalisation

Several explanations are consistent with our results, and they are not mutually exclusive; the present experimental design cannot separate them.

Synthetic data add volume, not diversity.

If the generated spectra lie within the envelope of the real training spectra, as the domain-gap findings suggest, then adding them to a linear classifier trained on ten selected features changes little, which is what we observed: Real+Syn2Ext left accuracy unchanged in 4-5 of the 12 settings and moved it in either direction in the rest.

The baseline was already strong.

The Real2Ext baseline reached a mean external accuracy of 0.838, and 1.000 for A2 vs NO at LTE with the split decomposition, leaving limited headroom in the easier tasks. Improvement was more plausible in the harder three-class tasks, but the effects there were of both signs and comparable in magnitude.

Limited statistical power.

With twelve paired settings per comparison, the 95% confidence intervals on the mean difference had halfwidths of roughly 0.03-0.04 and upper limits of at most +0.053. Improvements larger than about five percentage points can therefore be regarded as unlikely, but smaller benefits could not have been detected.

4.4 Task-Specific versus Joint Decomposition

No comparison between the joint five-class decomposition and the task-specific decompositions was significant (all p ≥ 0.147; Table 10). A practically useful reading is that one shared decomposition can serve several diagnostic tasks without a measurable cost in average performance, which simplifies deployment. The settinglevel differences were, however, sometimes large and of sign, up to +0.42 and -0.54 for Syn2Ext, and even the Real2Ext baseline differed by up to about 0.1 owing only to which cases survived outlier removal. We interpret this as evidence of instability in individual configurations rather than of a real advantage in either direction.

4.5 Comparison with Prior Work

In a recent review by the MRS Synthetic Data Working Group [31], only a small number of studies to date have created synthetic magnetic resonance spectroscopic data specifically tailored to clinical scenarios such as brain tumour classification. The closest MRS-specific precedent for generating spectra is the comparison of linear, non-linear and deep generative models by Olliverre et al. [6]. Their evaluation used a held-out subset of the same dataset, whereas ours uses an independently acquired multi-centre cohort; the weaker benefit we report may therefore reflect the harder test rather than a weaker generator, and our own internal results (Mixed2Real, 0.903) would have supported a more favourable conclusion. cNMF has been used for source extraction and decomposition-driven classification of MRS data [13] [14] [15]; we extend the same representation to a generative role and, to our knowledge, provide the first evaluation of that use against an independent external cohort. Our real-data baseline can be situated against the earlier INTERPRET-to-eTumour evaluation [5].

5 Conclusions

This study evaluated whether synthetic MRS spectra, generated by cNMF decomposition and resampling of the resulting weight distributions, can improve the external generalisation of brain tumour classifiers. The synthetic spectra produced by this approach are internally coherent and are readily classified by models trained on real data, confirming that the generative procedure captures genuine class structure. They did not, however, improve classification performance on an independently acquired multi-centre cohort relative to training on real data alone, and they proved a poor substitute for real training data when used in isolation. The pattern of results points to a generative distribution that is faithful to each class's central tendency but too narrow to supply the variability that augmentation requires, a shortcoming we interpret as arising from the criterion by which the generative models were selected rather than from the decomposition itself, although the present design does not test this directly.

Two contributions follow. The first is methodological: to our knowledge, this is the first evaluation of cNMF-based weight-space resampling as a generative strategy for MRS augmentation, extending a decomposition previously used for source extraction and classification into a generative role, and identifying the mismatch between reconstruction fidelity and augmentation utility as the critical design decision in doing so. The second is evaluative, and applies beyond the particular method studied here. Had this work relied on internal cross-validation alone, the same synthetic data would have appeared to succeed (although the internal estimates are themselves optimistic); it was only under independent external validation that its limitations became apparent. We therefore argue that independent, ideally multi-centre, external cohorts should be treated as a requirement rather than a refinement when assessing synthetic data augmentation in MRS, and we suggest that generative models intended for augmentation be selected on their distributional coverage rather than on their fidelity to a class average.
