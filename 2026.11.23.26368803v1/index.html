Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. Abstract

Background. Debate persists regarding the potential harm reduction role of Electronic Nicotine Delivery Systems (ENDS). This study estimated the effect of ENDS use on subsequent cigarette smoking days in the past 30 days among adult smokers.

We analyzed longitudinal data from Waves 4 to 6 of the Population Assessment of Tobacco and Health (PATH) Study, including 4,909 established current smokers. Bayesian Additive Regression Trees for causal inference was used to estimate the average exposure effect (ATE) of current ENDS use (characterized as no use, some-day use, and daily use) at Wave 5 (2018 -2019) on cigarette smoking days at Wave 6 (2021), adjusting for 26 covariates measured at Wave 4 (2016 -2018).

Results. Compared with no ENDS use, daily ENDS use reduced cigarette smoking by 6.17 days per month over a two-year period (ATE = -6.17, 95% CI: -8.16 to -5.29); whereas some-day use reduced smoking by 0.99 days (ATE = -0.99, 95% CI: -1.65 to -0.28). Compared with some-day use, daily ENDS users reduced smoking by 5.65 days per month (ATE = -5.65, 95% CI: -6.72 to -4.72). There was no evidence that these effects differed meaningfully across demographic or psychosocial subgroups.

Conclusions. Among established adult smokers, daily ENDS use reduced subsequent smoking days compared with some-day and no ENDS use. These findings support the potential role of ENDS as a harm reduction strategy for adult smokers while highlighting the need for better characterizing behavioral patterns and health consequences associated with sustained ENDS use.

Among US adult established current smokers, Daily ENDS use reduced cigarette smoking by approximately 6 days per month over a two-year period compared with some-day and no ENDS use. These effects do not vary meaningfully by sex, education, race/ethnicity, annual household income, mental health status, sexual orientation, or age, suggesting consistent impacts across diverse populations. Findings support the potential of ENDS as a harm reduction tool for adult smokers and warrents more understanding on sustained dual smokers who vaps.

Effects of Electronic Nicotine Delivery Systems Use on Reduced Cigarette Smoking Days

Cigarette smoking remains prevalent in the United States (US) and is the leading cause of preventable morbidity and mortality. 1 Smoking cessation therefore remains a public health priority. 2 Although evidence-based cessation interventions and U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) -approved pharmacotherapies are available, their uptake is limited and quit rates remain modest (e.g., 8.8% in 2022), [3] [4] [5] underscoring the need for alternative accessible and effective strategies.

Electronic Nicotine Delivery Systems (ENDS), commonly known as e-cigarettes, have emerged as a potential harm-reduction and cessation tool. 9 ENDS deliver nicotine via aerosol without the products of tobacco combustion and are therefore generally considered less harmful than cigarettes. [6] [7] [8] [9] [10] ENDS have become increasingly accessible and widely used among U.S. adults, with current use rising from 1.9% in 2012 to 7.0% in 2024, despite a temporary decline to 3.7% in 2020. [11] [12] [13] [14] [15] Most adult ENDS users are current or former cigarette smokers, particularly younger adults, 16 and many report using ENDS to reduce or quit smoking. Randomized clinical trials have provided growing evidence supporting the efficacy of ENDS for smoking cessation, 9 whereas findings from observational studies remain mixed. 17 This discrepancy suggests that further research is needed to assess the efficacy of ENDS on cigarette smoking under real-world conditions, across multiple smoking-related outcomes, and both in the overall population and socio-demographically diverse subpopulations. Notably, a recent review of 38 analyses found that ENDS use -particularly daily use compared with some-day or no use -was associated with a greater likelihood of smoking cessation. 17 Assessing the efficacy of ENDS use on cigarette smoking using observational data is challenging because ENDS exposure is not randomly assigned. Thus, exposed and unexposed individuals may differ in ways that influence the outcomes. 18, 19 To combat this potential bias, previous studies have adjusted for a variety of potential confounders, including sociodemographic, interpersonal, behavioral, and social environmental factors, though the specific confounders included have varied considerably across studies. Recent developments in modern causal inference methods, including the Bayesian Additive Regression Trees (BART) approach, provide a principled framework for estimating causal effects from observational data by explicitly accounting for observed confounding under a set of well-defined assumptions. 20, 21 Another challenge in assessing the efficacy of ENDS is the choice of outcome. Smoking behavior is commonly quantified using smoking frequency, the number of days smoked in the past 30 days (range: 0 to 30), hereafter referred to as smoking days. However, in the scientific literature, when used as an outcome variable, it is often dichotomized (e.g., splitting at a cutpoint such as none vs any use in the past 30 days) or categorized into small groups. 17 Key limitations of this approach include several well-documented statistical drawbacks 22, 23 : loss of information, as collapsing a continuous measure removes fine-grained variation and reduces analytic sensitivity and efficiency; reduced statistical power, increasing risk of false-negative findings; and potential distortion of effect estimates, as evidenced in simulation studies.

Although smoking days is inherently a continuous variable, it has rarely been analyzed as such. This outcome often exhibits clustering at both the lower and upper bounds, reflecting participants who quit smoking (0 days) and those who continue to smoke daily (30 days). The bounded and bimodal nature of this distribution complicates standard continuous-outcome modeling. 24 To our knowledge, our study is among the first to estimate the efficacy of ENDS use on subsequent smoking days. Using longitudinal observational data from Waves 4 -6 (2016 -2021) of the Population Assessment of Tobacco and Health (PATH) Study, we analyzed a cohort of established adult smokers and applied BART for causal inference to account for potential confounding by factors associated with both ENDS use and smoking behaviors. 20 Specifically, we addressed the following research questions.

Q1 (Average Exposure/Treatment Effect; ATE):

What would be the expected change in the average number of subsequent smoking days over a two-year follow-up period if smokers had used ENDS daily (i.e., daily ENDS use vs. no use) or on some days (i.e., some-day ENDS use vs. no use) compared to no ENDS use? Q2 (Group-specific Average Exposure/Treatment Effect; Group-specific ATE): Are any effects identified for Q1 moderated by participants' sex, education, race/ethnicity, annual household income, internalization, externalization, sexual orientation, or age?

Methods

Data source and study sample

The PATH Study is a nationally representative longitudinal cohort supported by the National Institutes of Health (NIH) and the FDA to inform tobacco regulatory science by characterizing population-level tobacco use patterns and associated health outcomes following the expansion of FDA regulatory authority in 2009. Since 2013, PATH has followed youth (ages 12-17 years; n = 13,651) and adults (ages ≥18 years; n = 32,320), including tobacco users and non-users, across multiple waves. Each wave collects extensive information on tobacco and nicotine product use, dependence, transitions in use, and health indicators, providing a comprehensive resource for studying tobacco behaviors and their context factors (see published design documentation for details). 25 In this study, we included 4,909 established current cigarette smokers who met the following criteria: a) participated in Waves 4 (12/2016-01/2018; Baseline wave), 5 (12/2018 -11/2019; Exposure wave), and 6 (03/2021 -11/2021; Outcome wave) data collection; therefore they have non-missing values on sampling weights for primary analyses; b) reported having smoked more than 100 cigarettes in their lifetime at baseline and had smoked within the past 30 days at both the baseline and exposure waves; c) reported ENDS use status at the exposure wave, d) no missing values on all baseline covariates; and e) reported their cigarette smoking days at the outcome wave. The participant inclusion flowchart is presented in Figure S1.

Measures

To facilitate causal interpretation, we enforced a strict temporal ordering of variables, such that covariates precede exposure and exposure precedes the outcome. This ordering ensures that causes temporally precede effects and mitigates issues arising from adjusting for potential mediators, colliders, or bidirectional relationships, which can bias the causal effect estimate. 26, 27 Details on study variables are presented in Table S1.

ENDS use exposure (Wave 5) was measured using two self-reported items. Participants were first asked whether they had used ENDS in the past 30 days. Those who reported "Yes" were then asked whether they had used ENDS not at all, on some days, or every day during that period. Based on these responses, ENDS use was categorized as no (current) use [reference group], some-day use, and daily use.

Smoking days outcome (Wave 6

). Similar to ENDS use, participants were asked whether they had smoked cigarettes in the past 30 days. Those who reported "Yes" were subsequently asked whether they had smoked not at all, on some days, or every day during that period. Only participants who reported some-day smoking were asked to indicate the number of smoking days in the past 30 days. Those who reported daily smoking or not at all were not asked the follow-up question about the number of smoking days; therefore, we assigned a value of 30 days to indicate daily smoking and a value of 0 days to indicate no smoking. Therefore, smoking days is a numerical value ranging from 0 to 30.

Covariates (Wave 4). We adjusted for 26 pre-exposure covariates (22 categorical and 4 continuous variables), as they are potentially associated with both ENDS use exposure and subsequent smoking days. 17, 28, 29 Pre-exposure covariates included age group, sex, race/ethnicity, educational attainment, annual household income, region, sexual orientation, absolute harm perception of cigarette smoking, relative harm perception of ENDS use compared to cigarette smoking, house policy of cigarette use, family tobacco use, internalization, externalization, heart disease, respiratory disease, or cancer in the past 12 months, body mass index; past month marijuana use and alcohol use; other combustible tobacco use, cigarette quit intention, secondhand cigarette smoking exposure in the past 7 days, time to first smoking after waking, and nicotine dependence. Covariates also included baseline measures of exposure and outcome variables. Missing values for categorical covariates were recoded as a "Don't know" category.

For the BART analyses, categorical variables were converted into dummy variables, resulting in a total of 64 dummy variables and four continuous covariates.

Analytic Strategy

Descriptive statistics of study variables were reported for the overall sample and by ENDS exposure level. To examine the effect of ENDS use on subsequent smoking days over a two-year period, we used BART for causal inference for the primary analyses and performed sensitivity tests to evaluate the impact of potential violations of key assumptions. Based on prior studies, 17 our primary research questions focus on two comparisons: Daily ENDS use versus No use (n = 4303), and Some-day ENDS use versus No use (n = 4638). For completeness, we also estimated the effects of Daily ENDS use versus Some-day use (n = 877). Accordingly, we conducted three parallel analyses, with each exposure comparison estimated in separate analyses.

BART is a flexible, nonparametric modeling approach that integrates boosted regression tree models within a Bayesian inferential framework. 30 It enables flexible modeling of an outcome variable conditional on a set of covariates, without imposing parametric assumptions. It constructs an ensemble of many small regression trees, where each tree makes a small contribution to the overall fit. This structure helps capture complex, nonlinear relationships between the outcome and the covariates, while effectively mitigating overfitting through three weakly informative priors. BART is fit using Markov Chain Monte Carlo methods.

BART for causal inference. BART has gained popularity for both its statistical development and its application to causal inference research. 20, 21, [31] [32] [33] A key advantage of BART over alternative methods (e.g., propensity score approaches) for causal inference is the fact that it doesn't require the iterative process of model fitting, data restructuring (for instance matching or weighting), and balance checking across multiple cycles that has the disadvantage of complicating the procedure and increasing "researcher degrees of freedom." 34, 35 BART has demonstrated superior performance in numerous complicated inferential settings including several causal inference data analysis challenges. 33, 36, 37 BART for causal inference methods is more easily conceptualized within the potential outcomes framework, 38 which posits two potential outcomes in response to a binary exposure: 𝑌𝑌(1), the outcome that would be observed under exposure, and 𝑌𝑌(0), the outcome that would be observed absent the exposure. Because only one of these potential outcomes can be observed for any participant, the other is counterfactual (or, potential). BART estimates causal effects by flexibly modeling the response surface, that is the relationship between pre-exposure covariates, exposure assignment, and the outcome, and using this model to predict counterfactual outcomes.

Technical details of the BART for causal inference model are provided in the Appendix and described elsewhere. 20, 21, 39, 40 Estimands. In our study, BART was used to make predictions (along with associated uncertainty) for a participant's potential outcomes under three distinct scenarios: daily ENDS use, some-day ENDS use, or no ENDS use in the past 30 days, respectively. The participant's causal effect for the comparison of any two of these exposure regimens was defined as the expected difference between the two corresponding potential outcomes. The estimands corresponding to Q1 take the average of these differences over all participants, whereas those for Q2 focus on specified subgroups.

Identification assumptions.

A causal interpretation of the estimated exposure effect relies on several key assumptions necessary for the identification of these causal estimands. 20 First, we assumed no unmeasured confounders (also referred to as ignorability or selection on observables). Given the extensive set of covariates included in the analysis, we believe that this assumption is plausible, ensuring that potential outcomes are independent of exposure assignment when conditioned on covariates. Second, we assumed the positivity condition (also referred to as overlap or common support), which requires that all individuals have a nonzero probability of receiving each exposure level across all levels of the covariates, allowing for meaningful causal comparisons. Finally, we assumed the Stable Unit Treatment Value Assumption (consisting of consistency and no interference), meaning that a participant's outcome depends solely on one's own exposure status, not influenced by the exposure status of others. It also requires that everyone receives the same version of the exposure. The assumption is more plausible if we consider broad definitions of the exposure (for instance, inclusive of different versions of ENDS and ignoring distinctions in amounts of consumption within exposure categories).

Unlike inverse propensity score weighting or propensity score matching, BART adjusts for confounding through flexible outcome modeling rather than by restructuring datasets to try to create comparability across groups. Therefore, covariate balance diagnostics commonly used to evaluate the comparability of samples in those approaches are not required here. However, automated common-support diagnostics were implemented to assess positivity.

Estimation.

In this study, we fit BART models with a continuous outcome under the potential outcomes framework for each of two research questions. Each model predicts potential outcomes conditional on both observed covariates and an estimate of the propensity score.

Including a propensity score estimate in the model for the exposure effect has been shown to yield more reliable results as a way to control for confounding. 41 For each research question, BART generated a posterior distribution of individual-level potential outcomes at each exposure level conditional on the covariates of these participants. For each analysis, we ran 10 independent Markov Chains with 200 burn-in iterations and 500 sampling iterations per chain, fixing the number of trees in the ensemble to 200. Trace plots were examined to ensure convergence of chains. A point estimate of ATE was obtained as posterior mean across retained MCMC posterior draws over the whole sample (Q1) and each specific subgroup (Q2). The uncertainty of these estimates were obtained through analyses using the resampling weights (see the Weighted Analysis section).

Because the outcome is continuous, potential outcomes were expressed in (smoking) days, with negative ATE estimate indicating reduced smoking days.

Diagnostics.

To assess the adequacy of BART models with respect to the challenging outcome distribution, we examined the distribution of the residuals. Residual distributions that were approximately symmetric and exhibited an absolute skewness value below 3 were considered indicative of an adequate model fit.

Sensitivity analysis.

To assess the robustness of ATE estimates to potential unmeasured confounding, we assumed the existence of a hypothetical unobserved confounder and characterized its associations with the exposure and outcome using two sensitivity parameters. 42, 43 ATEs were subsequently re-estimated across a range of plausible combinations of treatment-confounder and outcome-confounder associations. Results were summarized using contour plots, which display the estimated exposure effect under varying assumptions regarding the strength of unmeasured confounding.

Weighted analysis.

Sampling weights (all-waves weights: R06_A_S04WGT) were incorporated into primary analyses for the estimates of causal effects. To quantify uncertainty, we repeated the estimation procedure 100 times using the PATH replicate weights and constructed 95% credible intervals (CI) from the empirical distribution of the resulting estimates, following guidance from the PATH Technical Working Group. Sampling weights accounted for the multiple-stage sampling design, adjusted for attrition, and supported valid population inference. 44 Weighted sensitivity tests are not feasible and will require advances in methodology and software packages.

Missing values.

For categorical covariates, missing values were treated as a separate ("Don't know") category. Participants with missing values on any other study variables were excluded using complete-case analysis (3.8%), as the proportion of missing data was small and assumed to be ignorable.

Software. BART analysis for causal inference was conducted using the R bartCause package. Sensitivity analyses were conducted using the R treatSens package. 42, 43

Results

Descriptive statistics for baseline characteristics are presented for the overall study sample and stratified by ENDS exposure status in Table S2. Participants were mainly between 18 and 64 years old (95.6%), with approximately half female (54.2%) and about three-fifth as non-Hispanic White (62.8%). All participants were established current cigarette smokers at both the baseline and exposure waves.

At the exposure wave, 5.5% (n = 271) of participants reported daily ENDS use, 12.3% (n = 606) reported some-day ENDS use, and 82.1% (n = 4032) reported no (current) ENDS use. At the outcome wave, participants who had reported daily ENDS use exhibited the highest prevalence of zero smoking days (35.4%, n = 96) and the lowest prevalence of daily cigarette smoking (43.5%, n = 118), followed by some-day ENDS users (16.0%, n = 97; 67.3%, n = 408, respectively) and non-users (12.7%, n = 512; 75.6%, n = 3050, respectively). Consistent with these patterns, daily ENDS users had the lowest mean and median number of smoking days (mean = 14.87, SD = 14.09; median = 10), followed by some-day ENDS users (mean = 22.07, SD = 12.27; median = 30), whereas non-users had the highest values (mean = 24.02, SD = 11.24; median = 30). Figure 1 presents a histogram of the outcome variable.

Q1: ATE.

As shown in Figure 2 and Table S3, compared with no ENDS use, daily ENDS use reduced cigarette smoking by 6.17 days per month over a two-year period (ATE = -6.17, 95% CI: -8.16 to -5.29); whereas some-day use reduced smoking by 0.99 days (ATE = -0.99, 95% CI: -1.65 to -0.28). Compared with some-day use, daily ENDS use reduced smoking by 5.65 days per month (ATE = -5.65, 95% CI: -6.72 to -4.72). Across all analyses, only 0 to 2 participants were excluded to satisfy the common support requirement. In addition, residuals were approximately normally distributed, with absolute skewness values below the prespecified threshold (Table S4), indicating that the BART models provided an adequate fit to the data.

Q2: Group-Specific ATE.

We further examined subgroups and found no strong evidence that the group-specific ATE of ENDS use varied meaningfully by sex, race/ethnicity, annual household income, externalization, internalization, sexual orientation, or age group. As shown in Figure 3 and Table S3, subgroup-specific estimates exhibited substantial overlap in their 95% credible intervals, suggesting generally consistent ENDS exposure effects across subgroups. The only potential exception was observed in older age groups with extremely small sample size, where the estimates were less precise and less robust.

Sensitivity analysis.

Sensitivity analyses suggested that the estimated ATEs were relatively insensitive to moderate levels of unmeasured confounding. Across a wide range of plausible exposure-confounder and outcome-confounder associations, the ATEs remained negative and of similar magnitude. Furthermore, the observed confounders clustered in regions where the estimates changed minimally, suggesting that an unmeasured confounder would need to be substantially stronger than most measured confounders to meaningfully alter the study conclusions (Figure S2).

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this is among the first studies to apply a causal machine learning approach, BART for causal inference, to estimate the causal effect of ENDS use on subsequent smoking days while accounting for high-dimensional confounding. Using longitudinal data from the nationally representative PATH Study, we found that ENDS use reduced smoking days over a two-year follow-up among established adult smokers. Compared with non-users, daily ENDS use reduced smoking by approximately 6 days per month, whereas some-day ENDS use reduced smoking by about 1 day per month. These findings are consistent with previous studies reporting that daily ENDS use was associated with cigarette cessation, 17 while extending the literature by quantifying reductions in smoking days. Moreover, these findings were consistent among younger age, race/ethnicity, sex, household income, education, mental health status, or sexual orientation subgroups.

Unlike traditional regression approaches, which primarily estimate associations between exposures and outcomes, our study was conducted within the potential outcomes framework, 38, 45 in which causal effects are defined as contrasts between potential outcomes under alternative exposure conditions. For example, we estimated the expected difference in smoking days had smokers used ENDS daily rather than on some days. This framework provides a rigorous and transparent way of defining, estimating, and interpreting causal effects while explicitly distinguishing causal questions from associational ones. 39 Identification of these causal effects relied on several assumptions, including no unmeasured pre-exposure confounding, positivity, and no interference between participants. To strengthen the plausibility of these assumptions, we adjusted for a comprehensive set of baseline covariates. Sensitivity analyses further indicated that an unmeasured confounder would need to be substantially stronger than the measured confounders to meaningfully explain away the effect of daily ENDS use. Finally, given the PATH Study design and the geographic dispersion of participants, interference between participants was considered unlikely.

BART provides a flexible means for estimating causal effects from observational data.

Unlike traditional regression models, BART accommodates complex nonlinear relationships and high-order interactions without requiring investigators to prespecify interaction terms, transformations, or functional forms, thereby reducing the risk of model misspecification and limiting the researcher degrees of freedom. In the bartCause package, causal effect estimation is further strengthened by incorporating estimated propensity scores. Unlike many machine learning algorithms designed primarily for prediction, 46 BART is implemented within a Bayesian framework, allowing uncertainty in causal effect estimates to be quantified through posterior distributions and credible intervals. BART also combines an ensemble of many small regression trees with regularizing priors, which reduces overfitting while preserving the flexibility needed to model complex exposure-outcome relationships. Together, these features enable robust estimation of both population-average and subgroup-specific causal effects while minimizing reliance on restrictive modeling assumptions.

While the bimodal distribution of smoking days presents potential presents modelling challenges, the BART models appear to provide an adequate fit to the data. This approach allowed us to analyze smoking days as a continuous outcome, rather than characterizing smoking behavior into two (any vs. no smoking) or ordinal (daily vs. some-day vs. no smoking) categories.

Our findings add to the relatively limited literature examining smoking days as an outcome and have important implications for the potential harm reduction role of ENDS.

Although complete abstinence from all tobacco and nicotine products remains the ideal public health goal, 2 reducing the use of combustible cigarettes may represent an intermediate step toward lowering tobacco-related harm. In this study, daily ENDS use reduced smoking days by approximately 6 days per month over a two-year period, suggesting that some smokers may substitute ENDS for combustible cigarettes. Nevertheless, these findings should be interpreted with caution. Fewer smoking days do not necessarily translate into proportional reductions in health risks, particularly among individuals who continue smoking cigarettes or engage in dual use of cigarettes and ENDS. 47 The long-term health consequences of sustained dual use remain uncertain and may include continued exposure to cigarette smoke, 47 persistent nicotine dependence, delayed tobacco abstinence, and cumulative adverse health effects. 48 Accordingly, our findings should not be interpreted as evidence that ENDS are harmless or that switching to ENDS eliminates health risks associated with tobacco use. Importantly, individuals who do not currently use tobacco products, especially youth and tobacco-naïve adults, should not initiate ENDS or any other tobacco product.

This study has several limitations. First, causal interpretation depends on the assumption of no unmeasured confounders. Although we adjusted for a comprehensive list of pre-exposure covariates selected based on the literature and subject-matter knowledge, it is possible that some critical factors were omitted. Second, ENDS exposure was assessed at a single time point and categorized by frequency of use; we were unable to evaluate the effects of nicotine dose, product characteristics, or cumulative ENDS use over time. Third, the study conclusions should only be generalized with caution. At the time of exposure assessment (12/2018 -11/2019), the US ENDS market was dominated by pod-based ENDS products -particularly JUUL--which marked a significant shift in vaping technology and use patterns. 49 These devices differ substantially from the earlier devices (e.g., cigalike and box mods with slower and lower nicotine delivery) and newer products (2020s -present), which integrate smart technology such as Bluetooth connectivity, touchscreen interfaces, and app compatibility for personalized settings. 50 The effect of ENDS use on smoking cessation likely varies by device design, nicotine delivery, and therefore, user behavioral patterns. Further direct comparative research is warranted. Our findings should not be generalized beyond our target population, study period, and geographic setting.

Conclusion

Among established adult smokers, daily ENDS use reduced subsequent smoking days compared with some-day or no use. These findings support the potential role of daily ENDS use in tobacco harm reduction while underscoring the need to evaluate its long-term health effects and identify the smokers most likely to benefit.
