How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). Statcheck might still miss one-sided tests that are described in another sentence, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Abstract

Background: The parent-reported Sasakawa Sports Foundation Preschoolers' Activity Questionnaire (SSF-PAQ) is from a national survey of Japanese preschoolers' out-of-preschool exercise, active play, screen time, and sleep. We assessed the validity, reliability, measurement error, paper-electronic agreement, and practicality of the Original version and explored a 24-hour version (SSF-PAQ-24).

Methods: Children aged 3-6 years from 10 conveniently sampled preschools wore an accelerometer for seven days while parents kept a time-use diary and completed the SSF-PAQ on Days 7 and 10. Criteria were accelerometer-measured physical activity (PA), diary-recorded screen time, and diary-and accelerometerderived night sleep.

Results: The concurrent-validity sample comprised 167 children (Original, 127; SSF-PAQ-24, 40). Originalversion correlations were weak for organized PA and active play (ρ = 0.24) and moderate-to-vigorous PA (ρ = 0.29) but strong for screen time (ρ = 0.83) and night sleep (ρ = 0.78), which averaged 8.2 and 10.7 min/day above the criteria. Test-retest intraclass correlations were 0.78-0.96 for these measures and 0.27-0.89 for setting-specific items. Screen time was higher on paper than electronically. SSF-PAQ-24 activity correlations were not significant (ρ = 0.17 and 0.06). Completion took 6.5-9.7 minutes.

The Original SSF-PAQ's main measures showed good-to-excellent test-retest reliability, and screen time and night sleep correlated strongly with the criteria. Activity-time validity was limited but comparable to that of existing parent-report questionnaires. SSF-PAQ-24 activity items need re-examination in a larger sample.

INTRODUCTION

Physical activity (PA), screen time, and sleep in early childhood are critical to health and development, as outlined in the World Health Organization's 24-hour movement guidelines for children under 5 years. [1] [2] [3] [4] A pooled analysis across 33 countries found that only 14.3% of children aged 3-4 years met all three recommendations, 5 highlighting the need for continuous monitoring. 6 However, daily durations and guideline adherence do not indicate where PA could be increased. To design effective family and community interventions, the settings in which children exercise and play must be identified. Such assessments require scalable, accurate measurement tools. 7 While accelerometers objectively measure body movement, their high cost 8 and complex data processing 9 limit their use in large-scale surveys.

Conversely, parent proxy-report questionnaires are easy to administer and can capture the types and settings of exercise and play. However, a systematic review of 37 proxy-report questionnaires for children aged 0-5 years concluded that none was sufficiently valid and/or reliable and that the quality of evidence was mostly low or very low; only six questionnaires covered all three behaviours, and measurement error had been evaluated for only one. 7 Since then, the Movement Behaviour Questionnaire-Child (MBQ-C), which assesses active play, screen time, and sleep, was developed in Australia and evaluated in Australia and China. [10] [11] [12] In Japan, the Sasakawa Sports Foundation (SSF) surveyed 3,144 parents of preschool children nationwide in 2023. 13 The survey's activity, screen-time, and sleep items are collectively referred to in this study as the SSF Preschoolers' Activity Questionnaire (SSF-PAQ). Unlike the Japanese version of the MBQ-C, 14 which only provides a 24-hour overview, the SSF-PAQ asks about out-of-preschool organized sports or exercise lessons and outdoor and indoor active play by setting and day of the week, screen time, and weekday and weekend sleep schedules. 13 However, the measurement properties of the SSF-PAQ have not been evaluated; interpreting the survey data and designing future surveys require clarifying its correspondence with accelerometers and diaries, setting-specific reliability, measurement error, and response burden. Because the survey was conducted online 13 and validation in the intended mode of administration is recommended, 8 paper-electronic agreement should also be examined. Testing a 24-hour version (SSF-PAQ-24), which extends the activity and screen-time items of the Original version to preschool hours, also helps identify challenges in whole-day assessment.

This study aimed to evaluate the concurrent validity, test-retest reliability, measurement error, agreement between paper and electronic administration modes, and practicality of the SSF-PAQ Original version in Japanese preschoolers. A secondary, exploratory aim was to evaluate the concurrent validity and test-retest reliability of the SSF-PAQ-24.

METHODS

Study design

This cross-sectional validation study shared a sample and data collection period with a separate validation study of the MBQ-C. 14 Data were collected in May-June and October-December 2025. Reporting followed the STROBE statement (eTable 1). 15 The Meijo University Human Research Ethics Committee approved the study (no. 2024-43), and each child's parents/guardians provided written informed consent.

Participants and group allocation

Children aged 3-6 years were recruited through 10 conveniently sampled preschools in four Japanese cities; no exclusion criteria were applied at recruitment. The sample size was set to detect ρ = 0.25 between the Original-version activity items (exercise and active play) and accelerometer-measured PA, based on earlier correlations of 0.20-0.39 between parent-reported play and accelerometry; 11, 16 this required 123 children with valid accelerometer data (α = 0.05, 80% power). Allowing for non-consent and missing data 17 (eMethods 1), study documents were distributed to the parents of 623 children, and written informed consent was obtained for 208 children (33%).

Consenting children were allocated to six groups in a fixed, non-random rotation through a roster ordered by preschool and grade, to balance groups across both (eTable 2, eMethods 1). Once sufficient data for the Original version had been collected, subsequent participants were allocated to the 24-hour version. Parents completed their allocated version on Days 7 and 10, either in the same mode (paper or electronic) both times to assess test-retest reliability or once in each mode to assess paper-electronic agreement (Original version).

Study protocol

Children wore the accelerometer on Days 1-7 (including during sleep), while parents maintained a 7-day time-use diary and completed the SSF-PAQ on Days 7 and 10 (eMethods 1).

The SSF-PAQ

In the Original version, 13 parents recalled a usual week over the past month, reporting daily out-of-preschool time across three domains: organized sports/exercise, outdoor active play (by location), and indoor active play (by setting). For each domain, they also noted time spent in activities intense enough to make the child breathe harder. Additionally, parents reported screen time (excluding body movement-based screen use), and usual weekday and weekend bedtime/wake-up time. From these responses, four daily-duration measures (min/day) were derived: organized physical activity and active play (hereafter 'organized PA and AP'; organized lessons plus outdoor and indoor active play), moderate-to-vigorous PA (MVPA; total 'breathing harder' time), total screen time, and night sleep (from bedtime to wake-up time).

The SSF-PAQ-24 adds at-preschool rows to the play items and removes the 'outside of preschool' restriction from play and screen-time items, tracking these behaviours across the entire day. Criterion measures

PA was measured using a right hip-worn triaxial accelerometer (ActiGraph wGT3X-BT; ActiGraph LLC, Pensacola, FL, USA). Raw acceleration signals were classified into 15-second epochs using a machinelearning classifier for preschoolers 18, 19 (Preschool Hip Random Forest free-living classifier 18 within the R package actimetric 20 ). This yielded accelerometer-derived total PA and MVPA after excluding non-wear time. 21 Mean Euclidean Norm Minus One (ENMO) 22 provided a classifier-independent summary. To match each questionnaire's timeframe, Original-version criteria captured activity outside of preschool hours, while the SSF-PAQ-24 criteria captured whole-day amounts.

Parents recorded screen time and sleep in a 24-hour time-use diary 11, 23 over all seven days. The screen-time criterion was defined as diary-recorded sedentary (sitting or lying) screen time; screen use while standing was excluded (eMethods 1). The night-sleep criterion integrated diary-reported bedtimes and wake-up times with bed rest estimated by an algorithm developed for hip-worn data in young children. 24 Nights were classified as weekday or weekend based on whether the following day was a day off. Detailed processing methods for the accelerometer, diary, and sleep data are available in eMethods 1 and the companion paper. 14

Practicality

Practicality was assessed from the first Day 7 response: completion time, perceived response burden, and perceived clarity (eMethods 1).

Data cleaning and processing

A valid accelerometer day required ≥ 600 minutes of wear 25 between 07:00 and 21:00 hours. 13 The primary analysis required at least three valid weekdays and one weekend day or holiday. 26, 27 Children with implausibly high MVPA relative to step counts were excluded (eMethods 1). Daily activity values were truncated at 480 minutes for organized PA and AP, and 360 minutes for MVPA, following the Australian MBQ-C validation study. 11 The seven categories for SSF-PAQ screen time were converted to category midpoints, and night-sleep duration was calculated from reported bedtime and wake-up time. Weekday and weekend/holiday means were weighted as: (weekday × 5 + weekend/holiday × 2)/7.

Statistical analysis

Concurrent validity (Day 7 SSF-PAQ versus criterion) was assessed using Spearman rank correlation coefficients (ρ) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) derived via Fisher z transformation. 28 Separate analyses were conducted for weekday, weekend/holiday, and weekly-average values. Following Schober et al., 29 coefficients were interpreted as weak (0.10-0.39), moderate (0.40-0.69), strong (0.70-0.89), or very strong (≥ 0.90). Activity items were also correlated with mean ENMO. Systematic differences in screen time and night sleep were evaluated using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test and Bland-Altman analysis.

Test-retest reliability (Day 7 versus Day 10; same version and mode) was assessed using intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs) based on a two-way mixed-effects, single-measurement, absolute-agreement model. 30 ICCs were interpreted following Koo and Li 31 as moderate (0.50-0.75), good (0.75-0.90), or excellent (> 0.90). Measurement error was quantified using the within-child coefficient of variation (CV), the standard error of measurement (SEM) from a mixed model with random effects for participant and administration, and the smallest detectable change (SDC) at both the individual (SDCind) 32 and group levels (SDCgroup). 33 Paper-electronic agreement (Original version) was assessed with Spearman ρ, ICCs, and Wilcoxon signed-rank tests, while practicality metrics were summarized descriptively.

Validity and reliability were estimated by mode, while reliability and measurement error were calculated for setting-specific activity items. For the SSF-PAQ-24, at-preschool active play and out-of-preschool organized PA and AP were validated against corresponding period accelerometer-derived total PA. The same properties were evaluated for bedtime, wake-up time, midsleep (their midpoint), and social jetlag (the signed weekend-weekday difference in midsleep 34 ), against accelerometer-estimated sleep timing. Because the national survey also reports days per week, 13 Original-version days per week with any organized PA or AP (activity days) and with outdoor active play (outdoor-play days) were validated against accelerometerderived out-of-preschool total PA (Spearman ρ) and assessed for test-retest reliability (ICC; eMethods 1).

Except for missing diary clock times in the night-sleep criterion (eMethods 1), no missing data were imputed; analyses included all participants with complete data for the required variables (n in each table).

Sensitivity analyses re-examined concurrent validity under stricter and more lenient 35 wear-time criteria, among children aged 3-5 years, and with criteria recalculated from days reported as usual, accurately recorded, or both; for night sleep, criterion weekend nights were also reclassified by calendar weekend (eMethods 1). All analyses were performed using R version 4.5.0. Claude (Anthropic), a generative artificial intelligence tool, assisted with writing and checking the analysis code, which was verified by the corresponding author.

RESULTS

Participants

Of the 208 children whose parents consented, 171 provided valid accelerometer data, of whom 167 completed the Day 7 SSF-PAQ and formed the concurrent-validity sample (Original: n = 127; 24-hour: n = 40; Figure 1). Their mean age was 4.95 years and 52% were girls; children in the 24-hour version were older, included more girls, and more often attended nursery schools than those in the Original version (Table 1).

Concurrent validity

Concurrent validity varied by behaviour (Table 2). For the Original version (weekly average), correlations with the criterion were weak for the activity items (organized PA and AP, ρ = 0.24; MVPA, ρ = 0.29) but strong for screen time (ρ = 0.83) and night sleep (ρ = 0.78). In the 24-hour version, the activity correlations were not significant (ρ = 0.17 and 0.06), whereas screen time (ρ = 0.68) and night sleep (ρ = 0.63) correlated . moderately. Against mean ENMO, results were comparable except that the Original-version MVPA coefficient rose from 0.29 to 0.44. In the Original version, SSF-PAQ values exceeded the criteria by a mean of 8.2 min/day for screen time and 10.7 min/day for night sleep (95% limits of agreement, -72.3 to 88.8 and -33.2 to 54.7 min/day), without significant proportional bias (eFigure 1). Night-sleep validity was lower on weekends than weekdays in both versions (0.50 versus 0.73; 0.41 versus 0.57; eTable 3, Part A). Weekday 24-hour reports, when divided into out-of-preschool and at-preschool time, yielded weak criterion correlations (0.19 and 0.24, respectively), with both 95% CIs including zero (eTable 3, Part B).

Test-retest reliability

Weekly-average behaviours showed good-to-excellent test-retest reliability across both versions (Table 3): ICCs were 0.78-0.96 (Original) and 0.82-0.91 (24-hour), peaking for night sleep in both. Reliability by mode and setting is shown in eTable 3 (Part C) and eTable 4, respectively. In the Original version, weeklyaverage ICCs were good for six of the nine settings (0.76-0.89) but fell below 0.75 for outdoor active play in parks (0.66), indoor active play at facilities (0.57), and other outdoor active play (0.27). For outdoor active play in parks and on home premises, weekend ICCs (0.55 and 0.34) were lower than weekday ICCs (0.78 and 0.89).

Measurement error

Measurement error differed by behaviour (Table 4). In both versions, the weekly-average within-child CV was highest for MVPA and lowest for night sleep, and SDCind was highest for screen time and lowest for night sleep. Setting-specific values are in eTable 5.

Agreement between paper and electronic modes

Rank agreement between the paper and electronic modes of the Original version was strong across all behaviours (weekly-average ρ = 0.75-0.87; Table 5). Absolute agreement was moderate for organized PA and AP, MVPA, and screen time (ICC = 0.55-0.70) and excellent for night sleep (ICC = 0.91). Screen time showed a systematic difference, yielding higher values on paper (P = 0.002); the mean difference was similar in both administration orders (Table 5).

Practicality

Mean completion time was 6.5-9.7 minutes across groups and was shorter in the electronic mode for both versions (by 0.6 and 3.2 minutes; Figure 2). While 45-53% of parents rated the questionnaire items as hardly or not very burdensome, 46-59% rated them as very or somewhat clear.

Sensitivity analyses

Behaviour-specific patterns were unchanged under other wear-time criteria (360/960 min/day; eTable 6), at ages 3-5 years (eTable 7), and with criteria from usual days, accurately recorded days, or both (eTable 8).

Sleep timing

For sleep timing (pooled versions; eTable 10), SSF-PAQ bedtime, wake-up time, and midsleep correlated strongly to very strongly with the criterion (ρ = 0.78-0.91), showing small systematic differences (median within-child differences ≤ 16 minutes), excellent reliability (ICC = 0.92-0.99), and small measurement error (SEM = 3-15 minutes). Social jetlag correlated weakly with the criterion (ρ = 0.36) but showed good reliability (ICC = 0.83).

Day-based indicators

Original-version activity days (seven per week in 60% of children) did not correlate significantly with accelerometer-derived out-of-preschool total PA (ρ = 0.12), whereas outdoor-play days correlated weakly (ρ = 0.32); test-retest ICCs were 0.65 and 0.90, respectively (eTable 11).

DISCUSSION

This study is the first to evaluate the measurement properties of the SSF-PAQ, a questionnaire used in a national survey of preschoolers, alongside an exploratory 24-hour extension (SSF-PAQ-24). Whereas measurement error had been evaluated for only one of 37 proxy-report questionnaires for young children, 7 this study quantified it by behaviour and setting.

Although the SSF-PAQ activity items were limited to out-of-preschool time, easily observable by parents, and asked for detailed daily and setting-specific information, their correspondence with accelerometermeasured activity time was weak. Correlations were similarly low for the Japanese MBQ-C in the same sample (ρ = 0.12-0.26), 14 the original MBQ-C (ρ = 0.20-0.39), 11 and the Preschool-age Children's Physical Activity Questionnaire, which asks about home activity by type (r = -0.07 to 0.17). 36 Beyond the conceptual gap between questionnaire-reported time (spent in exercise and play) and accelerometer-measured time 14 (spent in actual movement), parents may have difficulty in quantifying young children's intermittent activity into specific durations. 7 Original-version 'breathing harder' time correlated more strongly with mean ENMO (0.44), a summary measure of activity volume, than with accelerometer-derived MVPA time (0.29). This suggests that parents may rely on their overall impression of how active the child is.

In the 24-hour version, correlations were also low and imprecisely estimated (ρ = 0.17 and 0.06). These children, 70% of whom attended nursery schools, spent a mean of 8 hours per day at preschool (Table 1), and parents have difficulty monitoring activity during childcare hours. 11 However, among the same children, weekday correlations were weak both outside of preschool (ρ = 0.19) and at preschool (ρ = 0.24; eTable 3, Part B). Thus, limited knowledge of preschool hours is unlikely to be the only explanation, and limiting questions to easily observed periods and asking for greater details may not overcome parents' difficulty in estimating activity duration.

. Although setting-specific validity was not tested, the weekly averages of six Original-version items (including organized lessons and indoor active play) showed good reliability, indicating consistent parent reporting. However, weekend ICCs were lower for outdoor active play in parks (0.55) and at home (0.34), suggesting that weekend outdoor-play items warrant further evaluation. Furthermore, because only 16-23% of responses exceeded 0 minutes for screen-based and 'other' active play, ICCs alone cannot determine if these low-frequency behaviours are captured reliably (eTable 4).

To help interpret group changes against measurement error, this study reported the SEM and SDC in units of time. In longitudinal surveys, SDCgroup (SDCind/√N) can determine whether a mean change exceeds measurement error. 33 For example, in a cohort of 100 children, the threshold values are approximately 7.0, 3.5, and 8.2 min/day for organized PA and AP, MVPA, and screen time, respectively. SDCgroup does not account for systematic response shifts or participant turnover, which must be considered separately. In contrast, individual changes smaller than the SDCind (Table 4) cannot be distinguished from measurement error.

Although Original-version screen time was measured in categories, its diary correlation was as high as that of sedentary screen time in the Japanese MBQ-C (0.83 versus 0.81 for the open-ended and 0.78 for the closed-ended formats), 14 with good reliability (ICC = 0.78). However, questionnaire values were systematically higher than diary values (mean difference, 8.2 min/day), mirroring MBQ-C findings, 14 with wide limits of agreement. Thus, group surveys can capture relative differences in screen time, but individual values need to be interpreted with caution.

The Original-version night-sleep correlation (ρ = 0.78) exceeded that of the Japanese MBQ-C in the same recruited sample (0.56). 14 This discrepancy may relate to format differences: the MBQ-C asks directly about usual night-sleep duration, whereas the SSF-PAQ derives it from separate weekday and weekend bedtime/wake-up times. In Iwasaki et al.'s study, 37 responses on usual sleep over the past 4 weeks (without distinguishing weekday/weekend) correlated with actigraphy-measured sleep on weekdays, but not significantly on weekends. Coupled with the strong-to-very-strong correlations found here for bedtime, wake-up time, and midsleep, these results suggest that a format separating day-of-week differences in daily schedules and using specific clock times as cues is useful. The drop in the weekend night-sleep correlation (0.49 to 0.33) when reclassifying criterion weekend nights as calendar Saturday and Sunday nights suggests that parents answered keeping nights before a day off in mind. Hence, explicitly asking about 'nights before a day off' may resolve this ambiguity and improve validity. Nevertheless, the 95% limits of agreement with the criterion were -33.2 to 54.7 min/day, indicating limited precision for individual estimates.

Paper and electronic formats ranked children similarly, but absolute agreement was only moderate for activity and screen-time items, with paper yielding higher mean screen times in both administration orders.

Therefore, these two modes should not be mixed when comparing group means.

The Original SSF-PAQ suits large-scale surveys comparing groups on out-of-preschool screen time, night sleep, bedtime, and wake-up time, which correlated strongly with the criteria and showed good-to-excellent test-retest reliability. Its activity items showed good reliability and validity comparable to existing parent-report questionnaires (weak correlations with accelerometry); 11, 14, 36 they can describe parent-reported exercise and active play and their settings. Reported time, shorter than accelerometer-measured PA (median, 73 versus 238 min/day; Table 2), should not be interpreted as PA duration. Activity days, mostly seven per week, were not significantly correlated with accelerometer-measured total PA. Outdoor-play days, which can be asked without duration estimates, correlated weakly with it, as did reported time, and showed good reliability; they are a candidate simple indicator. Both the SSF-PAQ and the Japanese MBQ-C 14 cover exercise or play, screen time, and sleep and have been evaluated in Japanese preschoolers. Because the validity of the SSF-PAQ-24 activity items could not be confirmed here, the Japanese MBQ-C, which most parents rated as low in burden, is currently a candidate for whole-day assessment.

This study has several limitations. First, the screen-time criterion was parent-reported, and the sleep criterion was based on parent-reported diary data refined with accelerometer-derived bed-rest endpoints; therefore, shared parent-report bias between the questionnaire and criterion measures may have influenced the observed correlations. Additionally, the night-sleep criterion represented the sleep interval rather than actual sleep time and was not validated against an objective reference such as polysomnography. Second, the questionnaire's recall period (the past month) did not match the one-week measurement window. Third, the short 3-day retest interval may have inflated reliability and underestimated measurement error due to participant recall. Fourth, convenience sampling limits these findings' generalizability. Fifth, the SSF-PAQ-24 sample was small (n = 40) and differed from the Original-version sample in age, gender, and preschool type; thus, its estimates were imprecise and differences between versions cannot be attributed to question format alone. Future longitudinal studies should evaluate setting-specific validity and responsiveness to behavioural changes to determine the questionnaire's utility for programme evaluation.

Conclusion

In Japanese preschoolers, the Original SSF-PAQ showed good-to-excellent test-retest reliability for its main measures. Concurrent validity was supported for out-of-preschool screen time, night sleep, bedtime, and wake-up time, which correlated strongly with the criteria; validity for activity time, assessed against accelerometer-measured PA, was limited but comparable to that of existing parent-report questionnaires.

Because the SSF-PAQ-24 activity-item correlations were imprecise in a small sample (n = 40), their validity should be re-examined in a larger sample. collection, analysis, or interpretation; manuscript writing; or the decision to submit the manuscript for publication.
