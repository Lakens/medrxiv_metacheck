Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Metacheck does not yet analyze automatically whether sentences which include non-significant p-values are correct, but we recommend manually checking the sentences below for possible misinterpreted non-significant p values.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Sex and APOE ε4 influence dementia risk, but whether its molecular predictors differ across sex and APOE ε4 strata remains unclear. We investigated plasma proteomic and metabolomic signatures predicting future dementia in 43,759 UK Biobank participants across four sex-by-APOE ε4 groups. The association between APOE ε4 carrier status and dementia differed by sex (interaction p = 0.027). Machine-learning models achieved higher discrimination in APOE ε4 carriers (AUC 0.85-0.90) than non-carriers (AUC 0.74-0.76), with the highest performance in female carriers. Most top molecular predictors were subgroup-specific, including C7, CDON and EDIL3 in female carriers and KLK4, MMP12 and TGFBI in male carriers. When UK Biobank models were applied to the Global Neurodegeneration Proteomics Consortium, 12/17 available top predictors in female carriers and 10/16 in male carriers remained highly ranked. These findings identify distinct molecular signatures of dementia risk across sex and APOE ε4 strata, supporting stratified biomarker discovery.

Main

Dementia is a leading cause of disability and mortality worldwide, affecting over 50 million people, with prevalence projected to exceed 150 million by 2050 (1).

Beyond age, sex and APOE ε4 genotype are two of the strongest risk factors for late-onset Alzheimer's disease (AD) (2). APOE ε4 carriers have increased dementia risk, with homozygotes exhibiting up to 15-fold higher AD risk than APOE ε3 homozygotes (3). An APOE-stratified genome-wide association study further identified genetic associations that differ according to APOE carrier status, suggesting distinct genetic mechanisms contributing to AD (4). Females are disproportionately affected by dementia (2, 5), with some studies reporting greater APOE ε4-associated risk in females than males (6). Emerging evidence further suggests that sex may modify disease progression, biomarker trajectories (7) (8) (9) (10), molecular profiles associated with AD (11), and potentially therapeutic response in AD (12). Finally, APOE ε4 carrier status, particularly homozygosity, is associated with increased risk of amyloid-related imaging abnormalities during anti-amyloid treatment (13).

Nevertheless, dementia risk prediction studies have largely focused on population-level models, with less attention to the molecular heterogeneity underlying risk across sex and APOE ε4 subgroups. Identifying subgroup-specific molecular signatures before clinical dementia onset may clarify heterogeneity in neurodegeneration, provide insights into the biological basis of variation in dementia risk, and inform future precision prevention strategies (14).

High-throughput plasma proteomics and metabolomics enable minimally invasive investigation of molecular changes associated with neurodegeneration. Machine learning can further model complex relationships within high-dimensional biological data and is increasingly applied to dementia research (15) (16) (17) (18). A pioneering large-scale study demonstrated the potential of plasma proteomics and machine learning for predicting incident dementia (19). However, whether the molecular features driving dementia prediction differ across sex and APOE ε4 strata remains insufficiently characterised. Furthermore, external validation, cross-platform transportability, and biological interpretation of machine learningderived molecular signatures remain limited.

Using UK Biobank (UKB) (20) as a prospective discovery cohort, we integrated plasma proteomics and metabolomics within sex and APOE ε4 strata to identify molecular signatures of future dementia risk. We evaluated the cross-cohort and cross-platform transportability of the identified proteomic signatures in the Global Neurodegeneration Proteomics Consortium (GNPC) (21), a clinically enriched cohort using a distinct proteomic platform. SHAP-based model interpretation, pathway enrichment, and association analyses were used to characterise the identified molecular signatures and explore their biological context.

Results

Cohort Characteristics (UK Biobank)

Participants with dementia were significantly older at baseline (median 66 years) compared with controls (median 58 years, p < 0.001). Years of education were lower among dementia cases (median 11 vs. 12 years, p < 0.001). The sex distribution differed modestly between groups, with males representing a greater proportion of dementia cases than controls (49.3% vs. 45.7%, p = 0.018). APOE ε4 genotype distribution differed substantially between groups, with APOE ε4 homozygotes accounting for 18.6% of incident dementia cases compared with 2.5% of controls (p < 0.001). Ethnic composition also differed between participants with and without dementia (p < 0.001). BMI category distributions did not differ between groups (p = 0.130; Supplementary Table 1).

When stratified by sex and APOE ε4 carrier status (Table 1), the largest subgroup was female non-carriers (n = 16,476 controls, 221 cases), followed by male non-carriers (n = 14,049 controls, 256 cases), female carriers (n = 6,643 controls, 364 cases), and male carriers (n = 5,437 controls, 313 cases). Among females, 5.2% of APOE ε4 carriers and 1.3% of non-carriers had dementia. Among males, 5.4% of carriers and 1.8% of non-carriers had dementia.

Baseline epidemiological associations (UK Biobank)

In multivariable logistic regression analyses (Figure 1) adjusted for age, years of education, BMI category, and ethnicity, the magnitude of the association between APOE ε4 carrier status and dementia differed significantly by sex (APOE ε4 carrier × sex interaction OR = 0.76, 95% CI 0.60-0.97, p = 0.027), with a stronger association observed in females than males. Among APOE ε4 non-carriers, males had higher dementia risk than females (male vs female OR = 1.29, 95% CI 1.07-1.55, p = 0.007), whereas no significant sex difference was observed among APOE ε4 carriers (male vs female OR = 0.98, 95% CI 0.83-1.15, p = 0.795). Multicollinearity diagnostics indicated low collinearity between predictors, with all non-intercept VIF values below 3 (Supplementary Table 9).

Feature Selection (Boruta)

Boruta feature selection identified between 22 and 71 stable features across the eight models, comprising four sex-and APOE ε4-stratified subgroups (female carriers, female non-carriers, male carriers, and male non-carriers), each evaluated with and without age as a predictor (≥40% stability threshold; Supplementary Tables 10 to 17). The highest numbers of stable features were identified in male and female APOE ε4 carriers (65-71, depending on age inclusion), whereas the fewest were observed in female and male non-carriers (22 in models including age). Five proteins reached the predefined stability threshold across all groups: EDA2R, ELN, GDF15, GFAP, and NEFL (Supplementary Table 18).

Machine learning model performance

Model performance varied across sex and APOE ε4 strata (Supplementary Table 19, Figure 2). Female carriers achieved the highest discrimination (AUC 0.88-0.90), with recall of 0.72-0.78 and specificity of 0.85-0.87. Male carriers also showed strong discrimination (AUC 0.85 . in both models), with specificity of 0.85-0.86 and recall of 0.69-0.71. Performance was lower in female (AUC 0.75-0.76) and male non-carriers (AUC 0.74-0.76).

Overall, differences in discrimination between models with and without age were small.

Feature Importance (SHAP)

To identify the features driving model predictions, we examined SHAP values for each subgroup-specific model. The relative ranking of predictors was highly consistent between models with and without age (Supplementary Table 27). Given the broadly preserved predictive performance and consistency in feature rankings between models with and without age, SHAP results from no-age models were used for primary molecular interpretation. This allowed us to examine the contributions of molecular predictors without chronological age entering the models directly, although age-related biological variation may still be reflected in the selected proteins.

Analysis of the top 20 SHAP-ranked features revealed both shared and subgroup-specific predictors, indicating substantial heterogeneity in predictive molecular signatures across sex and APOE ε4 strata (Extended Data Figure 4; Supplementary Tables 22 23 24 25 26).

Among the top 20 predictors, 11 were specific to female carriers and 10 to male carriers, compared with 14 in each non-carrier group (Figure 3, Supplementary Table 26). Despite this greater feature diversity among non-carriers, predictive performance was consistently higher in APOE ε4 carriers.

Overlap of top predictors across strata was limited (Figure 3). APOE ε4 carriers shared five proteins among their top-ranked features (BCAN, APOE, CA14, CEND1 and CDCP1), whereas non-carriers shared only LTBP2 and ELN. Additional overlaps included VGF in both male strata and SYT1 across female carriers, female non-carriers and male carriers. Across all four groups, GFAP, NEFL and GDF15 were the only proteins consistently represented among the top predictors.

Proteins dominated the top-ranked predictors, with two metabolomic measures (XXL_VLDL_C_pct and XXL_VLDL_TG_pct, representing the cholesterol and triglyceride content of extremely large VLDL particles) appearing among the top predictors only in male APOE ε4 non-carriers.

These findings suggest that the molecular features contributing to dementia prediction differ substantially across sex and APOE ε4 strata. Complete ranked SHAP feature importance lists for each subgroup are provided in Supplementary Tables 22 23 24 25 26.

SHAP-Based Gene Set Enrichment results

Contrast-based Reactome gene set enrichment analysis was used to investigate biological differences between female and male molecular signatures within APOE ε4 carrier and noncarrier strata (Figure 4).

Among APOE ε4 carriers, proteins contributing more strongly to prediction in females were enriched for GPCR ligand binding and downstream signalling, metabolism, cytokine signalling, gene transcription and extracellular matrix organisation. In contrast, proteins contributing more strongly to prediction in males were enriched for innate immune system processes, MAPK signalling cascades, receptor tyrosine kinase signalling, developmental biology, regulation of insulin-like growth factor transport and protein metabolism (Figure 4A).

A different pattern was observed among APOE ε4 non-carriers. Female-associated predictors were enriched for pathways related to signal transduction, immune system processes and protein metabolism. In contrast, male-associated predictors were enriched for extracellular matrix organisation and broader metabolic pathways (Figure 4B).

Although these findings did not survive correction for multiple testing and should therefore be considered exploratory, they suggest potentially distinct biological processes underlying the sex and APOE ε4-specific molecular signatures. Full enrichment results, including adjusted pvalues, are provided in Supplementary Tables 28 29 30 31.

External validation cohort, proteomic coverage and classifier performance

Because the UKB models for APOE ε4 carriers demonstrated the strongest predictive performance (AUC 0.85-0.90), we evaluated whether their proteomic signatures replicated in GNPC. Metabolomic data were unavailable in GNPC. However, all predictors retained through Boruta feature selection in the UKB APOE ε4 carrier models were proteins, allowing the original models to be evaluated using proteomic measurements alone.

Compared with UKB, GNPC participants were older and enriched for AD cases (Supplementary Table 32). Of the model predictors identified in UKB, 55/68 in female carriers and 57/71 in male carriers were available in GNPC (Supplementary Tables 33 and 34).

The external validation framework provided a stringent test of molecular transportability given differences in cohort composition (population-based versus clinically enriched), disease phenotype and diagnostic context (incident all-cause dementia in UKB versus prevalent clinically diagnosed Alzheimer's disease in GNPC), proteomic platform (Olink versus SomaScan), and cohort age structure.

Classifier performance was lower in GNPC than in UKB (Figure 5A, B). Prediction retained modest discrimination (AUC 0.63 for female carriers and 0.65 for male carriers), indicating partial transfer of the predictive signal. Full classifier results are provided in Supplementary Table 35.

Cross-cohort replication of molecular predictors

Among female carriers, 12 of the 17 UKB top 20 predictors available in GNPC (70%) replicated among the GNPC top 20: NEFL, BCAN, SYT1, APOE, CDON, CDCP1, LRIG3, GDF15, C7, CXCL9, EDIL3 and DCN (Figure 5C).

Of the 11 predictors unique to female carriers in UKB (Figure 3), six replicated in GNPC (C7, CDON, CXCL9, DCN, EDIL3 and LRIG3). As ODAM was unavailable in GNPC, this represented 6/10 (60%) available female-specific predictors.

Among male carriers, 10 of the 16 UKB top 20 predictors available in GNPC (63%) replicated among the GNPC top 20: KLK4, NEFL, BCAN, CDCP1, KHK, TGFBI, MMP12, GDF15, SCARF2 and C1QL2 (Figure 5D).

Of the 10 predictors unique to male carriers in UKB, six replicated in GNPC (C1QL2, KHK, KLK4, MMP12, SCARF2 and TGFBI). As ACTA2 was unavailable, this represented 6/9 (67%) available male-specific predictors.

Full SHAP feature importance rankings and the classification of shared and sex-specific molecular predictors in GNPC are provided in Supplementary Tables 36-38.

Cross-cohort SHAP concordance

SHAP feature importance across shared proteins, calculated using the original UKB-trained models applied to GNPC, was strongly correlated between UKB and GNPC in both female (n = 55; Spearman ρ = 0.72, p < 0.001) and male (n = 57; ρ = 0.70, p < 0.001) APOE ε4 carriers (Figure 5E, F).

Several overlapping top-ranked proteins clustered close to the perfect concordance line in both female and male APOE ε4 carriers, indicating preservation of relative feature importance across cohorts. Among female APOE ε4 carriers, CDON, LRIG3, GDF15, C7 and EDIL3 demonstrated highly preserved SHAP importance. Similarly, among male APOE ε4 carriers, NEFL, CDCP1, TGFBI, GDF15, MMP12 and C1QL2 clustered near the concordance line.

Associations of replicated proteins with dementia outcomes in APOE ε4 carriers (UKB)

Following cross-cohort evaluation in GNPC, the 12 proteins replicated among female carriers and 10 among male carriers were taken forward for association analyses with all-cause dementia, Alzheimer's disease (AD), and vascular dementia (VD). With the additional inclusion of GFAP in both groups and APOE and SYT1 in males, 13 proteins were evaluated in each sex. Analytic sample sizes varied by outcome, ranging from 6,700-7,007 female and 5,511-5,750 male APOE ε4 carriers, with outcome-specific case and control counts provided in Supplementary Table 39.

Female APOE ε4 carriers

Of the top 13 proteins tested, 11 were significantly associated with at least one dementia outcome after FDR correction (Figure 6A; Supplementary Table 40). Specifically, 11 proteins were significantly associated with all-cause dementia, 7 with AD, and 6 with vascular dementia. Four proteins (GFAP, NEFL, SYT1 and GDF15) were consistently associated across all three outcomes, indicating substantial overlap in biomarker profiles across dementia phenotypes.

GFAP and NEFL demonstrated the strongest positive associations across outcomes (Supplementary Table 40, Extended Data Figures 5 and 6). For example, higher GFAP levels were associated with increased odds of all-cause dementia (OR=1.74, 95% CI 1.53-1.98, q<0.001), of AD (OR=1.87, 95% CI 1.60-2.19, q<0.001), and of VD (OR=1.90, 95% CI 1.49-2.43, q<0.001). Higher SYT1 and GDF15 levels were also consistently associated with increased odds of all dementia-related outcomes.

In contrast, lower BCAN and CDON levels were associated with increased odds of AD and all-cause dementia. These inverse associations were consistent with SHAP patterns in UKB and GNPC, where lower levels contributed towards higher dementia risk predictions.

Patterns of statistical significance varied across dementia outcomes. BCAN, CDON, and LRIG3 showed significant associations with AD and all-cause dementia, but not vascular dementia, whereas CXCL9 and EDIL3 showed significant associations with all-cause dementia and vascular dementia. C7 and DCN reached statistical significance only for all-cause dementia.

Of the 13 proteins tested, 9 (C1QL2, GDF15, GFAP, KHK, KLK4, MMP12, NEFL, SYT1 and TGFBI) were significantly associated with at least one dementia-related outcome after FDR correction (Figure 6B; Supplementary Table 40).

Four proteins (GFAP, NEFL, SYT1 and KHK) were associated with all three dementia outcomes. As observed in female carriers, GFAP and NEFL had the strongest positive associations, with higher levels associated with increased odds of dementia-related outcomes (Extended Data Figures 7 and 8).

Patterns of statistical significance also varied across outcomes in male carriers. TGFBI showed significant associations with all-cause dementia and AD. C1QL2 and KLK4 reached statistical.

significance only for all-cause dementia, while GDF15 and MMP12 reached significance only for vascular dementia.

The directionality of the logistic regression associations was consistent with SHAP analyses from both the UK Biobank and GNPC machine-learning models. Higher circulating levels of GFAP, NEFL, GDF15, KLK4, SYT1 and MMP12 were associated with increased odds of dementia-related outcomes, whereas lower C1QL2, KHK and TGFBI levels were associated with increased dementia risk.

Discussion

In this study, we applied a stratified machine learning framework integrating plasma proteomics and metabolomics to characterise sex-APOE ε4-specific molecular signatures predictive of future dementia risk. To our knowledge, this is among the first studies combining sex-stratified APOE ε4 analyses with multimodal omics, interpretable machine learning, and external cross-platform validation to investigate molecular heterogeneity underlying dementia risk. Figure 7 integrates the principal molecular risk patterns identified across analytical approaches.

Three principal findings emerged. First, molecular signatures differed substantially across sex-APOE ε4 strata, revealing both shared and subgroup-specific molecular predictors. Second, APOE ε4 carriers demonstrated stronger molecular predictive signals than non-carriers, with female carriers showing the highest model discrimination. Third, several proteomic predictors and SHAP-derived feature importance rankings were preserved in GNPC despite differences in cohort composition, disease stage, outcome definition and proteomic platform. These findings support partial cross-cohort transportability of molecular predictor importance. One notable observation was the stronger predictive performance among APOE ε4 carriers compared with non-carriers, accompanied by a larger number of stable Boruta-selected features. This may reflect more consistent molecular associations with dementia risk among carriers, potentially driven by a greater contribution of Alzheimer's disease pathology to dementia outcomes in this group. This interpretation is biologically plausible given the involvement of APOE ε4 in amyloid accumulation (22, 23), neuroinflammation (24,25), lipid dysregulation (26), synaptic dysfunction (27), and blood-brain barrier impairment (28).

However, differences in dementia case numbers, case composition and statistical power may also contribute to the observed differences in model performance and feature selection.

Distinct molecular signatures emerged among female and male APOE ε4 carriers, reflecting differences in immune signalling, tissue remodelling, neuronal communication and metabolic processes. These patterns were supported by the integration of machine learning feature importance, exploratory pathway enrichment, cross-cohort preservation of selected predictors and conventional association analyses.

Several female APOE ε4 carrier-specific predictors demonstrated substantial preservation across cohorts, including C7, EDIL3, CDON, LRIG3, CXCL9 and DCN. C7, a component of the complement system, implicates inflammatory processes relevant to AD pathology (29, 30), while EDIL3 (31, 32) and DCN (33) point towards vascular regulation and extracellular matrix organisation. CXCL9 (34), a chemokine involved in immune cell recruitment and inflammatory signalling, further supports the immune-related component of this signature.

CDON (35) and LRIG3 (36), which participate in developmental and growth factor signalling, further point towards neurodevelopmental processes and cell-to-cell communication. Together, these findings highlight a female APOE ε4 carrier signature spanning immune and inflammatory signalling, tissue homeostasis and neurodevelopmental processes, extending beyond established markers of neurodegeneration.

In contrast, male APOE ε4 carriers showed enrichment for innate immune processes, MAPK signalling, receptor tyrosine kinase pathways, developmental biology, and growth factor signalling. Several male-specific predictors, including MMP12, TGFBI, SCARF2, KLK4, C1QL2 and KHK, showed preservation across cohorts. KLK4, which ranked among the strongest predictors in UKB and was the highest-ranked predictor in GNPC, has previously been implicated in plasma proteomic signatures of Alzheimer's disease, dementia and cognitive decline (37). Although KLK4 is highly expressed in prostate tissue, whether this contributes to its prominence in the male APOE ε4 carrier signature remains unclear. MMP12 and TGFBI further highlighted extracellular matrix remodelling and tissue repair, with MMP12 previously associated with future dementia risk (38, 39). C1QL2 (40) and KHK (41) pointed towards synaptic organisation and metabolic regulation, respectively. Overall, the male carrier signature emphasised tissue remodelling, cellular communication and metabolic processes.

Although immune-related pathways were represented in both profiles, their underlying components differed, with broader immune and inflammatory signalling featuring in female carriers and innate immune and MAPK-related signalling in male carriers. Consistent with the importance of sex-specific molecular investigations, a recent study of steroid hormones in Alzheimer's disease identified distinct sex-stratified associations, including elevated cerebrospinal fluid cortisol in females with AD and elevated plasma aldosterone in males with AD (42).

Several proteins captured biological processes shared across female and male APOE ε4 carriers in UKB, with varying degrees of preservation across cohorts. BCAN and CDCP1 were identified among the top predictors in both female and male APOE ε4 carriers in UKB and remained highly ranked when the UKB-trained models were applied to GNPC, supporting the preservation of these shared molecular signals across cohorts. In conventional association analyses, BCAN was the only predictor shared between female and male APOE ε4 carriers that showed significant associations with dementia outcomes (all-cause dementia and AD), with these associations observed exclusively among female carriers. BCAN encodes brevican, a brain-specific extracellular matrix proteoglycan involved in synaptic stabilisation, neuronal plasticity and maintenance of perineuronal nets, and disruption of extracellular matrix organisation has been increasingly implicated in Alzheimer's disease and cognitive decline (43). CDCP1 has been linked to systemic inflammation, immune activation, and blood-brain barrier dysfunction (44), processes increasingly recognised as important contributors to dementia pathogenesis. Although its role in Alzheimer's disease remains incompletely understood, the consistent identification of CDCP1 across APOE ε4 carrier groups suggests that inflammatory mechanisms may contribute to APOE ε4-associated molecular vulnerability. APOE was also identified among the top predictors in both carrier groups in UKB, although its cross-cohort replication was retained only in female carriers. APOE plays a central role in lipid transport and cholesterol homeostasis and represents one of the strongest genetic risk factors for Alzheimer's disease (26). These findings suggest that APOE ε4-associated dementia risk may involve altered lipid metabolism alongside disruptions in extracellular matrix organisation and cellular communication.

Despite substantial heterogeneity across strata, GFAP, NEFL and GDF15 were consistently identified among the most important predictors across all four groups in UKB. This finding is consistent with a previous large-scale UK Biobank proteomic analysis (19), which identified these proteins among the strongest predictors of incident dementia. GFAP and NEFL are wellestablished biomarkers of astroglial activation and neuronal injury respectively and have repeatedly demonstrated strong associations with incident dementia, Alzheimer's disease, and cognitive decline (45, 46). GDF15 has emerged as a marker of biological ageing, cellular stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, and systemic inflammation, processes increasingly recognised as contributors to neurodegeneration (19). The consistent identification of these proteins across strata suggests that neurodegeneration, glial activation, and age-related biological stress represent common pathways underlying dementia risk irrespective of sex and genetic background.

Although NEFL and GDF15 remained common across both APOE ε4 carrier sex strata in GNPC, GFAP showed less consistent preservation. This may partly reflect differences between the UKB and GNPC cohorts, particularly the contrast between incident dementia prediction in initially dementia-free individuals and prevalent Alzheimer's disease classification in a clinically enriched cohort. Differences in statistical power may also contribute to this finding. Plasma GFAP reflects reactive astrogliosis and has been shown to change early in the AD continuum, with elevations observed in cognitively unimpaired individuals with amyloid pathology and years before clinical symptom onset (45) (46) (47). Its relationship with AD pathology may also vary across disease stages. In contrast, NEFL reflects neuronal injury, while GDF15 captures broader processes related to ageing and cellular stress, which may remain informative across more heterogeneous disease contexts. NEFL and GFAP were significantly associated with all dementia-related outcomes across all sex and genotype strata. GDF15 was significantly associated with all three outcomes in female carriers and non-carriers, with VD in male carriers, and with all-cause dementia and AD in male non-carriers (Supplementary Tables 40,42).

In UKB, SYT1 was among the top predictors in both female and male APOE ε4 carriers, as well as female non-carriers, and conventional association analyses demonstrated significant associations with dementia outcomes in most strata. SYT1 encodes synaptotagmin-1, a presynaptic vesicle protein involved in calcium-dependent neurotransmitter release and increasingly recognised as a marker of synaptic dysfunction in Alzheimer's disease. Previous studies (48) have reported altered cerebrospinal fluid synaptotagmin-1 concentrations in both preclinical and symptomatic Alzheimer's disease, supporting its role as a marker of early synaptic pathology. Therefore, SYT1 may reflect a broader synaptic component of dementia risk rather than an APOE ε4-or sex-specific signal.

A major strength of this study is the cross-cohort and cross-platform preservation of molecular feature importance, with SHAP correlations of approximately 0.70 in both sexes despite differences in cohort composition, disease stage, outcome definition (incident all-cause dementia versus prevalent AD) and proteomic platform (Olink versus SomaScan).

Although the highest-ranking predictors across nearly all subgroups were proteins, direct comparison between modalities should be interpreted cautiously given differences in feature numbers and correlations among molecular predictors. The presence of lipid-related measures among male non-carriers may indicate differential contributions of metabolic pathways across biological risk strata. Future studies incorporating additional lipidomic and metabolomic platforms may clarify these relationships.

Classifiers excluding age retained broadly comparable discrimination to models including age, although small improvements in AUC were observed with age inclusion in some subgroups.

Given that age is the strongest established risk factor for dementia (2), the preservation of discrimination in models excluding age suggests that the identified omics signatures retain substantial predictive information without chronological age as an explicit predictor.

Current biomarker frameworks are largely applied uniformly across populations despite growing evidence that sex and APOE ε4 status modify Alzheimer's disease biology and progression. The identification of partially distinct molecular signatures years before incident dementia across sex-APOE ε4 strata suggests that early risk prediction, biomarker interpretation, and potentially future therapeutic targeting may benefit from more biologically stratified and personalised approaches. The particularly robust molecular signature observed in female APOE ε4 carriers, alongside distinct replicated biological profiles in male carriers, further illustrates how stratification may reveal molecular signals that could be obscured when populations are analysed collectively.

This study has several limitations. First, the analyses were observational. Therefore, the identified molecular signatures should be interpreted as predictors and associations rather than evidence of mechanistic causality. Second, pathway enrichment analyses were exploratory, with no pathways surviving false discovery rate correction; therefore, pathway-level interpretations remain hypothesis-generating. Nevertheless, the consistency of broader biological themes across SHAP interpretation, enrichment analyses and epidemiological association testing supports the plausibility of the identified sex-specific molecular patterns. Finally, KNN imputation was performed before cross-validation, potentially introducing information leakage and optimistic bias in performance estimates. However, a targeted sensitivity analysis in a subset of female APOE ε4 carriers using revised preprocessing showed comparable discrimination and highly correlated predictions, suggesting limited sensitivity to these changes in the evaluated subset.

This study demonstrates that dementia risk is characterised by partially distinct molecular signatures across sex-APOE ε4 strata, with APOE ε4 carriers exhibiting stronger and more reproducible molecular patterns than non-carriers. The preservation of molecular featureimportance patterns when UKB-trained models were applied to an independent cohort, despite differences in disease stage and proteomic platform, supports their potential biological relevance and transportability. These findings underscore the importance of stratified approaches for future biomarker discovery, risk prediction and precision medicine strategies in neurodegenerative disease.

Methods

Population (UK Biobank)

The main cohort of the study was the UK Biobank (UKB). This is a large-scale prospective study including over 500,000 participants in the UK, aged 40-69 years at baseline. For this study, we were interested in investigating participants with both proteomic and metabolomic data. UKB Proteomic data was generated using the Olink® Explore 3072 assay (49), and metabolomic data was generated using the Nightingale Health® NMR platform. To identify the overlap between proteomic and metabolomic data, the two datasets were linked at the individual level using the unique UK Biobank participant identifier (eid). Before quality control, 53,029 participants had proteomics and 488,082 had metabolomics. Only participants with both proteomic and metabolomic measurements at baseline were retained. After merging the two datasets, the population of interest was 52,505 participants. Prevalent dementia cases were removed (n = 55).

Molecules with 25% or more missing data were excluded (n = 4): CST1, GLIPR1, NPM1, and PCOLCE. In addition, participants with 25% or more missing values across the retained study variables were excluded. After excluding prevalent dementia cases and applying the missingness criteria, 44,236 participants remained, comprising 1,167 incident dementia cases and 43,069 controls. Those lost to follow-up were removed as well as participants with no APOE genotype information, resulting in a final sample of 42,605 controls and 1,154 dementia cases with a median follow-up of 15 years (Extended Data Figure 1).

For stratification analyses, the dataset was split by sex and APOE ε4 status, resulting in 23,704 females (APOE ε4 carriers n = 7,007) and 20,055 males (APOE ε4 carriers n = 5,750). Supplementary Table 2 includes the details of the sample numbers.

Outcomes

The outcome of interest was all-cause dementia, which included all subtypes of dementia recorded in the UKB. Dementia cases were identified from linked electronic health records (hospital inpatient records, primary care records, and death certificate registers). Diagnosis dates were defined as the earliest qualifying dementia diagnosis from these data sources. The median follow-up duration was 14.6 years (IQR: 13.8-15.3 years).

Predictors

The key predictors consisted of 3,244 omics (2,917 proteomics and 327 metabolomics). In addition, age, number of APOE ε4 alleles, ethnicity, years of education and BMI (body mass index) were included based on previous machine learning studies in dementia research (15, 16, 18, 50, 51). For the UKB Olink data, normalised protein expression (NPX) values provided by UKB were used directly, as these values are pre-normalised and log2-transformed by the assay pipeline. The complete list of proteins used can be found in Supplementary Table 3.

Metabolomic profiling was performed using high-throughput nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy by Nightingale Health Ltd. The platform quantified a broad range of circulating metabolites spanning multiple biological pathways, including lipids and lipoproteins, amino acids, glycolysis-related metabolites, ketone bodies, and other lowmolecular-weight compounds (52).

To minimise technical variation, metabolite measurements were processed using the standard technical correction procedure implemented in the ukbnmr R package. This approach applies robust linear regression-based correction to the 107 directly measured metabolites to account for known technical confounders, after which derived biomarkers and metabolite ratios are recalculated from the adjusted values. Additional metabolite ratios proposed within the ukbnmr framework and considered to have potential biological relevance were also included in the present analyses.

A total of 327 metabolite variables were included in the study, comprising 107 directly measured metabolites, 61 composite biomarkers, 81 predefined metabolite ratios, 76 additional metabolite ratios derived using the ukbnmr framework, and two additional Nightingale biomarkers (spectrometer-corrected alanine and glucose-lactate). The complete list of metabolite measurements is provided in Supplementary Table 4.

To facilitate reproducibility, UK Biobank field identifiers for variables included in the analyses are provided in Supplementary Table 1a.

Pre-processing pipeline

Given the high dimensionality of the dataset and computational constraints, imputation was performed once on the full dataset prior to model development. Missing values were imputed using k-nearest neighbours (k=5), implemented with the KNNImputer class from scikit-learn (53). The choice of k was guided by prior studies applying similar methods to high-dimensional biomedical data (18, 54). All classifiers were trained and evaluated on the imputed dataset.

Continuous variables were standardised to a mean of zero and a standard deviation of one within the nested cross-validation, using the StandardScaler class from scikit-learn. Standardisation was retained to harmonise feature scales for downstream analyses.

Stratification

To investigate subgroup-specific molecular risk signatures, the UKB population was stratified by sex and APOE ε4 carrier status. Sex was defined using UK Biobank Field 31 (female or male), which was obtained from the NHS central registry at recruitment and, in some cases, subsequently updated by participants. APOE ε4 carriers were defined as individuals with at least one APOE ε4 allele (ε2/ε4, ε3/ε4 or ε4/ε4). This resulted in four strata: female APOE ε4 carriers, female non-carriers, male APOE ε4 carriers and male non-carriers. The machine learning pipeline was fitted independently for each stratum.

Statistical analysis

Cohort descriptive statistics

Baseline characteristics were summarised for the overall cohort and stratified by dementia diagnosis (cases vs. controls). Continuous variables were summarised as median and interquartile range (IQR), while categorical variables (sex, ethnic group, BMI category, and number of APOE ε4 alleles) were summarised as counts and percentages. Differences between participants with and without dementia were assessed using the Mann-Whitney U test for continuous variables and Pearson's chi-squared test for categorical variables. Two-sided pvalues were reported for descriptive purposes. Statistical analyses and table generation were performed using Python (SciPy, pandas, and python-docx libraries).

Inferential statistics for APOE ε4 carrier status, sex, and dementia diagnosis

A multivariable logistic regression model was fitted with dementia diagnosis as the binary outcome (0 = no dementia,1 = dementia) using the statsmodels package (v0.14.4) in Python v3.12.9. The main predictors were APOE ε4 carrier status, sex (binary coded), and their interaction term (APOE ε4 carrier × sex). Sex was coded as female = 0 and male = 1, while APOE ε4 carrier status was coded as non-carrier = 0 and carrier = 1, with female noncarriers serving as the reference group. Models were adjusted for age at baseline, years of education, BMI category, and ethnicity (categorised as White, Black and Other/Unknown, with the latter comprising South Asian, Other, Mixed, Chinese and Unknown/NA). This analysis was intended to assess the interaction between sex and APOE ε4 carrier status on the odds of incident dementia during follow-up, rather than to estimate time to dementia onset.

Variance inflation factors (VIFs) were calculated to assess multicollinearity among predictors. Odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were obtained by exponentiating model coefficients. Additional contrast-based ORs were computed using linear hypothesis testing (model.t_test) to estimate subgroup-specific effects (e.g., APOE ε4 carriers vs. non-carriers stratified by sex).

Machine learning pipeline

XGBoost is an ensemble tree-based machine learning framework built on gradient boosting principles, where sequential decision trees iteratively minimise prediction error by correcting residual errors from preceding trees (55). The method is particularly well suited to highdimensional biomedical data because it can efficiently model nonlinear relationships and complex interactions between variables while remaining robust to complex feature structures (55). In addition, XGBoost is compatible with SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) based interpretation, enabling biologically interpretable feature importance analyses.

Given these properties and its strong performance in prior dementia studies (56-59), XGBoost was selected as the classifier in this study.

Independent XGBoost pipelines were developed separately for each subgroup: Female APOE ε4 carriers, Female non-carriers, Male APOE ε4 carriers, and Male non-carriers. For each subgroup, two independent modelling pipelines were developed using the same preprocessing, feature selection, hyperparameter optimisation and evaluation procedures, differing only in whether age was included as a candidate predictor. This resulted in eight subgroup-specific pipelines. This comparison was performed to assess the extent to which molecular signatures retained predictive discrimination without chronological age as an explicit model predictor.

The data were split into a training dataset and a testing dataset. The split, stratified by dementia incidence, was 70/30 i.e., 70% of the data was used for training and 30% to test the classifiers. The methodology applied to each pipeline is described in detail below.

Feature selection

To reduce dimensionality from over 3,000 features, we applied the Boruta algorithm (BorutaPy implementation) (60) with a Random Forest classifier as the base estimator, separately for each sex-APOE ε4 subgroup. Categorical variables were converted to categorical type and one-hot encoded; boolean variables were converted to binary format (0/1).

Boruta was applied to the training data only to avoid data leakage. Training was performed within a repeated stratified 5-fold cross-validation framework (with 3 repeats), using n_estimators='auto' to allow BorutaPy to determine the number of trees automatically, perc=100 to retain only features with importance greater than the most important shadow feature (minimising false positives), and a maximum of 50 iterations to balance stability with computational efficiency. Balanced class weights were used to address class imbalance.

A feature was deemed stable if selected in ≥40% of all cross-validation runs. Stable features for each subgroup were retained for subsequent machine learning model development, with the selected feature set held fixed throughout nested cross-validation.

Classifier development

For each sex-APOE ε4 subgroup, we trained gradient-boosted tree models using XGBoost within a repeated nested cross-validation framework (outer 5-fold CV; inner 3-fold CV for hyperparameter tuning; repeated 5 times). Models were fit on the Boruta-selected feature set (stability ≥ 0.40), retrieved from the original dataset and preprocessed within the final modelling pipeline (continuous variables standardised using a StandardScaler fitted on the corresponding model-fitting data, categorical variables one-hot encoded, and boolean variables converted to binary). Class imbalance was addressed using scale_pos_weight, computed as the ratio of negative to positive cases in the training dataset. All models used GPU acceleration with histogram-based tree growth (tree_method='hist', device='cuda').

Hyperparameters were optimised by randomised search (10 iterations, ROC-AUC scoring) over predefined ranges (Supplementary Table 5). For each subgroup, the best hyperparameters selected by the inner 3-fold cross-validation within each outer training fold were aggregated across the 25 outer-fold iterations (5 folds × 5 repetitions) to obtain a single configuration for final model fitting, reducing dependence on any individual training partition. Numerical hyperparameters (e.g., learning rate, subsample) were aggregated by taking the mean, while categorical hyperparameters (e.g., booster type) were aggregated using the mode. During nested cross-validation, decision thresholds were selected separately using predictions from each outer validation fold to achieve specificity ≥ 0.85 while maximising recall. The mean threshold across outer folds was used to generate the aggregated cross-validation confusion matrix.

The final XGBoost model was retrained on the training set using the aggregated hyperparameters derived from nested cross-validation. For probability calibration, the training data were further split into sub-training (80%) and calibration (20%) subsets, stratified by dementia status; the XGBoost classifier was refitted on the sub-training subset and subsequently calibrated using sigmoid regression (CalibratedClassifierCV, method = "sigmoid", cv = "prefit"), fitted on the calibration subset. For the final calibrated model, the decision threshold was selected on the calibration subset using the same criterion (specificity ≥ 0.85 while maximising recall) and subsequently applied to the held-out test set. Model performance was evaluated on the independent held-out test set using calibrated probabilities. Performance metrics included discrimination (ROC-AUC), calibration (Brier score), accuracy, specificity, recall, precision, and F1 score. Threshold-dependent metrics and confusion matrices were calculated using the previously selected calibration-derived threshold.

Sensitivity analysis

To assess potential data leakage arising from KNN imputation performed before the trainingtest split, a targeted sensitivity analysis was conducted in a subset of 2,000 female APOE ε4 carriers. The analysis used 68 previously selected features from models excluding age as a predictor and a fixed XGBoost configuration. Both preprocessing approaches were evaluated using the same stratified 70/30 training-test split. The original approach, in which KNN imputation was fitted to the full dataset before splitting, was compared with a revised approach in which imputation was fitted exclusively on the training data and subsequently applied to the test data. Model discrimination was evaluated using AUC, and agreement between predicted probabilities was assessed using Pearson's correlation coefficient.

Bootstrap evaluation

Two complementary bootstrap procedures were applied. First, for each subgroup's final model, uncertainty in performance metrics (ROC-AUC, recall, precision, F1 score, specificity, and accuracy) was quantified by stratified bootstrap resampling of the held-out test set with replacement (1,000 replicates). Dementia cases and controls were resampled separately, retaining the original sample size of each group in every replicate. Point estimates were paired with 95% percentile confidence intervals derived from the bootstrap distributions.

Second, to directly compare discrimination between the with-age and no-age pipelines, paired nonparametric bootstrap resampling was performed on the same held-out test set (1,000 stratified replicates). Within each replicate, cases and controls were resampled with replacement, and the same bootstrap indices were applied to both models before recomputing ROC-AUC. The difference in AUC (ΔAUC; AUCwith-age -AUCno-age) was recorded across replicates. We report the observed ΔAUC and its 95% percentile bootstrap confidence interval, calculated from the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of the bootstrap distribution.

Bootstrap resampling was selected because it does not rely on parametric distributional assumptions and enables robust estimation of uncertainty for performance metrics, as well as paired comparison of models evaluated on the same individuals.

Shap Feature interpretation

Feature importance and interpretation were computed using SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) (61), implemented with shap.Explainer applied to the trained XGBoost estimator. SHAP values were calculated on the transformed held-out test set, using the correspondingly transformed training set as the background dataset. Global feature importance was summarised as the mean absolute SHAP value for each feature, and features were ranked accordingly. SHAP summary and bar plots were generated to visualise the direction, magnitude, and relative importance of feature contributions. For reproducibility, we report the SHAP details used in Supplementary Table 6.

SHAP-Based Gene Set Enrichment Analysis

Pathway enrichment analyses were conducted as an exploratory approach to identify biological themes associated with sex-specific differences in UKB SHAP-derived protein importance rankings. Enrichment was performed using a pre-ranked Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) approach implemented in the clusterProfiler and ReactomePA packages in R (v4.4.0).

To investigate biological processes associated with sex differences in model-derived protein importance, separate female-versus-male contrasts were generated for APOE ε4 carriers and APOE ε4 non-carriers.

For each comparison, proteins were ranked using mean absolute SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) values obtained from the subgroup-specific UKB XGBoost models. Protein importance values from the female and male models were merged using the union of proteins retained in either model. Proteins absent from a subgroup-specific model were assigned a SHAP importance of zero, reflecting their absence from that fitted model. This approach was intended to compare the complete model-derived protein signatures, capturing differences in both feature selection and relative SHAP importance between sexes, rather than estimating intrinsic sex-specific biological effects. To account for differences in the distributions of SHAP importance between independently trained models, mean absolute SHAP values were standardised within each sex using z-score transformation. This allowed the subsequent contrast to capture differences in relative feature importance between the sex-specific predictive signatures, rather than differences in absolute SHAP magnitudes. A contrast score was then calculated for each protein as the difference between the standardised female and male SHAP values (female minus male), such that positive values indicated greater relative importance in females and negative values indicated greater relative importance in males.

Protein symbols were mapped to Entrez Gene identifiers using the org.Hs.eg.db annotation database. Where multiple proteins mapped to the same Entrez identifier, the protein with the largest absolute contrast score was retained. Proteins were then ranked according to their contrast scores to generate a pre-ranked gene list for enrichment analysis.

Gene Set Enrichment Analysis was performed using the gseGO() function against Gene Ontology Biological Process (GO:BP) terms and the gsePathway() function against the Reactome pathway database, using nPermSimple = 10,000. Multiple testing correction was performed using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure. Only gene sets containing between 5 and 500 genes were considered.

Enrichment results were summarised using normalised enrichment scores (NES), where positive NES values indicate pathways enriched among proteins with greater relative importance in females and negative NES values indicate pathways enriched among proteins with greater relative importance in males. Results were visualised using custom dot plots displaying pathway enrichment direction, gene set size, and false discovery rate-adjusted significance.

External replication of the proteomic signal (GNPC)

To evaluate cross-platform transportability of the UKB proteomic signal, external validation was performed using the Global Neurodegeneration Proteomics Consortium (GNPC) v1 harmonized dataset (21), accessed through the Alzheimer's Disease Data Initiative (ADDI) AD Workbench (62). GNPC is a harmonised multi-cohort proteomics resource enriched for neurodegenerative disorders, including AD, Parkinson's disease (PD), amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS), and frontotemporal dementia (FTD). External validation analyses were restricted to APOE ε4 carrier subgroups, as these models showed the strongest performance in UKB. In addition, the models chosen for validation were the models not including age as a predictor given the robust performance in UKB. Only GNPC baseline visits were included in the present analysis.

This external validation framework represents a stringent test of cross-cohort transportability due to substantial differences between datasets, including disease stage (incident dementia in UKB versus prevalent dementia in GNPC), outcome definition (all-cause dementia in UKB versus clinically diagnosed AD in GNPC), proteomic platform (Olink in UKB versus SomaScan in GNPC), and cohort age structure.

GNPC proteomic platform

Proteomic measurements in GNPC were generated using SomaScan SOMAmer technology (versions 3, 4, and 4.1), comprising approximately 7,000 aptamers per sample, whereas UKB proteomics were measured using the Olink platform. Preprocessing and harmonisation of the GNPC v1 dataset were performed as previously described by the consortium (21). In this study, we standardised GNPC protein abundance values using z-score normalisation, centring each protein to mean zero and scaling to unit variance. Cohort-specific standardisation was applied to harmonise feature scales while avoiding transfer of distributional parameters across proteomic platforms.

GNPC proteomic predictors and feature harmonisation

Neither carrier-specific model retained metabolomic predictors following feature selection; therefore, no metabolite imputation was required for external validation in GNPC. UKB Olink protein predictors were mapped to GNPC SomaScan proteins by protein symbol. Where multiple SomaScan aptamers corresponded to a single protein, aptamer values were collapsed using the median abundance across aptamers. For UKB model predictors not measured in GNPC, a constant value corresponding to the UKB training-set mean was initially assigned to all GNPC participants, thereby preserving the original model input structure. Following GNPC-specific z-score standardisation, these constant-valued predictors became undefined (NaN) due to zero variance and were handled by XGBoost's native missing-value mechanism. This pragmatic approach enabled application of the original UKB models without refitting. Feature ordering was matched exactly to the original UKB training matrices separately for female and male APOE ε4 carrier models before prediction.

GNPC outcome variable

The primary outcome in GNPC was AD diagnosis. Cases were defined as individuals with AD diagnosis (AD = 1). Controls were defined as individuals without AD diagnosis (AD = 0) and with a Clinical Dementia Rating (CDR) score <0.5. Individuals not meeting either definition were excluded from analysis.

GNPC classifier

Final UKB-trained XGBoost classifiers were exported in UBJSON format and applied directly to GNPC without retraining. The female APOE ε4 carrier model retained 1,000 boosting rounds and 68 input features, while the male APOE ε4 carrier model retained 1000 boosting rounds and 71 input features, confirming preservation of the original UKB-trained architectures. Predicted probabilities were generated after harmonising GNPC predictors to the corresponding UKB feature order. Classification thresholds previously optimised and calibrated in UKB were transferred unchanged to GNPC for external evaluation. Performance metrics included ROC-AUC, recall, specificity, precision, F1 score, and accuracy.

SHAP analysis and feature concordance

To assess biological concordance across cohorts, SHAP values were computed for the externally validated GNPC predictions using the UKB-trained XGBoost models. Global feature importance was summarised using mean absolute SHAP values. Concordance between UKB and GNPC feature importance rankings was assessed using Spearman correlation across shared proteins. Overlap among the top-ranked proteins between cohorts was additionally quantified, and concordance plots were generated to visualise preservation of feature importance structure across datasets.

APOE ε4 carrier replicated predictors associations with all-cause dementia, AD and vascular dementia (UKB only)

Exploratory, post-hoc association analyses were conducted to further characterise the relationships between proteins identified as replicated predictors across UKB and GNPC and multiple dementia-related outcomes within UKB APOE ε4 carrier populations, stratified by sex. As protein selection was partly informed by predictive importance within the same cohort, these analyses were intended to provide complementary epidemiological characterisation rather than independent confirmatory evidence.

Specifically, proteins consistently ranked among the top 20 predictors in both UKB and GNPC for each sex-specific APOE ε4 carrier group were selected for these association analyses. GFAP was additionally included in both sex-specific analyses because it was consistently identified across all UKB sex and APOE ε4 strata and has been widely implicated in early neurodegeneration (45, 46). APOE and SYT1 were also included in the male carrier analysis because they ranked among the top predictors in the corresponding UKB model and replicated in female carriers.

Analyses were performed separately in female and male APOE ε4 carriers using multivariable logistic regression implemented in Python (v3.12.9) with pandas (v2.2.3), numpy (v2.1.3), and statsmodels (v0.14). For each sex-specific analysis, only individuals carrying one or two APOE ε4 alleles were included. Three binary outcomes were evaluated independently: allcause dementia, Alzheimer's disease (AD), and vascular dementia (VD). These outcomes were not mutually exclusive, and participants with multiple dementia diagnoses could contribute as cases to more than one analysis. To ensure outcome specificity, a shared "healthy control" group was defined as individuals free from all three diagnoses simultaneously (Dementia = 0, AD = 0, and VD = 0). For each outcome, cases were compared against this common control population, to avoid contamination of controls with alternative dementia diagnoses.

Proteomic predictors were analysed individually, with a separate multivariable logistic regression model fitted for each protein-outcome combination. Protein abundance values were standardised to z-scores prior to analysis, while continuous covariates (age at baseline and years of education) were similarly z-score normalised. APOE ε4 allele count (1 or 2 copies) was modelled as a continuous gene-dose covariate, and categorical covariates (ethnic_group and BMI_levels) were encoded using categorical dummy variables. Models were adjusted for age at baseline, APOE ε4 allele count (included as a numeric gene-dose covariate), years of education, ethnicity, and BMI category.

Robust HC3 standard errors were used to reduce sensitivity to heteroscedasticity. Associations were summarised as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Multiple testing correction was performed separately within each sex-specific analysis and dementia outcome, across the proteins tested, using the Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) procedure. Statistical significance was defined as FDR q < 0.05. Results were visualised using heatmaps of log₂(OR), volcano plots, and forest plots of FDR-significant associations.

APOE ε4 non-carrier secondary association analyses (UKB only)

To further investigate the association of shared predictors across all strata with multiple dementia outcomes, sex-stratified multivariable logistic regression analyses were additionally conducted in the APOE ε4 non-carrier populations following the same analytical framework described in the previous Section. GFAP, GDF15 and NEFL were selected because they were consistently identified among the top predictors across all UKB sex and APOE ε4 strata. SYT1 protein was additionally included because it was identified among the top predictors in all UKB strata except male non-carriers. These analyses aimed to determine whether the shared predictors identified through the prediction models were also associated with multiple dementia outcomes in conventional association analyses for the non-carrier population.
