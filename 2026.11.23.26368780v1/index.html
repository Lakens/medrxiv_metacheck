Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

This module retrieves information from repositories.

Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Sepsis is a life-threatening medical emergency characterized by high mortality and variable treatment practices. Optimizing treatment from retrospective data is challenging due to sparse, delayed outcomes and complex physiological dynamics. We introduce the Generative Latent Protocol Model (GLPM), a probabilistic framework that structures clinical decisionmaking through a latent protocol variable anchored on the patient's admission state. This latent variable acts as an information bottleneck, decoupling the generation of treatment trajectories from the prediction of survival outcomes. Unlike methods that rely on value estimation or noisy return-to-go conditioning, our model is trained via Maximum Likelihood Estimation to capture the joint distribution of treatment trajectories and outcomes. At inference time, we treat treatment optimization as a probabilistic inverse problem: we sample latent protocols from a posterior distribution conditioned on a target survival probability. On the ICU-Sepsis benchmark derived from MIMIC-III, our best variant matches or exceeds eight offline baselines on all five datasets, with gains that are statistically significant against every baseline on the most heterogeneous dataset, and it offers interpretability through patient-specific counterfactual analysis.

1 Introduction

Sepsis, defined as "life-threatening organ dysfunction caused by a dysregulated host response to infection" (Singer et al. 2016), contributes to at least 1.7 million adult hospitalizations and 350,000 deaths annually in the US (Dantes et al. 2023). Despite its prevalence, treatment guidelines for high-stakes interventions-such as intravenous fluids and vasopressors-remain inconclusive, leading to highly variable practice patterns and suboptimal outcomes (Byrne and Van Haren 2017). This lack of conclusive guidance indicates a critical need for unified, data-driven tools (Komorowski et al. 2018).

The sequential nature of sepsis care naturally fits the Markov decision process (MDP) framework. As illustrated in Figure 1, the whole treatment process of a patient can be viewed as a trajectory, a state-action sequence where the outcome (survival) is observed only at the end. Although reinforcement learning (RL) has been widely applied to this domain (Raghu et al. 2017; Komorowski et al. 2018; Peng et al. 2018; Choi et al. 2024), standard online methods face significant challenges in retrospective settings, including estimation bias and the difficulty of reliable off-policy evaluation (Jeter et al. 2019).

Recent work has shifted toward offline, trajectory-centric learning, framing healthcare as sequence modeling (Liventsev and Fritz 2024). Algorithms such as Diffuser (Janner et al. 2022) and Decision Transformer (DT) (Chen et al. 2021) have shown promise by modeling trajectories directly. However, in the context of sepsis treatment, these methods struggle with two key limitations: (1) sparse, delayed rewards, making credit assignment difficult for return-conditioned models in binary-outcome settings; and (2) lack of temporal abstraction, as flat policies often fail to capture the hierarchical, high-level structure of clinical care.

In this work, we start from a generative modeling perspective. We hypothesize that effective clinical strategies lie on a low-dimensional manifold embedded within the highdimensional space of patient trajectories. We capture this structure using a latent variable z, which we view as the underlying clinical treatment protocol: a learned representation, rather than a named clinical guideline. Instead of learning a fixed policy mapping states to actions, we learn a joint distribution over latents, trajectories, and outcomes. Our model follows the Latent Plan Transformer (LPT) (Kong et al. 2024b) in linking trajectories and outcomes through a shared latent variable. Generative Actor Critic (Qin et al. 2025) conditions its latent prior on the initial state and uses separate latent components for the trajectory and return. We condition on the patient's admission state s 0 and use a single protocol to generate treatment actions and predict a binary survival outcome.

This probabilistic formulation enables a robust mechanism for personalized treatment planning that operates on two distinct timescales. First, we perform global learning to capture general medical knowledge. By training on the entire population via Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MLE), the model learns population-level regularities of treatment and outcome. Second, we enable test-time adaptation (or patientspecific planning) for individual cases. Rather than applying a fixed policy, we treat the latent protocol z as a tunable parameter. For a new patient, we perform an inference step to identify the specific protocol z from the patient's admission state to maximize the probability of survival. This allows us to "plan" via inference, effectively tailoring the strategy to the specific patient without requiring gradient updates to the model weights.

Contributions. Our main contributions are: 1.

Generative Latent Protocol Model: We introduce an s 0 -anchored hierarchical sequence model that captures treatment strategies via a latent bottleneck, trained by an Expectation-Maximization (EM) procedure on trajectory-outcome pairs.

2. Goal-conditioned posterior inference: We formulate treatment recommendation as a probabilistic inference problem, and demonstrate that sampling from the survival-conditioned posterior allows for precise control over treatment aggressiveness.

3. State-of-the-Art performance:

On the ICU-Sepsis benchmark derived from MIMIC-III, our method consistently outperforms offline baselines across datasets of varying quality.

4. Interpretability:

We demonstrate that the learned latent space captures clinically meaningful treatment phenotypes (e.g., "wet" versus "dry" fluid strategies) and supports counterfactual analysis to identify missed interventions in non-survivor trajectories.

2 Related Work

Decision making via sequence modeling. Conventional RL approaches optimize a policy π(a t |s t-1 ) to maximize expected return. However, sepsis treatment data is characterized by sparse, delayed rewards (outcome observed only at t = H) and complex physiological dynamics, making credit assignment difficult for standard value-based methods. Recent approaches have reframed decision-making as a sequence modeling problem. For instance, Decision Transformer (DT) employs an autoregressive Transformer to condition actions on desired returns and past trajectories (Chen et al. 2021), and the concurrent Trajectory Transformer plans by beam search (Janner, Li, and Levine 2021). Subsequent variants of DT include multi-task learning (Lee et al. 2022), online adaptation (Zheng, Zhang, and Grover 2022), and Q-function augmentation (Yamagata, Khalil, and Santos-Rodriguez 2023). Parallel to this, generative modeling has been applied to planning, most notably through diffusionbased trajectory generation (Ho, Jain, and Abbeel 2020; Janner et al. 2022); more recent planners make cross-trajectory stitching an explicit design objective (Luo et al. 2025).

While prior works like DT condition on return-to-go (RTG), we argue that RTG is a noisy signal in binaryoutcome settings. Moreover, return-conditioned models often struggle in stochastic environments, where a high return may come from luck rather than from the actions taken (Yang et al. 2023; Paster, McIlraith, and Ba 2022). Latent variable models instead condition policies on latent variables, often learned from sub-trajectories (Lynch et al. 2020; Ajay et al. 2021). Our work follows this trajectory-centric perspective but differs in two key ways: it introduces latent representations of treatment protocols and a principled MLE-based training strategy, using an EM-style training procedure to infer episode-consistent protocol latent variables that provide temporal abstraction.

Constraint-based and risk-sensitive RL.

Our approach of conditioning on target survival rates during inference draws from constrained and risk-sensitive RL. Constrained MDPs (Achiam et al. 2017) separate optimization objectives from safety constraints, while distributional RL (Bellemare, Dabney, and Munos 2017; Dabney et al. 2018) and riskconstrained methods (Tamar, Glassner, and Mannor 2015; Chow et al. 2018) enable policies tailored to specific percentiles of outcome distributions. These frameworks motivated our exploration of different target survival rates to balance safety and treatment efficacy. As was evident, our findings aligned with theoretical insights, demonstrating that achievable high-probability constraints yield better outcomes than theoretical perfection in stochastic clinical environments.

Latent variable sequence modeling. Latent Adaptive Planner updates a latent plan as new observations arrive during manipulation (Noh et al. 2025). In spatial planning, multi-horizon transition representations guide action selection toward a goal (Zhao et al. 2025; Kong et al. 2026a). In our setting, an episode-level latent represents the treatment protocol and is inferred before executing the policy. Related sequence models use latent initial states to generate continuoustime dynamics (Cheng et al. 2025) and latent thought vectors to guide language generation and revise reasoning traces (Kong et al. 2025 (Kong et al. , 2026b)). A critical component of these frameworks is the mechanism for posterior inference and exploration. While variational methods like VariBAD provide computationally efficient, amortized inference (Zintgraf et al. 2021), posterior sampling offers a principled alternative for balancing exploration and exploitation (Osband, Russo, and Van Roy 2013). When exact posterior sampling is infeasible, Langevin dynamics offer a practical approximation with provable guarantees for both online exploration (Ishfaq et al. 2024) and offline RL (Nguyen-Tang et al. 2024). Building on this foundation, we investigate Langevin sampling and variational inference to balance uncertainty quantification with computational constraints. A latent protocol z ∈ R d is obtained via neural transform z = U α (z 0 , s 0 ) with z 0 ∼ N (0, I d ). Conditioned on z, the trajectory generator p β (τ |z, s 0 ) and survival predictor p γ (y|z) model the trajectory τ and terminal outcome y, respectively.

3 Methods

3.1 Sepsis Treatment as Sequential Decision Making

We formulate sepsis treatment as a finite-horizon sequential decision-making problem. A patient trajectory is denoted by τ = (s 0 , a 1 , s 1 , a 2 , s 2 , . . . , a H , s H ), where s t ∈ S represents the physiological state, a t ∈ A denotes the clinical intervention, and H is the horizon. Each trajectory is associated with a binary terminal outcome y ∈ {0, 1}, indicating patient survival. Define the history h t = (s 0:t-1 , a 1:t-1 ), with the policy keeping only the most recent K steps. We learn from an offline dataset of trajectory-outcome pairs (τ i , y i ) and infer a latent protocol from a new initial state and a desired outcome at test time.

3.4 Fast-slow adaptation mechanism

This EM-style framework separates fast inference of local latents from slow learning of shared weights, as in latent thought models (Kong et al. 2025). A related meta-learning approach adapts a latent-space energy-based prior to each new task while holding the generator fixed (Kong, Pang, and Wu 2021). We distinguish between two sets of parameters: • Global "slow" parameters (θ): The neural network weights (α, β, γ) are shared across the entire population.

They capture population-level regularities of treatment and outcome, learned slowly over the entire dataset.

• Local "fast" parameters (z): The latent protocol z acts as a patient-specific parameter vector. During inference (the E-step), z is optimized specifically for the current episode (τ i , y i ).

This separation enables single-instance adaptation. By optimizing z via posterior sampling, the model effectively "finetunes" the policy's behavior to the nuances of a specific patient without modifying the global network θ. This allows for personalized treatment planning at test time while keeping the robustness of a population-level model.

The gradient estimator in Equation ( 2) relies on drawing samples from the posterior distribution defined by the energy function E(z 0 ) (Equation ( 4)). 4 Experiments

4.1 Setup

To evaluate our proposed method, we utilize the ICU-Sepsis environment (Choudhary, Gupta, and Thomas 2024), a standardized tabular MDP for sepsis management constructed from the records of approximately 17,000 sepsis patients in MIMIC-III (Johnson et al. 2016). This environment poses a challenging sequential decision-making task with 716 discrete states, a sparse, binary survival outcome, and 25 discrete treatment combinations, representing discretized dosages of IV fluids and vasopressors (Table 6, Section C).

Offline dataset construction.

Given the lack of standardized offline RL benchmarks for ICU-Sepsis, we constructed a suite of datasets following the D4RL methodology (Fu et al. 2020). We generated behavior policies of varying quality by training Soft Actor-Critic (SAC) agents online and capturing checkpoints at different performance thresholds. Random: 1M steps collected by a randomly initialized policy. Medium-Replay: the full replay buffer of a SAC agent trained until medium-level performance. Medium: 1M steps from a fixed, partially trained policy. Expert: 1M steps from a fully converged SAC policy (about 83% survival). Real: real-world clinical trajectories from MIMIC-III, mapped into the ICU-Sepsis MDP following Komorowski et al. (2018).

4.2 Main results

Table 1 details the comparative performance in the offline setting. No baseline surpasses our best variant on any dataset: BAOAB achieves the highest survival rate on Medium-Replay, Medium, and Real and ties for best on Expert, while SVI ties for the best result on the low-quality Random data. Among the baselines, LPT is the strongest on every regime except Real, where QDT leads, and DD degrades on Expert (80.6%), consistent with the cluster collapse of classifier-free guidance at narrow return distributions (Section E).

On Medium-Replay and Medium, BAOAB achieves 89.0% on both, ahead of the strongest baseline by 2.0 and 1.4 points respectively and well clear of the return-conditioned and generative-planning baselines (QDT 83.0%/86.0%; TT 81.8%/83.3%). It also exceeds the online behavior policies used to collect the data. The average margin over the baselines is largest on Medium-Replay, the most heterogeneous dataset; we attribute this to a trajectory-stitching effect discussed in Section 5. On Expert, BAOAB reaches 91.0%, matching the best baseline and clearing the 88.0% of BC and QDT (best diffusion planner: Diffuser, 86.0%). On Real, which arguably presents the hardest challenge due to noise and confounding, BAOAB achieves 83.2%, against the ≈ 80% reached by every baseline (best: QDT, 80.4%). Overall, the BAOAB integrator yields the highest-performing policies as dataset quality and heterogeneity increase, with SVI competitive on the low-quality Random regime.

Statistical significance.

Because several baseline standard deviations overlap, we quantify the BAOAB gains with a paired bootstrap (Table 2). On Medium-Replay, the trajectory-stitching regime, BAOAB's gain is significant against all baselines (p < 0.05). Across the full set, the breadth of significance tracks each baseline's strength: BAOAB significantly exceeds CQL on four of five datasets, BC and DD on three, TT and Diffuser on two, and DT, QDT, and LPT on Medium-Replay alone. Against QDT, the gains on Medium, Expert, and Real are consistent in direction (+2.8 to +3.0 points) but do not reach significance at five seeds. LPT is the hardest to separate from: BAOAB's only significant margin over it is the +2.0 points on Medium-Replay (p = 0.048), and the two tie on Expert. Because LPT already adopts a return-free latent abstraction in place of return-to-go, we attribute these residual differences to our s 0 -anchored prior and the better-mixing BAOAB chain, which surface in the heterogeneous Medium-Replay regime where trajectory stitching is most valuable.

Comparison with online RL. As a performance reference, agents trained online directly on the environment, a Stochastic Latent Actor-Critic (Lee et al. 2020) and PPO (Schulman et al. 2017), reach final survival of 81% and 86%. Our offline BAOAB model (mean 86.5% over the five datasets, Expert 91.0%) is competitive with these online agents despite training without any environment interaction. Inference is also cheap relative to the clinical timescale: a full BAOAB episode takes 1.18 s on one GPU against a 4-hour decision interval (Section I).

4.3 Prospective target conditioning

A central feature of our framework is prospective target conditioning: the ability to specify desired survival rates y ⋆ at inference time. This raises a practical question: which target yields the most effective strategy? We conducted an ablation study to evaluate the sensitivity of the model to different inference targets y ⋆ ∈ {0.85, 0.90, 0.95, 1.00} as shown in Table 3. Across datasets, y ⋆ = 0.95 consistently yields the best performance, while pushing the target to theoretical perfection (y ⋆ = 1.00) degrades it. This suggests that y ⋆ = 0.95 finds a safer, high-likelihood protocol that balances ambition with robustness, whereas y ⋆ = 1.00 leaves the search dominated by the prior.

Optimization landscape.

To explain why the extreme target underperforms, we visualize the gradient field ∇ z0 log p(z 0 |y ⋆ ) (Figure 3). For y ⋆ = 1.00, the field becomes nearly radial and is dominated by contraction toward a single attractor: the target-conditioned posterior is largely pulled back toward prior-preferred regions. For y ⋆ = 0.95, the field is no longer radial: it navigates z 0 toward highsurvival regions while staying anchored to the prior support. Equation ( 9) explains this difference. Its Gaussian term z 0 pulls the code toward the origin, and its outcome term scales with y ⋆ -f γ (z). Where the predictor saturates near one, the outcome term vanishes at y ⋆ = 1.00 and leaves only the Gaussian pull. At y ⋆ = 0.95, the outcome term changes sign wherever f γ exceeds the target, so it keeps shaping the search. This suggests that GLPM performs best when searching for the best realistic protocol rather than an idealized outlier.

4.4 E-step sensitivity and convergence

A natural concern is whether so few Langevin steps in the E-step yield a converged EM objective and a stable posterior. We vary the training-time Langevin steps N ∈ {1, 5, 10} on Medium-Replay. Over the ten EM iterations, the total negative log-likelihood falls steadily and flattens, and the posterior displacement ∆z stays flat, so each E-step moves the stored chain state by a nearly constant amount (Figure 7, Section F). Even one Langevin step per E-step thus gives a converging EM objective and a stable posterior. During testing, we keep the inference budget fixed at N = 25 (Table 4). Survival is highest at N = 5, while N = 1 under-runs the posterior and N = 10 brings no further gain. ∆z follows the same pattern: it grows from 12.6 to 15.8 between N = 1 and N = 5 steps and saturates at 16.0 with N = 10. Therefore N = 5 is a sufficient E-step depth.

We further test eight BAOAB chains started from independent prior draws and find that they need at most 13 steps of burn-in, within the 25-step inference budget, and agree with Gelman-Rubin ˆ︁ R = 1.03-1.05 (Gelman and Rubin 1992), below the standard 1.1 threshold.

4.5 Context-length sensitivity

We set the policy context length of GLPM to K = 10, which is the mean trajectory length in the Medium-Replay dataset.

A natural question is to ask how GLPM will perform if we change the context length. We vary K ∈ {5, 10, 20} and test BAOAB on Medium-Replay and keep every other hyperparameter at its default. Since DT also conditions on a window of the last K steps, we also include DT as a comparison. As the 90th percentile of the trajectory length is 21, K = 20 already covers whole trajectories in most cases. 1 As shown in Table 5, BAOAB scores higher than DT at every K, by 7.0 to 9.8 points. Its survival is highest at K = 10; K = 5 lowers it to 85.3%, and K = 20 gives 87.2% with a wider spread across seeds. DT rises slowly with K, from 78.3% to 80.2%, but is always lower than BAOAB. The advantage of BAOAB therefore does not depend on the context length, and K = 10 is the best of the three settings for BAOAB.

4.6 Qualitative analysis and interpretability

We examined the decision-making rationale of the generative model across three levels: latent space geometry, patientspecific counterfactuals, and population-level statistics.

Latent space geometry and controllability.

To verify whether the latent space captures coherent clinical strategies, we linearly interpolate between two learned protocols with opposite fluid use: a "dry" protocol z dry (low fluid doses) and a "wet" protocol z wet (high fluid doses), z λ = (1 -λ)z dry + λz wet for0 ≤ λ ≤ 1, and compare the recommended doses over 15 time steps (Figure 4). The wet protocol administers fluids at t = 3, one step before the dry protocol, and the two agree at every other step. For vasopressors, at six of the fifteen steps the wet protocol suggests lower doses than the dry protocol, and it never recommends higher ones. Intermediate values of λ switch between the two protocol doses step by step instead of taking values in between. Moving from z dry to z wet therefore trades vasopressor support for earlier fluids: a single direction in the latent space couples the timing of fluid resuscitation with the level of vasopressor support.

Preventing premature weaning. We compare GLPM's recommendations with a clinician's by replaying the record of Patient 27, a non-survivor from the test set with 33 four-hour steps. We infer z ⋆ with the planning procedure of Section 3.6 at y ⋆ = 0.95. GLPM diverges from the clinician at 7 of the 33 steps (21.2%; Figure 5), revealing two key differences: (1) early fluid intervention: GLPM starts fluids at t = 1, one step before the clinician's large bolus at t = 2, and gives no fluid at t = 2; and (2) de-escalation prevention: after the clinician lowers the vasopressor dose from 4 to 1 at t = 10 and from 4 to 2 at t = 20, GLPM lowers it less and keeps it above the clinician's dose for two steps each time, and when the clinician lowers it again from 2 to 1 at t = 21, GLPM keeps it at 3. Across the seven divergent steps, the net vasopressor change is +2 levels and the net fluid change is -2 levels. The model effectively corrects the reactive "rollercoaster" pattern of clinician care, favoring proactive stability.

Generalization across non-survivors.

Repeating the analysis for 50 further non-survivors with at least 20 steps (Section H), the divergence rate is 24.2% ± 9.8%: GLPM disagrees with the clinician at about one fourth of the steps. GLPM recommends higher vasopressor support, with a net change of +1.3 ± 4.5 levels, while the net fluid change is +0.1 ± 2.9 levels and is zero for most patients. De-escalation prevention occurs in 41 of the 50 patients, 1.8 ± 1.5 times per patient. The population behavior thus repeats the pattern of Patient 27: GLPM keeps vasopressor support higher and smoother than the clinician without giving more fluid.

5 Discussion

Mechanism and advantages. Our method's performance stems from two design choices: learning from full trajectoryoutcome pairs via MLE and utilizing a latent variable z for temporal abstraction. Unlike behavior cloning, which suffers from compounding errors over long horizons (Ross, Gordon, and Bagnell 2011), our generative approach improves stability by guiding action generation via the protocol. Unlike CQL, DT variants, and diffusion-based planners, which can struggle with sparse rewards or noisy return-to-go targets (Paster, McIlraith, and Ba 2022; Brandfonbrener et al. 2022), our method avoids value estimation biases by directly optimizing the s 0 -anchored joint distribution. LPT shares our return-free latent-abstraction view; our gains over it stem from the s 0 -anchored prior and the better-mixing BAOAB chain.

Why BAOAB wins on Medium-Replay.

Medium-Replay yields our largest and statistically strongest margin, and it is also the most heterogeneous dataset: the replay buffer of a partially trained SAC agent mixes low-survival exploratory trajectories with the later high-survival trajectories that followed, so a method that composes useful fragments across trajectories of differing quality is well positioned. Brandfonbrener et al. (2022) show that return-conditioned supervised learning (DT and its variants) cannot stitch trajectories in general, even with unlimited data, because conditioning ties each generated trajectory to the empirical return of individual trajectories rather than to their optimal composition. Luo et al. (2025) instead make stitching the central design objective of a diffusion planner. LPT demonstrated trajectory stitching using a latent plan inferred from a target return (Kong et al. 2024b). Our Medium-Replay results are consistent with this account, though the benchmark comparison does not isolate stitching as the source of the gain.

Clinician versus AI dosing.

Qualitatively, our learned policies tended to recommend higher vasopressor doses and lower IV fluid volumes than clinicians (Figure 6). While consistent with prior observations of the AI Clinician (Komorowski et al. 2018), these tendencies reflect the dynamics of the simulation benchmark and require careful validation before real-world interpretation.

Limitations and future directions. We acknowledge that ICU-Sepsis is a simplified tabular MDP with discrete states and a limited action space, preventing it from capturing the full physiological complexity of sepsis care or intermediate outcomes like organ recovery. Importance-sampling offpolicy estimates on held-out Real trajectories are uninformative for policies that depart from clinician behavior (Section J), so our evaluation rests on the simulator. Future work will extend latent protocol inference to real-world cohorts with irregular sampling and confounding, such as MIMIC-IV (Johnson et al. 2023; Huang, Yang, and Rahmani 2025), and integrate these protocols into clinical decision support workflows (Sendak et al. 2020).

6 Conclusion

In summary, we introduced a latent protocol inference framework that provides temporal abstraction and enables goalconditioned planning without reliance on step-wise rewards. Representing treatment strategies as latent vectors anchored on the admission state matches or exceeds strong offline baselines on every ICU-Sepsis dataset, offering a promising avenue for refining clinical decision-making through generative modeling.

Impact Statement

In the near term, the findings in this study should not be used to inform medical practice. Latent protocol inference is a methodological contribution that could inform future research on decision support for sepsis care, but any clinical application would require extensive prospective evaluation using real-time data and clinical trials.

Conservative Q-Learning (CQL). CQL mitigates overestimation in Q-function learning by penalizing Q-values for actions not well represented in the offline dataset (Kumar et al. 2020). Its conservative nature can lead to suboptimal policies when the dataset lacks high-quality actions (Uehara and Sun 2022).

Decision Transformer (DT).

DT reframes RL as sequence modeling, predicting actions with a causal Transformer conditioned on past states, actions, and return-to-go (Chen et al. 2021). Return conditioning is unreliable when outcomes are stochastic and the return signal is weakly informative (Paster, McIlraith, and Ba 2022; Brandfonbrener et al. 2022); with binary, delayed rewards, the return-to-go carries little information for most of the trajectory.

Q-Learning Decision Transformer (QDT).

QDT replaces DT's return-to-go with Q-value estimates obtained by multi-step temporal-difference learning to improve credit assignment (Yamagata, Khalil, and Santos-Rodriguez 2023).

Its reliance on Q-value estimation can introduce biases in sparse-reward settings.

Trajectory Transformer (TT).

TT tokenizes states, actions, rewards, and returns-to-go by per-dimension quantile discretization, models them with a GPT-style Transformer, and plans by beam search scored by predicted return (Janner, Li, and Levine 2021). It inherits the noisy-signal problem of return-guided planning, and beam search adds substantial inference cost.

Diffuser. Diffuser models entire state-action trajectories with a denoising diffusion model and steers sampling toward high return via value-function guidance (Janner et al. 2022), executing the first action of each sampled plan. Its iterative denoising makes planning computationally expensive.

Decision Diffuser (DD).

DD replaces value guidance with classifier-free guidance on a target return (Ajay et al. 2023).

On high-quality datasets with a narrow return distribution (e.g., Expert), classifier-free guidance is prone to cluster collapse: weak guidance leaves the sampler in a high-variance unconditional regime, while strong guidance amplifies the collapse, degrading performance (Table 1).

Latent Plan Transformer (LPT).

LPT couples a latent prior, a causal Transformer trajectory decoder, and an outcome predictor under the factorization p(z) p(τ |z) p(y|z), inferring the latent plan at test time by MCMC from a targetconditioned posterior (Kong et al. 2024b). It is the most closely related baseline: it shares our use of a latent abstraction in place of return conditioning and differs from our model in the factorization.

H Counterfactual Analysis Details

H.1 Patient Selection and Methodology

For counterfactual analysis, we selected non-survivor patients from the test set with trajectory lengths ≥ 20 time steps to ensure sufficient temporal resolution for detecting treatment patterns. Patient 27 was selected as a representative case study for the main paper (trajectory length: 33 steps).

The counterfactual protocol was inferred using N = 25 Langevin dynamics steps with step size h = 0.1. Given a patient's observed state sequence {s 0 , s 1 , . . . , s H }, we: 1. Infer a high-survival protocol z ⋆ ∼ p θ (z 0 |y ⋆ = 0.95) via Equation (8) 2. Generate counterfactual actions {a ⋆ 0 , a ⋆ 1 , . . . , a ⋆ H } autoregressively using p β (a t |s ≤t , a ⋆ <t , z ⋆ ) 3. Compare with the clinician's actual actions {a 0 , a 1 , . . . , a H }

We define divergence at time t as |a clinician t -a GLPM t | ≥ 1 in either the vasopressor or fluid dimension. We additionally detect two clinically meaningful patterns using dimensionspecific criteria: • De-escalation Prevention (vasopressors): The clinician reduces vasopressor dosage at time t (i.e., a vaso t < a vaso t-1 ), but GLPM maintains or increases support (i.e., a ⋆,vaso t ≥ a ⋆,vaso t-1 ).

• Early Intervention (dimension-specific initiation): -Early vasopressor initiation: a vaso t = 0 and a ⋆,vaso t > 0.

-Early fluid initiation: a fluid t = 0 and a ⋆,fluid t > 0.

Key Findings.

Across N = 50 non-survivors, GLPM makes selective edits rather than wholesale policy replacement (divergence rate: 24.2%±9.8%). On average, it recommends higher vasopressor support (net change: +1.3 ± 4.5 levels), while fluid recommendations remain near-neutral (net change: +0.1 ± 2.9 levels), indicating that increased hemodynamic support is not paired with uniformly more aggressive fluids. De-escalation prevention occurs 1.8 ± 1.5 times per patient on average, consistent with temporal smoothing of vasopressor support. These aggregate results align with the Patient 27 case study.

H.4 Limitations

We note several limitations of this counterfactual analysis: • Simulation Environment: All counterfactual trajectories are generated within the ICU-Sepsis simulation.

The recommended actions have not been validated in real clinical settings or with actual patient physiology. • Fixed State Sequences: We replay the patient's observed state sequence under the counterfactual protocol. In reality, different actions would lead to different subsequent states, so these results represent "what GLPM would have recommended" rather than "what would have happened." • Selection Bias: We analyze only non-survivors with sufficiently long trajectories. The patterns identified may not generalize to shorter stays or survivors.

• Causal Interpretation: While we use the term "counterfactual," these analyses do not establish causal efficacy. Prospective validation would be required to determine whether GLPM's recommendations actually improve outcomes.

I Inference Runtime

We benchmarked BAOAB inference (N = 25 Langevin steps) over 100 episodes on a single GPU (Table 11). Protocol inference is a one-time cost per episode; subsequent action generation is autoregressive. A full episode averages 1.18 s. Since ICU-Sepsis decisions are made in 4-hour blocks, this is about 0.008% of the decision interval. Langevin planning adds latency relative to single-forward-pass baselines such as BC or DT, but the difference is negligible at the clinical decision cadence.

J Off-Policy Evaluation on Real Trajectories

Survival on Real is measured by simulator rollouts of policies trained on MIMIC-III trajectories mapped into the ICU-Sepsis MDP (Choudhary, Gupta, and Thomas 2024), consistent with all baselines. To complement it, we computed selfnormalized weighted importance sampling (WIS) on heldout Real trajectories with bootstrap 95% confidence intervals (10,000 resamples; Table 12). WIS recovers the empirical clinician survival rate (0.755) exactly, since all importance ratios equal one when the evaluation and behavior policies coincide; this validates the implementation. For learned policies, cumulative importance ratios collapse over multi-step trajectories, producing intervals that span nearly [0, 1]. This is a well-documented limitation of importance sampling in sepsis treatment (Raghu et al. 2017; Komorowski et al. 2018; Jeter et al. 2019): learned policies recommend systematically different treatments from clinicians (Figure 6), so few trajectories carry non-negligible weight. Simulator-based evaluation remains the standard on this benchmark for this reason.
