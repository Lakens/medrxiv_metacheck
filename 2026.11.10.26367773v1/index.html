What we found

We found 3 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Mother-to-child transmission sustains the burden of hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection in sub-Saharan Africa, yet little is known about whether women giving birth in Uganda are protected.

We estimated the prevalence of protective hepatitis B surface antibody (anti-HBs) levels, described serological profiles and identified associated factors among women admitted for delivery at Kawempe National Referral Hospital, Kampala. In this cross-sectional study, 418 women were selected by systematic random sampling in 2024 and interviewed about sociodemographic characteristics, hepatitis B testing, vaccination and exposures. Anti-HBs was quantified by electrochemiluminescence immunoassay (protective: ≥10 mIU/mL); other HBV markers were screened with a rapid test. Prevalence ratios were estimated with modified Poisson regression. Overall, 264 women (63.2%) reported vaccination: 203 during the current pregnancy and 61 before it only. Protective levels were found in 322 women (77.0%; 95% confidence interval [CI] 72.8-80.8): 50.6% of never-vaccinated women, 82.0% of those vaccinated before the current pregnancy only and 95.6% of those vaccinated during it. After adjustment, vaccination before the current pregnancy only (adjusted prevalence ratio 1.54; 95% CI 1.24-1.89) and during it (1.86; 95% CI 1.57-2.20) were associated with protection; no sociodemographic or behavioural factor was. Eighty-two women (19.6%) had no serological evidence of immunity or infection; 65 had never been vaccinated. Of 300 women who reported a previous negative test, 50 (16.7%) had never been vaccinated, and 68 of 250 vaccinated women with known completion status (27.2%) had not completed three doses, mostly because they were not told to return or forgot. The rapid test detected hepatitis B surface antigen in 21 women (5.0%), but discordant marker patterns suggest some false positives. One in five women reached delivery susceptible to HBV, largely because a negative test was not followed by vaccination. Linking every negative antenatal test to vaccination could close much of this gap.

Introduction

Hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection remains a leading cause of cirrhosis and liver cancer worldwide. In 2022, an estimated 254 million people were living with chronic HBV infection and 1.1 million died of HBV-related causes [1]. Global prevalence of hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg) was estimated at 3.2%, and only 46% of newborns received a timely birth dose of hepatitis B vaccine [2]. In highly endemic settings, most chronic infections are acquired at birth or in early childhood. Without prophylaxis, up to 90% of infants born to mothers who are positive for hepatitis B e antigen (HBeAg) acquire the infection, and around 90% of infants infected perinatally develop chronic hepatitis B [3, 4]. The World Health Organization (WHO) therefore recommends a birth dose of hepatitis B vaccine within 24 hours of delivery, antenatal HBsAg testing and antiviral prophylaxis for eligible pregnant women [5, 6].

A recent meta-analysis estimated the pooled HBsAg prevalence among pregnant women in Africa at 6.8%, and at 4.9% in East Africa [7]. In Uganda, a national serosurvey reported an HBsAg prevalence of 10.3% [8], and the 2016-2017 Uganda Population-based HIV Impact Assessment reported 4.1% among adults aged 15-64 years, with wide regional variation [9].

Among pregnant women, HBsAg prevalence has ranged from 11.8% in northern Uganda [10] to 2.1% in Lwengo District [11] and 1.2% in Mbarara Municipality [12]. Ugandan infants have received hepatitis B vaccine as part of the pentavalent vaccine from six weeks of age [10], and a national birth dose was introduced only in September 2025 [13]. Since 2015, Uganda has offered free hepatitis B screening for adults, with vaccination for those who test negative and treatment for those who are infected [14]. However, uptake and completion of the three-dose series are constrained by vaccine stock-outs, limited follow-up and vaccine hesitancy, including among pregnant women in Kampala [15, 16].

A hepatitis B surface antibody (anti-HBs) concentration of at least 10 mIU/mL is the accepted correlate of protection [5, 17]. Antibody response to vaccination varies with age, sex, body mass index, smoking, comorbidity and the number of doses received [18], and antibody concentrations decline over time after vaccination [19]. Among vaccinated healthcare workers in northern Uganda, 83% had protective anti-HBs levels [20], but data for pregnant women are scarce; in Mbarara, only 14% of pregnant women had serological evidence of immunity [12]. Knowing how many women reach delivery unprotected, and where the screening-to-vaccination pathway fails, is needed to target antenatal interventions. This study aimed to determine the prevalence of protective anti-HBs levels and of serological profiles of immunity, infection and susceptibility, and the factors associated with protective anti-HBs levels, among women admitted for delivery at Kawempe National Referral Hospital (KNRH).

Materials and methods

Study design and setting

We conducted a hospital-based cross-sectional study. Participants were enrolled between May and June 2024, after administrative clearance had been granted by the hospital. The study is reported according to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guideline [21] (S1 Checklist). KNRH is a public, urban, specialised national referral and teaching hospital in Kampala, Uganda. Its maternity services include antenatal care, delivery, postnatal care, routine immunisation and child growth monitoring. About 2,100 women give birth at the hospital each month, of whom about 25% are referred from other facilities or self-referred.

Participants and sampling

Pregnant women admitted for delivery at KNRH on data collection days were eligible if they gave written informed consent, irrespective of their hepatitis B vaccination history. Women receiving treatment for HBV infection and women in active labour were excluded. All participants were aged 18 years or older. Participants were selected by systematic random sampling. The sampling interval was calculated by dividing the expected number of admissions for delivery during the data collection period (about 80 per day) by the required sample size, which gave an interval of 3.8, rounded to 4. Every fourth woman admitted on a data collection day was approached; a woman who declined was replaced by the next woman admitted.

Participants' files were marked to prevent re-enrolment.

Sample size

For the prevalence of protective anti-HBs levels, Kish's formula [22], n = Z²p(1 -p)/d², with an assumed non-protective prevalence of 22.9% as used in a previous Ugandan study [20], Z = 1.96 and a precision (d) of 5%, gave a minimum of 272 participants. For the analysis of associated factors, a sample size for comparing two proportions computed in G*Power (two-sided α 0.05, 80% power, expected proportions 0.192 and 0.052) gave 383 participants. The larger requirement was adopted, and the enrolment target was set at 420 to allow for incomplete questionnaires or unusable samples.

Variables

The primary outcome was a protective anti-HBs level, defined as an anti-HBs concentration of at least 10 mIU/mL (10 IU/L) [5]. Women who were HBsAg-negative were further classified by their combination of anti-HBs and hepatitis B core antibody (anti-HBc) results: vaccine-type immunity (anti-HBc-negative, anti-HBs ≥10 mIU/mL), immunity after past infection (anti-HBcpositive, anti-HBs ≥10 mIU/mL), past infection without protective anti-HBs (anti-HBc-positive, anti-HBs <10 mIU/mL) and susceptible (anti-HBc-negative, anti-HBs <10 mIU/mL). Secondary outcomes were the prevalence of HBsAg, HBeAg, hepatitis B e antibody (anti-HBe) and anti-HBc.

The following variables from the questionnaire (S1 File) were analysed: age (recorded in the categories 18-30, 31-40, 41-50 and 51 years or older), education, marital status, residence, previous caesarean delivery, attendance at all scheduled antenatal visits, alcohol use, smoking, self-reported chronic medical condition (including HIV, cancer, sickle cell disease or other chronic disease), previous blood transfusion, previous surgery, having cared for a person with hepatitis B, recreational drug use, multiple sexual partners, sharing of sharp objects, previous hepatitis B testing and its result, and self-reported past hepatitis B. Follow-up items asked only of subgroups (number of antenatal visits among women who had missed visits, reasons for not being vaccinated during the current pregnancy, injection drug use, safer sex practice and initiation of hepatitis B treatment) and two items on perceived availability and quality of antenatal hepatitis B services were not analysed for this paper. Hepatitis B vaccination status was derived from two questions and classified as never vaccinated, vaccinated before the current pregnancy only, or vaccinated during the current pregnancy. Among vaccinated women, completion of three doses, the schedule used and reasons for non-completion were recorded.

Vaccination history was self-reported and checked against hospital records where these were available.

Data collection

A structured questionnaire was pretested among 10 pregnant women at Kajjansi Health Centre IV, Wakiso District, and revised before use. It was administered in face-to-face interviews by the principal investigator and two research assistants (a laboratory technologist and a nurse), who were trained on the tool, quality control and sample handling. The principal investigator reviewed all questionnaires daily for completeness and consistency.

Laboratory procedures

Sample collection and rapid serological screening

From each participant, 4 mL of venous blood was drawn into an ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) tube and centrifuged at 3,000 rpm for 10 minutes, and the plasma was separated into cryovials. Plasma was screened on site with a rapid lateral-flow (immunochromatographic) fivemarker hepatitis B test cassette (Dewei Medical Equipment Co. Ltd, China) that qualitatively detects HBsAg, anti-HBs, HBeAg, anti-HBe and anti-HBc. Tests were read visually according to the manufacturer's instructions and were valid only if the control line appeared. The manufacturer reports a sensitivity of 98.7% and a specificity of 99.1% for HBsAg; performance data for the other markers were not available, and no confirmatory enzyme immunoassay, HBV DNA or alanine aminotransferase testing was performed.

Quantitative anti-HBs measurement

The remaining plasma was stored at -80 °C at the Department of Pharmacology and Therapeutics, Makerere University, and transported in cold boxes to the Nakasero Blood Bank laboratory, where anti-HBs was quantified with the Elecsys Anti-HBs assay on an Elecsys 2010 analyser (Roche Diagnostics GmbH, Mannheim, Germany). This is a double-antigen sandwich electrochemiluminescence immunoassay with a measuring range of 2-1,000 IU/L; results of 10 IU/L (equivalent to 10 mIU/mL) or more were classified as protective. The analyser was calibrated and quality control samples were run before each batch, and 50 µL of plasma per sample was analysed according to the manufacturer's instructions.

Statistical analysis Descriptive analysis

Data were double-entered in EpiData version 3.1 and analysed in Python version 3.13 with

NumPy version 2.5 and SciPy version 1.18. Categorical variables were summarised as frequencies and percentages with numerators and denominators, and prevalences were reported with Wilson 95% confidence intervals (CI). Anti-HBs concentrations were truncated at the limits of the assay measuring range and were not normally distributed, so they were summarised as median and interquartile range (IQR). Characteristics were compared between women with and without protective anti-HBs levels, and between HBsAg-positive and HBsAg-negative women, with Pearson's chi-square test, or Fisher's exact test when any expected cell count was below 5 (Monte Carlo estimate with 100,000 samples for tables larger than 2 × 2).

Regression analysis

Because the outcome was common, associations with protective anti-HBs levels were estimated as prevalence ratios (PR) with 95% CI using modified Poisson regression, that is, a generalised linear model with a Poisson distribution, log link and robust (sandwich) variance estimator [23].

In the main model (all women), vaccination status, age, smoking and chronic medical condition were included a priori as potential determinants of vaccine response [18], together with all other variables with p < 0.20 in bivariable analysis. Previous hepatitis B testing was not entered because, in the national programme, vaccination follows testing, so testing lies on the pathway to vaccination; serological markers were not entered because they partly define immune status.

Because schedule and completion apply only to vaccinated women, these variables were analysed in a separate model restricted to vaccinated women, adjusted for timing of vaccination, age group, smoking and chronic medical condition. As a sensitivity analysis, the main model was repeated among HBsAg-negative women. Analyses were complete-case, and the number of observations in each model is reported. Collinearity among covariates in the main model was assessed with variance inflation factors (VIF), all of which were below 1.7 (S2 File). No interaction or subgroup analyses other than those described were performed. Statistical significance was set at a two-sided p < 0.05. Full regression output is provided in S2 File, and the de-identified dataset with its codebook in S1 Data.

289 Discussion 290 In this study of women admitted for delivery at a national referral hospital in Uganda, 77.0% had 291 protective anti-HBs levels, but one in five (19.6%) had no serological evidence of either 292 immunity or infection and was therefore susceptible to HBV. Four in five of these susceptible 293 women had never been vaccinated. Vaccination was strongly associated with protection, and almost all women vaccinated during the current pregnancy were protected. No sociodemographic or behavioural characteristic was independently associated with protection.

Why women reached delivery unprotected

Our data show where the pathway from testing to protection breaks down. The largest gap was failure to vaccinate. One in six women who reported a negative hepatitis B test had never been vaccinated, and 57 unvaccinated women had never been tested at all, despite the availability of free adult screening and vaccination since 2015 [14]. Women who had been tested were far more likely to be protected than those who had not (86.0% vs 31.9%; Table 1), consistent with testing being the usual route to vaccination. Associations of protection with previous surgery, caesarean delivery, blood transfusion and full antenatal attendance in bivariable analysis disappeared after adjustment for vaccination. This suggests that contact with health services matters mainly because it creates opportunities for testing and vaccination. Offering the first vaccine dose at the antenatal visit at which a negative result is returned would address this gap directly.

The second gap was an incomplete vaccine series. A quarter of vaccinated women had not completed three doses, and the reasons they gave were mostly health-system failures rather than refusal: they were not told to return, they forgot, or the vaccine was out of stock. Similar barriers, together with vaccine hesitancy, have been described among pregnant women in Kampala, in other Ugandan populations [16, 24] and across sub-Saharan Africa [15]. Most women followed the 0, 1 and 6 month schedule, which cannot be completed before delivery if it is started after the first trimester. Schedules with shorter intervals between doses are acceptable for adults [5, 17].

Protection was more frequent among women vaccinated during the current pregnancy than among those vaccinated only before it. This is consistent with a recent dose producing higher antibody concentrations, which then decline over time [19, 25]. A concentration below 10 mIU/mL in a previously vaccinated woman does not necessarily mean loss of protection, because people who responded to vaccination usually retain immune memory [5, 19]. The 17 vaccinated women classified as susceptible may therefore overstate true susceptibility. Completion of three doses was not significantly associated with protection, possibly because of inaccurate recall of the number of doses. Hepatitis B vaccination during pregnancy is safe [26].

Protection among women who reported no vaccination

The proportion of women with protective levels (77.0%) exceeded the proportion who reported vaccination (63.2%) because half of the never-vaccinated women had protective levels. Almost all of these women had a vaccine-type profile (anti-HBc-negative), so natural infection explains little of this immunity, although rapid anti-HBc testing may miss some past infections. The most likely explanations are vaccination that women did not report or remember, including infant vaccination in the youngest women through the pentavalent vaccine [10], and misclassification of vaccination status. This finding indicates that self-reported vaccination history is an unreliable guide to protection in this population.

Hepatitis B infection markers

The rapid test detected HBsAg in 5.0% of women, which is close to the pooled estimate of 4.9% for pregnant women in East Africa [7].

It is higher than the 3.0% reported among women nationally [9] and the prevalence found among pregnant women in south-western Uganda [11, 12], but lower than the 11.8% reported in northern Uganda [10]. However, this estimate must be interpreted with caution. Most HBsAg-positive women also had protective anti-HBs, which is uncommon in true chronic infection. Most HBeAg-positive women were HBsAg-negative, and anti-HBe was often found without anti-HBc; both patterns are biologically implausible. Rapid HBsAg results also agreed poorly with women's reports of previous tests. Together, these findings suggest that the rapid multi-marker test produced false-positive results, particularly for HBeAg and anti-HBe. Because no confirmatory immunoassay or HBV DNA testing was performed, we cannot estimate the true prevalence of infection or the risk of transmission. These results support WHO's recommendation that reactive rapid tests be confirmed before women are classified as infected and assessed for antiviral prophylaxis [6]. The association between sharing sharp objects and HBsAg positivity is consistent with blood-borne transmission but is based on few cases.

Implications

This study did not evaluate interventions, so the following implications are based on the gaps we observed. The main avoidable gap was that a negative test was not consistently followed by vaccination. Linking every negative antenatal test to a first vaccine dose at the same visit, with reminders and a reliable vaccine supply, would directly address it. Now that the hepatitis B birth dose is part of Uganda's routine schedule [13], newborns can be protected regardless of their mothers' immunity; antenatal testing therefore remains essential mainly to identify infected mothers, using confirmed results, so that eligible mothers receive antiviral prophylaxis [6].

Strengths and limitations

Strengths of this study include systematic random sampling, quantitative measurement of anti-HBs with an automated immunoassay, cross-classification of anti-HBs with anti-HBc to distinguish vaccine-type from natural immunity, and reporting of all exposure and risk-factor variables collected. The study also has limitations. First, its cross-sectional design does not allow causal inference, and it was conducted at a single national referral hospital, so the findings may not be generalisable to women delivering at lower-level facilities. Second, vaccination history and exposures were self-reported. Vaccination cards were not systematically available, and the high prevalence of protection among never-vaccinated women indicates substantial misclassification. Third, HBsAg, HBeAg, anti-HBe and anti-HBc were detected with a rapid test whose performance for markers other than HBsAg has not been validated, without confirmatory immunoassay, HBV DNA or alanine aminotransferase testing, and the discordant patterns we observed indicate false-positive results. Fourth, HIV status was recorded only within a composite question on chronic illness, and body mass index, parity, diabetes and time since the last vaccine dose were not available, so residual confounding is possible. Fifth, age was recorded in broad categories, and 11 women reported being 51 years or older, which may reflect misreporting.

Sixth, a single anti-HBs measurement cannot distinguish susceptible women from vaccinated women with waning antibody but intact immune memory. Seventh, we did not record whether HBsAg-positive women were linked to care or whether their infants received prophylaxis, or whether susceptible women were later vaccinated. Finally, refusals were not recorded, so participants could not be compared with women who declined.

Conclusions

Most women delivering at KNRH had protective anti-HBs levels, but one in five reached delivery with no serological evidence of immunity or infection, and most of these women had never been vaccinated. The main avoidable gaps were that a negative test was not consistently followed by vaccination and that started vaccine series were not completed, largely for healthsystem reasons. Linking every negative antenatal hepatitis B test to vaccination, and confirming reactive rapid tests before women are classified as infected, could strengthen efforts to eliminate mother-to-child transmission of HBV in Uganda.
