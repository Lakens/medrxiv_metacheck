Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Whether spontaneous speech preserves how the brain encoded an experience is 2 unknown. We tested this question in opioid use disorder (OUD), in which drug cues 3 outweigh other rewards. Eighty-two people with OUD and 34 controls watched the 4 opening of the film Trainspotting during functional magnetic resonance imaging, 5 then retold it in their own words. Language-model analyses showed that people 6 with OUD dwelt on craving, personal identification and recovery, devoting more 7 of their retelling to drug scenes. In 65 participants with imaging, this speech bias 8 tracked how strongly the brain had distinguished drug from non-drug scenes during 9 viewing, in left frontoparietal cortex in controls but left temporal cortex in OUD.

10 Direct comparison showed left accumbens coupling positive in OUD and negative 11 in controls, and left superior frontal coupling weaker in OUD. Spontaneous speech 12 thus carries a trace of neural encoding whose route depends on what the experience 13 meant to the speaker.

1 Introduction

What a person says about an experience is shaped by how their brain encoded it. Naturalistic neuroimaging has shown that spoken recall during scanning can reinstate the neural patterns established at encoding [1] [2] [3] but the link between encoding and the spontaneous description that follows, outside the scanner, is rarely measured directly. Unconstrained speech unfolds over extended timescales, integrates perception with memory and emotion, and engages distributed neural systems supporting self-referential, social, and executive processes [4, 5]. Constrained laboratory paradigms have defined much of what is known about encoding and retrieval, yet their structure limits generalizability and clinical utility. Whether what a person says about an experience, in their own words and within the hour, preserves how their brain encoded it is unknown. If it does, speech becomes a readout of encoding, and people for whom the same content carries different meaning become a test of whether that readout runs through the same systems.

Drug addiction is the decisive test case: the same film carries different meaning for people with and without the disorder. Addiction models predict that drug content captures salience at the expense of other content and is weighted as self-relevant [6] [7] [8], so if speech preserves encoding, the route from one to the other should differ by group.

In the impaired Response Inhibition and Salience Attribution (iRISA) framework, this salience bias is coupled with diminished engagement of control and self-regulatory systems, reflected in exaggerated drug-cue responses across reward, salience, default mode, and control networks [9]. Its core tenet is a seesaw: because the value of a cue depends on what it competes with [10], drug cues outcompete other arousing reinforcers (such as food and sexual cues) sharing the same context, calling for designs that contrast drug content against non-drug content embedded in the same narrative rather than against neutral cues [11]. These neural biases track craving and relapse risk [12, 13].

Recent work has turned to complex, real-world stimuli, such as narrative films, during functional MRI [14]. Such paradigms elicit reliable, drug-biased neural responses in individuals with substance use disorders, and those responses can change with treatment.

In everyday life, people do not passively view drug cues in isolation; they remember, recount, and reinterpret them through language. Spontaneous speech carries addictionrelevant signals that predict subsequent drug use [15]. In the cohort studied here, the similarity structure of recall across participants tracks the similarity structure of neural responses during viewing [16], an analysis defined over whole transcripts and whole time series. Whether spontaneous speech carries a behavioral trace of the prior neural encoding of drug content specifically, rather than of the narrative as a whole, remains unknown; if it does, a few minutes of speech would index the neural salience bias that tracks craving and relapse risk [12, 13].

Here we combine transformer-based language models with fMRI to test whether what people say about a drug-themed film, within 45 minutes of leaving the scanner, preserves how strongly their brains separated drug from non-drug scenes while they watched, and whether the same regions carry that link in people with opioid use disorder (OUD) and in healthy controls (HC). Both groups viewed the same addiction-themed movie clip during fMRI and afterwards recalled the narrative and their emotional responses to it in their own words. Recall was analyzed with two complementary approaches: data-driven topic modeling to characterize semantic content, and sentence-to-scene similarity to align speech with specific moments in the movie. We then asked whether individual differences in the relative emphasis on drug versus non-drug content in speech were coupled to the 2 . neural contrast between drug and non-drug scenes during viewing. Following iRISA, we predicted group differences in coupling along both of its axes, mesolimbic salience attribution and prefrontal control.

People with OUD retold the film differently from HC participants: they dwelt on craving, identification with the film and recovery, and gave drug scenes a larger share of their recall. This behavioral bias was coupled to neural activity recorded earlier, during viewing, and the coupling dissociated by group along the two axes specified by iRISA: positive in the left nucleus accumbens in OUD and negative in HC, strong in the left superior frontal gyrus in HC and reduced in OUD.

2 Results

2.1 Free recall speech reveals markers of opioid use disorder

A total of 82 participants with OUD (65 males, mean age = 41.5, SD = 10.2) and 34 HC participants (22 males, mean age = 40.8, SD = 11.0; see Table 1) viewed the same movie clip featuring heavy drug-use cues during functional MRI scanning (the first 17 minutes and 3 seconds of Trainspotting; see Methods). After the scan, participants were asked to recall the movie's narrative and describe their experience of watching it, while their speech was recorded.

2.1.1 Topic Modeling

Using data-driven topic modeling, which groups sentences from the recall speech into coherent themes without predefined categories (BERTopic; see Methods), we identified 21 interpretable topics, each representing a cluster of semantically related sentences capturing a recurring theme (Supplementary Table S1). For each participant, we then quantified the prevalence of each topic.

Comparisons revealed significant differences in topic prevalence between groups (Table 2; see Methods). Four topics were significantly more prevalent among OUD than HC participants: "Personal feeling and identification with the movie" (Topic 2, g = 0.47, 95% CI BCa [0.22, 0.67], p corr = .015), "Triggered 'getting high' experiences and cravings" (Topic 7, g = 0.45, 95% CI BCa [0.27, 0.58], p corr = .015), "Personal usage craving and memories triggered by the movie" (Topic 11, g = 0.38, 95% CI BCa [0.25, 0.49], p corr = .008), and "Desire for rehabilitation" (Topic 13, g = 0.50, 95% CI BCa [0.30, 0.66], p corr = .015). No topic was significantly more prevalent among HC participants after correction.

2.1.2 Scene similarity

As a complementary, top-down analysis, we quantified how much of each participant's recall was devoted to each of the 15 pre-defined movie scenes using sentence-to-scene similarity mapping (see Methods). In brief, each recall sentence was assigned to the scene whose textual description it most closely matched, and we then computed, for each participant, the proportion of sentences mapped to each scene.

At the individual scene level, two scenes showed higher average recall proportions among OUD than HC participants in an uncorrected comparison: Scene 4 ("Mother Superior"; g = 0.43, 95% CI BCa [0.20, 0.62], p = .006) and Scene 8 ("Parent with drug addiction"; g = 0.35, 95% CI BCa [-0.03, 0.60], p = .048). Both scenes were explicitly related to drug use, but neither survived correction across the 15-scene family.

To test the expected drug-bias effect directly, we used the a priori classification of each of the 15 scenes as drug-related (n = 7) or non-drug-related (n = 8) based on explicit drug use or drug presence (see Methods). This classification was previously applied only to the movie scenes themselves [14, 16]; here we applied it to speech for the first time, recalculating, for each participant, the proportion of sentences related to any of the drug-related scenes. This single, pre-specified contrast revealed a group difference: OUD participants devoted a larger proportion of their recall to drug-related scenes than the HC group (OUD: M = 0.634, SD = 0.19; HC: M = 0.501, SD = 0.205; g = 0.68, 95% CI BCa [0.28, 1.10], p = .002) (Fig. 1). Within groups, OUD participants recalled drugrelated scenes disproportionately relative to non-drug-related scenes (paired t(81) = 6.38, dz = 0.71, p < .001), whereas the HC group allocated roughly equal attention to the two categories (paired t(33) = 0.04, dz = 0.01, p = .97). The two proportions sum to one within each participant, so the group difference in the drug-scene proportion (p = .002) is equivalent to the group × scene-category interaction. Together with the topic-modeling findings, these results indicate that OUD participants were biased toward drug-related content and toward scenes personally and emotionally relevant to their own drug use.

None of the group differences reported in this section was explained by the demographic, cognitive, or clinical variables that differed between groups (Table 1); adjusting for these variables left each effect intact (see Methods and Supplementary Results).

2.2 Drug-biased speech couples to accumbens encoding in OUD

and to prefrontal encoding in HC Given the drug-biased recall in the OUD group, we next asked whether this behavioral signature corresponded to brain activity during movie viewing. Neuroimaging analysis was restricted to participants with usable fMRI data who completed the entire study data collection, yielding 35 OUD and 30 HC participants (Supplementary Table S2), the same fMRI cohort analyzed in [16] and drawn from the same study as [14]. The drugbiased speech pattern identified in the full sample held in this subset: OUD participants devoted a larger proportion of recall sentences to drug scenes than HC participants (OUD: M = 0.618, SD = 0.17; HC: M = 0.492, SD = 0.22; g = 0.65, 95% CI BCa [0.08, 1.08], p = .012).

We first analyzed neural activity at the level of the region of interest (ROI) using the averaged blood-oxygen-level-dependent (BOLD) signal of cortical areas [17]. For each ROI, we computed a participant-specific Neural Contrast: BOLD activation during drug scenes minus activation during non-drug scenes (see Methods for the full scenewise processing). This measure reflects the degree to which each brain region responded preferentially to drug-related content during movie viewing. To link these neural patterns to behavior, we correlated the Neural Contrast across participants within each group with the language-AI-derived Speech Contrast, which quantifies the relative proportion of speech devoted to drug versus non-drug scenes during recall.

In the OUD group, coupling between the Speech Contrast (post-scanning recall) and the Neural Contrast (in-scanner viewing) was significant after network-wise FDR correction in three ROIs, all positive, all in the left hemisphere and anatomically clustered in the temporal cortex: the temporal pole (LH_Limbic_TempPole_7; r = 0.46, p corr = .042) and two adjacent regions spanning the temporal pole and superior temporal gyrus (LH_TempPar_1; r = 0.41, p corr = .035; LH_TempPar_2; r = 0.44, p corr = .035).

These effects point to left superior temporal cortex as the primary site of brain-speech coupling in OUD participants (full results, including uncorrected associations, in Supplementary Table S3).

In the HC group, coupling was significant after correction in 14 ROIs, all in the left hemisphere, spanning Control B (6 ROIs), Control A (4 ROIs), Default Mode A (2 ROIs), and Default Mode B (2 ROIs) networks. Correlations reached r = 0.68 in the left superior parietal lobule / intraparietal sulcus (LH_ContA_IPS_5; p corr = .001), r = 0.62 in the left rostral superior frontal gyrus (LH_DefaultA_PFCd_2; p corr = .002), and r = 0.62 in the left frontal pole (LH_DefaultB_PFCd_3; p corr = .004). The within-group test was two-tailed, so negative correlations were equally testable; they were present at the uncorrected level in both groups but none survived FDR correction (Supplementary Tables S3 and S5). The two within-group ROI sets did not overlap: no region was significant in both groups.

For each ROI we then compared the Speech Contrast-Neural Contrast coupling between groups (Fisher r-to-z; see Methods). One cortical region showed a significant group difference after correction, with stronger coupling in the HC group: the left superior frontal gyrus within the Default Mode A network (LH_DefaultA_PFCd_3; z = -3.07, p corr = .022). Within groups, this cortical coupling was strong and positive in HC (r = 0.55) and weakly negative in OUD (r = -0.19). We repeated the coupling analysis over the 15 subcortical structures of the Harvard-Oxford atlas [18] [19] [20] [21]. One structure showed a significant group difference in coupling after correction: the left nucleus accumbens (z = 3.55, p corr = .001), the largest effect observed anywhere in the analysis, cortical or subcortical. The difference reflected oppositely signed within-group correlations, positive in OUD (r = 0.34) and negative in HC (r = -0.52; Fig. 3): OUD participants who recalled more drug scenes showed greater nucleus accumbens differentiation of drug scenes, whereas HC participants who did so showed less. Neither within-group correlation survived correction on its own (OUD p corr = .361; HC p corr = .054). Within-group correlations for all 15 structures are provided in Supplementary Table S7. Both corrected group differences survived adjustment for the variables that differed between groups and re-testing under range-matching (Supplementary Results), and the two run in opposite directions across cortex and striatum. We tested that reversal directly as a region × group interaction (see Methods): the difference between the accumbens and superior frontal gyrus group differences in coupling exceeded every one of 10,000 group-label permutations (D = 6.62, bootstrap 95% CI [4.09, 9.47], p = .0001), and a Neural Contrast × Group × Region term in a participant-clustered regression confirmed it (β = 1.75, z = 3.67, p < .001).

We then related the five regions carrying the main results (left nucleus accumbens, left superior frontal gyrus, and the three left temporal regions coupled in OUD) to the 13 drug-use, craving, and withdrawal measures available in OUD, correcting across regions within each measure (see Methods). Neither effect survived refitting the moderation with the Speech Contrast as the outcome or a median-split comparison of the within-group correlations; we therefore do not interpret them as craving effects on coupling (Supplementary Results). Craving induced by the movie scenes (n = 35) did not moderate coupling anywhere, including the nucleus accumbens, where the interaction was flat and nominally opposite in sign, and the superior frontal gyrus (p = .70).

No other drug use measure survived correction against accumbens or prefrontal coupling. The nearest trends, accumbens coupling in participants with earlier onset of use (q = .053) and control-like prefrontal coupling with longer abstinence (q = .107), run in the expected directions but are too weak to interpret. Within the imaging subsample, the Speech Contrast was higher in participants with later onset of regular use (ρ = 0.38, p = .027, n = 35) and lower in those with longer duration of use (ρ = -0.38, p = .028, n = 34); neither association held in the full sample, so both are treated as subsamplespecific. Scene-induced craving was sampled on a fixed 30-s schedule rather than at the drug scenes themselves, so we recomputed it a priori over only the clips falling inside the drug scenes used for the two contrasts (19 of 35 clips), together with dispersion variants and a within-subject drug-minus-non-drug difference (six variables, uncorrected; see Methods).

In the full sample (n = 74 with per-clip ratings), the drug-clip maximum (ρ = 0.290, p = .011) and the within-subject drug-minus-non-drug difference (ρ = 0.266, p = .023) tracked the Speech Contrast; in the imaging subsample (n = 32), none of the six variables was associated with the Neural Contrast or moderated coupling in any region, the nucleus accumbens included (strongest r = 0.265, p = .136). 3 Discussion

What people said about the film, within 45 minutes of leaving the scanner, preserved how strongly their brains had separated drug from non-drug scenes while they watched it. In HC the link ran through left frontoparietal cortex and in OUD through left temporal cortex, and the groups diverged in opposite directions in the accumbens and the superior frontal gyrus: the salience and control limbs of iRISA. The two limbs are ordinarily inferred from separate tasks, often in separate samples; here both emerged from one measure in the same people.

Two complementary analyses converged: bottom-up topic modeling showed that participants with OUD over-represented themes of personal identification with drug content, triggered craving, and recovery, and top-down scene-similarity mapping showed disproportionate allocation of their verbal recall to drug-related scenes relative to HC. Both express the enhanced salience attribution to drug cues that models of addiction postulate, now measurable in what people say spontaneously. Two group differences survived correction, and together they express the two limbs of the iRISA model in opposite directions within a single paradigm. In the left nucleus accumbens, a core substrate of salience attribution, coupling was positive in OUD and negative in HC, the largest effect in the study: the more a participant's accumbens differentiated drug scenes during viewing, the more that participant's later speech dwelt on them. In the left superior frontal gyrus, a dorsal prefrontal region, coupling was strong in HC and reduced in OUD: controls recruited prefrontal cortex to carry scene-specific drug encoding into later speech, and this prefrontal route was not evident in OUD, consistent with the diminished executive engagement highlighted as core to addiction by the iRISA model [6, 7, 9, 11]. That the striatal half appears in an opioid cohort fits evidence that ventral striatal drug-cue reactivity [9, 11] and grey matter volume reductions [22] are especially characteristic of OUD. Neither within-group accumbens correlation survived correction on its own (the HC superior frontal correlation did), so this reading rests on the two group differences themselves, both of which survived correction, covariate adjustment, and range-matching.

The two within-group maps point to different routes from viewing to speech. In OUD, participants who recalled more drug-related content showed greater neural differentiation of drug versus non-drug scenes in the left superior temporal cortex, including the temporal pole and superior temporal gyrus, regions supporting auditory-linguistic processing and the personal-semantic knowledge that underlies autobiographical retrieval [23] [24] [25]. In HC, the same brain-speech relationship engaged more regions (14 versus 3), spanning left frontoparietal cortex across Default Mode and Control networks implicated in narrative comprehension, scene segmentation, and memory consolidation [1] [2] [3].

The two routes are the two halves of iRISA. In HC, speech about drug scenes tracked the frontoparietal regions that track any scene, the externally directed route from viewing to speech. In OUD, speech tracked temporal regions tied to personal semantic memory, and in the accumbens the link ran opposite to HC: what participants later said reproduced a personally weighted encoding, not a weaker version of the HC pattern.

The shared film is what makes the speech comparable across people and the drug bias interpretable: drug and non-drug content compete within one story, under one instruction, so what a participant chose to say is an outcome of that competition rather than a reaction to a cue in isolation. It also removes the variability that ordinarily confounds naturalistic speech paradigms, where topic and eliciting conditions differ from person to person, while retaining the ecological validity of unconstrained recall. Movieelicited recall is in this sense a "standardized free speech" paradigm, analogous to the picture-description tasks used in aphasia, dementia, and Alzheimer's disease research [26] [27] [28], but with a dynamic, emotionally complex and disorder-relevant stimulus in place of fixed, mostly neutral pictures. The same logic has precedent in health: readers assigned the perspective of a burglar or a home buyer later recalled the details relevant to that perspective [29]; here the perspective is supplied by the disorder.

Methodologically, transformer-based language models can extract clinically meaningful semantic structure from brief, unconstrained speech samples. Prior applications in psychiatry have relied on clinical notes and structured interview responses [30], social media posts [31, 32], or naturalistic speech (about consequences of use) in addiction [15].

Such sources capture naturalistic expression but lack a shared stimulus or a controlled environment, or a verified diagnostic status in deeply phenotyped individuals. Semantic embeddings and topics derived from a few minutes of narrative recall, collected with minimal instruction, reveal systematic group differences that align with theoretical predictions and correspond to the underlying neural process.

The neuroimaging contribution builds on two prior analyses of this cohort. Kronberg et al. [14] reported group-level drug-biased neural synchronization to this movie in this cohort, localized to orbitofrontal cortex and recovering with treatment; that analysis was defined over the group, did not detect subcortical results, consistent with other naturalistic studies that have generally not analyzed subcortical areas. King et al. [16] related whole-transcript semantics to whole-movie neural similarity across pairs of participants in the same cohort, a second-order measure blind by construction to which stimulus content drives the correspondence. Neither analysis relates drug-specific speech to drug-specific neural response in the same person. The coupling reported here is first-order: one drug versus non-drug contrast per participant on both the brain and behavior sides, correlated across participants. Linking neural substrates to speech in this way addresses a persistent translational gap in addiction neuroscience, where laboratory findings often fail to connect to the spontaneous cognition and behavior that characterize the lived experience of substance use disorders [33, 34].

This study has several limitations. The stimulus was a single movie (Trainspotting), so generalization to other drug contexts and substances is untested. The sample was predominantly male (75% of the speech analyses, 69% of the brain-coupling analysis) and unequal in size (82 OUD, 34 HC), and nicotine use was almost exclusive to OUD, leaving sex differences, HC-specific topic effects, and the contribution of nicotine unresolved.

Analyses were confined to a single dataset without independent training and testing samples, and the imaging subsample was further restricted to participants who completed both scanning sessions, for comparability with [14, 16], even though only session-1 data were analyzed here; replication in larger, multi-site cohorts with held-out validation is needed. Speech was collected about 45 minutes after viewing rather than immediately, which shows the effects survive a delay but adds variability from consolidation and from individual differences in recall. The clinical analyses were exploratory: correction was applied across regions within each measure rather than across the 13 measures, and the craving results therefore require independent confirmation. We did not test whether these linguistic markers predict treatment response or relapse, previously suggested for speechbased markers in cocaine addiction [15]. Finally, we analyzed linguistic content only, and the Speech Contrast is a single scalar chosen for interpretability that discards information present in the higher-dimensional sentence-level representations; acoustic and prosodic features, and explainability methods for the latent embedding dimensions, remain to be explored in future work.

Anchored to neural function and collected in minutes, the measure is a candidate for the low-burden outcome measures that addiction trials need [35], once it replicates in independent cohorts, and the same logic applies to any condition in which emotionally salient content is processed with a bias. Beyond addiction, a few minutes of unconstrained speech carries a trace of how the brain encoded an experience, and the route of that trace depends on what the experience meant to the speaker: in health it runs through frontoparietal cortex; in opioid addiction the prefrontal link weakens, the accumbens link reverses, and temporal cortex carries the trace instead.

4 Materials and Methods

4.1 Participants

Eighty-two individuals with OUD and 34 HC participants were recruited through a clinical trial evaluating intervention-related recovery outcomes in opioid addiction (NCT04112186), for which therapy-specific results will be reported separately. The Icahn School of Medicine at Mount Sinai Institutional Review Board approved all study procedures, which complied with all relevant ethical regulations, and all participants provided written informed consent. Participants completed all movie task procedures twice, during an initial baseline visit (Session 1) and approximately 15 weeks later (Session 2). During the interscan interval, OUD participants completed an 8-week group therapy program in addition to their regular inpatient treatment (see below). In this study, we utilized only data from session 1. For the analyses that involved fMRI data, we included only participants who completed the entire study, leaving a final sample of 35 OUD and 30 HC; this restriction keeps the sample identical to the fMRI cohort analyzed in [16] for comparability.

All OUD participants were recruited as inpatients enrolled in a treatment program that included prescription opioid medications for OUD (methadone: n=76, buprenorphine/naloxone: n=6) as well as regular therapy counseling and relapse prevention, trauma, and anger management courses, at a collaborating substance use rehabilitation organization in the New York City metropolitan area (Samaritan Daytop Village, Queens, NY). Age-and gender-matched HC participants were recruited through advertisements and word-of-mouth in the surrounding community.

Eligibility and participant characteristics were assessed during an initial screening with a comprehensive clinical interview administered by trained research coordinators supervised by clinical psychologists. Diagnostic evaluations included the Mini International Neuropsychiatric Interview, 7th Edition [36] and the Addiction Severity Index, 5th Edition [37]. All OUD participants met criteria for OUD with heroin as their primary substance of choice, and HC participants did not meet criteria for any current or past substance use disorder. Craving and withdrawal symptoms in OUD participants were assessed with the Heroin Craving Questionnaire (HCQ), a modified version of the Cocaine Craving Questionnaire [38], and the Short Opiate Withdrawal Scale (SOWS) [39], and their drug dependence severity was assessed with the Severity of Dependence Scale (SDS) [40]. Methadone or buprenorphine use was confirmed through urine toxicology at all screening and study visits. For all participants, current or past nicotine use and alcohol dependence were assessed with the Fagerström Test for Nicotine Dependence (FTND) [41] and the Short Michigan Alcohol Screening Test (SMAST) [42]. A brief medical examination including heart rate, blood pressure, alcohol concentration (with saliva test strips), breath carbon monoxide, and pregnancy testing when applicable (with urine test strips) was administered by trained research coordinators during each visit. Estimated verbal and nonverbal IQ were assessed with the reading subtest of the Wide Range Achievement Test-3 [43] and the Matrix Reasoning subtest of the Wechsler Abbreviated Scale of Intelligence [44]. Other neuropsychological measures included assessments for symptoms of depression (Beck's Depression Inventory) [45] in the past two weeks and anxiety (Beck's Anxiety Inventory) [46] in the past month. Group comparisons on demographic, neuropsychological, and substance use characteristics were performed using Mann-Whitney U tests for continuous variables and chi-squared tests for categorical variables in Python (scipy v1.15.2, statsmodels v0.14.4).

All participants were medically healthy at the time of study participation.

4.2 Experimental Protocol

During the session, participants viewed the first 17 minutes and 3 seconds of the movie Trainspotting with fMRI acquisition as described in our previous publication [14]. Scans were acquired on a Siemens 3T Skyra (Siemens, Erlangen, Germany) with a 32-channel head coil as part of a 2-hour MRI protocol that included other fMRI tasks [e.g., [47] [48] [49] and T1-, T2-, and diffusion-weighted scanning [e.g., 22, 50-52]. Details on the scanning procedures and MRI preprocessing steps can be found in our previous publication [14] and in the Supplementary Methods and Results. Within 45 minutes of completing the MRI procedures, participants were instructed to recall the movie narrative and describe their experience of watching it while their speech was recorded. A research coordinator gave instructions as follows: "Please verbally recall the movie you just watched, in order, with as much detail as possible. The details are more important than the order. While recounting the movie, please also describe any thoughts, feelings, or emotions you may have experienced. Please tell us what these thoughts were and indicate the specific items in each of the scenes that made you have these feelings, and how strong your emotions were." Participants performed the recall task uninterrupted, and no minimum or maximum time limit was imposed. Afterwards, participants completed a quality assurance questionnaire to assess their memory and comprehension of the movie, indicate whether they had seen it before, and rate their attention and understanding of the movie and the audio and visual quality as reported in King et al. [16].

Craving was measured at three points, as reported in King et al. [16]. Immediately before and immediately after the movie, participants rated their current heroin craving on a 0-9 scale ("Please rate how strong your desire for heroin is on a scale of 0-9"), giving the pre-scan and post-scan measures and their pre-to-post change. Following the speech task, participants rated craving on the same 0-9 scale for specific moments of the movie, sampled as 35 three-second clips taken at regular 30-second intervals across the segment; the mean and the maximum of these ratings are the scene-induced craving measures analyzed here (see Drug-scene craving below for how the drug-scene-restricted variants were derived). Because the scene ratings followed the recall task, they could not have shaped the speech analyzed here.

4.3 Speech Processing

Recordings were transcribed to text as in our previous publication, using OpenAI's Whisper automated speech recognition model [53]. Transcribed texts underwent manual quality assurance processing for accuracy, including removal of interviewer speech and irrelevant participant speech (e.g., asking for clarification on the task instructions) [16]. The transcripts were split into sentences using NLTK's sentence tokenizer (a Python package), then embedded into 384-dimensional vectors using all-MiniLM-L6-v2 pretrained model from the SentenceTransformers framework for Bidirectional Encoder Representations from Transformers (BERT) sentence encoding [54]. Notably, this publicly available model runs on a standard computer with very low resources, making the method usable across clinical settings.

4.4 Topic Modeling and Producing Topic Labels

We applied BERTopic, a transformer-based topic modeling framework, to the full corpus of recall sentences pooled across all participants (3378 sentences), to identify recurring themes in participants' recall narratives. BERTopic uses sentence embeddings in combination with a clustering algorithm and class-based TF-IDF (c-TF-IDF) to generate coherent topics from short text segments. Specifically, we first reduced the dimension-ality of embeddings using Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection. Clustering was performed using Hierarchical Density-Based Spatial Clustering of Applications with Noise at BERTopic's default settings, allowing the model to flexibly identify dense semantic clusters while excluding noisy sentences. The full parameters used can be found in the Supplementary Methods. We trained our clustering model on all sentences from both groups together. This pipeline yielded a set of semantically coherent topics without requiring predefined categories, thereby reducing researcher bias.

For each sentence, the model produced soft topic membership scores across the identified topics. These scores were then aggregated by computing the mean score for each topic across all sentences from a participant to obtain individual-level representations.

The resulting participant-level scores quantify the relative expression of each topic in a participant's speech rather than representing true probabilities. Statistical comparisons of topic prevalence between groups were conducted using Welch's unequal-variance tstatistic, pre-specified given the substantial heterogeneity of topic-prevalence variances between groups (OUD/HC variance ratios spanned 0.03 to 319 across topics), with a permutation test (10,000 label shuffles) to derive nominal p-values, followed by Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) correction across all identified 21 topics; effect sizes are reported as Hedges' g with bias-corrected-and-accelerated (BCa) bootstrap 95% confidence intervals (denoted CI BCa throughout; 10,000 resamples). The confidence interval and p-value are computed by independent resampling procedures, so an interval that crosses zero and a p-value below .05 are not mutually exclusive for effects near the significance boundary. Potential confounding effects of demographic variables (Table 1) were examined via correlation analyses, and when significant relationships were detected, results were re-tested using Analysis of Covariance.

To facilitate interpretability, topic labels were assigned by two human expert annotators and automated labeling using the Gemini-1.5-pro-preview-0514 large language model. Representative sentences, those that were clustered for a certain topic (had highest membership score for this topic), from each topic were provided to the model with a standardized prompt (Supplementary Methods) to generate concise and informative labels. For topics containing sensitive drug-related content, automated labeling was restricted due to content filters, and labels were assigned exclusively by human experts.

Human and model labels are reported side by side (Supplementary Table S1); because the human labels were assigned by consensus rather than independently, no inter-rater agreement statistic is reported.

4.6.2 Brain-Speech Coupling

Coupling between speech and neural activity was quantified by correlating the Speech Contrast with the Neural Contrast across participants using Pearson correlation, computed separately for the OUD and HC groups. Within-group significance was evaluated with a two-tailed permutation test (10,000 shuffles of the neural-contrast values, preserving the paired speech contrast). Between-group differences were quantified as a Fisher r-to-z difference and evaluated with a two-tailed permutation test (10,000 shuffles of group label across the pooled sample), since the direction of a between-group difference in coupling strength was not hypothesized a priori and could vary by region.

For the cortical atlas, multiple comparisons were controlled with network-wise false discovery rate (FDR) correction (Benjamini-Hochberg), applied separately within each of the Schaefer atlas's 17 networks in each hemisphere (34 families) [55], to respect functional organization while retaining sensitivity in a modest sample. For the subcortical atlas, FDR correction was applied globally across all 15 structures. This approach balances statistical control with sensitivity in modest sample sizes (OUD: N = 35; HC: N = 30).

This analysis identifies brain regions where individual differences in the verbal elaboration of drug-related content are associated with differential neural responsivity to drug versus non-drug stimuli.

Because the group differences in the left nucleus accumbens and the left superior frontal gyrus ran in opposite directions, we tested that reversal directly. The interaction statistic is the difference between the two regions' group differences in coupling, D = [z(r OUD ) -z(r HC )] accumbens -[z(r OUD ) -z(r HC )] SFG , where z is the Fisher transform of the within-group correlation and each bracketed difference is divided by its standard error, the square root of 1/(n OUD -3) + 1/(n HC -3). D is therefore the difference between the two regions' group-difference z statistics, not between the raw Fisher differences. Significance was assessed with a two-tailed group-label permutation test (10,000 shuffles), shuffling group across the pooled participants with group sizes preserved and each participant's Speech Contrast and two Neural Contrast values kept together. Because both region-wise differences are computed in the same participants they are not independent, so an asymptotic test on their difference would be invalid; the permutation respects that dependence by construction. A stratified participant bootstrap (10,000 resamples) provides a confidence interval. As a parametric check, the data were stacked with two rows per participant and the Speech Contrast regressed on Neural Contrast × Group × Region by ordinary least squares with standard errors clustered by participant, the three-way term being the interaction of interest.
