Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). Statcheck might still miss one-sided tests that are described in another sentence, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Abstract

This study evaluated machine learning models for screening elevated generalized anxiety symptoms from remotely collected neutral read speech in 1,788 adults from Botswana, Ghana, Nigeria, and Tanzania. Elevated symptoms were defined by Generalized Anxiety Disorder 7-item (GAD-7) questionnaire scores ≥ 5. Nine classifiers were assessed across nine scenarios defined by speech-task configuration (short sentence, long passage, or both tasks) and sex stratification under participant-separated, repeated nested cross-validation (CV). Acoustic-only models used the extended Geneva Minimalistic Acoustic Parameter Set, and context-augmented models added age, self-reported health, and sex where applicable. With feature selection, the highest mean area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC-ROC) was 0.594 for acoustic-only models and 0.699 for contextaugmented models, the latter in the female-only combined-task scenario. Shapley additive explanations (SHAP) identified fundamental frequency descriptors as prominent acoustic predictors, while health status and age were the leading predictors in context-augmented models. Performance varied across scenarios, and feature selection provided no consistent benefit. Secondary regression poorly estimated continuous symptom severity. These findings indicate limited discrimination from neutral speech acoustics alone and improved performance with participant context, warranting external validation.

I. INTRODUCTION

G ENERALIZED Anxiety Disorder (GAD) is a pervasive mental health condition that imposes a substantial global socioeconomic burden and affects individual quality of life [1], [2], [3]. Anxiety disorders affect approximately 4.7% of the global population, making them the most prevalent category of mental disorders worldwide [4]. GAD onset often peaks during the transition to young adulthood, when early intervention can improve long-term outcomes [5], [6], [7]. However, conventional clinical assessment relies on clinicianled interviews and self-report measures that are susceptible to recall bias, clinician subjectivity, and social desirability effects [8], [9]. These limitations motivate objective, scalable, and non-invasive screening tools suitable for naturalistic and remote settings [10], [11], [12].

These screening needs have prompted growing interest in digital biomarkers for mental health assessment [13], [14], [15]. Among these, vocal biomarkers offer a promising approach grounded in the psychophysiological links between emotional states and speech production [16]. Anxiety-related increases in muscle tension and changes in respiratory patterns can alter vocal production and acoustic features [17], [18], [19]. However, associations between these features and anxiety presence or severity remain inconsistent. This heterogeneity may reflect differences in the anxiety construct, speech task and elicitation conditions (including stress-elicited, neutral, or spontaneous speech), sample size, and participant demographic or health characteristics [20], [21], [22], [23], [24], [25].

To characterize complex patterns across high-dimensional acoustic features, research has increasingly adopted machine learning (ML) and deep learning (DL) approaches within affective computing [26], [16]. Applications include screening of generalized anxiety [27], social anxiety [28], [29], and other anxiety-related disorders [17], [30]. Beyond anxiety, these approaches address depression [31], [32], [33], posttraumatic stress disorder [34], [35], and other psychiatric and neuropsychiatric outcomes [36], [37], alongside general emotion recognition [38], [39], [40], [41]. These studies draw on clinical interview corpora such as DAIC-WOZ [42], crowdsourced datasets such as DEPAC [43], emotion-recognition collections such as EmoDB [44], and study-specific recordings from demographically diverse populations [45], [46], [47].

Across these applications, feature representations range from handcrafted acoustic descriptors to learned embeddings and multimodal combinations. Handcrafted acoustic low-level descriptors (LLDs) capture prosodic, energy, spectral/cepstral, and glottal properties [47], [39], [11], often organized into established feature sets such as Geneva minimalistic acoustic parameter set (GeMAPS) and its extended version (eGeMAPS) [48], ComParE [49], and emobase [50]. Neural approaches offer learned representations, including Wav2Vec2 embeddings [51] and X-vectors [52], that complement conventional acoustic descriptors. It is worth mentioning that combining acoustic representations with linguistic, physiological, or behavioral inputs, such as text, heart rate variability, and gait measures, can further improve classification [29], [37], [53].

Using these feature representations, conventional ML classifiers, including support vector machines (SVM), random forests (RF), k-nearest neighbors (KNN), multilayer perceptrons (MLP), logistic regression (LR), and Gaussian process (GP) models, have been widely applied to emotion recognition and anxiety screening. Although such pipelines often perform well on acted emotion corpora [38], [40], findings are more heterogeneous in anxiety and internalizing disorder screening. McGinnis et al. [11] achieved 80% accuracy in detecting childhood internalizing disorders using speech elicited immediately after an anxiety-inducing trigger during the Trier Social Stress Task adapted for children (TSST-C). Baird et al. [17] found that anxiety-related acoustic effects in sustained vowels were more detectable at higher Beck Anxiety Inventory (BAI) scores, particularly when participants spoke while smiling. For adult social anxiety, Alon-Ronen et al. [28] reported an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC-ROC) of 0.83 using a GP classifier, with performance varying by utterance type and higher accuracy in single-sex than combined-sex samples. In a broader GAD-screening context, Teferra et al. [27] reported more modest discrimination from impromptu speech, with AUC-ROC increasing from 0.57 using acoustic and linguistic features to 0.62 after adding demographic variables. Collectively, these findings suggest that conventional ML can identify informative vocal patterns, but its screening performance depends on the symptom construct, severity range, speech task, population characteristics, and evaluation setting [54].

Within conventional ML, boosted tree models such as XGBoost, LightGBM, and CatBoost provide practical tools for capturing nonlinear dependencies in high-dimensional acoustic and multimodal features. Park et al. [29] combined acoustic and physiological features from virtual-reality-based social anxiety sessions, with LightGBM achieving an AUC-ROC of 0.819 for trait anxiety. In a more naturalistic setting, Sousa et al. [46] analyzed smartphone-based conversational speech from pregnant women and reported an AUC-ROC of 0.71 for depression and anxiety screening using LightGBM with Melfrequency cepstral coefficients (MFCCs) and openSMILEderived features, although sensitivity remained limited under class imbalance. Feature selection also influenced performance as Requardt et al. [55] found that a compact RF-selected subset of acoustic features outperformed larger feature sets for recognizing emotions of drivers, including anxiety, from in-vehicle speech. Together, these studies support ensemble methods as a practical approach to structured acoustic and multimodal modeling, while highlighting the importance of feature selection and evaluation under realistic recording conditions.

In parallel, DL frameworks, including convolutional neural networks (CNNs), long short-term memory networks (LSTMs), attention mechanisms, and transformer-derived speech representations, have been explored to learn higherlevel vocal representations and reduce reliance on handcrafted features. Pretrained representations, such as Wav2Vec2 and Xvectors, can encode information beyond conventional acoustic descriptors. Brueckner et al. [56] found that pretrained speech representations outperformed MFCC-based baselines, although the best anxiety-detection performance remained moderate, with an unweighted average recall (UAR) of 0.60. These approaches remain constrained by limited clinical datasets, noisy self-report labels, class imbalance, domain shifts, and computational cost. More broadly, credible screening via ML and DL approaches requires leakage-free validation, appropriate feature selection, careful handling of speaker and demographic factors, training-fold resampling, and performance metrics suited to class imbalance [26], [54], [57]. Despite these advances and considerations, the extent to which neutral read speech collected remotely captures information relevant to screening elevated generalized anxiety symptoms remains uncertain. In particular, questions remain about how discrimination changes when participant context is included and how these patterns vary across speech tasks and sexstratification scenarios under participant-separated evaluation.

To address these questions, we evaluate conventional ML models for screening elevated generalized anxiety symptoms from remotely collected neutral read speech in a large multicountry African cohort. We compare acoustic-only eGeMAPS features with context-augmented features incorporating demographic and health information across nine scenarios defined by speech-task configuration and sex stratification. Shapley additive explanations (SHAP) characterize the relative contributions of acoustic and contextual predictors. A common modeling pipeline with participant-separated, repeated nested cross-validation (CV) supports these comparisons while reducing data leakage and tuning-related optimism. As a secondary analysis, corresponding regression models assess whether the same predictors can estimate continuous Generalized Anxiety Disorder 7-item (GAD-7) scores. Sections II-V present the methods, results, discussion, and conclusions, respectively.

II. MATERIALS AND METHODS

A. Participants and Study Design

The present cross-sectional study analyzed data from a multi-country remote investigation conducted during the COVID-19 pandemic among adults in Botswana, Ghana, Nigeria, and Tanzania. In the broader study, a comprehensive set of behavioral, health-related, cognitive, mental health, demographic, and speech data were collected from participants through the SurveyLex platform (Sonde Health, Inc., Boston, MA) using their own smartphones, tablets, laptops, or desktop computers. The present analysis focused specifically on screening elevated generalized anxiety symptoms from remotely collected neutral read-speech recordings using machine learning models. This work extends our earlier analysis of the same cohort, which focused on fundamental-frequencyrelated measures, by evaluating whether a broader acoustic representation can support generalized anxiety classification under naturalistic remote recording conditions [20].

Study procedures were approved by the relevant institutional review boards or ethics committees at Michigan State University, the University of Nigeria, the University of Botswana, the University of Ghana, and the University of Dar es Salaam. Trained research assistants recruited participants through electronic channels, including email and social media platforms. Eligible participants were adults aged 18 years or older who provided informed consent before participation.

B. Demographics and Clinical Measures

The demographic and health-related variables considered in this study included sex, age, and self-reported health status. These variables were used to characterize the analytic cohort and, where appropriate, as participant-level predictors in the machine learning analyses. Country was recorded as Botswana, Ghana, Nigeria, or Tanzania; sex was recorded as male or female; and age was analyzed as a continuous variable. Self-reported health status was binarized, with "very good" and "excellent" responses grouped as good health and "poor," "fair," and "good" responses grouped as poor health.

Anxiety symptoms were measured using GAD-7, a selfreport questionnaire assessing anxiety-related symptoms over the preceding two weeks [58]. Each item is scored from 0 to 3, yielding a total score from 0 to 21, with higher scores indicating greater symptom severity. For supervised classification, participants with scores of 5 or higher were classified as anxious (having elevated generalized anxiety symptoms), whereas those with scores below 5 were classified as nonanxious (non-elevated). This cutoff was retained to preserve sensitivity to mild symptoms [59], informed by our previous analysis of this cohort [20], and reduce class imbalance. For the secondary regression analysis, the original GAD-7 total score was retained as a continuous outcome.

Table I summarizes the demographic and clinical characteristics of the final analytic cohort. Overall, 1,788 adults were included, with a near-balanced sex distribution. Across the cohort, 1,096 participants were classified as non-anxious and 692 as anxious, indicating moderate class imbalance. Anxiety-label distributions varied by country, with the highest proportion of elevated symptoms observed in Botswana and the lowest in Tanzania. Self-reported health status also varied across countries and sex groups; 1,066 participants were categorized as having good health and 722 as having poor health, with Botswana showing the highest proportion of poor-health responses. Age distributions differed across countries, with younger average ages in Ghana and Botswana and older average ages in Nigeria and Tanzania. These cohortlevel differences supported the inclusion of participant-level variables in context-augmented models and motivated sexstratified modeling scenarios.

C. Speech Data Collection

Speech recordings were collected using the Sonde voicerecording application embedded within the web-based Sur-veyLex platform. Participants completed the recordings on their own devices, including smartphones, tablets, laptops, or desktop computers. Before recording, standardized on-screen instructions asked participants to use a quiet environment, minimize background sounds such as television or music, hold the device close to the mouth, and speak in a natural voice. Participants also completed a brief microphone check before proceeding. Audio files were stored in WAV format at a sampling rate of 48 kHz and converted to 16-bit PCM WAV format for analysis. Participants completed two neutral read-speech tasks: a short sentence (SS) and a long passage (LP). As summarized in Table II, the short sentence was designed to provide broad phonetic coverage [60], whereas the long passage provided an extended speech sample for acoustic feature extraction [61]. By design, these tasks were not intended to elicit anxiety or emotional arousal, but rather to support standardized speech collection across a large remote cohort without real-time clinical supervision. The data reflect practical conditions relevant to scalable voice-based screening because recordings were obtained using participants' personal devices in everyday recording environments. Together, these tasks enabled evaluation of whether acoustic features from short-sentence and long-passage recordings could support classification of elevated generalized anxiety symptoms.

D. Speech Sample Preparation and Inclusion Criteria

Speech recordings were preprocessed and quality-screened before acoustic feature extraction. Voice activity detection (VAD) was first applied to each audio file using the WebRTC Python interface to remove prolonged non-speech intervals, particularly leading and trailing silences [62]. The VAD aggressiveness parameter was set to 1, a relatively conservative setting intended to trim silence while retaining the primary speech content. Each recording was then manually reviewed to confirm that it contained the intended speech task and a single analyzable repetition. When needed, recordings were trimmed to remove extra speech, repeated attempts, or other non-task material.

Recordings were excluded if they were incomplete, corresponded to the wrong task, contained multiple repetitions that could not be cleanly separated, had poor audio quality, or were affected by excessive background noise. Automated denoising was not applied because noise-reduction procedures can alter acoustic properties relevant to voice-based modeling, particularly frequency-, perturbation-, and spectral-domain features. Manual screening was therefore used to remove noisy data and preserve the integrity of the acoustic signal under remote recording conditions.

The number of eligible recordings differed between the SS and LP tasks because participants completed the speech tasks remotely and not all recordings passed quality screening. Some participants contributed valid recordings for both tasks, whereas others contributed only one eligible speech sample. Missingness in the GAD-7 questionnaire was handled consistently with the earlier analysis of this cohort: when one or two items were missing, the missing responses were replaced with the mode of the participant's available responses; participants with more than two missing GAD-7 items or missing demographic information were excluded. Acoustic feature extraction from extended speech

E. Feature Extraction

The eGeMAPS acoustic feature set (version v02) [48] was extracted separately from each eligible SS and LP recording using the Python interface of openSMILE [63]. This standardized, interpretable representation comprises 88 recording-level features derived from 25 LLDs, capturing frequency, amplitude, spectral, cepstral, and temporal properties. It extends our earlier fundamental frequency (F 0 ) focused analysis of the same cohort [20] by capturing a broader range of potentially anxiety-relevant acoustic properties. Two feature configurations were evaluated. The primary acoustic-only configuration included eGeMAPS features. The secondary contextaugmented configuration additionally included age and binary self-reported health status in all modeling scenarios, and binary sex in combined-sex scenarios.

F. Machine Learning Pipeline

A machine learning pipeline was developed primarily to evaluate screening-level classification of elevated generalized anxiety symptoms. All pipeline steps, hyperparameter tuning, validation, and model-interpretation analyses were implemented in Python. The main software packages and versions used in the analysis are reported in Supplementary Table S1. Nine modeling scenarios were constructed by crossing speechtask configuration (SS, LP, or combined-task) with sex stratification (male-only, female-only, or combined-sex), yielding scenarios denoted as D 1 -D 9 . Note that in combined-task scenarios, SS and LP recordings were pooled as separate observations. Then, for each scenario, classifiers were trained using both acoustic-only and context-augmented feature configurations, as summarized in Table III.

All data-dependent preprocessing and model fitting were applied to training partitions within each CV fold to minimize leakage. Continuous acoustic features and age underwent Yeo-Johnson transformation to reduce skewness and standardization to zero mean and unit variance. Local outlier factor (LOF) identified training outliers through local density comparisons, and the synthetic minority oversampling technique (SMOTE) addressed class imbalance by generating synthetic minority-class samples. Within each outer training fold, randomized search evaluated 300 hyperparameter configurations using AUC-ROC as the objective. Across the 100 outer folds, this yielded 30,000 hyperparameter trials, corresponding to 300,000 inner-fold pipeline fits per classifier, modeling scenario, feature configuration, and pipeline variant. The selected configuration was refitted on the full outer training fold and evaluated on the held-out fold. Search spaces and fixed hyperparameters are provided in Supplementary Tables S2-S3. Classifiers included support vector classifier (SVC), decision tree (DT), random forest (RF), gradient boosting (GB), extreme gradient boosting (XGB), light gradient boosting machine (LGBM), multilayer perceptron (MLP), Gaussian naive Bayes (NB), and k-nearest neighbors (KNN).

Model performance was assessed on the held-out outer folds using AUC-ROC, balanced accuracy, average precision, and F1 score. AUC-ROC was used as the primary metric because it evaluates discrimination across decision thresholds and is appropriate for imbalanced binary classification. Balanced accuracy, also referred to as unweighted average recall (UAR), was reported to account for unequal class sizes. Average precision was used to summarize precisionrecall performance, which is particularly informative when the positive class is less prevalent. F1 score was also reported as a complementary threshold-dependent measure combining precision and recall. SHAP values were computed for for sampled held-out recordings and averaged per recording across repeated outer-fold evaluations. Then, features were ranked by the mean absolute value of these averaged contributions.

Secondary regression analyses predicted continuous GAD-7 scores using equivalent regressors, excluding NB because no directly corresponding regression estimator was available.

These analyses retained the same modeling scenarios, feature configurations, and fold-specific preprocessing, but used GroupKFold in both CV loops and omitted class-imbalance resampling. Hyperparameters were selected by minimizing root mean squared error (RMSE), using search spaces analogous to those in Supplementary Tables S2-S3. RMSE was the primary evaluation metric, complemented by mean absolute error (MAE) and the coefficient of determination (R² ).

III. RESULTS

This section reports model performance across the nine modeling scenarios (see Table III), two feature configurations (acoustic-only and context-augmented), and two pipeline variants (with and without feature selection). Figure 2 first summarizes the mean AUC-ROC values for all machine learning models when feature selection was included in the pipeline across repeated outer folds. In the acoustic-only setting, models trained on eGeMAPS features showed limited discrimination: male-only scenarios remained close to chance level, whereas female-only and combined-sex scenarios showed modestly higher performance. No acoustic-only result exceeded an AUC-ROC of 0.60; the highest value was obtained by the RF model in the combined-sex longpassage scenario, D 8 , with an AUC-ROC of 0.594. Adding participant-level context improved performance relative to the corresponding acoustic-only models. The strongest result was obtained by the XGB model in the female-only combined-task scenario, D 6 , with an AUC-ROC of 0.699, indicating moderate discrimination.

Additional performance metrics, including balanced accuracy, average precision, and F1 score, are summarized in Figs. S1-S3. Overall, balanced accuracy followed a pattern similar to that of AUC-ROC but with lower absolute values, supporting the same relative trends in modeling scenarios while accounting for class imbalance. Average precision was generally higher in the context-augmented setting than in the acoustic-only setting, indicating improved precision-recall performance for the elevated generalized anxiety symptom class. The F1 score showed a similar overall pattern, with higher values in the context-augmented setting than in the acoustic-only setting.

Feature selection had a model-dependent effect, with no consistent improvement across modeling scenarios and feature configurations (see Fig. S4). In the acoustic-only setting, RF achieved nearly identical AUC-ROC values in scenario D 8 with and without RFE (0.594 ± 0.049 and 0.596 ± 0.049, respectively). In the context-augmented setting, on the other hand, the highest AUC-ROC values in scenario D 6 were obtained by XGB with RFE (0.699 ± 0.056) and LGBM without RFE (0.692 ± 0.052). Notably, error bars represent standard deviations across repeated outer folds, illustrating variability across training and validation partitions and underscoring the importance of evaluating performance beyond a single split or CV run.

In the acoustic-only setting, feature contributions were distributed across multiple acoustic descriptors, with no single feature dominating the rankings. Across RF, LGBM, and XGB, F 0 -related features appeared most consistently among the top-ranked predictors, particularly the mean and percentile values of semitone-scaled fundamental frequency. Other features, including equivalent sound level, formantrelated descriptors, voiced-segment measures, spectral flux, and slope-related features, also appeared among the top 10, although their rankings varied across models.

In the context-augmented setting, the SHAP rankings changed markedly, with participant-level context variables showing the largest mean absolute SHAP values. Self-reported health status consistently had the largest contribution across the three models, followed by age, whereas binary sex did not appear among the top-ranked features. Acoustic descriptors remained represented among the top 10 features, including F 0 -related, loudness-related, formant-bandwidth, and jitterrelated measures. However, their mean absolute SHAP values were smaller than those of health status and age, indicating smaller, model-dependent acoustic contributions to predictions within the context-augmented models. It should be noted that since the SPL of the recordings were not calibrated, the SHAP contributions of loudness-related and sound-level features should be interpreted cautiously, as these measures may reflect differences in microphone sensitivity, recording gain, and mouth-to-microphone distance in addition to vocal loudness.

IV. DISCUSSION

This study evaluated whether remotely collected neutral read speech could support screening for elevated generalized anxiety symptoms. Acoustic-only models showed weak-tomodest discrimination, while adding demographic and health information improved performance, highlighting the importance of participant context in interpreting voice-based screening results.

In the acoustic-only analysis, the strongest performance was observed in the combined-sex long passage scenario, D 8 , where the RF model achieved similar AUC-ROC values with and without feature selection (0.594±0.049 and 0.596±0.049, respectively). These results indicate weak-to-modest discrimination and should be interpreted in light of the study design as recordings were remotely collected from neutral read-speech tasks and were not intended to elicit anxiety or emotional arousal. In contrast, studies reporting higher discrimination have often used anxiety-eliciting tasks or focused on specific anxiety subtypes, particularly social anxiety disorder (SAD). For example, Ciampelli et al. [45] reported AUC-ROC values of 0.854 and 0.739 for longitudinal SAD classification in males and females, respectively, using RF models trained on eGeMAPS features extracted from speech collected during the Trier Social Stress Test (TSST). In a more comparable audioonly setting, Brueckner et al. [56] used spontaneous speech to classify GAD-7-defined anxiety status and reported a UAR of 0.56 with an SVM model using eGeMAPS features, while their best deep learning model using Wav2Vec2 representations reached a UAR of 0.60. Sousa et al. [46] similarly analyzed smartphone-based free conversational speech for depression and anxiety screening in pregnant women and reported a best AUC-ROC of 0.71 using a combination of 64 MFCCs and eGeMAPS features (extracted by openSMILE) with a Light-GBM classifier. Although these studies differ in speech task, label definition, feature representation, and evaluation metric, they collectively suggest that identifying generalized anxiety symptoms from non-eliciting, remotely collected speech remains challenging, particularly when relying on compact handcrafted acoustic descriptors alone. Although methodological differences limit direct comparison, the reported modest performance, especially by Brueckner et al. [56] in a study setting comparable to ours, broadly aligns with our acousticonly findings. Our study extends this evidence to remotely collected neutral read speech in a large, multi-country African cohort, further highlighting the limited discrimination achieved with eGeMAPS features alone.

Speech tasks also influenced performance, although the effect was scenario-dependent. In the combined-sex scenarios, the LP task consistently outperformed both the SS and combined tasks in the acoustic-only and context-augmented settings with RFE (see Table IV). This pattern suggests that longer read-speech recordings may provide more stable estimates of eGeMAPS statistical functionals, particularly in larger and more heterogeneous samples. However, this advantage was not consistent in the single-sex scenarios, indicating that the benefit of longer recordings depended on the modeling population. This interpretation is consistent with Tasnim et al. [43], who reported lower prediction errors for longer and more linguistically rich speech tasks, such as picture description and prompted narrative, than for shorter phoneme-based tasks. Although standardized English texts reduced variation in linguistic content, differences in participants' language backgrounds and English proficiency in the present study, may have influenced prosodic and temporal characteristics, complicating the interpretation of task-related performance differences.

Sex-related differences also shaped classification performance. Female-only scenarios generally outperformed maleonly scenarios across both feature settings, although this does not establish that anxiety-related acoustic patterns were intrinsically more detectable in females. Possible sex-related differences in symptom reporting could also have influenced these comparisons, as classification relied on self-reported GAD-7 labels. In the acoustic-only setting, combined-sex scenarios showed a slight advantage over female-only scenarios, possibly reflecting the benefit of larger sample size and broader acoustic variability. In the context-augmented setting, combined-sex models remained competitive with femaleonly models, although this comparison should be interpreted cautiously because sex was included as a predictor in the combined-sex scenarios. These findings indicate that sex may influence both anxiety-related acoustic markers and model behavior. Prior studies provide related but not identical evidence. Ciampelli et al. [45] reported sex-dependent acoustic predictors and stronger SAD classification in males than females using speech elicited during the TSST, whereas Alon-Ronen et al. [28] found that sex-specific classifiers outperformed a sexunified configuration for social anxiety classification. These findings suggest that the value of sex stratification depends on the speech task, anxiety construct, sample characteristics, and feature configuration.

The context-augmented analysis evaluated whether adding participant-level demographic and health-related variables to eGeMAPS features improved speech-based classification of elevated generalized anxiety symptoms. These models consistently outperformed acoustic-only models in scenario-level and model-level summaries (see Tables IV and V). Performance was strongest in the female-only combined-task scenario, D 6 , with AUC-ROC values of 0.699 ± 0.056 for XGB with feature selection and 0.692 ± 0.052 for LGBM without feature selection, indicating moderate discrimination. SHAP analysis identified self-reported health status and age as the largest contributors to model predictions in the combined-sex, combinedtask scenario, D 9 (see Fig. 4), whereas acoustic descriptors contributed more modestly and varied across models. The present analysis extends our earlier F 0 -focused study [20] by highlighting several F 0 -related descriptors among the leading acoustic predictors across models in both feature settings, whereas F 0 -min was the most prominent acoustic contributor previously. The current semitone-scaled eGeMAPS descrip-tors summarize a broader range of F 0 characteristics than the earlier Hz-based measures, although their contributions remained smaller than those of health status and age in context-augmented models. Additionally, the importance of participant-level context is also consistent with Teferra et al. [27], who reported that GAD screening performance increased from an AUC-ROC of 0.57 using acoustic and linguistic features alone to 0.62 after adding demographic variables. Similarly, Park et al. [29] found that combining acoustic eGeMAPS features with physiological measures outperformed single-source models for predicting severe anxiety symptoms in SAD.

The secondary regression analysis provided a complementary perspective on the distinction between screening and symptom-severity estimation. The regressors did not adequately capture continuous variation across the full GAD-7 range, with predictions concentrated largely between 0 and 10. This restricted prediction range and the systematic underestimation of higher observed scores indicate limited ability to estimate symptom severity, despite the modest discrimination observed in the separate classification analysis.

Model choice also contributed to performance differences, although its effect was smaller than that of feature configuration. As summarized in Table V, tree-based models, including RF and boosting-based classifiers such as XGB, LGBM, and GB, showed the most consistent performance in the context-augmented setting. This pattern is consistent with prior speech-based mental health studies in which ensemble and boosted models have been effective for capturing nonlinear relationships among acoustic and participant-level predictors. In contrast, classifier differences were relatively small in the acoustic-only setting, and all models remained within a weak discrimination range. Thus, although model selection contributed to performance variability, the available predictor information, particularly the contrast between acoustic-only and context-augmented feature sets, had a larger influence on classification performance than the choice of classifier alone.

Lastly, it is worth emphasizing that the reported performance metrics must be interpreted in light of the rigorous ML pipeline developed to reduce data leakage and overly optimistic estimation. Data-dependent preprocessing, outlier detection, resampling, and feature selection were confined to training folds, while participant-level grouping prevented recordings from the same individual from appearing in both training and held-out CV partitions. Hyperparameter tuning was restricted to the inner CV loop, separating model selection from outer-fold performance assessment. Repeated outer evaluations characterized variability across data partitions, and complementary metrics supported interpretation under class imbalance. These choices are central to assessing speechbased screening models because violations can inflate apparent discrimination without improving generalization.

V. CONCLUSIONS

This study evaluated ML models for screening elevated generalized anxiety symptoms from remotely collected neutral read speech in a large, multi-country African cohort. Acoustic-only eGeMAPS features showed weak-to-modest discrimination, while adding demographic and health information consistently improved performance. SHAP analyses identified health status and age as the leading contributors in context-augmented models, with more modest, model-dependent acoustic contributions. Secondary regression was not able to capture continuous GAD-7 scores, with predictions concentrated in the low-to-mild range, indicating limited suitability for precise symptom-severity estimation. Performance varied with speechtask configuration, sex stratification, classifier, and feature processing, underscoring the importance of task design and rigorous evaluation. These findings support further investigation of voice-derived features alongside participant-level information for scalable remote anxiety screening. External validation, richer speech tasks, longitudinal data, and multimodal integration are needed to clarify their utility.
