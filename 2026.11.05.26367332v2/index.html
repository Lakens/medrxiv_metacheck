Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 225 papers containing 405 instances of non-exact p-values, the module correctly detected 269 cases (true positives) and incorrectly identified 78 (false positives). It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Aim and objectives:

This paper is part of a supplement reporting findings and learnings from the NEST360 Alliance.

We conducted a descriptive analysis of the KMC coverage, quality, and outcomes for LBW neonates across 66 neonatal units in four African countries implementing with NEST360: Kenya, Malawi, Nigeria, and Tanzania. Data on KMC coverage and quality in routine care in sub-Saharan Africa are limited. This study addresses this gap using individually linked records and a quality-of-care cascade to identify where neonates are lost along the KMC pathway.

Our objectives were: 1.

KMC Coverage: Describe KMC coverage over time at country population-level for admitted neonates eligible to receive KMC using aggregate data.

2. KMC quality: Describe quality-of-care cascades including key quality indicators for eligible neonates receiving KMC using individual-level data.

3. Outcomes for KMC:

To evaluate the association between KMC and neonatal survival at discharge, and to further assess whether this association varied by illness severity, birthweight, and timing of KMC initiation after accounting for individual-and clustering within hospitals.

METHODS

Overview

This study employed a multi-site, cross-sectional observational design using secondary data analysis. As part of the NEST360 program, routine data on all neonatal admissions were collected across participating hospitals for the analysis period spanning January 2021 to December 2024. The study is reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines [22] (see Appendix 1).

Study Setting

NEST360, established in 2019, has progressively expanded its implementation across 66 newborn units in level 2 hospitals. The analysis is restricted to hospitals collecting data starting in 2021. Following implementation, prospective data collection for all newborn admissions has been maintained at participating sites.

.

Data sources

The Neonatal Inpatient Dataset (NID) [23] was co-designed with African governments to be a parsimonious individual dataset routinely collected for every newborn admitted to a neonatal ward and used for quality of care improvement on that ward. The NID comprises a set of ~55 variables capturing neonatal outcomes, admission details and variables to describe high-impact care. Trained clerks' abstract information from medical records and input data into REDCap, a secure, web-based platform.

The NID functions as the principal data source in implementing hospitals in Malawi (nationally), Tanzania, and Nigeria. In Kenya, however, the NID variables are pulled from a research-focused larger dataset, the national Clinical Information Network (CIN) [24] resulting in alternative coding structures for certain variables.

Methods by objectives

Objective 1: KMC Coverage

In alignment with national standards and current evidence, NEST 360 implementing hospitals aim to initiate KMC for infants born with a birthweight of <2500g. Coverage was stratified by weight categories (<1000g, 1000-1999g, 2000-2499g) and were reported as yearly proportions on a country population-level.

Objective 2: KMC Quality

Individual-level data were aggregated by country to create quality-of-care cascades for neonates who received KMC by adapting the HIV clinical cascade framework established for HIV treatment by the World Health Organisation [25]. Reproductive, Maternal, Newborn, Child and Adolescent Health (RMNCAH) coverage cascade literature around service contact and coverage also informed cascade development [26, 27].

Clinical quality indicators were selected based on key domains of care for small and sick newborns eligible for KMC with established relevance to neonatal survival, including the timing and continuity of KMC, breastmilk feeding practices, and administration of continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) [11]. Timeliness of KMC was how soon in days after birth KMC was commenced, consistent with current WHO recommendations for immediate KMC for preterm and low-birthweight infants, irrespective of clinical stability [11, 28].

To evaluate quality and continuity of care across these domains, three related care cascades were developed. Kenyan facilities were excluded from all cascade analyses because KMC timing data were unavailable for the study period; breastmilk timing data required for the KMC and nutrition cascade were also unavailable. For the KMC care cascade, neonates eligible for KMC were defined as all newborns admitted during the study period with birthweight <2500g.

The cascade assessed, in sequence, documentation of KMC at any point during admission, initiation of KMC, initiation on the day of birth, and continuation of KMC from that first day through to discharge. Building on this cascade, the KMC and nutrition care cascade evaluated whether neonates initiated on KMC additionally received exclusive breastmilk feeding on the same day through to discharge. The KMC and CPAP care cascade further assessed integration of respiratory support among neonates eligible for both KMC and CPAP who received KMC and CPAP at any time during admission. We did not assess whether KMC and CPAP were administered concurrently. CPAP eligibility was defined as birthweight 1000-1499g, or birthweight 1500-1999g with respiratory distress and hypoxia, in line with clinical criteria applied with NEST360-supported hospitals that is consistent with current evidence [29].

Objective 3: Association between KMC and Survival at Discharge

To evaluate the association between KMC and survival at discharge from the neonatal unit, we conducted both primary and secondary multilevel analyses. The primary analysis assessed KMC receipt, while the secondary analysis examined the timing of KMC initiation among neonates who received KMC. Both analyses accounted for baseline illness severity and clustering of neonates within hospitals.

Neonatal Severity Score Development

The neonatal severity score was constructed using admission characteristics known to predict neonatal mortality, including gestational age, oxygen saturation at admission, and admission temperature, drawing on established neonatal mortality risk scores developed for lowresource settings [30]. Each variable was categorized into clinically meaningful severity strata and assigned ordinal scores reflecting increasing physiological instability. Individual component scores were summed to generate a composite severity score, which was subsequently categorized into mild (3-4), moderate (5-6), and severe (7-9) illness groups (Appendix 7).

The scoring framework was developed and piloted using neonatal inpatient data to assess its ability to predict survival at discharge. In the previously published CPAP analysis [40], the mixed-effects survival model incorporating clinical severity demonstrated good discrimination (AUC = 0.74). The severity score was subsequently applied in the present study to account for baseline illness severity when examining the association between KMC receipt and survival at discharge.

a) Primary Survival Analysis

The association between KMC receipt and survival at discharge was assessed using mixedeffects logistic regression, with survival modeled as a binary outcome. The model included KMC coverage (receiving any KMC), baseline illness severity, sex, and birth location as fixed effects, with facility included as a random intercept to account for clustering. An interaction between KMC receipt and illness severity was included to assess whether the association with survival varied by baseline severity. The contribution of the interaction was evaluated using a likelihood ratio test comparing models with and without the interaction term. Adjusted predicted probabilities of survival and corresponding 95% confidence intervals were estimated across severity categories using marginal effects.

b) Secondary Analysis: Timing of KMC Initiation

Among neonates who received KMC, a secondary mixed-effects logistic regression analysis assessed the association between timing of KMC initiation and survival at discharge. Kenya was excluded from this analysis because KMC initiation timing was not routinely collected.

Neonates from other countries with missing or undocumented KMC initiation timing were also excluded. Among eligible KMC recipients with documented initiation timing, timing of KMC initiation was categorized as same-day initiation (≤24 hours), early initiation (1-3 days), delayed initiation (3-7 days), and late initiation (>7 days). The model additionally adjusted for illness severity category, birthweight category, sex, and birth location, with hospital included as a random intercept.

Statistical Analysis

All analyses were conducted in R version 4.2.2. Mixed-effects logistic regression models were fitted using the lme4 package, with hospital included as a random intercept to account for clustering of neonates within hospitals. Adjusted predicted probabilities and marginal effects were estimated using the emmeans package in R. Model discrimination was assessed using ROC curves and AUC statistics. Statistical significance was evaluated using two-sided tests with an alpha level of 0.05.

RESULTS

Overview

Between 1 January 2021 and 31 December 2024, 375,238 newborns were admitted across 66 neonatal units in the four countries (Malawi = 37, Kenya = 13, Nigeria = 9, Tanzania = 7).

Malawi is the only country-wide scale which accounts for the disproportionate number of sites in this country. Of these newborns, 145,513 met criteria for KMC eligibility, of whom 125,844 (86.5%) had KMC documentation (administered or not administered), as shown in Figure 1 (Malawi: n = 68,274; Kenya: n = 37,282; Tanzania: n = 17,613; Nigeria: n = 2,675). Newborn characteristics across each country are described in Appendix 2.

Results by objectives Objective 1 -KMC coverage

Between 2021 and 2024, KMC coverage increased in all four countries over the study period (Figure 2): from 47.9% to 62.8% in Malawi, 37.2% to 54.5% in Kenya, 33.8% to 61.2% in Tanzania, and 61.1% to 88.7% in Nigeria.

In almost every country and year, coverage was highest among neonates weighing 1000-1999g, reaching 68.0-94.8% by 2024; the exception was Nigeria in 2022, when coverage was marginally higher among neonates <1000g. Coverage was lower in the <1000g and 2000-2499g categories, though both improved over time. The largest gains were in the 2000-2499g category, particularly in Tanzania (8.8% to 40.0%) and Nigeria (28.8% to 68.3%). Coverage among neonates <1000g remained low in most countries (16.5-35.7%), with the exception of Nigeria (46.2-77.9%).

Objective 2: Quality

Objective 2: Quality 108,221 neonates were eligible for inclusion in the quality analysis.

KMC Quality Cascade

88,562 (81.8%) had documentation indicating whether KMC was administered, of whom 48,047 (54.3%) were started on KMC. Among those started on KMC, 44,264 (92.1%) had a valid start date recorded, indicating a relatively small gap in start-date documentation.

Of newborns with a start date, only 12,934 neonates (29.2%) were documented as having KMC initiated on the day of birth. Of these, 8,110 (62.7%) neonates were documented as receiving KMC through to discharge.

Across the entire cascade, only 8,110 (7.5%) of 108,221 KMC-eligible neonates were both initiated on KMC on the first day of life and retained in KMC care until discharge (Figure 3).

KMC and Nutrition Quality Cascade

Among the 44,264 neonates who had a documented KMC start date, 30,003 (67.8%) received breastmilk within the first day of life. Of those who initiated breastmilk early, 29,448 (98.2%) were exclusively breastfed at discharge. However, because only 44,264 of 108,221 eligible neonates were started on KMC with a documented start date, largely because nearly half of neonates with documented KMC status were not started on KMC, the proportion of all KMC-eligible neonates who both received KMC and were documented as having initiated early breastmilk feeding was substantially lower (Figure 4). Overall, 29,448 (27.2%) of KMC-eligible neonates were initiated on KMC and received breastmilk on the same day, with both interventions continued through to discharge.

KMC and CPAP Cascade

Kenyan facilities were excluded from this cascade because KMC timing data were unavailable during the study period. Among the 25,654 neonates eligible for both KMC and CPAP, 22,241 (86.7%) had a documented record of whether or not they received KMC, of whom 11,985 (53.9%) were started on KMC and had start date documented. Of those, only 4,720 (39.4%) also received CPAP during admission, demonstrating that concurrent delivery of KMC and respiratory support was achieved in only a small minority of neonates who required both interventions (Figure 5). Overall, only 4,720 (18.4%) of the 25,654 KMC-and CPAP-eligible neonates had documented receipt of both KMC and CPAP during admission.

Objective 3: Association between KMC and Survival at Discharge a) Primary Analysis

After adjusting for patient-level characteristics, and clustering within hospitals, neonates documented as receiving KMC consistently demonstrated markedly higher predicted probabilities of survival across all severity categories. Both the absolute and relative survival advantages associated with KMC increased progressively with worsening clinical severity, with the greatest differences observed among severely ill neonates (Table 1).

Overall, after adjustment for patient-level characteristics, and hospital-level clustering, KMC receipt was associated with higher survival across all illness severity groups (Tables 1 and 2).

The association between KMC and survival varied significantly by baseline illness severity, with the magnitude of the association increasing with greater severity. Among neonates with mild illness, KMC was associated with substantially higher odds of survival (aOR 10.39, 95% CI 9.67-11.16), with significant interaction effects observed for moderate (interaction aOR 2.29, 95% CI 2.06-2.55) and severe illness (interaction aOR 4.99, 95% CI 3.91-6.35).

Increasing illness severity was independently associated with lower odds of survival. Male and outborn neonates also had lower adjusted odds of survival than female and inborn neonates, respectively (Table 2).

The ROC (Receiver Operating Characteristics) Analysis

The mixed-effects KMC survival model demonstrated good discrimination, with an area under the ROC curve (AUC) of 0.8699, indicating that the model has an 87% probability of correctly distinguishing between a neonate who survived and one who did not survive (Appendix 3).

b) Secondary Analysis

Time of KMC Initiation

Among neonates who received KMC, later KMC initiation was associated with higher adjusted survival compared with same-day initiation after accounting for baseline illness severity, birthweight, sex, birth location, and facility-level clustering. Adjusted predicted probabilities showed lower survival with increasing baseline illness severity across KMC initiation groups, with the lowest predicted survival observed among severely ill neonates receiving same-day KMC (Figure 7).

However, the apparent survival advantage associated with later initiation should be interpreted cautiously, as neonates in the delayed and late initiation groups had necessarily survived long enough to receive KMC, introducing potential survivor selection bias. Detailed adjusted estimates are presented in Appendix 4. In these analyses, later KMC initiation remained positively associated with survival, while greater illness severity, male sex, and outborn status were associated with lower survival. Birthweight was also strongly associated with survival, with progressively higher adjusted odds among higher birthweight categories (Appendix 4).

Birthweight-stratified predicted probabilities further showed that differences in survival by timing of KMC initiation were most pronounced among smaller and more severely ill neonates (Appendix 4). These secondary findings should therefore be interpreted as associations rather than evidence that delaying KMC improves survival. To support the use of these findings in clinical training and advocacy, a plain-language summary of the key associations has been developed and is presented in Appendix 5.

DISCUSSION

This study presents a large multi-country analysis of KMC coverage and quality in sub-Saharan Africa, drawing on prospectively collected, individually-linked data from 66 hospitals across four countries over a four-year period. By adapting quality-of-care cascades such as used from HIV, to KMC and to other neonatal care interventions [31] [32] [33] , this analysis quantifies where, and to what extent, eligible neonates are lost from the care pathway. These results challenge the tendency to treat KMC coverage as a unitary indicator and demonstrates the analytical value of disaggregating the care pathway to identify where, and for whom, delivery of evidence-based KMC care consistently falls short.

Across these 65 sites in the four countries, KMC coverage increased over the study period, with Nigeria and Tanzania recording the largest absolute increases (27.6 and 27.4 percentage points, respectively). Malawi's higher baseline coverage, with steady and sustained improvement in coverage builds on long-standing national institutionalisation of KMC, having integrated the intervention into national guidelines as early as 2005, and pre-service training established. This extended implementation history may have fostered greater acceptability among both healthcare staff and caregivers, and created conditions in which localised adaptations, such as hospital-level innovations in KMC positioning support, could be established and sustained [34] [35] [36]. Nigeria's coverage figures may reflect national newborn care policies and the establishment of KMC champions at hospital level [37, 38]. Tanzania's improvements in overall coverage, alongside coverage in the 2000-2499g category that, despite marked improvement, remained below that of other weight categories, illustrate a pattern observed across settings in which policy revision precedes changes in clinical practice, as the 2022 extension of KMC eligibility to all infants under 2500g does not appear to have been fully operationalised in this weight category within the study period. In Kenya, coverage increased gradually but at a slower pace than other countries, reflecting documented challenges in translating national policy into consistent frontline practice, including resource constraints, limited staff time, which have been identified as barriers to sustained uptake [39].

Infrastructural limitations have also been identified as barrier to high quality KMC in Kenya [40]. Notably, Kenya's expansion of free maternity services has increased demand for hospital-based newborn care, and it is plausible that growth in the volume of eligible neonates outpaced the expansion of KMC capacity, contributing to lower overall coverage rates [41]. It is important to note that these findings are drawn entirely from NEST data collection, which operated at scale only in Malawi; coverage trends for Kenya, Nigeria, and Tanzania therefore may not be representative of national-level trends in those countries. Taken together, these country-level findings underscore that coverage trajectories are shaped by the intersection of policy maturity, health system capacity, and equity, and that strategies to improve KMC uptake must be context-specific.

The quality cascade findings carry direct implications for how KMC programmes in LMICs are designed, monitored, and resourced. The most striking observation is the marked gap in the timeliness of initiation: only around one in eight eligible neonates (12.0%) were recorded to be initiated on KMC on the first day of life, and fewer than one in ten (7.5%) were initiated on the first day and continued through to discharge, despite guidance explicitly recommending initiation as soon as possible after birth [11]. The finding that most KMC in this cohort was not initiated on the first day of life may not be due to clinical prioritisation, but the practical consequences of an infrastructure gap. The 2022 WHO policy recommending KMC prior to clinical stability was published only shortly before the latter years of this study's analysis period, and evidence from comparable level-2 care settings in sub-Saharan Africa suggests that substantial hospital-level infrastructure improvements are required to implement KMC prior to stability safely [8, 42]. Where the conditions for safe early initiation do not yet exist, premature implementation carries clinical risk, and programmes must therefore ensure that advocacy for earlier KMC is accompanied by proportionate investment in the enabling and necessary infrastructure. Programmes should also consider incorporating timeliness of initiation as a standard quality indicator alongside coverage, shifting monitoring frameworks towards the concept of effective coverage, which accounts for both coverage and the quality of the care delivered.

Infrastructure and workforce readiness are critical determinants of whether KMC can be delivered consistently at the hospital level. Service readiness assessments across these same hospitals and time periods have highlighted substantial gaps in the infrastructure needed to support small and sick newborn care [31]. Bed occupancy was highly variable, with almost a third of hospital-months exceeding full capacity, and nursing staffing ratios, particularly at night, frequently fell short of recommended levels, especially in Kenya [31]. A comprehensive Health Facility Assessment (HFA) tool for small and sick newborn care, developed and operationalised by NEST360, has also been used to systematically evaluate hospital readiness across the same 66 hospitals in Kenya, Malawi, Nigeria, and Tanzania [43]. At baseline, overall service readiness for small and sick newborn care was a median of 51% [IQR 48-57%] [19]. Family-centred care, which encompasses KMC, parental involvement, and the support systems needed to sustain these practices, was the most poorly resourced component of neonatal care across all four countries at just 27% [IQR 18-40%] [19]. These gaps extended beyond equipment to the workforce capacity and physical environment needed to support mothers and families in practising KMC, highlighting that hospital readiness for KMC remains substantially limited across these settings. Translating policy into consistent practice requires a paradigm shift towards care of the mother-infant dyad, supported by a whole-systems approach addressing workforce, infrastructure, and institutional culture.

The co-intervention cascades provide additional insight into the co-coverage of newborn care interventions. Within the nutrition cascade, 67.8% of neonates with a documented KMC start date received breastmilk on the first day of life, and of those, 98.2% were exclusively breastfed at discharge. While these figures are encouraging, they must be interpreted in the context of the substantially reduced denominator at this stage of the cascade. The apparent success in breastfeeding applies only to those neonates who had already progressed through earlier documentation and initiation steps, and therefore could reflect a selected group receiving more consistent overall care. Early initiation of breastfeeding within the first hour of life is associated with sustained exclusive breastfeeding and reduced neonatal mortality [44, 45]. However, NEST360 currently records breastmilk initiation only by day of life, meaning that this one-hour target cannot be assessed. Capturing timing of first feed in hours would enable hospitals to determine whether practice aligns with this recommendation and to identify modifiable delays in early initiation. Within the CPAP cascade, among neonates receiving KMC who were also assessed as requiring CPAP, about 39.4% had CPAP administered. As with the primary cascade, it is not possible to determine whether this reflects under-delivery of respiratory support, incomplete documentation, or both. However, given that a significant proportion of KMC-eligible neonates will also require concurrent respiratory support, this figure warrants attention and reinforces the case for strengthening both clinical capacity and documentation systems for co-interventions alongside KMC.

The greatest loss along the cascade occurred at KMC initiation, where 45.7% of neonates with documented KMC status were not started on KMC, considerably larger than the loss attributable to documentation gaps (18.2%). This indicates that non-provision of KMC, rather than failure to record it, is the major driver of the coverage shortfall observed. Nonetheless, documentation gaps remain a systemic challenge in routine data capture rather than a problem specific to any single intervention, and routine data cannot fully distinguish nondelivery of care from failure to document care that was provided. Evidence from comparable settings indicates that the absence of standardised recording tools and reporting processes is a recognised barrier to monitoring progress at scale [46, 47]. Strengthening and standardising documentation of KMC must therefore be treated as an integral component of programme design and scale up [48] [49] [50], both to improve the accuracy of coverage estimates and to ensure that monitoring frameworks can meaningfully distinguish between failure to deliver and failure to record. Survival outcomes were analysed across birthweight and severity strata. These findings suggest that the observed relationship between KMC timing and survival is strongly influenced by baseline neonatal risk, with timing becoming less prognostically important among larger neonates who generally experienced favourable survival outcomes across all timing groups. These findings also suggest that although increasing illness severity markedly reduced neonatal survival, KMC remained strongly associated with improved survival and appeared to provide the greatest relative benefit among the most critically ill neonates.

The findings of this study must also be considered in light of recent evidence on the benefits of KMC prior to stability. Recent evidence indicates that immediate KMC for infants weighing 1.0 to 1.799kg, regardless of clinical stability, may reduce neonatal mortality by up to 25% [28]. While KMC prior to stabilisation does not significantly affect early neonatal mortality at fewer than seven days, likley since these very vulnerable babies need more intensive care, it has been shown to reduce in 28-day mortality in both iKMC trial and OMWANA, especially with higher duration [8]. These findings strengthen the case for earlier initiation and higher duration each day during admission and after lend urgency to the infrastructure investments discussed above. The fact that fewer than one in ten eligible neonates in this cohort received KMC on the first day of life must therefore be understood not only as a quality gap, but as a missed opportunity to deliver an intervention with meaningful survival benefit.

Our analysis has a number of methodological strengths. The scale and longitudinal nature of the dataset, encompassing over 145,000 KMC-eligible neonates across 66 hospitals and four years, provides statistical power and cross-country comparability. Unlike aggregated national estimates, individually linked inpatient data from the NID allow each neonate to be followed from admission to discharge or death, enabling multi-stage quality cascades, adjustment for severity, and identification of the specific points at which care is lost. This granularity represents the principal contribution of the NID to understanding, and improving, the delivery of small and sick newborn care. The development of coverage indicators for KMC within routine health information systems remains an active area of international policy work, with individual case-note data recognised as the most appropriate source for capturing inpatient KMC indicators, despite known data quality challenges [46]. This study demonstrates that structured improvement programmes can achieve meaningful and sustained increases in KMC coverage across diverse health system contexts in sub-Saharan Africa. However, the cascade analyses presented here reveal that coverage, as conventionally measured, is an insufficient indicator of programmatic success. The stepwise losses from eligibility to documented, timely, and continuous KMC delivery illustrate that the distance between a policy commitment to KMC and its realisation as effective care remains considerable. The 2023 WHO Global Position Paper on KMC calls for a fundamental reorganisation of maternal-newborn care, with immediate initiation following birth as the new standard of practice [51]. Achieving this standard across high-burden settings will require investment not only in clinical training and commodities, but in the data systems, institutional accountability structures, and enabling environments that include infrastructural changes to support the mother-infant dyad. Several limitations must be acknowledged. Firstly, there was a substantial amount of missing data for KMC and birthweight. It is plausible that the most critically ill and smallest neonates were least likely to have accurately recorded birthweight and KMC data, which would mean these neonates are under-represented in the analysed sample and could bias the birthweightand severity-stratified findings. KMC timing data were unavailable for Kenyan facilities, which were therefore excluded from all cascade analyses. Elsewhere, start dates were documented for 92.1% of neonates started on KMC, so missing timing data are unlikely to have substantially affected estimates of timely initiation, although the proportion initiated on the first day of life (12.0%) should be considered as underestimated. Data completeness in routine hospital records across LMICs is highly heterogeneous, and incomplete recording in nonstandardised case notes may systematically underrepresent indicator values [52]. These limitations highlight gaps in the infrastructure that support consistent care delivery. The analysis is further limited to NEST360 phase 1 implementation hospitals, which received structured programme support to support KMC uptake and are therefore unlikely to be representative of the broader hospital landscape in each country. Findings should be interpreted in this context and not generalised to non-programme settings without caution.

A limitation is the absence of a KMC duration indicator generally and also within NEST360 data systems. Duration of skin-to-skin contact per day is a major determinant of KMC health impact [53, 54], which reflects a current gap in NEST360 data collection. To accurately capture KMC duration in future, practical approaches such as integrating duration recording into routine documentation should be considered, a practice that has proven reasonably accurate in a validation study in Uganda [55, 56]. Novel digital tools could monitor skin-to skin contact and hence measure KMC duration more reliably [57]. NEST360 plans to test KMC duration measures and include in the NID, and importantly use the data to increase KMC impact.

For the world's smallest and most vulnerable newborns, the gap between what is known to work and what is delivered results in lives lost. Closing that gap demands a shift from measuring whether KMC is offered to ensuring coverage and quality are tracked and data used for KMC for every eligible newborn at the right time and duration in every hospital, and country. As national targets to ending preventable neonatal deaths approach their 2030 deadline, the evidence presented here underscores that scale-up of small and sick newborn care must be accompanied by sustained investment in quality, accountability, and the health systems capable of delivering both.
