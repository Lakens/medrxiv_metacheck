To fix Exact P-Values

What we found

We found 2 imprecise p values out of 39 detected p values.

Show details

Reporting p values imprecisely (e.g., p < .05) reduces transparency, reproducibility, and re-use (e.g., in p value meta-analyses). Best practice is to report exact p-values with three decimal places (e.g., p = .032) unless p values are smaller than 0.001, in which case you can use p < .001.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (e.g., “decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

What we found

We found 21 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download files from additional data repositories reach out to the Metacheck development team.

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

The rapid, unregulated expansion of the call center industry in developing countries has increased occupational health risks for young adults, particularly with regard to auditory health.

Prolonged exposure to acoustic trauma, long working hours, and the high intensity nature of call center work can negatively affect young adults at the start of their working lives and may contribute to permanent noise induced hearing loss (NIHL). This damage is often irreversible but preventable if identified at an early stage. This study aims to evaluate the burden of auditory impairment among call center employees in Egypt and to propose evidence based mitigation strategies.

Methods A pilot cross sectional study was conducted among employees of various medical call centers over approximately three months (January to March 2026). Participants were recruited through voluntary (self selected) sampling. Data were collected using a structured questionnaire on work history, and statistical analysis was performed to examine associations with work duration and intensity. Three semi structured, open ended interviews were conducted in March 2026 to explore the symptoms in greater depth.

Forty six call center employees completed the questionnaire. The most frequently reported symptoms were mishearing words (82.6%), difficulty understanding speech in background noise (80.4%), needing others to repeat themselves (78.3%), and sensitivity to loud sounds (78.3%); 60.9% reported tinnitus.

Higher auditory symptom severity was associated with a history of acoustic shock (Mann Whitney U = 344.0, p = .004) and with greater cognitive symptom burden (Spearman's ρ = .416, p = .004), but not significantly with shift type (p = .177), years of work (p = .885), or daily and weekly working hours. Qualitative interviews described auditory strain that builds through the shift, disrupted sleep, informal coping, and the absence of employer guidance or symptom reporting pathways.

Self reported auditory symptoms were common in this preliminary sample of call center employees and were associated with acoustic shock exposure and cognitive symptom burden, but not significantly with shift pattern or length of service. Because the study was cross sectional, relied on self reports, and used a small volunteer sample (pilot study), the findings cannot establish causation or true prevalence. Recommended measures include acoustic shock limiters with incident reporting, audiometric screening that prioritizes at risk employees, safe volume education with regular breaks, and educational programs on auditory hazards and early warning signs to prevent actual auditory damage. Larger multi site studies with audiometric confirmation are also needed.

Introduction

Noise is defined as unwanted and disturbing sound (Occupational Safety and Health Administration, 2002). It is estimated that approximately 16% of adult hearing loss worldwide is attributable to occupational noise exposure (Nelson et al., 2005). Although occupational hearing loss is traditionally associated with the mining and manufacturing industries, the digital transformation of the global economy has created new high risk groups, most notably call center operators handling various accounts. Regardless of the industry, the resulting damage is the same: progressive hearing loss.

In developing countries, economic instability and limited employment opportunities push young adults and recent graduates toward careers in the telecommunications sector, particularly call center services. These roles are accessible because of minimal entry requirements and competitive wages compared with other service sectors, which makes them attractive short term employment options for young people.

Call center employees frequently work long shifts with rotating schedules, which may disrupt circadian rhythm and impair melatonin secretion, causing chronic fatigue and elevated blood pressure (James et al., 2017), along with a high incidence of psychological distress, anxiety, and burnout. Fatigue, sleep impairment, and disrupted melatonin secretion contribute to long term health deterioration (Toker & Güler, 2022).

From an auditory perspective, continuous exposure to unregulated noise from calls, call disconnections, and unexpected high intensity acoustic signals are stressors that may increase the risk of noise induced hearing loss (NIHL), tinnitus, and central auditory processing dysfunction.

The Research Gap

Despite the rapid expansion of the call center industry, particularly with Egypt emerging as a regional hub for business process outsourcing and customer service operations, research on noise induced hearing loss among call center operators remains limited, particularly in the Middle East and Egypt. Previous studies have addressed related concerns in different populations (Pawlaczyk Luszczynska et al., 2018). For example, a study of call center operators in a European setting found that most operators had normal hearing, although a meaningful minority showed early high frequency notching despite reporting auditory and visual symptoms. This highlights the importance of subjective symptom reporting, since symptoms may occur before measurable hearing loss is detected, and the need for further studies. However, that study was conducted in a European context and did not specifically examine occupational hearing among call center workers in Egypt. Similarly, a recent review (Chen & Su, 2025) focused primarily on the mechanisms of auditory damage and noise induced hearing loss in broader workplace populations rather than specifically examining call center employees. Furthermore, limited attention has been given to the psychological and cognitive strain that may accompany auditory symptoms, and to early subjective indicators that may precede clinically detectable hearing impairment.

This gap directly shaped the objectives of the present study, which aimed to determine the prevalence and severity of self reported auditory symptoms among call center employees in Egypt and to identify the occupational and lifestyle factors that may be strongly associated with symptom severity. Rather than relying solely on audiometric assessment, the study adopted a symptom based approach to capture early auditory complaints and their relationship with cognitive and psychological symptom burden. The findings provide additional evidence from an understudied occupational population and suggest that auditory symptoms may be highly prevalent among call center employees. In the present sample, approximately 80% of participants reported mishearing and difficulty understanding speech in background noise, while a history of acoustic shock emerged as one of the strongest correlates of symptom severity. A significant association was also observed between auditory and cognitive symptom burden, suggesting that auditory difficulties among call center employees may extend beyond hearing related complaints alone.

Study Objectives

The main objective of the study was to evaluate the extent of auditory dysfunction among call center operators in Egypt and to investigate the factors that may influence its prevalence and severity. The specific objectives were: 1.

To identify the prevalence of self reported auditory dysfunction among call center employees, including tinnitus, difficulty hearing in background noise, and other early symptoms of hearing loss. 2. To assess the severity and pattern of hearing impairment, including which ear is more affected. 3. To examine the association between shift work and auditory dysfunction, focusing on whether night or rotating shifts increase the risk or severity. 4. To explore lifestyle and occupational factors that contribute to auditory risk, including duration of headset use, personal audio device use, smoking, and past medical history. 6. A Spearman correlation showed a significant, moderate positive association between auditory and cognitive symptom severity (ρ = .416, p = .004, n = 45), indicating that employees who reported more severe auditory symptoms also tended to report greater cognitive strain. Examining the individual cognitive items, slowed information processing showed the strongest association with auditory severity (ρ = .500, p < .001), while difficulty concentrating (ρ = .287, p = .053) and headache (ρ = .282, p = .061) showed borderline, non significant trends, and mental exhaustion was not significantly associated (ρ = .213, p = .154). The cognitive items also correlated with one another (ρ = .417-.587, p ≤ .004), suggesting they reflect a coherent underlying construct of listening related cognitive load.

1.5 Objective 3 -Shift Work and Auditory Dysfunction

Composite auditory symptom severity did not differ significantly across shift type (day, night, or rotating shift) (Kruskal-Wallis H(2) = 3.461, p = .177). This finding does not support shift timing, in itself, as a determinant of auditory symptom severity in this sample; cumulative exposure factors examined below (e.g., acoustic shock history) appeared more strongly related to symptom severity than shift schedule.

1.5.1 Cognitive Symptoms by Shift Type

The distribution of employees across shift categories was unbalanced: 5 employees (10.9%) worked day shift, 12 (26.1%) worked night shift, and 29 (63.0%) worked a rotating shift (Figure 6). Cognitive symptom severity was compared across the three shift categories using the Kruskal-Wallis H test, the appropriate nonparametric test for a grouping variable with more than two levels. The test showed no significant difference in cognitive symptom severity across shift categories (H(2) = 0.851, p = .653, n = 45), and the null hypothesis was retained.

This mirrors the finding for auditory symptom severity by shift (Section 1.5, p = .177): neither auditory nor cognitive symptom burden differed meaningfully by shift schedule in this sample. Taken together, shift timing does not appear to be a driving factor behind either symptom domain, reinforcing that acoustic shock exposure and the auditory-cognitive symptom link (Sections 1.4 and 1.6) are more informative targets for prevention than shift pattern. As with the auditory comparison, the small and unbalanced subgroup sizes (day shift, n = 5) limit the power of this test to detect a true difference, so this should be interpreted as an absence of evidence for a shift effect rather than confirmed evidence of no effect.

The clearest risk factor to emerge was a history of acoustic shock exposure (e.g., a sudden loud sound through the headset): employees who reported such an event had markedly higher auditory symptom severity than those who did not (mean rank 27.25 vs. 14.93; Mann-Whitney U = 344.0, standardized test statistic = 2.87, p = .004), and this was corroborated by a significant correlation (ρ = .414, p = .004). 1.7 Objective 6 -Implications for Prevention

Taken together, the results point to acute acoustic events (acoustic shock) and cumulative listening load (reflected in the auditory-cognitive symptom correlation) as the two most actionable targets for intervention, rather than shift schedule or duration of employment per se.

Thematic Analysis of Interview Data: Occupational Auditory Strain, Sleep Disruption, and Psychological Load Among Call Center Workers

Method Note

Three semi structured, open ended interviews (Participants 1, 2, and 3) were conducted in March 2026 using an interview guide adapted from questions used in the published literature (Appendix 2). The guide covered four a priori topic areas: (1) perceived auditory strain and fatigue, (2) sleep disturbance due to night shifts, (3) anxiety, mental fatigue, coping strategies, and headset use habits, and (4) unreported or subtle symptoms. Transcripts were bilingual (Arabic-English) and machine transcribed. Two of the three transcripts (those of Participants 2 and 3) contained substantial automatic speech recognition (ASR) errors and unsegmented speech, so close paraphrase was used instead of direct quotation for those sections, consistent with standard practice for degraded qualitative data. Analysis followed Braun and Clarke's (2006) six phase approach to thematic analysis: familiarization, initial coding, theme searching, theme review, theme definition and naming, and write up. Participants: P1customer service/back office agent (fixed day shift, later flexible hours); P2call center agent (rotating shifts); P3sales agent (rotating/night shifts, high pressure targets).

Theme 1: Auditory Strain Accumulates Progressively Across the Shift

All three participants described a clear within shift trajectory rather than a static level of discomfortsymptoms were mild or absent at shift start and intensified toward the end.

Sub theme 1.1 -Fatigue proportional to call intensity.

P1 distinguished a high volume "frontline" role (constant, back to back calls) from a self paced back office role and reported that auditory and physical fatigue ("awful headaches," needing painkillers) were markedly worse during the frontline period. P3 similarly linked ear pain directly to shift progression, describing mild discomfort early on that became a "slight pain" by the end of the shift, and explicitly confirmed that this was shift related rather than incidental.

Sub theme 1.2 -Compensatory volume escalation.

Both P2 and P3 reported raising the headset volume as the shift progressed in order to keep understanding callers. P3 described pushing the volume to very high levels through a browser extension in the later hours of the shift, framing it as a direct response to reduced clarity rather than a stable preference. Sub theme 1.3 -Tinnitus, pressure, and ear clogging sensations. P1 reported a progression from occasional muffled hearing to persistent ringing (tinnitus) that continued even after leaving the job, alongside a sensation of pressure or clogging localized to the ear kept under the headset. This was severe enough to prompt a medical visit, but the examination performed (visual otoscopy) did not include audiometric or frequency testing. This is a methodological gap, since no objective threshold data exist to corroborate the self report. P2 and P3 reported pressure sensations during or after calls but were less certain about buzzing specifically; they noted that symptoms correlated with, and increased with, shift intensity.

Theme 2: Sleep Is Structurally Disrupted by Shift Demands, Independent of "Night Shift" Framing Sleep disruption was reported across all three participants, but the mechanism differed by role rather than by a simple day/night distinction.

Sub theme 2.1 -Reduced sleep volume and duration under full time and rotating schedules.

P1 reported that sleep was largely stable during a hybrid schedule but deteriorated once physical, symptom related discomfort (ear pressure) began to interfere, more than because of the shift timing itself. P3 reported a much sharper effect: sleep dropped from a baseline of about 9 hours to 5-6 hours of poor quality sleep under a full overnight schedule, and P3 described daytime recovery sleep as never equivalent to true rest. P2 described the lack of a clear sleep schedule, difficulty coping with shift work, and progressively decreasing sleep hours.

Sub theme 2.2 -Downstream effects on functioning.

P3 linked poor sleep directly to reduced work performance in a role that "needs you to be...full of energy," and to broader emotional overload and a reduced capacity to manage daily life outside work. P1 did not report the same degree of cognitive or mood impact from sleep specifically, attributing daytime tiredness more to cumulative workload than to sleep loss per se.

Theme 3: Psychological Load Is Role Dependent, With Performance Linked Anxiety Concentrated in Sales

Sub theme 3.1 -Anxiety tied to income and performance stability. P3, who works in a target driven sales role, reported that anxiety was conditional on performance ("when I'm doing well at work I don't feel that anxious") and directly linked poor sleep and fatigue to fear over job and income stability. On the structured symptom checklist, P3 confirmed difficulty concentrating and trouble sleeping but denied most somatic anxiety symptoms (sweating, trembling, palpitations).

Sub theme 3.2 -Interference with life outside work.

P3 described concrete downstream effects, including missed social plans and difficulty attending personal obligations, and attributed these explicitly to rotating and overnight shift patterns rather than to the sales role alone.

Sub theme 3.3 -Normalization among staff without performance targets.

P1's account (customer service, not commission or target based) framed the stress as diffuse and comparative ("stressful from all sides": study, work, and life stage) rather than attributing it to a single dominant cause. P1 also noted that colleagues who treated the job as a primary income source seemed comparatively more at ease with the workload.

Theme 4: Coping Is Informal, Substance Assisted, and Entirely Self Directed

Sub theme 4.1 -Self directed micro breaks. P1 described discretionary strategies, such as stepping away from the desk, eating, and taking short naps, used opportunistically rather than as part of any structured break policy.

Sub theme 4.2 -Stimulant reliance.

P3 reported becoming a heavy smoker and relying on caffeine (espresso shots) specifically to manage shift induced exhaustion, framing this as a direct coping response to the energy demands of the sales role. Sub theme 4.3 -Unilateral headset use as informal ear "resting." Both P1 and P2 reported habitually keeping one ear free of the headset (typically the non dominant or left ear) to preserve situational awareness, switching sides when the free ear became uncomfortable. This suggests an unprompted, self taught compensatory behavior rather than anything trained or company issued.

Sub theme 4.4 -Absence of employer guidance.

All participants independently confirmed that no formal guidance existed on headset volume limits or break frequency. P1 noted that the headset hardware had a built in, color coded loudness indicator, but no policy enforced attention to it. P3 confirmed this and added that management actively discouraged removing headsets rather than encouraging rest breaks.

Theme 5: Symptoms Are Underreported and Normalized as "Part of the Job" Sub theme 5.1 -No formal reporting pathway used. None of the participants had proactively reported hearing related symptoms through any formal channel (occupational health, HR, or a doctor specifically citing work) before this study. P1's medical visit was self initiated outside any workplace process, and P3 explicitly confirmed that hearing or ear problems had "never" been raised with anyone before this discussion. Sub theme 5.2 -Peer level awareness without escalation. P3 confirmed noticing similar struggles among coworkers, describing colleagues as "struggling" and "tired," but reported that this was discussed informally among peers ("that's how I go") rather than escalated. This is consistent with a workplace culture that treats these symptoms as an expected cost of the job rather than as an occupational health signal.

Summary of Themes

Across all three accounts, auditory strain presents as a cumulative, within shift phenomenon compounded by unmanaged compensatory volume increases and occurring in a policy vacuum in which no employer guidance on safe headset use exists. Sleep disruption and psychological load appear to interact with auditory symptoms, rather than operating independently of them, particularly in high pressure, target driven roles. Critically, symptom reporting is almost entirely absent from formal channels, which suggests that surveys and self report instruments that rely on participants having already raised concerns with a doctor or employer will substantially undercount prevalence. This supports the study's premise that a psychological and behavioral early symptom pathway (tinnitus, subjective muffling, perceived ear pressure) may be more sensitive than threshold based audiometry in this population, and it reinforces the case for pairing self report data with objective audiometric testing rather than relying on either alone.

2. Discussion

>Discussion

2.1 Principal Findings

This study examined self reported auditory dysfunction and its correlates among 46 call center employees in Egypt. Consistent with the study's first objective, auditory symptoms were highly prevalent: more than three quarters of employees reported mishearing words, difficulty following speech in noise, needing conversations repeated, and heightened sensitivity to loud sounds, and roughly six in ten reported tinnitus. This symptom profile is consistent with early, subclinical auditory strain rather than frank hearing loss, and is broadly in line with what would be expected in an occupational group with prolonged daily headset use and continuous exposure to call related acoustic stimuli.

The composite auditory severity score was approximately normally distributed around a moderate level of symptom burden, with a smaller subset of employees reporting markedly elevated scores (up to 33 out of the composite maximum). This right leaning spread suggests that while most employees experience a low to moderate symptom load, a minority may be experiencing a more concentrated pattern of auditory strain -a group that would benefit most from targeted screening.

2.2 Acoustic Shock as the Dominant Risk Factor

Of all the occupational and medical variables tested, a self reported history of acoustic shocka sudden, unexpectedly loud sound transmitted through the headset -showed by far the strongest and most consistent association with auditory symptom severity, both as a correlate (ρ = .414, p = .004) and as a group comparison (p = .004). This aligns with the broader occupational health literature on call center and telecommunications workers, where acoustic shock incidents (e.g., feedback squeals, line faults, or malicious loud noises from callers) have been identified as a distinct hazard separate from routine background noise exposure (McFerran & Baguley, 2007; Westcott, 2006). The magnitude of this association, in a modest sample, suggests acoustic shock history may be a more sensitive marker of at risk employees than conventional exposure metrics such as years of service or shift pattern -both of which showed negligible correlations with symptom severity in this dataset (ρ = .022 and non significant shift comparison, respectively).

2.3 The Auditory-Cognitive Link

The significant correlation between auditory and cognitive symptom severity (ρ = .416, p = .004), driven most strongly by slowed information processing (ρ = .500, p < .001), is consistent with the concept of listening effort: when auditory processing is compromised, cognitive resources are redirected toward decoding speech and monitoring sound, leaving less capacity for other cognitive tasks and manifesting as fatigue, slowed processing, and difficulty concentrating. In a call center setting, where accurate real time listening under noisy conditions is central to job performance, this auditory-cognitive coupling has direct implications for both employee wellbeing and operational performance (e.g., error rates, handle time). The borderline associations for headache and concentration difficulty (p = .053-.061) likely reflect the same underlying mechanism and may reach significance in a larger sample.

2.4 Factors That Did Not Emerge as Significant

Contrary to some prior assumptions, shift type was not significantly associated with auditory symptom severity (p = .177), suggesting that when shift is treated as a simple three category variable, it does not capture meaningful variation in auditory risk in this cohort. This may indicate that cumulative or event based exposures (such as acoustic shock or headset volume habits) are more proximally related to symptom severity than the time of day pattern of work itself, or it may reflect limited statistical power to detect a true shift effect. Similarly, years of work and daily/weekly work hour categories showed negligible correlations with severity, which may reflect adaptation over time, survivorship (more severely affected employees may have already left the role), or simply that these coarse categorical exposure measures do not capture cumulative noise dose with sufficient precision.

Headphone/device volume (p = .065) and middle ear disease history (p = .072) both showed trends toward higher severity that did not reach the conventional significance threshold. Given the small size of some subgroups (e.g., only 10 employees reported ear disease), these should be interpreted as hypothesis generating rather than null findings, and would benefit from confirmation in a larger sample. Likewise, the medical history variables that showed no association (cholesterol, hypertension, head trauma) had very small affected subgroups (n = 2-5), so the absence of a significant result more likely reflects insufficient power than a true absence of effect and should not be over interpreted as reassuring.

2.5 Limitations

• Cross sectional design: causal or temporal relationships (e.g., whether acoustic shock precedes or follows symptom onset) cannot be established.

• All outcome and exposure measures were self reported; no audiometric testing (pure tone audiometry) was performed to objectively confirm hearing status, so findings reflect perceived rather than measured auditory function.

• Modest overall sample size (N = 46), and several subgroup comparisons involved very small cell sizes (n = 2-10), limiting statistical power to detect true associations for several variables.

• Single center/single country sample, which may limit generalizability to call center workforces with different equipment, noise control policies, or work cultures.

• Recall bias is possible for retrospective exposure items such as acoustic shock history and years of work.

• Composite severity scores, while normally distributed, combine heterogeneous symptoms into a single score, which may obscure symptom specific patterns; nonparametric tests were used throughout for consistency, which is a conservative but not the only defensible analytic choice given the approximately normal distribution of the composite scores.

• Objective audiometric testing was not performed. This decision was informed by prior literature indicating that hearing loss in this occupational population typically does not reach diagnostic clinical thresholds (Pawlaczyk Luszczynska et al., 2018); the study therefore prioritized capturing patient reported auditory and stress symptoms, which may be more sensitive to early or subclinical occupational effects than audiometry alone. However, this means the study cannot confirm or rule out measurable hearing loss in participants, and self reported symptoms are subject to recall and reporting bias. The achieved sample size (n=46) was below the a priori target of 100-120 because reaching the target was not logistically feasible within the data collection period of almost three months; the study is therefore a pilot study, and the small sample limits statistical power and the precision of subgroup comparisons; findings should be interpreted as preliminary. Voluntary (self selected) sampling restricted to three companies via WhatsApp introduces potential selection and non response bias, and many of those reached did not complete the questionnaire, limiting generalizability to the broader call center workforce in Egypt • Multiple group comparisons were performed at α = .05 without correction for multiplicity, and the sample size did not allow multivariable adjustment for potential confounders (e.g., age, sleep duration, headphone use outside work); the acoustic shock association is therefore best regarded as exploratory.

• There was no comparison group of non call center workers, so the observed symptom frequencies cannot be attributed to call center work alone. The qualitative component comprised only three discussions, and the machine transcribed Arabic-English recordings contained transcription errors, limiting the depth and transferability of the themes

2.6 Recommendations

In line with the study's sixth objective, these findings support several practical, evidence based recommendations for hearing conservation in call center environments: • Implement or verify acoustic shock (loud noise burst) limiters on all headsets, and establish a formal incident reporting pathway so acoustic shock events are logged and followed up with hearing checks.

• Introduce periodic audiometric screening for employees, prioritizing those who report acoustic shock events or high symptom burden on routine surveys.

• Incorporate brief cognitive symptom screening (e.g., concentration, fatigue) alongside auditory symptom checks, given their observed correlation, to catch early listening effort related strain.

• Provide employee education on safe headset volume levels and encourage regular listening breaks, particularly for employees who report several hours of daily headset use.

• Given the high overall prevalence of early symptoms, consider organization wide awareness campaigns on early warning signs of noise related auditory strain, rather than targeting only high tenure or night shift employees specifically.

2.7 Conclusion

In this pilot cross sectional study of 46 call center employees in Greater Cairo, which relied on self reported symptoms, auditory symptoms were common: more than three quarters of participants reported mishearing words, difficulty understanding speech in background noise, needing others to repeat themselves, and sensitivity to loud sounds, and about six in ten reported tinnitus. Symptom severity was most clearly associated with a history of acoustic shock (p = .004) and with concurrent cognitive symptom burden (ρ = .416, p = .004), whereas shift type, years of work, and working hours showed no significant association. Because the subgroups were small and unbalanced (only five day shift employees), a shift effect cannot be excluded, and the borderline trends for headset/device volume (p = .065) and middle ear disease history (p = .072) warrant testing in larger samples. Themes from the interviews were consistent with these findings, describing auditory strain that builds over the shift, compensatory increases in headset volume, disrupted sleep, and no employer guidance or reporting pathway for hearing related symptoms.

These findings should be read as preliminary associations rather than causal effects: the design was cross sectional, all measures were self reported without audiometry, the sample was a self selected volunteer sample smaller than planned (pilot study), and there was no comparison group, so the true prevalence of hearing loss among call center workers cannot be inferred. Even so, they identify acute acoustic events and cumulative listening load, rather than shift schedule or tenure, as the most practical targets for prevention. Acoustic shock limiters with incident reporting, audiometric screening that prioritizes employees with shock events or high symptom burden, safe volume education, and regular listening breaks are recommended, and larger multi site studies pairing symptom questionnaires with audiometry are needed to confirm these results. The response patterns and effect sizes from this pilot study can inform sample size planning for that larger study.
