What we found

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

We compared the effects of WL, TLL, and no label on unhealthy food item selection and negative affect among UK adults with and without an eating disorder (ED).

We conducted two online randomised controlled trials. In Study 1, 924 UK adults without an ED diagnosis were randomly assigned (1:1:1) to a WL, TLL, or no labels condition and completed a food-choice task. In Study 2, 240 UK adults with an ED diagnosis completed a baseline no-label food choice task and were subsequently randomly assigned (1:1:1) to WL, TLL, or no labels for a follow-up food choice task. The primary outcome was the number of task-specific unhealthy food items selected. Negative affect before and after food choice tasks was measured as a secondary outcome.

In Study 1 (data collected 02/25 -04/25), unhealthy food selection differed by condition (p = .013). Of five products selected, WL participants selected 0.20 fewer unhealthy items than TLL participants (8.0%; p = .038) and 0.28 fewer than controls (10.7%; p = .004). In Study 2 (data collected 11/25 -02/26), unhealthy selection decreased by 0.39 items with WL (17.7%; p = .015), 0.13 with TLL (5.3%, p = .286), and 0.00 with no labels. The reduction was greater with WL than with no labels (p = .029). Label condition did not differentially affect task-related negative affect in either study (Study 1, p = .757; Study 2, p = .448).

Interpretation WL tended to be associated with lower unhealthy food selection compared to TLL in UK adults. There was minimal evidence that WL or TLL caused negative emotional responses.

Introduction

Rising rates of obesity and non-communicable diseases (NCDs) are a significant public health concern. Approximately 63% of adults are living with obesity or classified as overweight and 1 in 5 children in England are leaving primary school with obesity (1). The likelihood of obesity is notably higher in areas of greater socioeconomic deprivation, where factors such as limited access to affordable healthy food and safe spaces for physical activity present a challenge (1).

Tackling these issues requires multifaceted policy interventions.

Front of pack (FOP) labelling is a public health policy aimed at improving the diet of the UK population by providing comprehensible nutritional information on packaged food products (2). Systematic reviews and meta-analyses conclude that FOP labelling supports consumers to make healthier food choices (3, 4). There is also evidence suggesting FOP labelling encourages reformulation of food products, but this varies by FOP label and implementation type (5).

In the UK, traffic light labels (TLL) have been implemented as a standardised voluntary FOP labelling system since 2013. TLL presents nutritional information per portion and uses green, amber, and red colour-coding to indicate if a food item is low, medium, or high in saturated fat, fat, salt, and/or sugar (6). Calorie content is also displayed but not colour-coded. Quasiexperimental evidence suggests that its introduction has improved the nutritional quality of household food purchases (7).

Chile introduced mandatory FOP nutrient warning labels (WL) on packaged foods high in calories, saturated fat, salt, and/or sugar in 2016 alongside other related policy interventions (8). Since then, several other counties have adopted or are considering adopting a FOP WL policy, including the UK, to help reduce rates of obesity and related NCDs (2, 9). Research postimplementation of mandatory FOP WL has demonstrated a reduction in consumer purchase of high calorie, HFSS foods in addition to reformulation (10) (11) (12).

There is less research directly comparing effectiveness of TLL and WL. A systematic review and meta-analysis indicated that while both TLL and WL reduce the probability of selecting an unhealthy item relative to a control, WL perform better than TLL (4). It is theorised that this is due to WL being easier to process and emotionally respond to (e.g., concern about the high nutrient content). Whereas TLL may be more likely to confuse the participants if a product has, for example, both an amber and green label, which can lead participants to incorrectly perceive a product to be healthier than it is (13). The detailed numerical information and reference intake percentages displayed on TLL have also been highlighted as a point of confusion (14). However, this comparison was limited to eight studies with heterogenous label formats, position, study population, study design, and experimental settings (4). There are very few studies comparing TLL and WL directly in a representative UK sample using a realistic food choice task.

Understanding the relative costs and benefits of different FOP labelling systems is important to ensure optimal policy design and implementation. If labelling systems are well-understood and accepted, then they are more likely to be effective (15). Conversely, if individuals cannot understand the labelling system or feel annoyed by it, then this could lead to individuals ignoring labelling systems (16). It is also important to consider how vulnerable groups may respond to different FOP labelling systems. It is estimated that approximately 3.4 million or 1 in 20 people aged 16 or over in the UK have an eating disorder (ED) (17). People living with an ED are likely to be at increased risk of harm from food labelling. Most research to date into food labelling and individuals with an ED has centred on response to out-of-home (OOH) calorie labelling (18). A systematic review and meta-analysis found that ED pathology was related to being more aware of OOH labels, paying more attention to caloric intake, and adjusting behaviour in response to the labels (18). Qualitative findings suggest a polarised response to OOH calorie labels among individuals with an ED, ranging from them being reassuring and giving "a sense of control" to causing emotional distress and negatively impacting ED recovery (19). However, limited research has examined how different FOP labelling systems, including WL, may affect the behaviour of people living with an ED (20).

In a UK context, there is scarce research comparing FOP TLL to WL and no research has examined this in context of people living with an ED. Therefore, there is a need to (i) understand and compare how these different types of labelling systems influence food choice and (ii) identify any potential unintended psychological consequences, particularly among individuals with an ED.

The present research comprises two online RCTs in which participants made a series of hypothetical supermarket food choices across different food categories that either displayed WL, TLL, or no label (control). The aims of this work were to compare the impact of TLL and WL on food choice and negative emotional responses in (i) individuals without an ED (Study 1) and (ii) individuals with an ED (Study 2). Specifically, we hypothesised that both WL and TLL will affect how many unhealthy food items are selected (vs. control), but WL will have a larger impact on discouraging unhealthy food choices than TLL, and WL will have a larger impact on negative emotional responses than TLL.

We also examined calories and nutrients selected, and measures of participant perceived effectiveness and preference for the different FOP labelling systems.

Methods

The studies were pre-registered on the Open Science Framework (https://osf.io/879kh) and ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT07339969). Both studies received institutional ethical approval (ref: 15576).

Study design

In both studies, participants were recruited to take part in an online RCT where they were required to make hypothetical food choices under different FOPL conditions. In Study 1 (between-subjects), participants were randomly assigned (1:1:1) using the Qualtrics randomiser function to (1) WL group, (2) TLL group, or (3) no label group (control). In Study 2 (mixed design) participants completed a baseline food choice task in part one (no label present), before being randomly assigned (1:1:1) using the Qualtrics randomiser function to (1) WL group, (2) TLL group, or (3) no label group (in which they completed the same task a second time). The second part was required to be completed between 24 hours and 1 week after completing the baseline task. A mixed design was adopted for Study 2 to increase statistical power, because it was not feasible to recruit a similarly large sample as Study 1 when restricted only to participants with an ED.

Participants

For Study 1, the inclusion criteria were current UK resident; over 18 years of age; fluent in English. The exclusion criteria were fasting or other form of restrictive eating e.g., intermittent fasting, elimination diet (FODMAP), religious restrictions, allergy restrictions, calorie/macronutrient restriction, food group restriction; clinical ED or disordered eating; dietary restrictions/intolerances including Vegan, Vegetarian, Gluten-free, Dairy-Free, Sugar-Free, Low salt. Recruitment was also stratified to be representative of the UK population in terms of sex, age, and education level (21) (22) (23), see Appendix A for specific stratification plan.

For Study 2, the inclusion and exclusion criteria were the same as for Study 1, except a current or past ED diagnosis was part of the inclusion criteria.

For both studies, participants were recruited using the online participant panel provider Prolific. For Study 2, Prolific recruitment was supplemented with targeted social media recruitment (posting in ED support groups on Facebook with permission) and the NIHR Be Part of Research registry. Be Part of Research is a free UK-wide service from the National Institute for Health and Care Research (NIHR), in collaboration with the NHS and the UK's devolved administrationswith over 700,000 volunteers signed up. Individuals that were registered with Be Part of Research who expressed an interest in taking part in research relating to 'eating disorders' were identified by the service and sent information about the opportunity to take part in the study. Prolific participants were paid at a rate of £6/hr. Participants recruited outside of Prolific were entered into a prize draw for a £100 Amazon voucher, and £1 was donated to the ED charity 'Beat' for each completed response. Procedures Qualtrics XM survey software was used to create the survey questions and different trials within the food choice task. An online information sheet and consent form were automatically displayed to participants via Qualtrics before the survey started. Participants were required to confirm that they had read the information sheet, and consent to taking part, to proceed with the survey. The survey took approximately 15 minutes to complete. Upon completion of the survey, an online debrief sheet was displayed. In Study 2, an exit button was present on all pages which automatically directed participants to the debrief form, which included links to relevant support pages/phone numbers if the survey had triggered ED symptoms.

Each participant, irrespective of the group completed five trials within the main task. Participants viewed a selection of six packaged food items for scenarios involving labels predominately relating to (1) sugar (cereal), (2) salt (crisps), (3) fat (assorted savoury snacks), (4) saturated fat (pasta ready meals), and (5) calorie content (lunch salads) of the food products. Products were kept as similar as possible within each trial. For example, all cereals were chocolate flavoured and all crisps were salt and vinegar flavoured. Across the five scenarios, eleven products (36.7%) were not high in any nutrient, eleven (36.7%) were high in one nutrient, six (20.0%) were high in two nutrients, and two (6.7%) were high in three nutrients. See Appendix B for included items and 'high in' nutrient distribution per task. Pricing was not displayed to isolate the impact of the labels on food choice.

For each scenario, three items were 'high in' the key nutrient (e.g., sugar for the cereal scenario) and three items were not high in that nutrient. Criteria for products labelled 'high in' was consistent with labelling criteria for a red TLL in the UK; >25% of the nutrient reference intake per 100g, or >30% per portion (24). For the calorie content scenario only, >25% per 100g / portion (after rounding) was considered 'high in'. The design of the WL was informed by the results from patient and public involvement (PPI) from a study co-designing and testing 'high in' WLs for food items in the UK (25). The design of TLL was as it exists on UK products currently, except calorie information was removed for all scenarios apart from the calorie-specific (lunch salads) scenario, in which calorie information was also colour-coded. Packaged products were selected from Tesco online supermarket. Label height was matched across TLL and WL conditions and was approximately consistent with TLL label size and placement on the Tesco website.

Participants were shown all six options at once and were asked to choose one item, imagining that they would actually pay for, receive, and eat this item. In total, participants chose five different items, one from each trial. After participants made their food choices, they were asked questions about the task they had just completed.

The tasks and questionnaires were identical in Study 1 and Study 2, unless indicated.

Measures

Participant characteristics

Sociodemographics

Participants were asked to report characteristics including age, sex, ethnicity, race, highest education level and household income. Education and household income were measured to characterise SEP. Self-reported weight and height were asked to calculate body mass index (BMI). Participants in Study 2 also reported the specific ED they have been diagnosed with.

Food choice motives

The Food Choice Questionnaire (FCQ) assesses the motives influencing an individual's food choices (26). We included the health motives scale as health motivated individuals may be more likely to pay attention to and be influenced by a nutrition label when making food choices. The five items that measure health motives were included; mean score was calculated with a higher score indicating greater health motives.

The eating disorder examination-questionnaire short (EDE-QS)

The EDE-QS is a shorter version of the EDE-Q which provides a single overall score reflecting general ED symptom severity (27). This 12-item questionnaire with responses rated on a 4-point Likert scale, ranging from 0 ('not at all' / '0 days') to 3 ('markedly' / '6-7 days') was administered. The mean response to the 12 items was calculated. Higher scores indicate higher ED symptomatology.

Primary outcome

Total number of unhealthy food items selected Total number of selected unhealthy food items across the 5 trials (0-5). Unhealthy food items were coded as items eligible for a WL or a red TLL for the task-specific nutrient e.g., red for salt in the crisps trial.

Secondary outcomes

Total calories and nutrients from selected food items Total number of calories, salt (g), sugar (g), fat (g), saturated fat (g) from selected food items across the 5 trials was calculated.

Positive and negative affect schedule (PANAS) Negative affect was assessed using the five PANAS items corresponding to negative affect (29). These items were included pre-and post-food choice task. Responses to 'How afraid/upset/nervous/scared/distressed do you feel right now?' were conveyed using a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 ('Very slightly or not at all') to 5 ('Extremely'). Mean pre-and postfood choice task scores were calculated, with a higher score indicating greater negative affect.

The below secondary outcomes were assessed in relation to the typical TLL (including calories) and WL (calorie WL included).

Negative arousal An adapted four-item measure, previously used to assess responses to health WLs regarding snack selection (28) was included. Responses were conveyed using seven-point scales: 'How afraid/worried/uncomfortable/disgusted does the label on food make you feel about consuming the product? With scores ranging from 1 (not at all afraid/worried/uncomfortable/disgusted) to 7 (very afraid/worried/uncomfortable/disgusted). A mean score was calculated for the four items, with a higher score indicating greater negative arousal.

Reactance and avoidance Two items which have previously been used to measure response to health WLs on snacks were included (28); Reactance: 'Are these labels annoying?' , Avoidance: 'Are you likely to avoid these labels?' . Responses were examined using a 7-point Likert scale ranging from 1 ('not annoying' / 'not at all') to 7 ('extremely annoying' / 'very likely'). Items were scored separately with a higher score indicating greater reactance / avoidance.

Acceptability of labels

Acceptability was assessed using the item 'Do you support or oppose putting this label on food products?' . Responses were examined on a 7-point Likert scale ranging from 1 ('strongly oppose') to 7 ('strongly support'). A higher score indicates greater support for the label.

Perceived message effectiveness (PME)

The three-item UNC PME scale was adapted for use (25, 30). The three items were Discouragement: 'This label discourages me from wanting to consume a food product'. Unpleasantness: 'The label makes consuming a food product seem unpleasant'. Concern: 'This label makes me concerned about the health effects of consuming a food product'. Responses were conveyed using a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1('not at all') to 5 ('a great deal'). The mean response across the three items was calculated, with a higher score indicating higher PME.

Additional outcomes

Label awareness

Participants were asked 'Did you notice a nutrition label when making your food choices (e.g., when choosing crisps, pasta ready meal, cereal, savoury snacks, and lunch salad?' ('yes' / 'no' / 'unsure').

Label influence

Participants were asked 'Did the presence of a nutrition label on the food items influence your food choice?' ('Yes' / 'No' / 'Unsure'). If 'yes', how did the nutrition label influence your food choice? Response options (order of presentation randomised): 'it made me select a healthier food item' / 'it made me avoid items high in fat/saturated fat/salt/sugar' / 'it made me select a less healthy food item' / 'it prompted me to think about my food choice before deciding' / 'it didn't influence me and I ignored it' / 'not sure'.

Aim guessing

Participants were asked 'What do you think the aim of this study is?' with an open-text response option. Two researchers independently coded study aim awareness ('aware' / 'unaware') and resolved any disagreements through discussion. To be coded as 'aware', responses were required to mention the impact of labelling on food choice / emotion (or similar).

Data analysis

Descriptives

Descriptive statistics were used to summarise participant characteristics, as well as primary, secondary, and additional outcomes. Continuous variables are summarised as means and standard deviations, and categorical variables are summarised as counts and percentages, split by labelling condition and overall.

Study 1

Results from primary analysis were considered significant at p <.05. Results from all other analyses were considered significant at p <.01 to account for multiple analyses.

Primary analysis

A one-way ANOVA was used to assess the difference between the number of unhealthy food items selected in the three label conditions.

Secondary analyses

Separate one-way ANOVAs were used to assess the impact of the different label conditions on (i) total fat, saturated fat, sugar, salt, and calories in selected items, (ii) negative arousal (iii) reactance, (iv) avoidance, (v) acceptability and (vi) perceived message effectiveness.

A mixed ANOVA (within-subject factor: time point of negative affect rating, between-subjects factor: condition) was used to assess the impact of different label conditions on negative affect.

Exploratory analyses

Separate ANOVAs were used to assess interaction effects between label condition and relevant participant characteristics (age, sex, highest education, household income, health motives) in terms of (i) number of labelled items selected and (ii) negative affect.

Planned comparisons

For all primary, secondary, and exploratory analyses, planned follow-up t-test comparisons were used for any significant main effects or interactions (where relevant) to examine specific differences between the three label conditions.

Sensitivity analyses

Sensitivity analyses were conducted with aim guessers removed for primary and secondary analyses, to assess if results were the same after removing any participants that correctly guessed the study aims.

Study 2

Analyses were the same as for Study 1, except mixed ANOVAs were used for outcomes measured at both baseline and follow-up, with time point as the within-subjects factor and label condition as the between-subjects factor. The condition x time interaction assessed whether change from baseline to follow-up differed between the three label conditions. For change in negative affect, a 3 x 2 x 2 mixed ANOVA was conducted, with condition as the between-subjects factor and time (pre-task or post-task) and session (baseline or follow-up) as within-subjects factors. For exploratory moderation analyses, interactions between condition, time and the relevant participant characteristic were examined. Outcomes assessed only at follow-up (e.g., negative arousal in response to the label) were analysed using between-subject ANOVAs. Baseline-adjusted ANCOVAs were conducted as sensitivity analyses for outcomes measured at both baseline and follow-up (see Appendix E).

Data exclusions

Participant data was excluded from relevant analyses if they (i) did not meet the inclusion criteria, (ii) failed both attention checks, and/or (iii) provided a data point deemed to be an outlier (> 2.5 the median absolute deviation [MAD] value from condition median) for primary or secondary outcomes (31). Participants were also excluded from Study 2 if they did not have usable follow-up data.

Power analysis

For both studies, we powered to our primary outcome (unhealthy food item selection). A metaanalysis found small effects of WL for reducing probability of selecting an unhealthy item relative to a control (OR = 0.65, or d = 0.24) and TLL (OR = 0.81, or d = 0.45) (4).

For Study 1, to detect a difference of d = 0.24 between conditions in follow-up independent samples t-tests (p < 0.05, 80% power, GPOWER 3.1) requires 274 participants per condition (total N = 822). We therefore aimed to recruit a total of 946 participants to account for loss of data (up to 15%).

For Study 2, to detect a mixed interaction effect of f = 0.12 (or d = 0.24) in our planned ANOVA (p < 0.05, 80% power, GPOWER 3.1), a total sample size of N = 246 is required. We aimed to recruit a minimum of 246 participants and up to a maximum of 400 participants (if feasible during the 3-month data collection period) due to uncertainty around effect size estimation for power calculation (as WL vs. TLL has not previously been tested in an ED population), and possible data exclusions.

Sensitivity analyses

In Study 1, only 13 participants (1.4%) correctly identified the aim of the study. We reconducted the primary and secondary analyses with aim guessers removed, and findings were largely unaltered. The only change was that the negative arousal effect no longer met the adjusted threshold, F(1, 600) = 6.35, p = .012, η² = .010.

In Study 2, 35 participants (14.6%) correctly identified study aims. We reconducted the primary and secondary analyses with aim guessers removed, and the only changes were for fat, saturated fat, sugar, and salt selection, which all no longer met the adjusted threshold (p >.01). This loss of statistical significance may partly reflect reduced power following the exclusion of 35 participants, particularly for fat and sugar, for which the effect size estimates remained similar.

Exploratory moderation analysis

Exploratory analyses examined whether age, sex, highest education (bachelor's degree or above versus no bachelor's degree), household income category score, and health-related food choice motives moderated the effect of label condition on unhealthy food-item selection and change in negative affect. No interaction met the preregistered p < .01 threshold in either Study 1 or Study 2 (see Appendix C).

Discussion

Across Study 1 and 2, WL tended to outperform TLL in terms of reducing unhealthy item selection, and the amount of saturated fat and salt selected (Study 2 only). Conversely, TLL was perceived by participants to be more effective than WL for discouraging unhealthy food selection in the sample without an ED diagnosis and also preferred as a labelling format in both samples. In both studies we found no evidence that negative affect increased as a result of exposure to either labelling type. Together, findings suggest that among studied UK adults WL is most effective for changing food choice and TLL was generally preferred by participants, but neither appear to cause unintended psychological harm.

These findings are consistent with a small number of studies which indicate WL may outperform TLL in terms of reducing unhealthy food choice (3, 4). We replicate these findings in a UK sample specifically, and tentatively in a UK sample with an ED diagnosis. Participants in both samples indicated greater support for TLL relative to WL as a FOP labelling policy, and this was particularly evident among participants with an ED. Participants without an ED perceived TLL to be more effective than WL, although there was no difference for participants with an ED. Theoretically it would make sense for WL to be perceived as more effective due to clearer indication of the (un)healthfulness of the product (4). It may be that because the TLL has been implemented voluntarily in the UK since 2013, UK consumers are more familiar with this label and therefore view it more favourably.

In terms of negative emotional responses to the labels, neither TLL nor WL appeared to evoke negative arousal relative to no label. When compared to each other, negative arousal was higher for TLL than WL among participants without an ED, but not among participants with an ED. Psychological reactance was higher for WL than TLL for participants with an ED, but there was no difference for participants without an ED. Other comparisons (e.g., avoidance) revealed no differences between labels or study populations. While the above outcomes produced inconsistent evidence, no change in negative affect was observed before and after being exposed to either of the labels. Collectively these findings suggest that neither acute exposure to TLL nor WL elicits notable increases in negative affect, including among individuals with an ED. This is contrary to related qualitative research which indicates that OOH calorie labelling causes emotional distress among individuals with an ED (19). It may be that because TLL and WL do not specifically focus on calories they are less distressing.

The majority female and younger age distribution of participants in Study 2 is representative of the demographics of people with an ED in the UK (17). However, this sample were also predominantly degree educated. There is limited data available on ED prevalence and education, but ED is known to impact individuals across all educational backgrounds, so this may limit generalisability. The ED sample consisted predominantly of participants reporting diagnoses of anorexia, binge eating disorder and/or bulimia. However, due to sample size we were unable to examine whether findings differed by ED type. It is therefore important to acknowledge that the impacts of labelling across ED types may not be not uniform (e.g., individuals with anorexia may be more negatively impacted by calories vs. individuals with binge eating disorder (19).

In both studies, choices were hypothetical and in the absence of price considerations, which is a limitation. However, in the real world, price would be consistent across labelling types, and hypothetical food selection task findings have been shown to directly translate into real-world behaviour (25). Moreover, in all food selection tasks except the calorie-specific task, TLL without calories were used and calorie WL were omitted. TLL were also applied to all products (under voluntary implementation coverage is approximately 75% of products (32)). This makes results less generalisable to the real-world as calorie information may or may not be required to be displayed in mandatory FOP labelling approaches. However, if this was the case it may be that both WL and TLL could evoke greater negative affect among people with an ED due to displaying calorie content (19).

From a policy perspective, our findings suggest that implementation of a mandatory WL policy in the UK could reduce selection of unhealthy food. Indeed, recent findings from Chile indicate that WL have been estimated to have reduced rates of childhood obesity in Chile since their implementation (33) and, if mandated in the UK are estimated to yield greater reductions in cardiovascular disease and obesity relative to TLL (34). WL may also be more effective than TLL for individuals with lower health literacy due to their simple, clear messaging (3). However, it would be important to engage in consultation with individuals with an ED to ascertain potential impacts of WL on ED behaviours, in addition to potential negative emotional impacts assessed here.

Implementing TLL mandatorily would be a positive (but likely less impactful on food choice) alternative step and would be received favourably by the UK public based on our findings. Due to the voluntary nature of the current TLL policy in the UK, an estimated quarter of products (often unhealthy) do not to display the label (32). Other TLL improvements indicated in related research should also be considered, including design and portion size consistency (14).

Conclusion

Overall, WL appears to be more effective than TLL in changing hypothetical food selection among individuals without an ED, and tentatively individuals with an ED. There were no substantial negative emotional effects observed in response to either label, relative to no label, although mandatory implementation of TLL may initially be more publicly acceptable. The UK Government should consider a mandatory FOP WL strategy or substantial improvements to the current FOP TLL strategy.
