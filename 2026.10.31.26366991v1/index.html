Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). Statcheck might still miss one-sided tests that are described in another sentence, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Abstract

Event-related potentials (ERP) may reveal residual cognitive processing in disorders of consciousness, but clinical translation is limited because group-level effects do not guarantee reliable single-subject detection. Few ERP paradigms have been benchmarked in healthy participants and compared directly with patients in disorders of consciousness. We tested a hierarchical ERP battery in healthy controls and patients in minimally conscious state (MCS) to (1) estimate upper-bound single-subject detectability in healthy participants, (2) compare detection rates with those of MCS patients, and (3) evaluate prognostic value for recovery of consciousness. Forty-eight healthy controls and 26 patients in MCS completed six auditory paradigms: multifeature mismatch negativity (MMN), passive and active oddball P300, the subject's own name, semantic oddball, and sentence N400. Effects were classified using blinded expert visual scoring, region-of-interest ANOVA, cluster-based permutation tests, and support vector machine decoding. Associations between binary ERP classifications and recovery were tested in 22 surviving patients at follow-up. In healthy controls, visual scoring identified MMN to at least one deviant and P300 in both oddball tasks in all 48 participants. Command following as demonstrated by task-related P300 enhancement between active and passive oddball conditions was detected in 81%. Command following was detected in 92% in the semantic oddball task. Sentence-congruity effects (N400 and/or P600) were identified in 69%, despite a strong group-level response, while the own-name effect was detected in only 35%. In patients, detection rates were lower: MMN was found in 27% for the short-duration deviant and 65% when any of five deviant types counted as positive; passive oddball P300 in 35% and active oddball P300 in 31%; sentence-congruity effects in 12%; active-versus-passive oddball modulation in 12%; and semantic oddball effects in 8%. Visual scoring and machine learning generally detected more effects than ANOVA and cluster-based testing, and agreement varied substantially across paradigms. Binary ERP classifications were not significantly associated with recovery (14 recovered, eight did not), although prognostic analyses were limited by sample size. Group-level ERP effects overstated single-subject detectability in healthy controls, whereas the absence of group-level effects in MCS obscured reliable ERP responses resembling those of healthy controls in a few individual patients. In MCS, ERP effects associated with semantic processing and command following were generally uncommon, and binary ERP indicators did not predict recovery in this sample. Our findings establish a benchmark for selecting and refining ERP paradigms and detection methods to assess residual cognition after severe brain injury.

Introduction

Patients who survive severe acute brain injuries may evolve into chronic disorders of consciousness (DOC), most commonly unresponsive wakefulness syndrome (UWS) and minimally conscious state (MCS). MCS is defined by minimal but reproducible behavioral signs of self or environmental awareness 1 . Compared with UWS, patients in MCS have a substantially higher probability of regaining consciousness and functional communication 2 , making accurate prognostication particularly important.

Non-invasive measures such as EEG can reveal residual sensory, cognitive, and language processing not apparent at the bedside and may complement behavioral assessment in predicting outcome [3] [4] [5] . Although evidence is not yet strong enough to support routine clinical use [6] [7] [8] , several studies have reported that MMN, P300, and N400 are more often present in patients who later regain consciousness than in those who remain in UWS [9] [10] [11] . P300, particularly when elicited by subjectively meaningful or task-relevant stimuli 12, 13 , as well as stronger late positive responses in self-referential paradigms using the subject's own name 14, 15 have been associated with better outcomes. However, no single ERP paradigm currently provides sufficient accuracy for routine prognostic use, motivating the use of multi-paradigm approaches 3, 4, 8 . ERP markers of higher-order processing may show stronger relationships with recovery than markers of low-level sensory change detection. We therefore adopted a hierarchical view of ERP paradigms that range from automatic sensory prediction violations to self-referential and semantic operations [16] [17] [18] . At the lower end of this hierarchy, mismatch negativity (MMN) and P3a elicited in passive oddball paradigms index automatic change detection and involuntary orienting. Higher in the hierarchy, P3b in active oddball tasks reflects instruction comprehension, maintenance of a task set, and sustained voluntary attention 3, 19, 20 . Larger P3b responses during active compared with passive conditions are typically interpreted as evidence of command following 21 . The subject's own name elicits enhanced late positivity compared with other first names, probing recognition and autobiographical memory 14, 15, 22 . N400 paradigms probe sentence level semantic integration. N400 effects elicited by incongruent sentence endings have been observed in some patients with DOC 10, 16 . The semantic oddball paradigm requires participants to count words from a specific semantic category and engages both command following and lexical semantic processing 23, 24.

The clinical usefulness of cognitive ERP paradigms is constrained by modest single-patient sensitivity and specificity 25 . In addition, clinical ERP protocols are not always systematically validated in healthy participants, so the baseline sensitivity of a given protocol under optimal conditions is often unknown. Even in healthy individuals, strong group-level effects do not guarantee single-subject detection, as demonstrated for N400 26, 27 and for P3b in active oddball tasks 28 . Kallionpää et al. (2019) found auditory N400 effects in 60-90% of participants depending on the analysis method, with conservative approaches missing effects evident on visual inspection. Paradigms intended for clinical use should therefore first demonstrate high single-subject sensitivity in healthy controls. Few studies have reported the detection rates in healthy volunteers and even fewer have compared these rates with those in DOC patients. We address this gap by testing healthy controls and MCS patients in six cognitive ERP paradigms.

Single-subject ERP detection methods include univariate statistics (ANOVA or t-tests) within predefined regions of interest 29, 30 . Nonparametric cluster-based permutation tests 31 reduce reliance on predefined electrodes and time windows while controlling familywise error. Multivariate machine learning methods exploit spatial and temporal patterns: support vector machines (SVM) and related classifiers can detect subtle but reliable differences between conditions and have been successfully used to decode covert command following in individual DOC patients 25 . Expert visual inspection remains a reference standard for EEG pattern recognition. In their systematic comparison of five single-subject N400 analysis methods, Kallionpää et al. (2019) found higher detection rates with visual scoring and some statistical approaches than with cluster-based tests, but no single method identified effects in every participant. We therefore compare expert visual scoring, region of interest ANOVA, clusterbased permutation tests, and machine learning with SVM.

We quantified single-subject detectability and prognostic value across a hierarchical ERP battery. First, we established baseline sensitivity for each paradigm and detection method in healthy adults, providing an upper bound for patient detection rates. Second, we applied the same battery and analysis pipeline to MCS patients and compared the presence of ERP components between groups. Third, we compared the four detection methods. Fourth, we entered binary single-subject classifications into multivariate logistic regression models to predict recovery of consciousness.

Methods

Participants

Healthy controls

Forty-eight healthy controls (33 female, 13 male, 2 other) participated in the study (mean age 31 years, SD = 11.5). Recruitment was conducted via mass emails at the University of Tübingen. Inclusion criteria were an age of 18-59 years and native German language proficiency. Exclusion criteria were any history of neurological or psychiatric disease and the use of any medication during the study. All participants provided written informed consent and received either 10 euro compensation or course credit.

Patients

Twenty-six patients with MCS were included. Data from the semantic oddball paradigm were missing for one patient, leaving 25 patients for that analysis. Mean age was 67.04 years (SD = 13.9). Mean time since brain injury to EEG recording was 67.04 days (SD = 24.7). See Table 1 for a description of the sample.

Patients' legal representatives received written information about the study and provided written consent prior to enrollment. They were informed that participation had no effect on medical treatment and that participation could be terminated at any time without any consequences.

Patients were admitted to the BDH-Klinic Hessisch Oldendorf (Institute for Neurorehabilitation Research, Hannover Medical School) and were included according to the following criteria: diagnosis of MCS, age between 18 and 89 years; time since injury of 1 to 6 months and stable circulation and respiration. Exclusion criteria were background EEG activity below 3 Hz, a history of depression, schizophrenia, drug abuse, sleep disorders, or any neurological disorders, and epileptic activity in EEG recordings.

The patients were assessed using the Coma Recovery Scale-Revised (CRS-R) 0-3 days before the EEG measurement 32.

The study was approved by the local Ethics Committee of the Faculty of Medicine of the University of Tübingen and the Ethics Committee of Hannover Medical School (No. 7485).

Procedure

The order of paradigms was fixed within each group. In healthy controls, paradigms were presented in the following order: N400, passive oddball, active oddball, mismatch negativity (MMN), subject's own name (SON), semantic oddball. In patients, the order was: SON, passive oddball, active oddball, N400, MMN, semantic oddball. See Figure 1 for a graphical representation of each paradigm.

All stimuli were delivered auditorily via pneumatic earphones (3M E-A-RTONE). Participants in both groups were seated in a comfortable chair throughout the experiment. To better mimic hospital conditions, healthy participants were not instructed to keep their eyes open or closed, but they were asked to avoid moving unnecessarily during the tasks.

Experimental paradigms

Multifeature Mismatch negativity

Mismatch negativity (MMN) was assessed using a multifeature paradigm, also known as the Optimum-1 paradigm 33, 34 . Frequent harmonic standard tones were interspersed with multiple deviant types that differed in acoustic features or spatial presentation, allowing efficient assessment of several auditory discrimination processes within one sequence.

The task comprised three blocks of 615 tones. Each block started with 15 standards and then alternated between 300 standards and 300 deviants. In total, 1845 stimuli were presented, including 945 standards and 900 deviants (180 of each of the five deviant types; intensity, pitch and location deviants were split equally between their two variants, 90 each). Deviants differed from standards in only one dimension and included: (1) duration (shorter), (2) intensity (louder or softer), (3) pitch (higher or lower), (4) location (left-only or right-only presentation), and (5) timbre (simplified spectrum).

Standard stimuli were harmonic tones with a fundamental frequency (f1) randomly drawn between 300 and 500 Hz. Each f1 was used to construct a harmonic complex with components at f1, f1*2, f1*4, f1*8, and f1*16. Standards were 75 ms long with 5 ms fade-in and 5 ms fadeout and were presented at 60 dB SPL.

Pitch deviants increased the fundamental frequency by 50% (higher pitch) or halved it (lower pitch). Intensity deviants were presented at approximately 70 dB (louder) or 45 dB (softer). Duration deviants were shortened to 25 ms. Location deviants were presented monaurally to the left or right ear, while standards were binaural. Timbre deviants retained only the fundamental frequency (f1) and removed higher harmonics.

Stimulus onset asynchrony (SOA) varied between 475 and 525 ms. The paradigm lasted approximately 15 minutes.

Passive and active oddball

Participants completed passive and active oddball tasks using the same stimulus parameters. In total, 400 stimuli were delivered (320 standards, 80 deviants). Each stimulus lasted 100 ms with 5 ms fade-in and 5 ms fade-out. SOA was 850-950 ms. Stimuli were delivered monaurally so that all standard tones were played to one ear and deviant tones to the other ear.

Tones were harmonic complexes with five components (f1, 2*f1, 4*f1, 8*f1, 16*f1). For each participant, the fundamental frequency (f1) was randomly selected from a set of four values for each stimulus type (standards: 300, 400, 440, 500 Hz; deviants: 95, 120, 150, 160 Hz). The first five stimuli were always standards. The remaining sequence was pseudorandomized with the constraint that deviants could not occur in immediate succession.

In the passive oddball task, participants were instructed to listen to the tones. The active oddball task started immediately after the passive task and used the same stimuli. Before the active task, participants were instructed to silently count the rare tones. Healthy participants reported counting an average of 79.3 ± 10.9 (mean ± SD) deviants.

Each oddball task lasted about 6 minutes.

Subject own name (SON) and name conditioning

Name conditioning is a variant of an oddball paradigm with an additional learning and recognition component (described in more detail in 35 ). For brevity, we refer to this paradigm as the Subject's Own Name (SON) paradigm.

A harmonic tone (conditioned stimulus, CS+) was paired with the participant's own name (SON), and another tone (CS-) was paired with an unfamiliar name (other first name, OFN). A frequent standard tone was presented without any association. Tone-name pairing was counterbalanced across participants. OFNs were matched to the SON by the number of syllables. Name loudness was normalized to match the tones.

Conditioned tones and standards were 100 ms in duration (5 ms fade-in and fade-out) and consisted of harmonic tones created in a similar way to the P300 stimuli, but with different base frequencies and frequencies that were always different from those used in the other experiments. Stimuli were drawn from one of five sets of base frequencies (Hz): [162.5 247. OFN and SON were presented 600 ms after the paired tone onset, with a 500 ms silent interval between tone offset and name onset. After standards, SOA was 1300-1400 ms. After CS+ and CS-trials, the next stimulus followed the end of the name after 700-800 ms (SOA = name duration + 1300-1400 ms). Conditioned sounds (tone + SON and tone + OFN) were presented monaurally to opposite ears, with the side counterbalanced across participants.

The first five tones were always standards. Then 60 tone+SON and 60 tone+OFN stimuli were interspersed among the remaining 275 standards. The experiment lasted about 11 minutes.

N400

The N400 paradigm included 200 sentences, with 100 semantically congruent and 100 incongruent cloze endings. After the end of each sentence, ISI was 950-1050 ms. After the cloze word, ISI was 1950-2050 ms. Participants were instructed to listen to the sentences. Instructions were delivered in a prerecorded female voice. The experiment lasted about 17 minutes.

Semantic oddball

Participants completed a semantic oddball task that had been conceptualized previously, a version of which was validated in healthy controls 23 and DOC patients 30.

A total of 400 spoken words were presented from five semantic categories: body parts, transportation, clothing, animals, and furniture. Twenty-five distinct words were selected (five per category) and each word was presented 16 times, yielding 400 presentations. Of these, 320 were non-target (ignored) and 80 were target (counted) words from a randomly selected semantic category. SOA varied between 1150 and 1250 ms. Participants were instructed to silently count words from the target category. Instructions were delivered in a prerecorded female voice.

Healthy participants counted an average of 71.7 ± 13.1 (mean ± SD) words. Presentation order was randomized. The experiment lasted approximately 8 minutes.

Electroencephalography

EEG was recorded using two systems. Patients were recorded in the hospital with a 32-channel Brain Products LiveAmp system using BrainCap caps with built-in passive electrodes (Multitrodes). FCz served as the online reference and Fpz as ground. The sampling rate was 500 Hz. Healthy controls were recorded with a 64-channel Brain Products ActiCHamp system with active electrodes placed according to the extended 10-20 system. Cz served as the online reference and Fpz as ground. Sampling rate was 1000 Hz. Impedance was kept below 25 kOhm.

For subsequent analyses, recordings were harmonized by selecting the 32 electrodes that were present in both systems. Because passive and active electrodes can differ in their susceptibility to environmental noise, line noise was attenuated during preprocessing. In the hospital setting, line noise was not excessive and was effectively suppressed by low-pass filtering.

Preprocessing was identical for both groups and performed in EEGLAB 36 . Continuous data were filtered with a 0.5 Hz high-pass filter and a 45 Hz low-pass filter using the pop_eegfiltnew function with default parameters. A 0.5 Hz high-pass cutoff, rather than 0.1 Hz, was used to reduce prominent low-frequency artifacts in patient recordings, largely related to movement. Data were then downsampled to 250 Hz and rereferenced to the common average. Independent component analysis (ICA) was computed using AMICA 37 . To improve ICA decomposition, a copy of the continuous data was high-pass filtered at 2 Hz for ICA training. The resulting ICA decomposition was then transferred to the dataset filtered between 0.5 to 45 Hz. Components reflecting eye movements and high-amplitude muscle activity were removed, as were components localized to a single electrode that could be clearly distinguished from physiological EEG activity.

ERP baseline correction used -200 to 0 ms for all paradigms except MMN (-100 to 0 ms). Epoch windows were as follows: N400, -200 to 1200 ms; passive and active oddball, -200 to 800 ms; SON, -200 to 1500 ms; MMN, -100 to 500 ms; semantic oddball, -200 to 1000 ms.

After epoching, remaining artifact-contaminated epochs were identified by visual inspection and discarded. Before statistical analysis, epoched data were re-referenced to the average of the mastoid electrodes.

Effect identification methods

For all automated procedures (ANOVA, permutation tests, machine learning), artifact-free trials were used for each participant. In the name conditioning paradigm, we compared responses to the names only because group-level findings in controls were weak or absent in the time windows corresponding to expected learning effects observed previously 38 . To assess command following in the active oddball paradigm, we compared P300 responses between active and passive oddball, expecting a larger P300 amplitude in the active task. In the N400 paradigm, both N400 and P600 ERPs were counted toward effect presence. Visual scoring used the same condition comparisons as the automated procedures.

Visual scoring

Two coauthors (YGP and BK) independently scored the presence of each ERP component of interest. To conceal group assignment, pooled patients and controls received new random IDs, retained across paradigms within each participant. Scorers reviewed ERP curves averaged by condition in multiple formats: (1) midline electrodes Fz, Cz, and Pz, (2) a multiplot showing all electrodes, and (3) global field power. Standard error of the mean was shown on the figures 1 and 3. After independent scoring, the scorers met multiple times to resolve disagreements and reach consensus. Initial agreement is shown in Figure 3.

ANOVA

Following a previously established approach 30 , we used ANOVA in predefined time windows and channels (see Table 2

below).

If either the main effect of Condition or the Condition x Channel interaction was significant (p < 0.05), we marked the component as present for that participant.

Analyses were implemented in R version 4.4.2.

Cluster-based permutation tests

Cluster-based permutation tests were run in FieldTrip 31 with a minimum cluster size of 2 channels and 5000 permutations. The same time windows as in the ANOVA were used (except that N400 and P600 were tested together in a single 300-1200 ms window), but amplitudes were not averaged across time or channels, allowing the algorithm to detect significant clusters in time and space. Eighteen channels were used to reduce peripheral coverage while retaining most ANOVA channels plus additional channels: Fz, Cz, Pz, F3, F4, FC5, FC1, FCz, FC2, FC6, C3, C4, CP5, CP1, CP2, CP6, P3, and P4.

Machine learning with support vector machine

Single-subject decoding analyses were performed on ERP data using MATLAB's fitcecoc function with SVM learners and one-vs-all error-correcting output codes. Analyses included 30 scalp channels (excluding mastoid electrodes) and used epoched data.

Decoding was performed separately for each participant and paradigm, using a time-window decoding approach: for a predefined time window of interest (TOI) specific to each paradigm (same as for ANOVA and permutation tests; see Table 2), features were constructed by concatenating voltages across all channels and all time samples within the TOI, producing a spatiotemporal feature vector for each trial.

To balance trial counts, each decoding run randomly sampled the minimum number of artifactfree trials per class (minTrials), where minTrials was the minimum available across included conditions for that participant. Each class contributed ceil(minTrials/2) trials to training and the remainder to testing. Accuracy, defined as the proportion of correctly classified held-out trials, was averaged across 10 runs with different random samples and splits.

Statistical significance of decoding accuracy was assessed with a label permutation test. For each of 1000 random permutations of trial labels, TOI decoding accuracy was averaged across ten random train-test splits to obtain a null distribution of decoding accuracy. The p-value was computed as (𝑘𝑘 + 1)/(𝑛𝑛𝑛𝑛𝑛𝑛𝑛𝑛𝑛𝑛 + 1), where 𝑘𝑘 is the number of permuted accuracies greater than or equal to the observed accuracy and 𝑛𝑛𝑛𝑛𝑛𝑛𝑛𝑛𝑛𝑛 = 1000.

Group-level statistics

To describe group-level trends and estimate effect sizes, we used paired t-tests on mean amplitude averaged within the same time windows as those used for ANOVA. A single electrode was used for each effect, with Pz for all effects except MMN, where Fz was used.

Outcome in the patients

Outcome data were obtained for 26 patients via a phone interview based on the GOS-E. Four patients were deceased at follow-up, 14 had regained consciousness (diagnosis EMCS or better), and 8 had not (MCS or worse). The average follow-up time from EEG recording to outcome assessment was 183.8 days (range 179-279 days).

Results

Group comparison

As manipulation checks and to estimate effect sizes, we tested standard effects for MMN, N400, and P300 in passive and active oddball tasks. We also tested the semantic oddball condition effect and the response to the participant's own name, as a replication of our previous work.

In healthy controls, effects were strong across all paradigms except SON. Semantic oddball showed dz = 1.16, comparable to passive oddball with dz = 1.09 (see Table 3). Active oddball produced a larger effect size of 1.69. N400 also showed an effect size of similar magnitude, dz = 1.16. MMN had the largest effect size, with dz = 2.14 for shorter deviants, and deviant-specific effect sizes of 2.48 for intensity, 2.08 for pitch, 1.25 for location, and 1.98 for timbre. Unexpectedly, the response to the participant`s own name did not show a significant grouplevel effect.

In patients, the only significant effect was observed for P300 in the active oddball task, but with the opposite sign, with a larger amplitude for standards than for deviants in the P300 time window.

Scoring methods comparison

Cluster permutation tests yielded the lowest detection rates (Figure 3). In healthy controls, visual scoring achieved 100% detection for MMN and both P300 paradigms. Semantic oddball detection was also high, exceeding the command-following estimate from the active versus passive oddball comparison. Compared with visual scoring, machine learning showed similarly high MMN and P300 oddball detection, lower N400 detection, and numerically more commandfollowing detections.

In patients, machine learning produced numerically higher detection rates than visual scoring.

Interrater and intermethod agreement

Agreement was highest between cluster-based permutation tests and ANOVA, and lowest between ANOVA and machine learning (see Figure 3). Methods disagreed substantially in the SON paradigm. Agreement was also limited for passive oddball, falling below 70% for some method pairs. These pairs involved ANOVA, which detected the passive P300 in only 34 of 48 controls, whereas visual scoring and machine learning detected it in all controls. Agreement between methods for N400 was low (55-74% across method pairs).

For visual scoring, interrater agreement before consensus was above 80% for all paradigms indicating a good level of agreement 39, 40 , except for the subject`s own name paradigm.

Clinical outcome prediction

Excluding deceased patients, multiple logistic regression models tested whether binary singlesubject classifications from each paradigm predicted recovery of consciousness (yes/no). No significant effects emerged, including when analyses were repeated separately for visual scoring, ANOVA, machine learning, and cluster permutation tests.

Discussion

Group-average ERPs were an imperfect guide to individual detectability in both healthy adults and patients with MCS. In healthy adults, most paradigms elicited canonical responses, with SON being an exception. Yet a strong group-level N400 effect coexisted with substantially lower single-subject detectability, illustrating that robust group statistics do not guarantee reliable individual detection. In patients, the discrepancy ran in the opposite direction: grand averages often lacked canonical components, although some patients showed several ERPs resembling those of healthy controls. Variation in etiology, lesion profiles, and arousal may obscure responses present in only a subset of patients. Our findings demonstrate the importance of single-subject analysis, with healthy control detection rates providing a practical upper bound benchmark for interpreting patient results.

The highest single-subject classification accuracy was achieved in the oddball family of tasks, especially for MMN. Rutiku et al. 41 reported similarly high within-subject detection performance in 15 participants using the Optimum-1 and local-global paradigms. In the localglobal paradigm, Faugeras et al. 42 reported the MMN in 7/8 healthy controls (87.5%).

In our MCS sample, MMN detection for the short deviant was 27%, which closely matches Fischer et al. 9 (27%) and Faugeras et al. 42 (32.1%). Some studies reported much higher rates in very small samples: de Tommaso et al. 43 reported 100% MMN detection in 4 MCS patients, but such estimates are difficult to generalize. When visual scoring considered MMN responses to any deviant type, the rate increased to 65%. It is possible that some patients responded to one deviant but not to another due to anatomical differences in the source of the MMN suggesting that multifeature paradigms such as Optimum-1 may improve sensitivity in DOC. On the other hand, such paradigms raise a multiple-comparisons problem if no clear correction strategy is applied.

What does the lack of basic responses such as MMN in some patients tell us? In theory, the relative independence of the MMN from attention makes it a good marker of preserved sensory discrimination in DOC. Prior work has linked MMN presence to improved chances of awakening from coma 44 . At the same time, current clinical guidelines stress that sensitivity in DOC studies is often modest and that absence of MMN cannot be interpreted as evidence for absence of residual cognition 4, 6.

Oddball P300 effects were among the most reliable responses in the healthy group. This applies to both passive and active oddball tasks, and supports the idea that oddball P300 is one of the most reproducible ERP phenomena in cognitive neuroscience. Because of this reliability, oddball P300 is also a strong candidate for technical testing of EEG systems. If a device cannot detect the oddball effect in nearly all healthy participants, the signal-to-noise ratio is likely insufficient for more demanding clinical paradigms.

In our sample, active oddball P300 was present in around 31% of patients, which is consistent with previous reports. Steppacher et al. 10 reported a similar proportion, with 11/37 MCS patients showing P300. Faugeras et al. 42 reported a lower rate for the more demanding global P3b effect, 4/28 recordings (14.3%), which is expected because the global-local paradigm places stronger demands on temporal integration and task processing. Overall, this pattern supports the view that P300 is a useful marker of residual auditory-attentional processing in MCS patients.

N400 showed a clear dissociation between group-level strength and single-subject detectability. In healthy controls, the group-level effect size was large and similar to passive P300, but single-subject detection was clearly lower, with around two thirds or fewer participants classified as positive. This indicates greater interindividual variability in N400 morphology, timing, or topography than in oddball P300.

Our healthy control N400 detection rate of 69% fits well within the range reported previously.

Across studies, single-subject detection in healthy participants has ranged from 17% to 88%, including 17% (2/12) and 50% (6/12) in two variants reported by Cruse et al. 26 , 57% (11/19) in Rohaut et al. 45 , 62% (48/79) in Kallionpää et al. 27 , and 88% (14/16) in Beukema et al. 16 . In patients, our detection rate was 3/26 (12%), which is low but still falls within the broad range of prior reports: 12.5% (1/8) in Beukema et al.

(2016), 20.5% (8/39) in Steppacher et al. (2013), 21% (8/38) in Kotchoubey et al. (2005), 36% (5/14) in Rohaut et al. (2015), and 62.5% (5/8) in Formisano et al. 46.

The breadth of these estimates suggests that N400 detectability depends not only on individual differences, but also on paradigm design. In particular, active N400 paradigms may improve detection in healthy participants 16, 26, 27 , but they also require preserved instruction comprehension and sustained attention, which limits their applicability in many MCS patients. From a clinical perspective, passive N400 paradigms do not appear to provide a highly sensitive marker of preserved language processing in MCS.

We used two tasks requiring command-following. In healthy volunteers, both performed well. Command-following inferred from active versus passive oddball P300 modulation reached 81%, and the semantic oddball task reached 92%. Our visual classification rate for semantic oddball was even higher than that for oddball-based command following, which suggests that semantic oddball may be a more efficient single-task option than running passive and active oddball back-to-back.

In active oddball tasks, a likely reason for the absence of an additional increase in P300 amplitude in some participants is reduced task engagement, since P3b can be strongly attenuated or abolished when attention is directed away from the relevant stimuli 47, 48 . In our healthy sample, we could partially verify task engagement using reported counts. Most participants counted the deviants reasonably well, and only two showed large deviations from the expected number. Despite this, visual scoring detected P300 responses in every healthy participant in the oddball paradigms. Faugeras et al. 42 also reported perfect detectability in healthy participants, including P3b in all 8 participants in the local-global paradigm.

Previous work using P300-based command-following paradigms in DOC also reported high detection rates in controls: Lulé et al. 49 found 14/16 healthy controls positive (87.5%). Across the broader oddball command-following literature, detection rates in MCS patients vary depending on task design and comparison strategy. Schnakers et al. 20 reported 9/14 MCS patients with task-related active modulation. Schnakers et al. 21 reported 8/16 patients with enhanced P3 amplitude in active versus passive listening, and in the same study active counting of own-name occurrences versus passive listening identified 9/20 MCS patients. These rates are impressive given the task complexity, but other studies show that sensitivity can drop sharply with more demanding paradigms. Pokorny et al. 50 , using a dual-stream auditory attention task, reported successful classification in 8 out of 10 healthy controls but 0 out of 12 in MCS patients.

In our patient sample, we identified 2 patients with command following in the semantic oddball task. This is slightly lower than the rate reported in the only previous study using this paradigm in DOC, which reported 5/38 (Kotchoubey et al., 2005). However, we are cautious about interpreting these 2 positives as strong evidence of consciousness because neither patient showed any other significant effects, including MMN. This pattern raises the possibility of false positives.

The SON paradigm produced unexpectedly low detection rates. This was already evident at the group level, where the effect was not significant, and it translated into low single-subject detection rates in both healthy controls and MCS patients. This contrasts with prior studies. Sergent et al. 18 reported 60% (9/15) and Schnakers et al. 20 reported perfect detection rate of P300 to the patient's own name 100% (12/12) single-subject detection in healthy volunteers. In MCS patients, some groups reported perfect detection of P300 to the patient's own name: (6/6) 14 and (14/14) 20 . Meanwhile, Sergent et al. 18 reported that 50% (4/8) of MCS patients showing elevated positivity response to their own name compared with the other name. A likely explanation of our failure is that our implementation embedded the names in a conditioning design, which may have reduced the salience of the name stimulus. If the presentation of one's own name becomes predictable because it is paired with a conditioned tone, the emotional or self-referential impact may be weakened. Given the low healthy-control detectability, the current SON implementation should be redesigned and revalidated before being considered for clinical use.

Considering all paradigms, only a minority of MCS patients showed detectable higher order ERP effects, while basic auditory change detection was present in about two thirds of the sample. Such low detection rates are striking in patients with established behavioral evidence of consciousness, many of whom could follow commands. Low detection in passive P300 and N400 paradigms further shows that this discrepancy extends beyond explicit command following. Even passive tasks may exceed patients' available sensory or cognitive resources, while altered EEG responses after brain injury may make ERPs less consistent and harder to detect. A similar dissociation was reported by Bodien et al. 52 , who found no task-based fMRI or EEG response in 62% of patients with observable command following.

Our hierarchical approach was designed to probe progressively more complex processing, from automatic discrimination to semantic processing and command following. In the patient data, this hierarchy was present to some extent, but not perfectly. In most cases, lower-level responses such as MMN and passive or active oddball P300 were present in patients who also showed higher-order effects. Conversely, many patients with no MMN also showed no responses in any other paradigm. At the same time, there were exceptions. A few patients showed a semantic oddball or N400 effect without a detectable MMN or passive oddball response. In our sample, there were only three such cases with higher-order components present but without detected MMN. This means the hierarchy is informative but not absolute. Also, absence of MMN cannot be treated as strong evidence for absence of preserved mid-level sensory processing in a specific patient.

Our approach is conceptually similar to earlier efforts to probe multiple cognitive capacities in a single EEG session. Sergent et al. 18 tested several dimensions of cognition in a 1.5 h protocol. They observed heterogeneous profiles, including cases where higher-level effects appeared without lower-level effects. The advantage of a hierarchical battery is that it gives a richer cognitive profile than a single paradigm. The main weakness is that long batteries can increase fatigue and state fluctuations.

Regarding the suitability of our current battery for clinical practice, some paradigms should be improved. In addition to the SON paradigm, the passive N400 paradigm also appears substandard for single-subject clinical use. It may be replaced by a more reproducible language task. A simpler contrast such as words versus pseudowords may yield better single-subject sensitivity. Supporting this, Ben Salah et al. 53 reported that only 1 healthy control out of 13 did not show a significant ERP effect (N400 or P600).

Nearly all healthy participants showed significant effects in most paradigms with at least one of the classification methods, but the methods differed in sensitivity. ANOVA and cluster-based permutation tests yielded lower classification rates overall, which makes them more conservative than visual scoring and machine learning in our dataset. Kallionpää et al. 27 also found that cluster permutation tests were not the most sensitive approach for N400 detection in healthy subjects.

Visual scoring was the most balanced method in our data. It gave very high rates in paradigms with strong expected effects and low rates in the SON paradigm, where even the group-level effect was absent. Initial agreement between the two scorers was above 80% for all paradigms except SON, which is acceptable, but not excellent. Although disagreements were resolved in consensus meetings, this process is time-consuming and still potentially vulnerable to subjective bias.

Machine learning was the second-best method overall and showed higher detection rates than visual scoring for active-versus-passive command following in both controls and patients. This fits the broader BCI literature, where ML is often preferred for single-subject decoding because it can account for individual variability in the spatial and temporal localization of the effect. At the same time, some of our ML-positive findings may reflect false positives. For example, ML yielded relatively high SON positivity in patients despite low visual detection and weak grouplevel effects. These could be true spatially distributed effects, but they could also reflect overfitting. A further caveat concerns single-subject SON contrasts. Within each participant, SON and OFN are acoustically different words preceded by different conditioned tones, so a reliable single-subject difference can arise from stimulus-specific auditory responses rather than self-relevance; counterbalancing removes such differences only at the group level. Consistent with this, the SON-OFN difference at Pz was positive in only 7 of 17 visually SONpositive controls and 19 of 35 ML-positive controls, whereas semantic oddball positives showed the expected polarity in nearly all cases (43 of 44 visually positive and 42 of 42 MLpositive controls). Single-subject SON detections by machine learning, therefore, should not be taken as good evidence of self-name recognition without directional criteria or an acoustic control.

Our findings align with comparative studies. Herrera-Diaz et al. 54 found higher sensitivity for visual scoring than permutation-based methods in a multifeature MMN variant, at least for frequency and intensity deviants, while duration deviants reached 100% sensitivity with both methods. Gabriel et al. 29 reported MMN in 20/27 conscious subjects by visual analysis and 17/27 with a simple predefined-window t test, which is conceptually close to our ANOVA approach. Overall, no single method is optimal for all paradigms, and a combined strategy may be preferable in clinical settings.

ERP presence did not predict 6-month outcome. The small sample (14 patients regained consciousness, 8 did not, and 4 died) limited power to detect meaningful associations. Only a few studies have formally predicted outcome using ERP variables alone 8 , and larger studies have also found that P300 is not a reliable predictor 55 . Our findings extend this caution to N400 and semantic oddball. This negative finding does not mean ERPs are clinically irrelevant, but it does suggest that single binary ERP indicators, at least in a sample of this size, have limited standalone prognostic value.

Limitations

We did not explore the full multiverse of possible analyses, including time windows, and channels for every method. There are many other reasonable analytical choices, and some of our decisions were necessarily arbitrary, even though they were guided by prior ERP and DOC work.

Additional limitations include the relatively small patient sample for prognostic modeling, the fixed order of paradigms within each group, and the use of different EEG systems in controls and patients. Although we harmonized channels and preprocessing, some residual systemrelated variance is unavoidable. Finally, reducing each paradigm to a binary present versus absent classification likely discarded useful information about effect size, latency, and topography, but was necessary to keep the analysis manageable.
