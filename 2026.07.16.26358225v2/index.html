How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Introduction

Transanal Irrigation (TAI) is used to treat a range of conditions, including faecal incontinence, chronic constipation, neurogenic bowel dysfunction and low anterior resection syndrome (LARS). Evidence from randomised controlled trials and observational studies supports its clinical effectiveness (1)(2)(3)(4)(5)(6). TAI for faecal incontinence and constipation was incorporated into NICE guidelines in 2018 (7). Widely used pyramid models place TAI as a treatment escalation in patients whose symptoms persist despite conservative treatment, prior to considering surgical management (8)(9) (10). Expert guidance emphasises supervised training and structured follow-up, with the choice of device and irrigation regimen tailored to the individual (11)(12)(13). TAI use is increasing in England, with the number of patients receiving communitydispensed prescriptions rising by 72% between 2020 and 2025 (14). Despite this uptake and a variety of available systems, studies demonstrate a reluctance to initiate treatment and limited adherence at 12 months (8)(9) (10). It is hoped that a validated patient-reported experience measure (PREM) will aid healthcare professionals (HCPs) to systematically evaluate modifiable aspects of treatment and comparisons between systems, with the potential to improve adherence and support the development of new TAI devices.

The expanding range of TAI products makes device selection challenging for HCPs, who must consider patients' ability to use the device and their underlying condition.

Designing and selecting devices for home use is further complicated by users' diverse ages, abilities and home environments. Systematic reviews highlight gaps in collecting user feedback on home medical devices (11) (12). Furthermore, it has been demonstrated that many usability challenges only emerge post-market (13).

Although decision algorithms can support device selection (14)(15)(16), a PREM specifically designed to assess the user experience of TAI could further inform clinical decision-making and identify patients who may benefit from device modification or change.

As TAI is used to treat a broad range of conditions, treatment success is often measured using condition-specific patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs), such as the Cleveland Clinic Constipation score, St. Mark's incontinence score and the LARS score (17)(18) (19), or generic quality-of-life PROMs, such as the Short Form-36 (20) (21). While these instruments assess condition severity or general health status, they do not capture the user's experience of TAI. We define TAI user experience as user's perceptions of performing irrigation, including the training and support, practical and physical aspects of device use and integration into daily life.

No PROM or PREM currently exists to compare the user experience of TAI across different conditions or devices. The development and validation of a TAI-specific PREM has therefore been identified as a key research priority (6).

With active involvement from international expert clinicians and TAI users throughout, we aim to develop a PREM assessing adult's experience of TAI. The BIQ is intended to identify areas for improvement, support discussions between users and HCPs and inform service evaluation, rather than assess bowel symptom severity or treatment effectiveness. This study represents the first phase of validation, with potential future applications in decision-making, product comparison and TAI research. Interviews were conducted by a qualitative researcher (AS) and observed by two clinicians (EF TH). Two reviewers (RB TH) independently analysed transcripts to generate statements. Their findings were compared, and differences in statement wording were resolved through discussion between reviewers. The research team reviewed the resulting 215 statements, removing duplication and refining wording for clarity.

Methods

Development and early validation of the Bowel Irrigation

Stage Two: Online Delphi Survey

A Delphi consensus exercise was undertaken to integrate findings from the literature review and qualitative interviews with the perspectives of TAI users and HCPs. This was led by RB and EF who collectively bought industry and clinical experience in TAI. The online format facilitated international participation, while anonymous rating aimed to minimise the influence of professional hierarchy. The Delphi consensus exercise was not prospectively registered. TAI users aged ≥18 years or older who could independently complete an Englishlanguage online survey were eligible. TAI users were eligible after at least 3 months use, enabling inclusion of both relatively new users and long-term users. Eligible HCPs included specialist nurses, gastroenterologists and colorectal surgeons with experience initiating and troubleshooting TAI; all but one HCP had more than 10 years' experience. No numerical recruitment target was prespecified, recruitment prioritised relevant clinical expertise and lived experience. HCPs were recruited through direct outreach to relevant experts, professional referrals and existing research collaborators. The Delphi survey was pilot tested by members of the research team before distribution to participants to assess clarity of instructions, technical functionality and completion time.

A three-round online reactive Delphi survey was conducted, with participants rating items generated from Stage One. In Round One (December 2024), experts rated their agreement that each item reflected a positive user experience on a 6-point agreement scale and could provide optional free-text comments. Experts were given 10 days to complete the survey, with a reminder sent. Items reaching consensus for inclusion or exclusion were removed, while remaining items progressed to subsequent rounds. Participants received an anonymised summary of the responses from the first round and were invited to participate in the second round of the survey in January 2025 and a third round in February 2025.

Data Analysis and Consensus

Delphi survey data were analysed using Microsoft Excel. Expert responses to itemlevel agreement scales were coded into "Top 2 Box" score (T2B) (very high/ high agree), "Medium 2 Box" score (M2B) (somewhat agree/ disagree) and a "Bottom 2 Box" score (BTB) (very high/ high disagree). An item-level content validity index score was then calculated for each item as the number of experts selecting T2B relative to the total number of experts.

Items achieving ≥85% agreement overall and ≥80% within each expert cohort were included in the final BIQ, while those achieving ≤40% overall and ≤45% agreement in each cohort were excluded from subsequent voting rounds (Table 1). Free-text comments were reviewed by the research team; no formal qualitative analysis was undertaken. This process was repeated across three rounds, with minor adjustments to the inclusion and exclusion thresholds to support development of a concise and clinically practical instrument (Table 1).

Stage Three: Content Validity Assessment

Virtual cognitive interviews were conducted by a clinician (EF), with previous experience of cognitive interviewing, using a semi-structured interview guide (Appendix 3). Using the verbal probing technique, TAI users explained their responses and commented on item interpretation, wording, recall period and response options.

Interviews were conducted in two rounds of three participants, with iterative refinement until no major comprehension issues remained.

The QQ-10 was mailed to 35 TAI users, with 32 TAI users completed the BIQ, followed by the QQ-10 and repeated BIQ one to six weeks later for reliability assessment. Established TAI users were recruited from a single site (County Durham and Darlington NHS Trust). Five-point Likert (strongly disagree to strongly agree) were converted to a 0-4 scale. Scores for "value" (items 1-6; maximum 24) and "burden (items 7-10; maximum 16) were calculated by summing item scores.

Higher value scores and lower burden scores indicate greater face validity.

Stage Four: Temporal Stability Assessment

The temporal stability of the BIQ was assessed using a test-rest design. Paperbased versions of BIQ and QQ-10 were posted to participants for completion, with a reminder sent after 4 weeks. Participants were asked to complete the BIQ on two occasions, one week apart. This interval was chosen to minimise recall of previous responses while reducing the likelihood of meaningful changes in participant's experiences of TAI. The QQ-10 was completed at the initial administration only.

For item-level test-retest reliability, the sample size was based on detecting a linearly weighted kappa of 0.80 against a minimally acceptable value of 0.50, with 80% power (31). For a four-category response scale, the estimated requirement was 31 participants. Test-retest agreement was evaluated using exact agreement, agreement within one response category, Spearman's rank correlation coefficient and weighted kappa coefficients. Weighted kappa values were interpreted according to established criteria, with values above 0.80 indicating excellent agreement (32).

Patient and Public Involvement

Patients were not involved in designing the study. Individuals with experience of transanal irrigation (TAI) contributed to questionnaire development through qualitative interviews and as expert panel members in the Delphi survey, ensuring that the questionnaire reflected issues important to TAI users. Users also participated in cognitive interviews and questionnaire evaluation. took part in cognitive interviews to assess clarity and acceptability and completed face validity and test-retest reliability assessments. Study participants will be offered a plainlanguage summary of the findings and notified when the study is published.

Results

An overview of the four-stage process used to develop and validate BIQ is summarised in Figure 1.

Stage One: Item Generation

Nine participants, representing a range of professional and user perspectives on TAI, participated in semi-structured qualitative interviews. The nine participants originated from five different countries (UK, Denmark, Sweden, Ireland and the Netherlands.), five (55.6%) were female and three (33.3%) were TAI users. TAI users' diagnoses included rectocele, chronic constipation and neurogenic bowel dysfunction. Their experience of TAI ranged from 6 months to over 5 years. Analysis of these interview transcripts, supplemented by literature review, generated 215 candidate statements. These were divided into three over-arching domains: (1) product and user-related, (2) training and education, and (3) support and services, comprising 20 sub-domains (Table 2). Following iterative review, 73 statements were retained for inclusion in the Delphi survey.

Stage Two: Online Delphi Survey

Of the 21 individuals sent the survey, 20 responded to all three rounds. These 20 participants originated from five different countries (Denmark, UK, Sweden, Ireland and the Netherlands), 13 (65%) were female. Twelve were HCPs; seven specialist nurses and five physicians specialising in gastroenterology or colorectal surgery, all with experience initiating TAI across diverse diagnoses. Eight (40.0%) were TAI users with faecal incontinence, chronic constipation and neurogenic bowel and between 3 months and over 5 years' TAI experience.

After applying the inclusion and exclusion criteria (Table 1) across three rounds, 35 statements were directly included, 13 were excluded, and 25 did not meet consensus. This left 60 potential statements following three rounds of the Delphi survey. Five sub-domains (instructions for use, ordering, accessibility, additional support, and follow-up) were subsequently removed based on expert rankings, eliminating 15 statements. Further exclusion criteria based solely on user opinion were applied, with items achieving a user I-CVI of 100% retained (2 statements) and those with a user I-CVI below 45% excluded (5 statements). This process left 40 items, 19 of which had not yet reached consensus. Following an online consensus meeting, led by RB, and refinement with the research group (RB EF), 29 items remained. Similar items were then reworded and combined, resulting in a preliminary 15 item BIQ, ordered to reflect the typical patient journey through the TAI process.

Changes and reasons for retention, merging or exclusion were documented in an item-tracking table (Appendix 4).

The preliminary 15 item BIQ addressed training and support (1 item); usability (5 items), and user experience (9 items). Each of these may be prioritised depending on who is administering the BIQ, for example a clinician may prioritise user experience whereas product developers may prioritise usability. Following item generation and refinement, recall period and response options were developed for the BIQ. TAI users were asked to reflect upon the previous week and rate their agreement with statements on a 4-point Likert scale (strongly agree/agree/disagree/ strongly disagree).

Stage Three: Content Validity Assessment

Six female TAI users, with between 3 months and 9 years' experience using a range of TAI systems, completed cognitive interviews. Overall, participants considered the BIQ comprehensive without being burdensome. Minor revisions were made to item wording, format and chronology following participant feedback. A one-week recall period was considered appropriate for most items; however, responses to the training and support item consistently reflected participant's initial clinician-led training session, irrespective of how long they had been using TAI, resulting in formatting changes.

The two statements participants found the comprehension of most challenging were (1) 'I experienced no pain during irrigation" and (2) "I was able to use the irrigation product away from home". Users tended to focus on anorectal pain and did not consider abdominal pain; if they had experienced one or the other, they were then uncertain how to answer the statement. This statement was subsequently divided into (6) rectal pain and (7) abdominal pain, increasing the BIQ to 16 items. Participants generally interpreted "away from home" as another private residence, rather than public toilet, which they considered unrealistic. Despite this, the question was retained in its original form to ensure the BIQ could capture experiences in all environments outside the user's home.

Thirty-two TAI users completed the QQ-10 following completion of the BIQ, of these 30 (93.8%) were female. Quantitative assessment demonstrated positive feedback (Figure 2,3). There were high median scores for the "value" domains (median of 21 (IQR 19-22.5)). There were low median scores for the "burden" domain (median of 0 (IQR 0-2.3)), suggesting acceptability and face validity of the tool.

Stage Four: Temporal Stability Assessment

The same thirty-two TAI users completed the BIQ on two occasions between one and six weeks apart. Participants were predominantly female (93.8%), with a median age of 52 years (IQR 34-54 years). The primary indication for TAI was chronic constipation or constipation-predominant irritable bowel syndrome (93.8%).

Participants had been using TAI for a median of 9.5 months (IQR 5-17 months).

Established users with longstanding conditions were selected on the assumption that their clinical status would remain relatively stable over the assessment period. Testretest analyses were conducted using paired responses for each item. There were 16 missing response items, out of a total of 512, equating to 3.1% missing data. Missing responses were not included, test-retest analyses were conducted using all available paired observations for each item.

The results indicate a strong stability with exact agreement ranging from 83.9% to 100% across items, while agreement within one response category was 100% for all items (Table 3). Spearman's rank correlation coefficients ranged from 0.811 to 1.000, and weighted kappa coefficients ranged from 0.797 to 1.000, demonstrating strong consistency over time. The lowest reliability estimates were for Q6 and 7 ("I was free of abdominal" / "I was free of rectal pain during irrigation"), κ= 0.840 and κ=0.797 respectively. Overall, these findings provide preliminary evidence of generally high test-retest reliability for the BIQ. However, the estimates should be interpreted cautiously because clinical stability between administrations was not formally verified.

Discussion

The Bowel Irrigation Questionnaire (BIQ) is a novel PREM designed to assess the user experience of TAI (Figure 4). Expert clinicians and TAI users were involved throughout development, with priority given to the user perspective. With an increasing number of TAI devices available, a standardised assessment of user experience is needed to enable meaningful comparison between devices and support evidence-based device selection. Although created in collaboration with Qufora (Denmark), the BIQ is designed to evaluate user experience across all TAI devices and is not specific to any individual manufacturer or product. PROMS typically assess symptoms and quality of life, whereas PREMs assess the experience of care. Although PROMs and PREMs are traditionally captured separately, patients' experience and outcomes are interconnected (34)(35) (36). The BIQ reflects this relationship, with 12 statements addressing user experience and four addressing outcomes: "I felt like my bowel was emptied after irrigation", "irrigation gave me a sense of control over my life and daily activities", "I was free of abdominal pain" and "I was free of rectal pain".

During cognitive interviews, TAI users did not differentiate between experience and outcome questions and valued the opportunity to describe TAI's impact on their quality-of-life; both contribute to a comprehensive assessment of the user experience of TAI. The BIQ has been classified as a PREM because most items emphasise experience. Used alongside condition specific PROMs, the BIQ could help distinguish inadequate symptom improvement from difficulties with training or device use, informing more targeted discussion. The BIQ therefore complements rather than replaces existing PROMs.

At present the BIQ presents an item-level clinical profile organised into three conceptual sub-domains; no total or sub-domain scores are calculated. Individual responses identify strengths and difficulties to support discussion and troubleshooting between HCP and users. Further research is required to determine whether summary scores are appropriate and to establish their interpretation.

There are some limitations to consider. No a priori content validity index thresholds or predefined criteria for removing sub-domains were specified; instead, decisions were made iteratively based on quantitative rating and qualitative feedback to balance content validity and patient burden. This may have introduced subjectivity and influenced the final composition of the instrument. Delphi consensus has no universal definition; a systematic review found a median agreement threshold of 75%, with only 48.8% of studies setting a threshold a priori (33). High levels of agreement were observed throughout the Delphi process (median I-CVI was 0.75, 0.80 and 0.75 for Rounds 1-3), possibly reflecting the crossover of experts used in both the interview process and the Delphi survey. A threshold of I-CVI>0.85 was applied to limit questionnaire length. Following the final Delphi round, user consensus was given additional weight, with statements achieving 100% agreement among TAI users retained to prioritise user perspective.

The generalisability of findings is limited by the study population, which consisted predominantly of female patients with constipation using Qufora TAI equipment. The findings may not extend to males, other diagnoses or users of other systems. Sociocultural factors, including attitudes towards intimate procedures, privacy and access to facilities, may influence TAI acceptance and willingness to report difficulties. Although the Delphi panel represented three European countries, subsequent BIQ evaluation was undertaken at a single UK centre and does not establish cross-cultural or linguistic validity. Further validation in more diverse populations and settings is warranted.

Conclusion

We present the Bowel Irrigation Questionnaire (BIQ), a tool consisting of 16 items rated using a 4-point Likert scale, designed to assess the user experience of TAI.

The BIQ was developed through a structured process of item generation, refinement and preliminary testing. Preliminary evidence suggests that the instrument demonstrates good content validity and temporal stability in female patients with constipation. Further independent psychometric evaluation in broader patient populations, including men and patients with other diagnoses, and across different TAI products is required before BIQ can be recommended in clinical practice.
