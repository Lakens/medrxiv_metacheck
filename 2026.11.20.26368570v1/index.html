Reporting p values imprecisely (e.g., p < .05) reduces transparency, reproducibility, and re-use (e.g., in p value meta-analyses). Best practice is to report exact p-values with three decimal places (e.g., p = .032) unless p values are smaller than 0.001, in which case you can use p < .001.

To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 11 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Full text

Abstract

X chromosome (Xchr) contribution to complex traits remains incomplete, due to its unique inheritance pattern and its inactivation mechanism. Therefore, we gathered data from 800,000 individuals from the UK Biobank and dbGaP, and performed a sex-stratified phenome-wide association study of Xchr variation across 3,741 complex traits. Heritability analyses revealed sex-specific patterns and an overall involvement of Xchr in complex phenotypes. We identified 6,531 loci significantly associated with 402 complex traits, with a higher proportion of associations consistent with dominant inheritance than observed for autosomes. Although effect directions were largely concordant between sexes, loci with significant effect-size differences were enriched for escape-gene associations. Escape genes were depleted among trait-associated loci and strongly enriched for risky disease effects. These association patterns enabled prediction of escape status for unannotated genes. This overview of Xchr contribution to complex traits suggests inactivation escape as a clear risky feature for females.

Introduction

Commonly placed at the end of human karyotypes, the X chromosome (Xchr) is the eighth largest chromosome, accounting for nearly 5% of the genome, approximately 6% of all annotated genes, and about 3% of known human genetic variation 1, 2 . Beyond its size and gene content, the distinctive biology of this sex chromosome, together with the effects of its allele-specific inactivation process, positions its genomic analysis as a promising avenue for advancing our understanding of the genetic and molecular mechanisms underlying complex disorders 3, 4 . Genome-wide association studies (GWAS) have identified thousands of variants associated with complex traits 5, 6 . But one important gap of information affects our understanding about the role of Xchr in common diseases. Currently, only about 0.4% of all complex-trait-associated variants reported in the GWAS Catalog map to Xchr, a striking underrepresentation. This is nine times lower than expected when compared with autosomes of similar genetic variability, such as chromosome 16, which account for 4% of associations 7 . This underrepresentation could be due to the traditional exclusion of the Xchr from most GWASs because of the additional methodological costs 8, 9 . The particular pattern of inheritance of this sexual chromosome and the effects of its specific allelic inactivation processes in females usually translate into allelic imbalances between sexes and the loss of statistical power during genetic association studies 6, 10, 11 . The presence of a single copy of this chromosome in males and, effectively, also in females through allelic inactivation, requires a different analysis strategy than that applied to autosomes. To address this complexity alternative protocols and analytical strategies have been proposed to investigate the contribution of Xchr across multiple complex traits [12] [13] [14] [15] [16] . These approaches commonly include sex-stratified analyses and sex-specific genotype-coding schemes that adapt the statistical model to the distinct allele composition in males and females [12] [13] [14] . Whether this marked underrepresentation reflects a genuinely smaller contribution of Xchr to complex trait biology or, instead, results from its historical exclusion from genetic studies remains unknown 8, 9.

Beyond the methodological challenges in analyzing the contribution of Xchr in complex diseases, understanding how escape from inactivation impacts complex disease susceptibility remains an unsolved challenge 3 . Although XIST-mediated inactivation of one chromosome balances gene dosage between sexes 17, 18 , approximately 15-30% of X-linked genes escape this silencing [19] [20] [21] [22] . Such escape is expected to generate sex-biased transcriptional profiles in different tissues and cell types 19, 23, 24 , that have been used to systematically classify X-linked genes according to their escape or inactive status 21, 25, 26 . Beyond dosage effects, escape from Xchr inactivation (XCI) has also been proposed as a contributor to sex-differential susceptibility in complex phenotypes such as cancer and autism 27, 28.

Despite multiple efforts to classify Xchr genes as subject to escaping or inactivation, the condition for more than 1,483 genes, according to GENECODE 29 , is inconclusive 19, 26 . The creation of classification models were basing their decisions so far on multiple genomic elements surrounding the test gene, such as regulatory elements and repeats, which are correlated with escape events [30] [31] [32] . Apart from the assessment of expression and the genomic context, no other features, such as the relationship with disease, have been explored to achieve the classification of Xchr genes.

In this study, we aim to characterize the genetic contribution of Xchr to complex diseases and clinical traits, while bridging the existing gap in understanding how X-linked genetic architecture and X-inactivation influence disease risk. Leveraging UK Biobank and dbGaP resources for nearly 800 thousand individuals, we perform a sex-stratified phenome-wide association analysis of the non-PAR Xchr region across 3,741 complex traits. We quantify and characterize the contribution of Xchr to these diseases at the level of heritability and associated loci. Beyond this global characterization, we also investigate how X-inactivation and escape influence genetic association and risk, and test whether these disease-associated patterns can distinguish gene classes using a machine learning framework. The resulting insights refine our understanding of X-chromosome inheritance, clarify its role in complex disease risk, and open avenues for more precise sex-specific prevention and therapeutic strategies.

Results

Overall strategy

To study the role of the non-pseudoautosomal (non-PAR) region of Xchr in complex traits, we aggregated and harmonized genetic and phenotypic data from 792,968 individuals (428,493 females and 364,475 males; Supplemental Table 1) sourced from dbGaP and the UK Biobank (UKBK) and applied non-PAR Xchr-specific quality control 16 (Methods). Leveraging five genetic reference panels https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?egaJ5G5, 33 (Methods), we imputed 418,231 as average across cohort and 469,009 specifically on the UKBK of high-quality Xchr variants (INFO≥0.7; Supplemental Table 2), comprising single-nucleotide variants, small insertions/deletions, and larger structural rearrangements 1 , which served as the basis for downstream genetic analyses (Figure 1). We curated the phenotypic data by filtering case and control subjects for binary disease traits. In total, 3,741 complex traits passed these filters, including 3,387 diseases and 354 quantitative disease-related clinical measures. From these, 63 and 24 are female and male-exclusive phenotypes, respectively (Supplemental Table 3). Using sex-specific X-chromosome genotype coding, we performed sex-stratified phenome-wide association studies (PheXWAS) with REGENIE 34 , evaluating additive, dominant, and recessive models in females, and a hemizygous model in males (Methods). To complement sex-stratified analysis, we also meta-analyzed the results. The resulting associations were then used to investigate the contribution of Xchr to complex phenotypes by comparing its heritability estimates with all chromosomes, and by identifying and characterizing specific X-linked locus-gene-phenotype associations.

Xchr contribution to complex traits

Within the UKBK cohort, we first calculated heritability metrics for all chromosomes separately for females (h 2 F ) and males (h 2 M ), across 3,663 complex traits with at least 5,000 individuals, using linkage disequilibrium score regression 35 (LDSC; Methods). Overall, considering both sexes, we detected significant heritability (q ≤ 0.05) in at least one chromosome for 76% (2,780) of traits. On average, each chromosome contributed significantly to 37% of the traits, whereas Xchr showed a significant contribution (h² X ) to 33% (929; Supplemental Table 4; Supplemental Figure 1). Estimates of h² X varied substantially across traits, with an average of 0.002 and a range from 0.0003 to 0.0409. Beyond the overall h² X , sex-stratified h² X distributions were comparable between females and males on their respective significant phenotypes (median F = 0.0017; median M = 0.0018; Mann-Whitney U p = 0.93; Supplemental Figure 2), although sex-specific patterns emerged across phenotype categories built on our data for better interpretability. In females, the highest median h² X estimates were found for diseases classified under "Nervous system", "Digestive system" and "Musculoskeletal/connective". In contrast, in males, the highest median h² X contributions were found within the "Nervous system", "Digestive system" and "Mental disorders" (Figure 2A; Supplemental Figure 3). Across significantly associated phenotypes in both sexes, h² X was consistently lower in males than females (median ratios male-to-female = 0.87; paired-samples t-test corrected p = 4.4x10⁻¹¹; Figure 2B; Supplemental Figure 4). In the disease category, 7 of 12 showed higher h² X in females, with the largest difference in "Endocrine system" , whereas "Abnormal clinical findings" exhibited the largest difference in the opposite direction (Figure 2C).

To understand the contribution of Xchr relative to autosomes, we defined Chromosome Relative Involvement (CRI) as the percentage of total heritability (h² T ) attributable to each chromosome, normalized by their corresponding number of imputed variants (Supplemental Table 5, Supplemental Figure 5). Within both sexes, the median CRI X is significantly lower than the median CRI A , calculated from the distribution of per-autosome values (median CRI X = 5.3%; median CRI A = 8%; Mann-Whitney U p = 2.4x10⁻³¹; Supplemental Figure 6), ranking 6th in females and 2nd in males from the tail in contribution (Figure 3A). Furthermore, CRI X values were approximately half of those derived from chromosomes with similar genetic variability, such as chromosome 16 (medians CRI X = 5.3%; medians CRI16 = 11.9%; Brunner-Munzel test p X,16 = 2x10⁻⁴⁵).

Building on the comparison of X-chromosome contribution to that of the autosomes, we next examined how X-chromosome contribution to disease is distributed across disease categories. The highest median CRI X in males was observed for "Nervous system" , "Genitourinary system", and "Abnormal clinical findings", whereas in females the highest values were observed for "Respiratory system" , "Diseases of the blood" and "Skin/subcutaneous tissue" (Figure 3B). Interestingly, 34 diseases exhibited heritability entirely attributable to Xchr (CRI X = 100%), including "osteopathies in diseases classified elsewhere" (M90), "coagulation defects and inflammatory disease of the cervix uteri" (N72) in females; as well as "secondary malignant neoplasm of kidney and renal pelvis" (C79.0) and "Inflammatory polyneuropathy" (G61) in males (Supplemental Figure 7).

Identification of X chromosome trait associated loci

Beyond global heritability measures, we next investigated the involvement of Xchr in complex traits at the level of significantly associated loci. These loci were extracted from the PheXWAS results across all tested complex traits using a p-value threshold derived from n-of-1 permutation test 36 (Methods). For each female associated locus and disease, we selected the most likely associated inheritance model by prioritizing that with the lowest p-value (Figure 1). Under these criteria, we identify 205,638 significant variant-phenotype associations mapping to 6,531 unique independent loci, (r² > 0.8) across 402 complex traits (Figure 4A; Methods; Supplemental Table 6). A fraction of associations were discovered in coinciding phenotypes between UKBK and dbGaP, yielding a substantial cross-cohort replication (79% and 80%, respectively; Supplemental Table 7 8). Consistent with the allelic composition of the Xchr and the process of XCI in females, a substantial fraction (27%) of associations was best explained by the dominant inheritance model, showing approximately 1.6-fold more than previously observed for autosomes 15 . The recessive model explains 7% of the final results, 1.6-fold less than shown for autosomal variants (Figure 4B). In addition, we observed an important variation in the number of associated loci between phenotypes, with 21 traits accumulating more than 50 loci (Supplemental Figure 8). Among females, "mean platelet (thrombocyte) volume" and "menarche" showed the highest numbers of associated loci, with 233 and 129, respectively. Among males, "hair/balding pattern 4", consistent with heritability results, and "testosterone levels" were associated with 297 and 128 loci, respectively (Figure 4A).

We next examined sex-specific patterns in greater detail for non-sex-exclusive traits 37 (Methods). Significant associated loci were classified into five categories according to their patterns of association across sexes: sex-shared (no differences in association or effect), sex-differential (significantly different effect sizes in the same direction), sex-opposite (opposite directions of effect between sexes), female-specific (association detected only in females), and male-specific (association detected only in males). 39% of classifiable loci were shared between female and male, whereas the majority (61%) showed different behaviours between sexes. Specifically, 25.3% and 17% exhibited female-and male-specific association signals, respectively. Among the remaining loci, 18% showed significantly different effect sizes between sexes while retaining the same direction of effect. Only 9 loci (0.3%) with different effect sizes displayed opposite directions between sexes (Figure 4C-D; Supplemental Figure 9).

Functional implications of Xchr-associated genes

To evaluate the functional landscape of Xchr-associated loci, we first mapped all 6,531 significant loci to 601 unique genes using colocalization analyses against cis-eQTL summaries statistics, complemented with gene proximity (Methods; Supplemental Table 9). These represent 28% of all genes in the Xchr, including coding, non-coding and microRNAs (Supplemental Table 10). A functional enrichment analysis of these trait-associated genes by considering both sexes together (Methods) shows 8 significant enriched functions (Supplemental Table 11), from which the top 3 correspond to "toll-like receptor 8 signaling pathway", "neurexin family protein binding" and "Protein-protein interactions at synapses". When stratifying the enrichment analyses by the genes mapped to sex-specific loci in the non-sex-exclusive phenotypes, we found only in males 35 significantly enriched pathways, including "cellular response to testosterone stimulus" and "Postsplicing complex" (Supplemental Table 12; Methods).

To evaluate whether Xchr preferably contributes to complex traits through specific tissues, we performed an enrichment analysis against expression profiles of 54 GTEx tissues (Methods; Supplemental Table 13). 27 tissues showed significant enrichment, with brain regions (e.g. hypothalamus, amygdala and caudate-basal ganglia) occupying the top four positions and accounting for 10 of the 27 (37%; Supplemental Figure 10). This pattern is consistent with the "Nervous system" phenotype category showing the largest h² X contribution across trait categories (Supplemental Figure 3).

Impact of chromosome X inactivation status in complex trait

To investigate the relationship between XCI status and phenotype susceptibility, we first characterized XCI patterns across all genes in Xchr identified as significantly associated with complex traits in females. Using multiple independent annotations 38 , we classified 563 genes into 275 inactive, 105 escapee, and 183 with uncertain XCI status (Supplemental Table 14, Methods). Genes with uncertain status were excluded from subsequent comparisons. We observed that escape genes accounted for only 25% of all loci with a trait association, compared with 39% of non-disease-related loci, indicating a significant underrepresentation of escapees among trait-association loci (χ² test p = 3×10⁻¹²⁷; Methods; Figure 5A). As expected the complementary trend was observed for inactive genes. Consistent with this depletion, the same was observed when using independent annotations from the GWAS Catalog with 29% of escapees mapping to trait-associated loci versus 39% mapping to non-disease loci (χ² test p = 0.02).

Functionally, trait-associated escapees and inactive genes were significantly enriched in 5 and 51 pathways, respectively, with no overlap between the two sets (Supplemental Table 15; Methods). Escapee-enriched pathways included "Estrogen metabolism" and "The activation of arylsulfatases", whereas inactive-gene pathways included "Nucleotide metabolism" and "Ectoderm differentiation". We next examined whether escapee loci were disproportionately concentrated in specific phenotype categories (Figure 5B; Supplemental Table 16). Escapee loci were present in 28 of 29 categories, but only three categories contained more escapee loci than expected (expected proportion = 36.5%), most notably "Ear and mastoid" (72%; two-proportion z-test q = 3x10⁻⁷) and "Mineral metabolism" (50%; two-proportion z-test q = 2x10⁻⁴). Conversely, 17 categories were enriched for inactive loci relative to expectation (63.5%), including "Eye and adnexa" (97%; two-proportion z-test q = 2×10⁻⁴) and "mental and behavioural traits" (94%; two-proportion z-test q = 1×10⁻⁴).

We next investigated the magnitude and direction of the genetic effects associated within escape and inactive genes. Overall, effect sizes were significantly larger at escapee-associated loci, with a 1.04-fold increase in median effect size relative to inactive genes (Wilcoxon rank-sum test p = 0.0003; Supplemental Figure 11). The expected impact of the biallelic expression of escape genes on effect sizes becomes more evident when we evaluate the distribution and enrichment of escape genes across associations with different patterns of sex-specificity (Supplemental Table 17). Whereas the proportion of escape genes among sex-shared associations closely matched expectations (observed = 24% vs expected = 25%; Figure 5C; two-proportion z-test p = 0.88), sex-differential loci showed a significant enrichment of escapee associations (observed = 44% vs expected = 25%; Figure 5C; two-proportion z-test p = 6x10⁻¹⁵), which showed effect sizes , on average, 2-fold larger in females than in males (Figure 5D; Pearson's test p = 1x10⁻¹⁰²). None of these escapee associations displayed larger effect sizes in males than in females. Consistent with escapee genes largely being involved in female-specific biology, we observe that female-exclusive phenotypes were enriched for escapee genes (observed = 41% vs expected = 25%; Figure 5C; two-proportion z-test p = 1x10⁻⁷), in contrast to associations identified in male-exclusive phenotypes, which showed the opposite trend (2%; Figure 5C; two-proportion z-test p = 1x10⁻³⁶).

We next asked in which direction loci associated with escape genes influence female health. Classifying disease associations as risk (β > 0) or protective (β < 0), on the GWAS catalog, the autosomes accounted for 52% of risky and 48% protective associations. When comparing escapees and inactive with these background proportions, escapees are biased towards risky associations (70% risk vs 30% protective; two-proportion z-test p = 0.02), while inactive loci showed non-significant differences between the risky and protective categories, with 53% and 47%, respectively (two-proportion z-test p = 0.83; Figure 5E; Supplemental Table 18). GWAS Catalog showed the same pattern, with risk associations outnumbering protective ones for escapees genes in comparison with the autosomes (69% vs 31%; two-proportion z-test p = 0.002), but not for inactive genes (risk = 57% vs protect = 43%; two-proportion z-test p =0.25). When we stratified by effect direction, escapee and inactive associations showed comparable absolute effect sizes between protective and risky sets. (Figure 5F).

To identify potential sex biases towards the disease risk associated with gene inactivation status, we analysed disease associations showing different effects between sexes: the non-sex-shared category (sex-differential and female-specific together). Consistent with our findings, escape genes accounted for more risky associations than protective ones (risky = 64% vs protective = 36%). Unexpectedly, associations involving inactive genes showed the opposite pattern, with a strong predominance of protective over risk-increasing associations (protective = 89% vs risky = 11%; binomial test p = 0.0007; Supplemental Table 19).

Chromosome X gene state prediction

Finally, to evaluate whether the differences in disease-association patterns between inactive and escape genes were sufficiently large to enable reliable discrimination between the two classes, we trained an XGBoost 39 classification model using female-related association summary statistics, including association p-values, effect sizes, and standard errors, among others (Methods). 308 mapped genes to trait associations loci were retained for classification (220 for training and 88 for testing). Model performance was successful on the held-out test set, with 69 of 88 (78%) classified genes. The model correctly assigned 49 genes, including 45 inactive and 4 escape genes, corresponding to a weighted precision of 89% and weighted recall of 71% (Methods; Supplemental Figure 12). When applied to genes, for which the inactivation or escape status of the targeted gene remains unknown, we could classify a total of 618 lead variant associations targeting 158 genes, where 89 and 50 were labeled as inactive and escape, respectively (Supplemental Table 20).

Feature importance analysis indicated that the discrimination between escapee and inactivation status was driven predominantly by the sex-difference state of the signals and their inheritance model information (Figure 6B; Supplemental Table 21). Inactive patterns were mostly characterised by also showing association in males' and by the recessive inheritance model, whereas escape genes were mostly associated with the additive and dominant ones (Figure 6C; Supplemental Figure 13). Consistent with the observed link between escape and non-recessive inheritance, loci classified under the dominant and the additive models were significantly enriched for escape loci with a 33% and 31%, respectively (expected = 25%; dominant two-proportion z-test p = 3x10⁻⁸; additive two-proportion z-test p = 1x10⁻¹¹; Figure 6D). These findings highlight the distinct sex-specific genetic signatures that differentiate escapee from inactive gene states.

Limitations

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting our findings. Although our study leverages one of the largest Xchr resources to date, sample size still limits the full characterization of its contribution to complex phenotypes, and larger cohorts will further improve signal resolution. Our analyses were restricted to non-ultrarare genotyped variants, and imputation constraints reduce the ability to capture rare and structural variation, which likely plays an important role in Xchr architecture. Current escape and inactive annotations enabled quantification of X-inactivation escape effects, yet a more complete and accurate classification will be essential to refine their contribution. Methodological constraints also limited escape-status prediction, leaving 67 genes unclassified; integrating complementary functional data may enhance future inference. Despite these limitations, this work represents the most comprehensive assessment of Xchr involvement in complex disease and highlights escape from X-inactivation as a widespread and potentially protective feature of Xchr biology.

Discussion

In this study, we provide a population-scale characterization of the contribution of the non-PAR region of Xchr to complex traits by integrating sex-stratified genetic analyses across nearly 800,000 individuals and 3,867 complex phenotypes. In contrast with previous studies 3, 4, 7 , our results show that Xchr contributes to complex disease architecture to a comparable extent to that of autosomes of similar size. This suggests that its historical underrepresentation in GWAS mainly reflects systematic exclusion from studies rather than a reduced biological contribution.

Heritability measurements show an implication of Xchr across a broad range of disease categories, with sex-specific differences emerging for particular phenotypic domains, including nervous, circulatory, respiratory, and congenital diseases. Although theoretical models assuming shared causal effects between sexes predict higher heritability in males than in females 3, 4 , our empirical analyses reveal a disease-dependent relation. In median, female heritability estimates exceed those in males, consistent with the presence of sex-differentiated effects that modulate the expected variance relationship. These observations suggest that deviations from the shared-effects model, driven by sex-specific genetic architecture, differential regulation or escape patterns, substantially shape Xchr heritability across complex traits.

We also identified thousands of independent X-linked associations across hundreds of traits, providing new genetic biomarkers and confirming the wide functional impact of this chromosome. The enrichment of dominant inheritance patterns among X-linked associations agrees with the XCI and the effective allelic composition in both sexes. Importantly, even though effect directions were generally aligned between sexes, sex-diferential loci associated with escape genes displayed stronger female effects, consistent with their increased dosage. Our analyses further reveal the relationship between XCI escape and complex trait susceptibility.

Escape genes were less frequently associated with disease loci than expected based on their genomic abundance, but when involved, they showed an enrichment for risky associations.

These results suggest that escape from XCI contributes to increasing the risk of disease in females, which in turn could explain their underrepresentation among disease genes.

The distinct trait-association patterns between escape and inactive genes also enabled the development of a ML model to classify XCI status from genetic association features, allowing the annotation of previously unresolved X-linked genes. Previous studies have shown that long interspersed nuclear elements (LINEs), mammalian-wide interspersed repeats (MIRs), Alu elements, short ACG/CGT-containing motifs, and additional regulatory features enriched near transcription start sites can distinguish inactive from escape regions on Xchr 30, 31 . Our study, based on complex-trait association patterns, extends and complements these observations. These results establish inactivation escape as an important factor of female-specific genetic architecture and encourage the incorporation of escape biology into future studies of complex disease, sex differences, and precision medicine.

Methods

UK Biobank cohort

UK Biobank is a large-scale biomedical database with 500,000 participants, most of them of European ancestry 40 . This study was performed based on the information from the last update of the cohort in 2023, and this cohort comprises individuals aged between 50-90 years which were recruited in diverse regions of the United Kingdom between 2006 and 2010. Study variables were derived from multiple sources, including hospital inpatient records, self-reported questionnaires, nurse-led interviews, clinical assays of biological samples, anthropometric measurements, and genotype array data.

From 2,431 available fields, 501 were manually selected and curated. Fields were categorized by UKBK as binary, categorical (single or multiple), or quantitative, and subsequently, we harmonized them into either disease outcomes (binary format) or disease-related clinical measurements (quantitative format). Categorical variables with an inherent ordinal structure were treated as quantitative measurements. Quantitative variables were transformed for the analysis by applying Rank Inverse Normal Transformation. Remaining categorical variables were either one-hot encoded when no control category was defined or binarized by contrasting each case category against a designated control. For each binary trait, we required a minimum of 100 cases in at least one of the two sexes. For traits belonging to a tree-structured class, controls were restricted to individuals without a diagnosis of the target disease or any other disease within the same branch. Finally, for binary traits, a minimum case-control ratio of 1:100 was maintained. When the ratio was lower, the oldest controls were selected until the desired ratio was achieved. This processing yielded 3,386 disease outcomes and 354 disease-related quantitative traits for downstream analyses. dbGaP cohorts A total of 46 cohorts covering 36 different complex traits were initially selected and downloaded from the database of Genotypes and Phenotypes (dbGaP) under their respective project identifier (Supplemental Table 1). A trait was determined to be analyzable when, considering all cohorts for that trait, genotype and phenotype data were available for at least 10,000 individuals with at least 100 cases. For each cohort, an initial step of data cleaning and formatting was applied in order to obtain genotypic information in PLINK format 41 , keeping those individuals for whom both genetic and phenotypic information was available. As in UKBK, we maintain a minimum case-control ratio of 1:100, in case the ratio was lower, we selected the oldest controls until we achieve the desired ratio.

Genetic quality control

A standard quality control was performed, both at the sample and at the variant level 16 . At the sample level, individuals were excluded based on the following filters: (i) genotype missingness ≥ 0.05; and (ii) ancestry outliers, which were identified using the 1000 Genomes Project reference panel 42 and the smartPCA software 43 . At the variant level, variants fulfilling the following criteria were removed: (i) genotype missingness ≥ 0.05; (ii) minor allele frequency (MAF) ≤ 0.01; and (iii) Hardy-Weinberg disequilibrium (p<1e-20). A tailored quality control pipeline specific for the requirements of Xchr was implemented, consisting of filtering based on MAF ( ≤ 0,01 ) and genotype missingness (≥ 5%). These filters were performed on males and females separately. Then, variants in Hardy-Weinberg disequilibrium (p < 1e-20) were filtered out in non-affected female samples. Finally, variants that showed differences in frequency and genotype missingness between non-affected males and females (p < 1e-06) were excluded (Supplemental Table 2).

Phasing and imputation

Xchr imputation across 47 cohorts was performed using GUIDANCE 15 , an integrated framework capable of conducting haplotype phasing and genotype imputation using one or multiple reference panels. First, the phasing step was performed with Shapeit(v2) 44 in dbGaP and Shapeit(v4) 44 in UKBB. Then, imputation was performed using Impute(v2) 45 . Importantly, due to the large size of the UKBB and the computational resources required for its analysis, data was split into chunks of 1,000,000bp. Both phasing and imputation were conducted using five reference panels; https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?iu45GZ000G phase3 https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?QOeu14 , GCAT 1 , HRC 46 , GoNL https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?CVW8xk and UK10K 48 . The results obtained from the different panels were merged following the strategy in Alonso et al., 2021 33 , thus keeping the variants with the best info score obtained across all panels, and filtering the variants with MAF > 0.1%.

The resulting dataset for the association analysis consisted of genetic variants with an info imputation score ≥ 0.7 and MAF > 0.1% (Supplemental Table 2). This oxford format was converted into plink format files (hard-call-threshold = 0.9) using QCTOOLV2 49.

Association analyses

Sex-stratified genome-wide association analyses (GWAS) were performed separately for each complex phenotype using Firth logistic regression from REGENIE 34 . For association analyses of binary traits, the first 7 genetic ancestry principal components (PCs) were included as covariates. For quantitative disease-related measurements, age was included in addition to the same 7 PCs. In females, three inheritance models (additive, recessive, and dominant) were tested. To enhance statistical power, summary statistics from both sexes were subsequently analysed under the assumption of independence using a fixed-effects meta-analysis implemented in GWAMA 50 . p-value threshold estimation N-of-1 permutation test thresholds were applied to PheXWAS and meta-analysis summary statistics from UK Biobank 36 . The 5% false discovery rate (FDR) thresholds were estimated after stratifying by sex, inheritance model (additive, recessive, dominant), phenotype type (binary or quantitative), and allele frequency (Supplemental Table 22).

For dbGaP cohorts, Bonferroni correction was applied. The number of independent loci was estimated after linkage disequilibrium pruning of imputed data using PLINK 41 (r² = 0.8), accounting for both common and rare variants. Approximately 200,000 independent loci were obtained. To obtain a 95% confidence level, the significance threshold was calculated by dividing 0.05 by the number of independent loci. The resulting p-value threshold corresponded to 2.5×10⁻⁷.

Heritability measures estimation

LD scores were computed with LD Score Regression(LDSC) 35 following the Pan-UK Biobank guidelines 51 , stratifying by chromosome and sex. SNP-based heritability (h²) was estimated using LDSC 35 on UKBK phenotypes with more than 5,000 individuals, using summary statistics from the additive model and restricting to genetic variants with MAF ≥ 0.01 outside the HLA region. Estimates were filtered using criteria consistent with Pan-UK Biobank, retaining h² values between 0 and 1 (accounting for standard errors) , λ GC > 0.9, and ratio < 0.3. A Z-test was used to assess whether h² differed from zero, and p-values were adjusted using the Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) method. Sex differences in non-sex-exclusive phenotypes were evaluated using a two-sample Z-test (Wald Z-test), with FDR correction applied to account for multiple testing.

Independent loci definition

For each phenotype, all genome-wide significant variants identified across the three GWAS analytical approaches (males, females, and meta-analysis) were aggregated.. These variants were subsequently clustered based on linkage disequilibrium using an r² threshold ≥ 0.8 with PLINK 41 . Each resulting linkage disequilibrium cluster was defined as an independent association signal and the variant with the lowest p-value was designated as the lead variant.

Replication between UKBB and dbGaP cohorts

Phenotypes were manually harmonized between UK Biobank and dbGaP (Supplemental Table 7). Genome-wide significant loci identified in each cohort were tested for replication in the reciprocal cohort. Replication was defined as nominal significance (p < 0.05) for the same variant or for a proxy variant in linkage disequilibrium (r² ≥ 0.2).

Sex-specific effect-size differences analysis

To assess the impact of sex on genetic associations, independent signals were classified into five categories, following the guidelines described in Khramtsova et al. 37 . First, colocalization analysis 52 was performed to evaluate whether signals were shared between sexes, selecting the hypothesis with the highest posterior probability ( ≥ 0.7). Signals assigned to hypothesis 4 were considered shared (PP.H4 ≥ 0.7). Signals classified under hypothesis 1 or 2 were defined as male-specific (PP.H1 ≥ 0.7) or female-specific (PP.H2 ≥ 0.7), respectively.

Once an independent association was identified in both sexes, the equality of its lead variant effects was tested using a statistical test 53 . After correcting for multiple testing using the Benjamini-Hochberg method by type of phenotype (binary or quantitative), signals with equal effects were classified as sex-shared effect (q-value ≥ 0.3). Signals with different effects (q-value ≤ 0.05) were further divided into sex-differential or sex-opposite categories, depending on whether the effect direction was the same or opposite, respectively.

Gene mapping

Colocalization analysis 52 was performed between each independent GWAS signal and cis-eQTLs from GTEx v8 54, 55 . Loci were defined as the signal's variants ±1 Mb. Each signal was tested for every X-chromosome gene and GTEx tissue against the sex-stratified eQTL summary statistics (female, male, or sex-combined) matching the sex class of the GWAS signal. Sex-specific tissues were analysed with the corresponding single-sex data. Effect sizes, variances, allele frequencies, sample sizes, and trait standard deviations from both datasets were used. Signals with a posterior probability of a shared causal variant (PP.H4) > 0.7 were considered colocalized. In cases where multiple genes satisfied the colocalization criterion, the colocalized gene closest to the lead variant was retained as the target gene. If no gene met this threshold, the gene nearest to the lead variant.

Gene annotation

Gene inactivation status was curated from a supplemental table compiling annotations from up to five independent studies 19, 26, 38, 54, 56 . For each gene, the consensus inactivation status was assigned by majority vote across the studies reporting on that gene. In cases of a tie, the gene was classified as "escape." Genes classified as escape were further subdivided into "facultative escape" and "constitutive escape": a gene was annotated as facultative escape if at least one of the contributing studies reported it as such, and as constitutive escape otherwise.

Background escapees loci definition

All imputed genetic variants located on the non-pseudoautosomal region (non-PAR) of Xchr were pruned for linkage disequilibrium using PLINK 41 , with an r² threshold of 0.8 and a window size of 150 kb. A representative variant was thereby selected from each independent locus. Each representative variant was then annotated for linkage disequilibrium (r² ≥ 0.2) with any variant previously associated with a phenotype, using both our own association results and the GWAS Catalog 7 . Finally, loci were mapped to their closest gene through their selected variants; in cases where a gene was already implicated through colocalization in our association analyses, that gene assignment was added to that representative variant. Through this approach, each locus was classified into one of three categories: associated with our phenotypes, associated to GWAS Catalog phenotypes, or not associated with any phenotype. Moreover, each locus was annotated with the phenotype(s) with which it was associated with our XWAS analyses.

Gene prediction model

The input dataset was prepared by removing significant associations that were not relevant to females. Specifically, associations classified as male-specific, those for male-exclusive phenotypes, and those lacking statistical power (female p-value ≥ 0.05) were excluded. Only signals located within 150 kb of the gene of interest, mapping to a single gene, and mapping to inactive or constitutive escapee genes were retained, resulting in 3,093 observations. For each association, the input consisted of the female PheXWAS summary statistics across all inheritance models, the corresponding male results, and the sex-differential analyses, together with one-hot encoded categorical features describing the sex-differential classification, the between-sex colocalization result, and the inheritance model. To reduce overfitting and mitigate class imbalance, a balanced set of associations was created and subsequently split into 70% training and 30% testing. Moreover, the rest of inactive associations excluded from the balancing step were added to the test set, but to prevent data leakage, associations mapped to genes present in the train set were excluded from the test set. The dataset sizes were 1,265 and 157 associations in the train and test sets, respectively. In addition, 631 associations targeted genes with unknown X-inactivation status.

An extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost) 39 classifier was used to predict X-inactivation status. Its hyperparameter optimization was performed using a 5-fold cross-validation within a grid-search strategy over the following parameters: learning rate (0.01, 0.1, 0.2), maximum tree depth (2, 3, 4, 5), number of estimators (1-100), and subsample ratio (0.8, 1.0). The model selection was based on classification accuracy and the optimal hyperparameter configuration was: learning rate = 0.2, maximum depth = 4, number of estimators = 90, and subsample ratio = 0.8. The trained model was evaluated in the test set. Because multiple associations could map to the same gene, predictions were aggregated at the gene level with a majority-voting strategy. The X-inactivation state assigned to each gene corresponded to the class most frequently supported across associations.

The trained model was then applied to predict the X-inactivation status of all associations passing the filters described above. This enabled comparison of model predictions with existing literature for genes with a known state, and assignment of a new inactivation status to genes that previously lacked one (Supplemental Table 20).

Feature importance was initially assessed using the importance scores generated by the XGBoost model. Then, to further characterize feature contributions and improve model interpretability, SHapley Additive exPlanations 58 values were calculated for all features. SHAP analyses were used to quantify the contribution of individual variables to model predictions at both the global and local levels, comparing feature contributions between correctly and incorrectly classified associations in the test set.
