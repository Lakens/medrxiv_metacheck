Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Chronic tinnitus, the persistent perception of phantom sound, affects 14 to 15% of adults yet lacks a validated neurophysiological biomarker, in part because group-average EEG analyses obscure the individual heterogeneity that defines the condition. In a multi-site case-control study of 544 participants (268 with tinnitus, mean age 45.4 ± 16.1 years, 52% female) across seven independent international sites, we applied normative modelling to quantify how far each person's resting-state EEG deviates from age-and hearing-adjusted healthy norms. Fusing spectral-power and connectivity deviations, a classifier detected tinnitus with an area under the curve of 0.832 under site-grouped cross-validation of precomputed normative features. The single most discriminative feature, rank-stable across all seven sites, was theta-band right orbitofrontal (pars orbitalis) connectivity, which dominated attribution by roughly an order of magnitude. Theta hyperconnectivity spanned frontoparietal and dorsal attention networks while primary auditory cortex showed negligible involvement, favouring failed top-down gating over bottom-up auditory overactivity. Features identifying tinnitus presence were orthogonal to symptom severity. These findings reframe chronic tinnitus as a frontoparietal attention-network disorder and nominate right orbitofrontal theta hyperconnectivity as a site-robust diagnostic feature. Data were collected within the TIDE project (ClinicalTrials.gov NCT06520865).

I. INTRODUCTION

Tinnitus, the persistent conscious perception of sound in the absence of an external acoustic source, affects approximately 14 to 15% of adults worldwide, with around 2% experiencing symptoms severe enough to impair daily functioning [1]. It is among the most prevalent chronic sensory complaints and carries elevated rates of depression, anxiety, sleep disturbance, and cognitive decline [2]. Following international consensus [3], "tinnitus" (the percept alone) is distinguished from "tinnitus disorder" (the associated distress or functional disability). No symptom-modifying treatment exists, and available approaches target secondary distress rather than the underlying neural disturbance [4], [5]. A central reason for this stagnation is the absence of a validated, objective neural biomarker: without a reliable brain-based marker of the phantom sound percept, patient stratification, target identification, and treatment monitoring remain tied to subjective measures [6], [1], [7].

Chronic tinnitus is increasingly understood as an emergent property of large-scale network reorganisation rather than a localised auditory-cortex pathology [8], [6]. Peripheral deafferentation triggers maladaptive plasticity, from altered thalamocortical synchrony [9] to aberrant top-down gating by frontal circuits that normally suppress persistent, low-salience signals [10], [11], and resting-state studies report hyperconnectivity among the auditory, default-mode, and attention networks [12], [8], [13]. These accounts converge on a distributed, top-down disturbance rather than a single locus, but the bulk of previous data in humans has not been robustly replicated, and no single approach exists to detect tinnitus at the individual level. Supervised classifiers applied to band power, source-space oscillatory features, and deep contrastive representations can distinguish the tinnitus brain state from controls with high reported accuracy: a linear support-vector machine separated 129 patients from 142 controls at 94% test accuracy [14], and a multi-band contrastive deep network achieved subject-independent diagnosis across 187 patients and 80 controls [15]. These estimates derive almost exclusively from single-site, within-cohort validation, however, where shared hardware, participant pool, and noise covariance inflate apparent performance beyond what transfers to new sites [16]. Two structural limitations therefore prevent existing classifiers from serving as clinical biomarkers.

The first is cross-site generalisation: differences in amplifier hardware, montage, room acoustics, and operator protocol introduce batch effects that can rival the tinnitus signal itself [17], and although harmonisation methods such as ComBat partially correct them, including for restingstate EEG features specifically [18], no EEG tinnitus classifier has been validated under held-out site conditions. The second is neurobiological heterogeneity: patients vary widely in tinnitus laterality, chronicity, associated hearing loss, and psychological burden. These dimensions are weakly correlated and likely reflect distinct pathophysiology [19], [1], yet conventional casecontrol analyses estimate a single average difference and discard the individual variation needed for subtyping [20]. Normative modelling offers a principled alternative. It characterises a healthy reference distribution to yield per-subject deviation scores relative to age-and hearing-corrected norms [20], [21]. Applied to resting-state EEG, it has mapped individual source-connectivity deviations in Parkinson's disease that scale with clinical severity [22], localised epileptogenic tissue in focal epilepsy [23], [24], and, most directly analogous here, predicted antidepressant response from individual EEG-connectivity deviations [25]. It has not yet been extended to tinnitus.

Here we address both limitations by applying normative modelling to resting-state EEG in a multi-site case-control design of 544 participants (268 chronic tinnitus, 276 controls) across seven independent international sites in the TIDE consortium [26]. The TIDE protocol additionally acquired task paradigms, including auditory oddball and gap pre-pulse inhibition of acoustic startle (GPIAS) recordings, the present work focuses on the resting-state EEG, with the task paradigms analysed in companion papers. Individual deviation scores, corrected for age, sex, and hearing thresholds, were estimated from controls alone under leave-onesite-out (LOSO) validation, and rather than committing to a fixed pipeline we benchmarked feature representations, connectivity measures, and classifiers within the same scheme, projecting TreeSHAP [27] attribution onto the cortical surface to localise the dominant signature. We ask three questions: (i) can normative EEG deviations identify chronic tinnitus at the single-subject level across sites unseen during training? (ii) which circuits and frequency bands carry the diagnostic signal? and (iii) do the most discriminative features converge on a stable, cross-site neurophysiological signature? II. RESULTS

C. Multi-site LOSO yields strong, consistent classification across all seven held-out sites

Under the primary scenario, per-site LOSO AUC ranged from 0.757 (BCG) to 0.876 UKT), with all seven sites individually exceeding 0.750 and bootstrap 95% confidence intervals confirming every site exceeded chance (Table I, Fig. 2e,g). This site-level range is substantially narrower than in comparable single-site EEG tinnitus studies, demonstrating that the normative pipeline learns features generalisable across hardware, noise covariance, and other situational factors. Individual-level error analysis showed that false negatives (n = 85) and false positives (n = 52) span the full probability range rather than clustering at the decision boundary, with per-site rates varying and UKT showing the largest asymmetry (Supplementary Fig. S2).

F. Pars orbitalis shows frequency-selective hemispheric reversal

We asked whether the orbitofrontal signature carries a hemispheric bias, and whether any asymmetry is specific to it, computing a per-subject lateralisation index (LI, Methods) for parsorbitalis and, as a comparison set, four ROIs spanning the auditory hierarchy and salience circuitry (Fig. 3f). Parsorbitalis showed frequency-selective reversal: in theta both groups were right-dominant but tinnitus more strongly so (LI tin = -0.213 vs. -0.012, p FDR < 0.0001), while in α 1 the pattern reversed to left-dominance in tinnitus (LI tin = 0.134 vs. -0.037, p FDR < 0.0001), indicating distinct oscillatory mechanisms that cannot be reduced to a single hemisphere or band. None of the four auditory and salience ROIs (transversetemporal, superiortemporal, supramarginal, insula) showed a significant lateralisation difference after FDR correction, reinforcing that the dominant reorganisation is prefrontal and attention-network-based rather than auditory or salience-lateralised.

G. EEG oscillatory deviations detect tinnitus presence but not symptom burden

Despite accurate detection (AUC = 0.819), regression of THI and TFI from the same 680 features yielded strongly negative R² across every model and target: under site-stratified crossvalidation Ridge reached R² = -0.60 (THI) and -0.45 (TFI), and LightGBM did no better (-0.67, -0.54), all below zero and therefore worse than a constant group-mean baseline. Fold values were highly variable (THI range -1.26 to -0.14), indicating systematic rather than stochastic failure (Supplementary Fig. S3). The strongest single-feature association reached only ρ = +0.15 (THI) and +0.10 (TFI), with none surviving FDR correction, confirming that no single neural metric tracks distress severity.

H. Normative deviations are sparse, topographically selective, and individually heterogeneous

Feature-level prevalence maps (proportion with |Z| > 1.96 per ROI-band, aggregated by network, Fig. 1b) showed a sparse, selective hyperdeviation pattern: most features sat near the ≈ 5% chance rate, with a subset reaching 10 to 16%, localised in regional PLI to FPN ROIs in theta and alpha (parsorbitalis-lh α 2 : 12.7%, θ: 10.8%), paralleling the SHAP-ranked . features. Hypodeviation was near-zero (0.3% vs. 2.5% expected) while hyperdeviation reached 5.3%, so tinnitus deviations are exclusively upward at every scale. Yet 96 to 98% of tinnitus subjects showed at least one extreme deviation in regional PLI, the hallmark of a heterogeneous condition: nearly every subject carries a neural fingerprint of abnormality, but in a subjectspecific combination of ROIs and bands, so it is the spatial-spectral identity of the deviating features, captured by the trimodal Mahalanobis representation, not their raw count, that provides diagnostic value. Age analysis showed no significant age-deviation correlation in tinnitus (global mean r s = 0.053, p = 0.389) and no age×group interaction surviving FDR correction, indicating an age-stable signature, notably parsorbitalis-rh ×θ showed a modest positive age trend in controls (r s = 0.225, p FDR = 0.003) absent in tinnitus (r s = 0.134, p FDR = 0.185), suggesting the normal age-related rise in orbitofrontal theta connectivity is obscured by the tinnitus-related elevation (Supplementary Fig. S5a). Trimodal scatterplots confirmed power and PLI Z-scores are imperfectly correlated (|r| ≈ 0.3 to 0.5), validating the Mahalanobis fusion (Supplementary Fig. S5b).

A. A normative multi-modal fusion framework reaches the clinical-utility range

The three-modality Mahalanobis normative model achieved AUC = 0.819 (ensemble: 0.832) across 544 participants at seven independent international sites under LOSO cross-validation, with per-site AUC ranging from 0.757 to 0.876.

To our knowledge all prior EEG tinnitus classifiers were single-site and none reported held-out multi-site validation [15], [14], so a direct AUC comparison is inappropriate: within-site cross-validation inflates apparent generalisability by exploiting site-specific covariance. These results therefore establish the first tinnitus classifier validated across independent sites and the first detection estimate not inflated by within-site optimism. Two design choices explain the advance. Normative modelling first converts each feature into a Z-score relative to a healthy reference, decoupling individual deviation from site, age, sex, and audiometric confounds, multi-modal fusion then stacks power, regional node strength, and global node strength into a covariance space whose Mahalanobis distance captures joint deviations no single modality encodes. Together, cross-site validity, a stable neural correlate, and a short inexpensive recording outline a low-barrier route toward objective diagnosis complementing the questionnaires on which stratification currently depends. The area under the curve, which integrates performance across all thresholds, rather than any single operating point is the appropriate summary of clinical value: a fixed threshold trades sensitivity against specificity, so that specificity near 0.80 yields sensitivity near 0.68 and misses roughly one in three tinnitus cases.

The operating point should therefore be matched to the intended use, favouring sensitivity for screening and specificity for confirmation, with Youden's index (J = sensitivity + specificity -1) offering one principled, application-tunable choice.

B. Theta frontoparietal hyperconnectivity favours disrupted predictive coding and frontal gating

The single largest group deviation is a theta-band connectivity increase within the FPN, with a parallel elevation in the dorsal attention network (DAN, p FDR < 0.001). This is consistent with the frontostriatal gating model [10], [11], in which ventromedial prefrontal cortex and nucleus accumbens normally suppress tinnitus-related activity at the thalamic reticular nucleus, when this fails the percept persists. Our data cannot test the subcortical link directly, but the sustained cortical theta engagement is what that model predicts at the cortical level: prefrontal and parietal association cortices left in continuous engagement with an internally generated percept the gate has not resolved, imposing an ongoing attentional demand that the DAN elevation mirrors.

This emphasis is supported by earlier but underweighted EEG evidence of abnormal prefrontal coupling in chronic tinnitus [8] and of oscillatory disturbance beyond auditory cortex [12], [36], [9]. When individual deviations are modelled rather than group means, this prefrontal involvement emerges as the single most discriminative feature of the tinnitus brain state, the intrinsic-state counterpart of the task-evoked prefrontal over-recruitment that scales with perceptual intrusiveness [37].

One point needs care in interpretation. We measure phase synchrony, which quantifies how tightly two regions' oscillations are coupled, not the direction of information flow. Elevated synchrony therefore means prefrontal and parietal cortices are more tightly phase-coordinated in tinnitus, on its own it does not indicate whether top-down or bottom-up signalling drives the coupling, a distinction that matters when relating our findings to predictive-coding accounts framed in terms of directional prediction error. Because midfrontal theta is a robust, transdiagnostic index of cognitive control that scales with conflict and difficulty [38], frontoparietal theta hyperconnectivity is most parsimoniously read as sustained over-engagement of the control network rather than its failure: circuitry transiently recruited to resolve conflict appears tonically recruited in chronic tinnitus, allocating control resources to a signal that is never behaviourally resolved. That mechanism is not tinnitus-specific, and we do not claim otherwise, what is specific is the fingerprint, a right pars orbitalis theta anchor with an intact auditory cortex, that a normative model assigns reproducibly to the individual. Whether that fingerprint separates tinnitus from other control-network disorders is a question our single-disorder design cannot answer.

The predictive-coding framework specifies why the recruitment might persist: tinnitus arises when the auditory generative model assigns abnormally high precision to spontaneous activity, so that compensatory neural noise is misread as sensory evidence [39], and the precision-weighting that sustains this is itself a frontoparietal function. Direct EEG evidence that patients with minimal hearing loss show a top-down subtype driven by maladaptive predictive coding rather than peripheral drive [40] is consistent with our theta hyperconnectivity as the expression of this aberrant precision-weighting. A complementary, band-specific reading is that prefrontal-parahippocampal theta loops progressively embed the percept in long-term memory, with the hippocampus maintaining tinnitus as an auditory object held in memory rather than acting solely as a limbic emotion generator [41]. This aligns with the beta-band medial-temporal deviations we observe (Fig 3d) and offers a concrete mechanism for chronification that our cross-sectional data are consistent with but cannot yet prove.

C. Right pars orbitalis is the central tinnitus anchor

The most striking explainability result is the perfect fold stability of parsorbitalis-rh, which ranked as the most informative feature in every LOSO fold. This robustness across seven heldout sites is unprecedented in the tinnitus EEG literature and indicates a genuine, generalisable signature rather than an artefact, earlier work missed it because single-site univariate case-control analyses do not retain the individual multivariate deviation structure in which this feature is strongest. The right parsorbitalis is an orbitofrontal subdivision of the limbic-prefrontal circuit governing salience evaluation, emotional tagging, and prediction error [42], with connectivity to amygdala, anterior cingulate, and auditory association cortex placing it at the intersection of emotional processing and auditory gating. This position is coherent with the triple-network model, in which tinnitus emerges when interplay between salience, executive-control, and defaultmode systems is disturbed so that a self-generated percept is repeatedly flagged as salient and cannot be released from attention [19]. The lateralisation is frequency-selective: theta showed accentuated right-hemisphere dominance in tinnitus (LI tin = -0.213 vs. LI ctrl = -0.012), whereas α 1 (8.5 to 10.5 Hz) reversed to left-dominance (LI tin = 0.134 vs. LI ctrl = -0.037, p FDR < 0.001), suggesting right-prefrontal theta reflects salience and gating while the α 1 left-shift may index compensatory left-attentional recruitment. The same orbitofrontal locus was independently identified as a connectivity hub in a parallel resting-state MEG study [43], indicating that the signature recurs across modalities.

D. Tinnitus is biased towards top-down, not bottom-up cortical alterations

A theoretically important null result is the complete absence of significant deviation in primary auditory cortex ROIs and connectivity (transversetemporal, superiortemporal) after controlling for hearing loss [44]. Auditory-cortex hyperactivity has historically been treated as a hallmark of tinnitus, inferred from animal models of deafferentation and reported in human M/EEG studies relating auditory-cortex gamma activity to the percept [36], [45]. These rarely controlled for audiometric confounds with the precision of a per-feature normative model. This does not imply the auditory cortex is uninvolved in generating the percept, only that residual auditory-cortex deviation does not distinguish tinnitus patients from hearing-loss-matched controls. It fits a gatingfailure account in which the cortex produces the initial phantom signal after deafferentation, but chronic conscious perception depends on whether the OFC/FPN system suppresses it, so that the distinguishing feature of non-habituated patients is frontoparietal hyperconnectivity rather than auditory-cortex overactivity. Neuromodulation evidence reinforces this: stimulation confined to auditory cortex yields small, inconsistent benefits, whereas prefrontal targets more reliably shift the percept and are among the more consistently effective [1]. The network whose modulation most affects chronic tinnitus is the same frontoparietal system that dominates our classifier.

E. Neural deviation and subjective burden are orthogonal

The signature indexes the percept, not its burden. All regression models were systematically worse than a constant predictor (Ridge-THI: R² = -0.60, Ridge-loudness: R² = -2.51), a genuine dissociation rather than weak signal: features that classify tinnitus presence cannot predict how loud or distressing it is. This matches the anatomy of our deviations, confined to attentional and cognitive-control networks (FPN, DAN) with no limbic locus surviving FDR correction, whereas tinnitus distress localises to salience, limbic, and default-mode circuitry [46], [47] that THI and TFI capture but our features do not. Subjects who deviated most strongly were not the most distressed, consistent with the heterogeneity of person-specific deviations within a diagnosis [28], [48]. A severity biomarker therefore cannot be built by aggregating deviation magnitudes, but requires the circuit-level mechanisms that govern whether an abnormal oscillatory state is appraised as distressing. Detection and severity monitoring thus demand different pipelines and population stratification, as illustrated by a companion paper by Lu et al. using a cortical adaptation of gap pre-pulse inhibition of the acoustic startle (GPIAS), where impaired inhibition correlated with tinnitus distress [49].

Perceptual character is equally decoupled. Misclassified subjects span the full predictedprobability range rather than clustering at the 0.5 boundary (Supplementary Fig. S2), and SHAP places false-negative subjects within the healthy range of frontoparietal PLI deviation, so a subset of tinnitus subjects genuinely present as neural controls. In the 7 × 7 transfer analysis, training on one site and testing at another yielded near-chance AUC (0.45 to 0.66), whereas the same LGBM pipeline reached 0.757 to 0.876 when trained on six sites. The gap reflects insufficient per-site data (median n ≈ 78) and residual site variance, and an analogous single-to-multi-site gap for EEG classification of consciousness states [16] marks this a general challenge for clinical EEG biomarkers. These results argue for consortium designs as the standard, for which the TIDE protocol provides a template [26]. Harmonisation learned on controls only proved necessary: applied jointly to case-control data, ComBat risks absorbing tinnitus-related variance into the siteeffect estimate, whereas the controls-only strategy preserves individual deviation while removing technical offsets [17], [21]. a random effect offers a principled remedy with demonstrated calibration gains [51] and is already present as commented infrastructure in PCNtoolkit [52]. With a median of n tin ≈ 39 per site, per-site confidence intervals are wide and a single atypical site disproportionately influences the transfer matrix, which a replication cohort from an eighth held-out site would resolve. All analyses are cross-sectional, so the FPN-theta deviation cannot be assigned a causal role, the most consequential open question is whether normative Z-scores track chronification, which longitudinal modelling of Z-score trajectories could test directly, including whether the orbitofrontal theta signature precedes chronification and reverts with intervention. The binary classifier is also agnostic to clinically relevant subtypes (tonal versus noise-like, unilateral versus bilateral, normal-hearing tinnitus, hyperacusis comorbidity), which a one-class normative extension implementable in PCNtoolkit could resolve through unsupervised subtyping. Finally, the LOSO AUC of 0.819 approaches but does not meet the threshold for regulatory-grade diagnostic use, which will require the additional site data above and formal validation against pre-specified criteria consistent with FDA/EMA biomarker qualification. We also note that the analysis did not follow a pre-specified statistical analysis plan. Instead, we explored feature representations, connectivity measures, preprocessing levels, and classifiers to identify the model that best differentiated patients from controls. This exploratory strategy introduces analytic flexibility that can inflate apparent performance, classifier folds excluded the test site, but upstream harmonisation and reference-distribution estimation preceded classifier cross-validation, as detailed in SM4.

G. Limitations and future directions

Consequently, these estimates are not fully independent end-to-end validation of the pipeline. A confirmatory study applying the frozen pipeline to a new cohort under a pre-registered analysis plan remains necessary. The approach itself is architecturally transferable to any condition where heterogeneity and multi-site acquisition undermine case-control statistics, as shown in Parkinson's disease, Alzheimer's disease [22], and temporal lobe epilepsy [24], and it adds a chronic sensory disorder, dominated by top-down attentional and emotional circuitry rather than a bottom-up auditory signature, to that landscape.

C. Source reconstruction and parcellation

Source reconstruction was performed using the fsaverage template brain [50]. A three-layer boundary element model (scalp, outer skull and inner skull,) were used to compute a forward solution (mne.bem.make watershed bem). Noise covariance was estimated using an ad-hoc diagonal model, which assumes equal and uncorrelated sensor noise (mne.make ad hoc cov). A minimum-norm inverse operator was computed using the dSPM method from mne.minimum_norm.

apply_inverse_epochs. Since all subjects within a given site share the same amplifier and electrode layout, and are mapped to the same fsaverage template, a single inverse operator was computed per site and applied to all subjects at that site, avoiding redundant computation while preserving site-specific channel geometry. Label time courses were extracted for each epoch (mne.extract label time course) across all 68 parcels of the Desikan-Killiany (aparc) atlas [58] (34 per hemisphere), excluding the medial wall. Within each parcel, vertex-wise source estimates were averaged after flipping vertex signs to align with the label's dominant orientation. The resulting label time courses formed the basis for all subsequent feature extraction.

D. Feature extraction

Spectral power: Band-limited spectral power was estimated per epoch using multitaper spectral estimation [59]. Ten frequency bands were defined following established EEG band conventions [60]: delta (1-6 Hz), theta (6.5-8.5 Hz), broad alpha (α 0 : 8.5-12.5 Hz), alpha-1 (α 1 : 8.5-10.5 Hz), alpha-2 (α 2 : 10.5-12.5 Hz), broad beta (β 0 : 12.5-30 Hz), beta-1 (β 1 : 12.5-18.5 Hz), beta-2 (β 2 : 18.5-21 Hz), beta-3 (β 3 : 21-30 Hz), and gamma (30-40 Hz). Power spectral density was integrated over each band by averaging across epochs, and log-transforming to approximate normality, yielding a 68 × 10 power feature matrix per subject.

Functional connectivity: Phase Lag Index (PLI) [61], Phase Locking Value (PLV) [62], and magnitude-squared coherence (COH) [63] were computed per frequency band using complex Morlet wavelet convolution (spectral connectivity time from, MNE-Connectivity [32]). PLI was chosen as the primary connectivity metric because its insensitivity to zero-lag contributions from volume conduction and common references makes it robust to EEG-specific artefacts [61].

For 68 source ROIs, pairwise connectivity produced 68 × 67/2 = 2278 unique pairs per band.

Node strength metrics: Two aggregate connectivity metrics were derived per ROI per band.

• Regional node strength was defined as the sum of PLI (or PLV/COH) values restricted to anatomically adjacent parcels, those sharing a cortical boundary in the Desikan-Killiany atlas on the fsaverage surface, as encoded in a precomputed binary adjacency matrix. This metric captures short-range, anatomically constrained synchrony for each ROI.

• Global node strength was defined as the sum of PLI (or PLV/COH) values across all other parcels in the graph, irrespective of anatomical adjacency, capturing each ROI's total longrange network integration.

Both metrics yielded a 68 × 10 matrix per subject, giving three feature matrices in total: spectral power, regional node strength, and global node strength, each entered into its own separate normative model.

Aperiodic parameters:

The aperiodic (1/f ) component of the power spectrum reflects local excitation-inhibition (E:I) balance and has been implicated across neurological and psychiatric conditions [64], it was parameterised using FOOOF [65], yielding the slope (exponent) and offset per ROI per epoch, then averaged.

E. Multi-site harmonisation

Site-induced batch effects in all feature matrices were removed using ComBat [66] as implemented in the neuroHarmonize library [17]. Critically, harmonisation parameters were learned exclusively on control subjects to prevent tinnitus-related variance from being absorbed into the site-effect estimates, then applied to all subjects (controls and tinnitus). Candidate biological covariates were age, sex, PTA4 and HF-PTA; hearing definitions, available-ear handling and covariate selection are detailed in Supplementary Methods SM3.

F. Normative modelling

For each of the three feature modalities (spectral power, regional node strength, global node strength) and each feature column (ROI × band), a separate Bayesian linear regression (BLR) normative model was fitted to the control subsample using the PCNtoolkit library [67]. The model comprised: a B-spline basis expansion for nonlinear age modelling (degree = 3, n knots = 5), heteroskedastic variance (age-dependent residual spread), fixed effects for sex and the selected hearing covariates, and standardised feature inputs.

Hearing-covariate selection is described in Supplementary Methods SM3.

Two complementary fitting procedures were run. (i) A full model was trained on all controls and applied to tinnitus subjects, producing Z-scores that quantify each tinnitus participant's deviation from the healthy norm: Z (i) = (y (i) -μ(i) )/σ (i) . In the full model, site was included as an additional batch-effect term to absorb any residual inter-site variance not fully removed by

ComBat. (ii) An unbiased LOSO evaluation was run on controls: for each of seven site-folds, the model was trained on the six remaining sites' controls and applied to the held-out site's controls, so every control received exactly one Z-score from a model it never trained on. Site was not included as a batch-effect term in the LOSO models for two reasons: ComBat harmonisation had already removed systematic inter-site offsets, and more critically, PCNtoolkit cannot estimate a batch-effect parameter for a site that was entirely absent from the training fold, which would cause inference to fail for the held-out site. This yielded a fully held-out control Z-score matrix directly comparable to tinnitus Z-scores. Normative calibration quality was verified using a site-stratified 80/20 split of controls, confirming N (0, 1) approximation.

H. Classification

Three classifiers were benchmarked under the same conditions: random forest and radialbasis-function SVM as classical ensemble and kernel-based baselines [69], and LightGBM (LGBM) [31] as a modern gradient-boosted tree framework well-suited to high-dimensional structured data. The primary classifier was selected empirically based on LOSO ablation performance, all three were embedded in a scikit-learn [69] pipeline of median imputation was evaluated for the top scenario. Feature selection variants (SelectKBest k = 50, recursive feature elimination, ElasticNet) were also compared against the no selection baseline.

J. Model explainability

TreeSHAP values [27] were computed using leave-one-site-out (LOSO) cross-validation (seven folds, one site heldout per fold) to ensure that SHAP attributions reflect genuinely out-of-sample importance. Within each fold, the trained LightGBM model was wrapped in a TreeExplainer from the SHAP package [30], with interventional feature perturbation and the imputed, standardised training data as background reference. Mean absolute SHAP values were computed across subjects for global feature ranking, additionally, fold-level stability was assessed by computing per-site mean |SHAP| for the top features.

CONCLUSION

We demonstrated that normative modelling of resting-state EEG can detect chronic tinnitus with clinically meaningful accuracy (AUC = 0.832) while remaining interpretable and site-robust.

The converging evidence from classification, network analysis, and individual-level deviation mapping points to a coherent picture: chronic tinnitus is dominantly characterised by frontoparietal theta hyperconnectivity anchored in right parsorbitalis. The orthogonality between neural deviation and symptom burden clarifies rather than limits the clinical contribution: we delineate here a reproducible neural phenotype of tinnitus presence that can objectively confirm an internally heard pitched sound independent of subjective report, which is precisely the function of a diagnostic biomarker and a distinct problem from quantifying the burden it produces. Our findings offer a replicable normative framework and a refined pathophysiological target i.e. a orbitofrontal-frontoparietal circuit, for the next generation of tinnitus biomarker development and neuromodulatory intervention.
