Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Marginal Significance works

List all sentences that describe an effect as ‘marginally significant’.

The marginal module searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The list of terms is a subset of those listed in a blog post by Matthew Hankins. The module returns all sentences that match terms describing ‘marginally significant’ results.

Some of the terms identified might not be problematic in some contexts, and there are ways to describe ‘marginal significance’ that are not detected by the module.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). Statcheck might still miss one-sided tests that are described in another sentence, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. Some differences come from the ground truth: it lacks p-values with a correction name (“p Bonf = .061”), and counts partial eta squared written with its subscript after it (“η 2 p = .05”), “p = .05”, and some table notes and figure legends (“† p < .10, *p < .05”), which this module leaves out, as non-significant p-values. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

If you want to extend the package to be able to download files from additional data repositories reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. Abstract

Eating disorders (EDs) are serious psychiatric conditions that typically onset during adolescence and are often marked by a chronic and severe illness course. Capturing ED symptoms as they emerge is critical for charting risk markers and associated outcomes to inform early intervention and treatment efforts. The Adolescent Brain Cognitive Development SM (ABCD ® ) Study is one such key study that could accelerate these efforts, but has been limited to date by a lack of clear, clinically informed definitions capturing youth at risk for ED-related concerns. The current manuscript aims to address this gap by defining ED symptom indices (ED SI ) with a reproducible framework developed in collaboration with ED researchers and clinicians to identify youth with patterns of ED symptoms and behaviors that approximate subthreshold and diagnostic-like presentations. We describe item-level definitions and decision rules used to construct the ED SI , which include symptom indices of anorexia nervosa, bulimia nervosa, binge eating disorder, and other specified feeding or eating disorders. We then compare youth meeting ED SI criteria with those identified by existing ED categorical definitions in ABCD Study data releases. By providing transparent definitions and analytical tools for identifying youth with potentially clinically meaningful ED behaviors, the ED SI framework aims to facilitate more reproducible and interpretable research related to EDs using the ABCD Study dataset, while also providing a structure to examine the emergence, persistence, and change of ED-like symptoms longitudinally across adolescence and into young adulthood in future research.

INTRODUCTION

Eating disorders (EDs) are severe psychiatric illnesses that typically emerge in adolescence (Galmiche et al., 2019; Uhlhaas et al., 2023). They are characterized by persistent disruptions in eating behaviors and related cognitive processes that impair food consumption. EDs often result in marked physical and/or psychosocial impairment (American Psychiatric Association, 2022), with extreme cases leading to mortality (Attia & Walsh, 2025; Hambleton et al., 2022; Krug et al., 2025). Globally, approximately 22% of children and adolescents report symptoms of disordered eating, with 1.2% of males and 5.7% of females aged 11-19 receiving a clinical diagnosis (López-Gil et al., 2023; Volpe et al., 2016). Mid-to-late adolescence (~age 15.5) represents the peak period for ED symptom onset (Hudson et al., 2007; Solmi et al., 2022). This is a critical time for intervention, as untreated EDs are associated with increased risk of persistent illness and impairment. Consequently, it is imperative to catch emerging ED symptoms early to facilitate faster referral for treatment (Attia & Guarda, 2022; Kalindjian et al., 2022; Mills et al., 2024).

The gold standard for diagnosing EDs is through comprehensive, clinician-administered interviews. These interviews assess cognitive and behavioral symptom frequency, duration, weight history, compensatory behaviors, and psychosocial impairment secondary to the ED (American Psychiatric Association, 2022; Forbush et al., 2020). However, accessible and clinically-informed self-report questionnaires have also been developed for broader, nonspecialist use when clinician-administered interviews are not feasible or practical (Kaufman et al., 1997; Luce & Crowther, 1999; Morgan et al., 1999), such as in large population-level and longitudinal cohort studies. One such questionnaire is the Kiddie Schedule for Affective Disorders and Schizophrenia -Computerized Version (KSADS-COMP). The KSADS-COMP is a guided, self-administered, computerized assessment that aims to mirror the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, 5th Edition (DSM-5) (American Psychiatric Association, 2022). It has been widely used and validated for youth ages 6-18 and their caregivers (Kaufman et al., 1997; Townsend et al., 2020) and assesses weight distress, weight-control behaviors (e.g., caloric restriction, purging), binge eating, and related psychopathology.

It is important to acknowledge that current ED categories derived from the KSADS-COMP and similar assessments mirror DSM-5-like symptoms rather than clinical diagnoses. Additionally, branching logic and scoring algorithms are proprietary, posing challenges for rigor and reproducibility (Martin et al., 2026). For example, in the ABCD Study, the branching logic for the KSADS-COMP ED module is not openly available, making it difficult for users to determine the exact criteria used to include participants in specific categories. Further, the naming of categories generated by proprietary software are often misinterpreted as diagnostic labels in youth samples, as evidenced by papers using these categories to compare with epidemiological estimates of eating disorders (Murray et al., 2022; Rozzell et al., 2019). Despite these limitations, non-clinical assessment questionnaires remain highly valuable for identifying ED behaviors and symptoms in large, non-clinical samples (Hail et al., 2024; Lauth et al., 2010; Marques et al., 2022; Townsend et al., 2020).

Therefore, the current manuscript examines the extent to which ED classifications derived from questionnaire-based assessments such as the KSADS-COMP align with DSM-5 diagnostic criteria. Using data from the Adolescent Brain Cognitive Development (ABCD) Study, a large, diverse longitudinal cohort study in which caregivers completed the ED module of the KSADS-COMP annually (and youth biannually), we developed symptom-level indicators that apply transparent, DSM-5-informed logic to the available KSADS-COMP items. The goal is not to establish clinical diagnoses, but to provide researchers with a reproducible framework for identifying youth with patterns of ED symptoms and behaviors that approximate DSM-5 presentations, including potentially clinically meaningful subthreshold presentations. While these eating disorder symptom indices (ED SI ) were developed in collaboration with clinicians who work with and/or treat youth with EDs and researchers with expertise in ED assessment, the numbers of youth meeting criteria in this manuscript are for descriptive purposes only. Together, these new ED symptom indices (ED SI ) were developed with clear branching logic to promote reproducible and transparent research of ED-like behaviors in the ABCD Study.

We first describe the item-level definitions and decision rules used to construct the ED SI.

We then compare the youth identified by the ED SI with those identified using existing KSADS-COMP-derived classifications, examining the extent of agreement and discrepancy between the two approaches. These comparisons are intended to characterize general differences between classification methods, which may be due to proprietary methods. Finally, because the ABCD Study administered the KSADS-COMP repeatedly across development, the ED SI provides a framework for examining the emergence, persistence, and change in ED-like symptoms longitudinally. By providing transparent definitions and reproducible analytic tools, this work aims to facilitate more rigorous and interpretable research on ED symptoms using questionnaires such as the KSADS-COMP. Collectively, this manuscript provides a roadmap for developing the ED SI.

METHODS

KSADS-COMP Assessment

The KSADS-COMP is a self-administered computerized questionnaire completed by caregivers and youth. Caregivers were given the option to complete this questionnaire in English or Spanish, whereas only the English version was available to youth; it was completed in person or via Zoom (Karcher & Barch, 2020). In the 2-and 4-year follow-up visits, the KSADS-COMP questions were read to youth by a research assistant. From the 6-year follow-up onward, youth completed questionnaires independently, but were monitored by a research assistant. While caregivers completed this questionnaire annually, youth reports were collected only biannually beginning at the 2-year follow-up assessment.

Variable naming KSADS-COMP

Item-level response data were collected using KSADS-COMP's web interface, scored using proprietary algorithms, and subsequently transferred to the ABCD Study as item-level, symptom-level, and "diagnosis" variables. Note that the KSADS-COMP "diagnosis" label does not correspond to clinical diagnosis, and is often incorrectly referred to as such given the nomenclature of the variable. Moreover, the KSADS-COMP-generated ED "diagnoses" are reported as "present" and "past", where "present" indicates the past two weeks, and "past" reflects at any point in their lives ('ever').

ED SI nomenclature creation

The current manuscript uses the KSADS-COMP item-level data to develop new DSM-5aligned ED SI (e.g., Binge Eating Disorder Symptom Index [BED SI ]). Given the nature of the KSADS-COMP item-level data, categorization of the ED SI approximated the DSM-5 inclusion criteria as closely as possible while allowing greater flexibility to capture the greatest number of youth as possible exhibiting potential ED symptoms. We refrained from using the terms "diagnosis", "diagnostic," and similar language when referring to the KSADS-COMP-derived categories or these new ED SI . We included the following ED SI categories: anorexia nervosa restricting type (AN-R SI ), anorexia nervosa binge/purge type (AN-BP SI ), including atypical anorexia nervosa (AAN-R SI , AAN-BP SI ) and anorexia nervosa-weight criteria unknown (AN-Rwcu SI , AN-BP-wcu SI ) for both subtypes, bulimia nervosa (BN SI ), and binge eating disorder (BED SI ). OSFED-AN-R SI and OSFED-BED SI were generated for youth who did not meet the full criteria for AN-R SI and BED SI , respectively, but still exhibited some symptoms (see below for how each category was determined). Due to KSADS-COMP question wording, it was impossible to distinguish between OSFED-AN-BP and OSFED-BN; therefore, they were combined into a single OSFED-AN-BP/BN SI category. Additional youth who exhibited weight-control symptoms without endorsing specific weight-control methods were included in the OSFED-general SI category. Notably, this manuscript focuses on reporting "present" symptomatology; we intentionally excluded questions that probed "past" symptoms, given ambiguity in the timeframe that those questions aimed to target. Other Specified Feeding or Eating Disorder Symptom Index (OSFED SI ).

Subthreshold ED presentations (e.g., not meeting all criteria listed in Tables 1-3 but still endorsing ED symptoms) are captured by the OSFED symptom index. Although this category is vague in the DSM-5, we aimed to capture as many nuances as possible in subthreshold symptom presentations, allowing researchers the flexibility to merge or separate groupings relevant to their research questions (Jenkins et al., 2021).

Thus, we created four OSFED SI presentations: OSFED-AN-R SI , OSFED-AN-BP/BN SI, OSFED-BED SI , and OSFED-general SI . The OSFED-AN-R SI and OSFED-BED SI categories capture presentations of AN-R SI and BED SI symptoms, respectively, where not all initial criteria were met. The OSFED-AN-BP/BN SI category describes common presentations of binge-eating or the binge/purge cycle, but frequency and/or duration thresholds were not met. It was impossible to disentangle OSFED-AN-BP SI and OSFED-BN SI cases using the available KSADS-COMP data; therefore, they were combined into a single category. OSFED-general SI captures any clinically significant eating pathology for participants not already captured by a more stringent symptom index or specific to any particular weight control method. By comparison, KSADS-COMP defines two OSFED categories: OSFED-BN and OSFED-BED.

Full operational criteria are detailed in the Supplementary Materials (Tables S1-S4).

Statistical Analysis

Data were prepared using Python 3.13.5 and pandas 2.3.1 for data cleaning and analysis. All criteria-relevant data were coded into categorical variables with short descriptive variable names. For example, a score of 1 or higher on the "fear of weight gain" question was coded as "fear_obese". Participant-reported values for the duration of a given ED symptom were converted to numeric month values for the duration criteria. These variables were then used to define ED SI.

We compared the number of participants meeting the KSADS-COMP and ED SI criteria across time points, from the 3-year follow-up onward (3-, 4-, 6-, and 7-year follow-ups in the caregiver-report, and 4-and 6-year follow-ups in the youth report). Results are reported for the number of youth who met the KSADS-COMP and ED SI criteria, separated by caregiver-vs. youth-report, by year, and sex. In cases where at least 1 but fewer than 10 youth met criteria, exact cell counts were suppressed in accordance with best practices for reporting small cell sizes (Seastrom, 2017). We used the Jaccard index to quantify the overlap between ED classifications (i.e., the number of participants who met both the KSADS-COMP and ED SI criteria divided by the number who met either criterion). Values closer to 0 indicate no overlap, whereas values closer to 1 indicate complete overlap. When applicable (i.e., when there were sufficient youth for a meaningful comparison), chi-squared tests of proportions (conducted in R version 4.5.1) were used to assess the statistical significance of differences between proportions obtained from the KSADS-COMP and the ED SI . Yates' continuity corrections were automatically applied to account for the chi-squared test's tendency to overestimate significance in tables with small cell counts. These results are also stratified by sex to account for established differences in ED pathology by biological sex (Allen et al., 2013; Sanzari et al., 2023).

RESULTS

In the sections that follow, we first quantify the number of youth that meet criteria for any given ED SI category, followed by the number of youth that meet criteria for the equivalent ED-related category by KSADS-COMP. Note, that these method comparisons are for descriptive purposes only, given that it was not possible to pinpoint exactly where our ED SI definitions differed from KSADS-COMP given the proprietary nature of its branching logic. The impact of objectively measured BMI vs. caregiver-reported estimates of height and weight on categorizations was tested. Jaccard indices were calculated between participants who met the KSADS-COMP criteria for AN-R (using self-reported anthropometrics) and those who met the ED SI criteria for either AN-R SI , AAN-R SI , or AN-R-wcu SI (using objective BMI data when possible) (see Table 4; see Table 5 for the same analyses conducted across the binge/purge subtype). Jaccard indices ranged from 0.00 to 0.11 for the restricting subtype, and from 0.00 to 0.18 for the binge/purge subtype, indicating minimal overlap. Thus, differences in BMI calculations between the ED SI and KSADS-COMP were not the primary cause of differences in categorization. However, the specific criteria and decision rules used in the KSADS-COMP to determine AN in this dataset were not transparent, limiting the ability to make a precise comparison.

Youth-report. Across all time points, no youth met the criteria for either AN-R SI or AN-BP SI (see Tables 4, 5; Figures 2B, 2D display frequency over time). Meanwhile, 42 youth met the KSADS-COMP criteria for AN-R, and 37 youth met the KSADS-COMP criteria for AN-BP. Similar comparative analyses were conducted using caregiver-report data, and the Jaccard indices ranged from 0.07 to 0.10 for the restricting subtype and from 0.06 to 0.10 for the bingepurge subtype. The minimal overlap similarly reveals that usage of objectively measured BMI accounted for only a few differences in classification.

DISCUSSION

The current manuscript presents a revised categorization strategy for utilizing KSADS-COMP ED item-level data to generate DSM-5-aligned ED SI with the ABCD Study data. Compared to KSADS-COMP-defined categories, Jaccard indices revealed noticeable differences in the number of youth captured across methods, particularly for AN SI and OSFED SI ; the differences for BN SI and BED SI were less divergent. Overlap among OSFED categories was more difficult to gauge, given that some of the distinct definitions provided by ED SI are not captured by KSADS-COMP. Given the complexity and heterogeneity of ED presentations,

A B

Other Specified or Feeding Eating Disorder transparent phenotyping is critical. Currently, it is unknown how KSADS-COMP categories are defined. Therefore, we provided transparent ED SI definitions to capture youth who endorsed cognitive and behavioral signs and symptoms of AN, atypical AN, BN, and BED, as well as clinically relevant subtypes (e.g., AN-BP, AN-R) and subthreshold presentations (e.g., OSFED). Therefore, this new ED SI categorization equips researchers with a structured approach to pursue ED-related scientific questions.

A primary aim of this manuscript was to compare the youth identified by ED SI to those captured by the KSADS-COMP ED module scoring algorithm. At the aggregate level, differences were most pronounced for AN-spectrum presentations: the ED SI identified virtually no cases of AN-BP SI and substantially fewer cases of AN-R SI and AAN SI than the KSADS-COMP. Across all time points, relatively few youth met criteria for AN SI regardless of reporter, reflecting lower than expected numbers based on broader epidemiological estimates (AN prevalence ~1%) (Udo & Grilo, 2019). The requirement that youth be objectively underweight may be the most plausible explanation for these low counts. Indeed, substantially more assessment instances were classified as AAN-R SI , for which low weight was not required, including 222 caregiver-reported and 150 youth-reported instances across time points. It is possible that more youth would have met these criteria but lacked sufficiently reliable BMI data due to remote assessments or errors in height and weight measurements, resulting in biologically implausible values. Youth who met all other AN SI criteria but lacked usable BMI data were therefore classified in the AN-wcu SI category (n caregiver =109; n youth =40 across timepoints and subtypes). Given these data availability constraints and the small numbers within individual subtypes, combining AN SI , AAN SI , and AN-wcu SI presentations may provide researchers with a more analytically useful measure for examining AN-spectrum phenotypes.

By contrast, the ED SI and KSADS-COMP produced more similar aggregate counts for BN and BED. The prevalence of BN SI and BED SI was also more consistent with epidemiological estimates from pediatric and broader samples (Kjeldbjerg & Clausen, 2023). For example, at the 6-year follow-up, when participants were approximately 15-17 years old, 1.2% of females and 0.3% of males met criteria for BN SI . These estimates are comparable to previously reported point-prevalence estimates of 1.5% among females and 0.1% among males (Galmiche et al., 2019). Although BED typically has a later mean age of onset of approximately 23 years (Galmiche et al., 2019), youth-reported BED SI was already identified in 1.6% of females and 0.4% of males at 6-year follow-up. These estimates align with previously reported pointprevalence estimates across age groups of 2.3% and 0.3% among females and males, respectively (Galmiche et al., 2019). Collectively, these findings suggest that the ED SI definitions capture an overall burden of binge-eating-spectrum symptomatology broadly consistent with prior epidemiological findings.

The ED SI also identified a substantial number of participants with OSFED SI across four groups: youth with subthreshold AN-R, AN-BP or BN behaviors or BED; and youth with bingeeating or compensatory behaviors who did not meet criteria for another defined category. Direct comparison with KSADS-COMP was limited because several corresponding OSFED subtypes, namely OSFED-AN-R and OSFED-AN-BP/BN, were not defined in KSADS-COMP. Although these research-oriented OSFED SI presentations do not correspond directly to the specific OSFED categories described in the DSM-5, they were intentionally designed to capture a broad range of transdiagnostic ED symptoms. Identifying these presentations may facilitate research on early or subthreshold symptoms that could precede the development of full-threshold EDs or be associated with clinically relevant comorbidities.

As the ABCD Study cohort enters the peak window for ED onset in mid-to-late adolescence (Hudson et al., 2007; Solmi et al., 2022), we anticipate that full-threshold ED SI counts, particularly AN SI , will rise in future data releases. In the meantime, the comparatively higher yield of AAN SI , AN-wcu SI , and OSFED-AN-R SI cases offers a valuable starting point for prospective designs aimed at identifying AN-like phenotypes and premorbid risk factors before youth cross the full diagnostic thresholds. We therefore encourage researchers to incorporate all four of these categories (i.e. AN SI, AAN SI , AN-wcu SI , and OSFED-AN-R SI ) when studying ANrelated phenotypes, as this category may capture youth on similar risk trajectories showing cognitive/behavioral signs and symptoms consistent with those with AN SI who have significantly low weight for their developmental stage. Subthreshold ED presentations also carry a degree of functional impairment comparable to full-threshold disorders (Fairweather-Schmidt & Wade, 2014; Stice et al., 2009) and most often precede them (Solmi et al., 2024). Thus, OSFED SI categories may help identify youth who could be particularly informative for investigating premorbid risk factors within the ABCD Study, and may reflect earlier presentations of EDs, providing valuable insight for informing early intervention and prevention efforts.

Importantly, although categorization counts appeared similar across methods, Jaccard indices revealed substantial disagreement in which individual youth met criteria for specific categories (e.g., AN, BN, BED). Agreement was highest for BN SI (Jaccard indices of 0.88-0.95 for caregiver-report and 0.88-0.91 for youth-report), moderate for BED SI (0.66-0.80), and lowest for AN SI /AAN SI (0.00-0.35) and OSFED SI (0.02-0.19). Thus, similar aggregate prevalence estimates did not necessarily reflect agreement at the individual level. Concordance was greatest for BN SI and BED SI, which may reflect greater reliance on visible, outward behaviors and relatively frequent symptoms.

Regarding BED, disagreement may stem in part from differences in how the DSM-5 criteria were operationalized. Our empirically supported operationalization explicitly excluded youth who engaged in compensatory behaviors from meeting the criteria for BED (Abber et al., 2026). Individual-level examination of youth classified as having BED by KSADS-COMP suggested that its algorithm may not have consistently applied this exclusion, potentially accounting for some of the observed discordance. For AN SI and AAN SI , lower concordance may instead reflect differences in how weight and symptom criteria are operationalized. When available, our AN SI /AAN SI classifications incorporated objectively measured height and weight, whereas the KSADS-COMP ED module relied on caregiver-or youth-reported anthropometric estimates. The approaches also differed in how they operationalized the "fear of weight gain" criterion. Our AN SI /AAN SI /AN-wcu SI criteria used a more liberal threshold (e.g., > 1 symptom on a 0-4 scale), whereas the KSADS-COMP ED algorithm imposed a more stringent cutoff (e.g., >3). However, sensitivity analyses indicated that the more liberal threshold for "fear of weight gain" did not account for the observed differences between methods. Together, these findings suggest that discordance in AN-related classification may be driven more by differences in the measurement and operationalization of each criteria than by differences in overall case frequency.

Even more pronounced discrepancies emerged for OSFED SI and KSADS-COMP OSFED groups, showing consistently low Jaccard indices, suggesting that the two methods largely identified different youth with ED symptoms. This was expected to some degree, given that our OSFED SI definitions were intentionally constructed to be inclusive of any youth who endorsed some level of ED symptoms, including OSFED-AN-R SI and OSFED-AN-BP/BN SI categories that had no KSADS-COMP analogs. The larger pool of youth with OSFED SI may also help researchers more comprehensively identify youth with subthreshold ED symptomatology in the ABCD Study dataset. In turn, this could help benchmark proxy prevalence estimates of OSFED in non-treatment-seeking samples, given that existing OSFED prevalence estimates have largely been derived from clinical samples (Solmi et al., 2024).

Moreover, regardless of classification method, groupings from youth-vs. caregiverreports differed substantially at the 4-and 6-year follow-ups (~ages 13-17 years). This is consistent with previous findings of informant differences in the ABCD study's mental health measures (Baskin-Sommers et al., 2026; Costello et al., 2011; Karcher & Barch, 2021). Youthreported cases for AAN, BN, BED, and OSFED consistently exceeded caregiver-reports across both ED SI and KSADS-COMP. For example, at 6-year follow-up, youth-reported AAN and BN instances were approximately 1.6-fold and 2-fold higher than caregiver reports, respectively. While a full exploration of these informant discrepancies is beyond the scope of this manuscript, these patterns underscore that ED SI surveillance in the ABCD Study should carefully consider data from both reporters depending on the research question, where inclusion of both reporters could provide complementary and/or additive information.

Toward more reproducible ED research in ABCD

It is important to note that a precise item-level comparison between ED SI and KSADS-COMP across all included ED categories is not possible, as the KSADS-COMP scoring algorithm is not publicly available. Ultimately, the misalignments between these two methods underscores the critical need for reproducible, standardized, and explicitly operationalized criteria, which the ED SI directly addresses. This is particularly relevant given recently documented inconsistencies among ABCD data including neuroimaging, much of which has been attributed to heterogeneous, non-transparent case definitions across studies (Martin et al., 2026). By providing fully transparent, item-level criteria and openly available code, ED SI offers a common methodological foundation from which ABCD study researchers can consistently derive and report on ED symptoms. More broadly, the longitudinal design and multimodal depth of the ABCD Study make it uniquely positioned to advance understanding of the developmental origins of EDs. The comparatively higher numbers of youth with AAN SI and OSFED-AN-R SI relative to full-threshold AN SI is particularly valuable in this context, as ABCD's temporal design will allow researchers to pinpoint when and for whom subsequent ED symptomatology evolves (Solmi et al., 2024).

This work needs to be interpreted considering certain limitations. The KSADS-COMP does not assess all DSM-5 ED categories, including avoidant restrictive food intake disorder (ARFID), pica, and rumination disorder. Thus, ED SI cannot capture the full spectrum of adolescent eating pathology. ED SI also approximates rather than fully replicates DSM-5 criteria, given constraints to specific items available in the KSADS-COMP, including the collection of objective BMI data to distinguish between AN subtypes and other ED categories, as described above. The KSADS-COMP scoring algorithm is not publicly documented; thus, our explanations for discrepancies between ED SI and KSADS-COMP classifications are inferential. Analyses presented here were restricted to KSADS-COMP Version 2 (corresponding to 3-year follow-up onward), given challenges in curating data across KSADS-COMP versions in current releases; however, corrections are expected in the 7.1 patch release. Finally, ED SI reflects youth-or caregiver-reported rather than clinician-assessed ED symptomatology and thus should not be interpreted as formal diagnoses.
