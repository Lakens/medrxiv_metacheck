How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Marginal Significance works

List all sentences that describe an effect as ‘marginally significant’.

The marginal module searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The list of terms is a subset of those listed in a blog post by Matthew Hankins. The module returns all sentences that match terms describing ‘marginally significant’ results.

Some of the terms identified might not be problematic in some contexts, and there are ways to describe ‘marginal significance’ that are not detected by the module.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

Full text

Abstract

The cellular composition of blood is fundamental to human physiology and is routinely measured to assess health and disease. Circulating immune cells show extensive heterogeneity, but the organising principles and extremes of variation across individuals remain undefined. Here, using single-cell transcriptomics across two biobank cohorts encompassing ~6,000 individuals, we show that human blood occupies recurrent archetypal states defined by coordinated cell composition patterns. We delineate three major archetypes spanning naive lymphoid, myeloid and cytotoxic composition, and two minor archetypes characterised by type I interferon-driven inflammation and expanded premalignant CD5+ B cells. We develop cell2clone to infer lymphocyte clonal expansions, revealing how reactive clonal processes drive the cytotoxic archetype and premalignant clonal expansion establishes the CD5+ B cell archetype, creating outlier states that uncover context-specific effects of germline variants. Clonal haematopoiesis and mosaic chromosomal alterations influence blood states in an archetype-dependent manner. The archetype framework links blood states to common disease risk and clinical blood counts and, through a machine-learning model ArcheTypist, classifies infection and cancer immunotherapy outcomes. This population-scale framework has implications for diagnostics, early disease detection, immunotherapy stratification and mechanistic insights into disease susceptibility and therapy response, providing an organising model for identifying compositional subtypes across human tissues.

Introduction

Human blood composition varies substantially across individuals, reflecting differences in immune history, ageing, environmental exposure, infection and somatic evolution. Single-cell transcriptomic studies have revealed extensive heterogeneity in circulating immune cells, identifying previously unappreciated cell populations and dynamic cell states associated with health and disease [1] [2] [3] [4] [5] . However, despite increasingly detailed catalogues of blood cell types, the organising principles underlying inter-individual blood variation are not well understood.

Specifically, it is unknown whether variation in cell type abundance reflects largely independent fluctuations across cell types or if blood composition follows recurrent patterns of coordinated cellular variation. A unified framework of clinically meaningful blood subtypes could refine the concept of normal blood and reference ranges, and would allow placing individuals on trajectories of blood composition between health and disease. Rare or expanded cell states have been identified in specific physiological and pathological contexts, including cytotoxic CD4⁺ T cells in supercentenarians 6 and interferon-responsive myeloid states during viral infection 7 . However, it is not known whether such states represent isolated anomalies or instead occupy extremes of broader trajectories of blood composition across individuals. Population-scale atlases provide an opportunity to define normal tissue composition based on variation across the population, classify individuals based on cell composition and define outliers at the fringes of normal tissue composition.

Inter-individual variation, blood composition subtypes and extreme blood states could be driven by clonal expansion of cell types. Adaptive immune responses induce antigen-driven proliferation of lymphocytes 8 , while somatic alterations generate premalignant clonal expansions through myeloid 9 or lymphoid 10 clonal haematopoiesis and mosaic chromosomal alterations 11 . How these adaptive or premalignant clonal processes influence blood composition at population scale remains poorly understood. In addition to somatic alterations, rare outlier blood states generated by somatic clonal expansion may also create distinct regulatory contexts in which germline variants influence gene expression, contributing to cell type expansion and blood composition.

Understanding the organisation of blood variation has important clinical implications. The complete blood count (CBC) is a cornerstone of clinical medicine, providing a rapid and costeffective readout of haematologic and immunologic status 12 . Measurements such as white blood cell count and leukocyte differentials are routinely used to detect infection, inflammation, haematologic malignancy and bone marrow dysfunction. Despite its clinical utility, the CBC compresses complex biological processes into a small set of aggregate measurements, offering limited insight into the cellular and molecular states underlying blood variation across individuals. Moreover, patient-specific blood count setpoints define disease trajectories 13 , but the underlying regulatory mechanisms and cellular and molecular processes maintaining these states or leading to deviations from them (e.g. leukocytosis, lymphocytosis and monocytosis 14 ), remain unclear. Integrating molecular measurements at cellular resolution with conventional haematologic traits holds the promise of resolving the cellular and molecular mechanisms underlying both normal and abnormal blood states, bridging molecular and clinical phenotypes. Identifying subtypes of blood composition could provide a new approach to classifying the immune status of an individual, unlocking tools for predicting outcomes of infections and immune interventions.

Here, we leverage blood single-cell genomics of thousands of individuals across the UK Biobank 15 (UKB) and Genes & Health 16 (G&H) cohorts integrated with concurrent CBC measurements to show that human blood occupies recurrent archetypal compositional states defined by coordinated variation across immune cell populations (Fig. 1a). We propose that blood samples across a population can be grouped into archetypes with similar cell composition patterns, capturing coordinated variation across molecular and clinical measurements. We delineate trajectories toward extreme blood phenotypes and clonal and somatic processes that shape outlier blood states, define transcriptional programmes associated with these extremes, and uncover context-specific germline regulatory effects emerging in clonally expanded outlier cell states. Finally, we demonstrate how the archetype framework links blood immune states to common disease risk and can be used to explain clinical blood count abnormalities, classify COVID-19 infection outcomes and predict cancer immunotherapy-related adverse events. To support application of these approaches, we present computational tools for classifying blood and tissue archetypes (ArcheTypist, made available at http://github.com/TeichLab/ArcheTypist) and for adaptive lymphocyte clonality inference (cell2clone, available at http://github.com/TeichLab/cell2clone).

In the future, blood archetypes based on our refined molecular and cellular analysis, potentially in conjunction with other clinical diagnostic modalities, can serve to better diagnose and identify early stages of disease. This paradigm also has potential for precision diagnostics of populations with distinct risk profiles for cardiometabolic, autoinflammatory and infectious diseases. Finally, we envision that our conceptual framework for blood presented here can serve as a prototype of identifying clinically relevant tissue composition subtypes beyond blood using emerging high-resolution datasets of increasing scale, for example population-scale spatial transcriptomics datasets of biopsies in tissues such as skin.

Results

Blood archetypes across human populations

To define the organising principles of inter-individual blood variation, we sought to identify subgroups across over 6,000 samples across 9.8 million peripheral blood mononuclear cell transcriptomes from the UKB (European ancestry) and G&H (British Pakistani and British Bangladeshi) cohorts (CARDINAL dataset, Giacopuzzi et al. 17 ) based on the compositional space of co-varying cell types across 79 fine-grained celltype_3 (CT3) states (Fig. 1b, Supplementary Fig. 1). Graph-based clustering of samples based on cell state fractions identified 15 blood composition clusters or subtypes, which we annotated based on the most enriched cell types. Based on similarity of enriched cell types, we further grouped the composition subtypes into three major blood archetypes (Fig. 1c-e, Supplementary Tables 1 2): naive lymphoid (comprising composition subtypes enriched in naive CD8+, effector memory, Th2, and early and late naive CD4+ T cells), cytotoxic (including composition subtypes enriched in conventional NK, effector memory, cytotoxic CD4+, ZNF683 high terminally differentiated CD8+ T cells, and adaptive NK cells) and myeloid (including composition subtypes enriched in monocytes as well as effector memory T cell and NK cells).

Additionally, two rare archetypes included one characterised by expanded CD5+ B cells consistent with monoclonal B cell lymphocytosis (MBL), a precursor state to chronic lymphocytic leukaemia 18, 19 , and another defined by a type I IFN signature across multiple lineages, potentially implying inflammatory stimuli such as viral infection. We visualised the archetypes and composition subtypes on a force-directed graph, building a map of blood composition across populations (Fig. 1c).

Blood archetypes showed distinct demographic associations. The naive lymphoid archetype was more predominant in younger individuals and females, whereas the myeloid archetype was more pronounced in males both in UKB and G&H, suggesting generalisability of the archetypes across populations with distinct ancestries and age structures (Fig. 1d, Extended Data Fig. 1a, Supplementary Table 3). Among the fine-grained composition subtypes, the naive lymphoid CD8+ T cell-high subtype showed the strongest association with young age in both sexes, suggesting a basal composition that can progress to other archetypes (Extended Data Fig. 1a). In contrast, the cytotoxic archetype increased with age and was strongly associated with cytomegalovirus (CMV) infection (Fig. 1d). Despite these associations, the archetypes showed substantial overlap in terms of age and sex, indicating that the archetypes capture variation beyond basic demographic factors. Our approach suggests that blood variation across populations can be broadly summarised between an axis defined by myeloidbiased and naive or cytotoxic lymphoid-biased archetypes, with more fine-grained composition subtypes identifiable in each of the archetypes.

Gene regulation underlying archetype trajectories

To chart the limits of blood compositional space, we sought to define trajectories that position individuals along the major axes of blood variation (Fig. 2a). We inferred trajectories and pseudotime across samples (instead of cells) using scFates 24 with cell state fractions as the feature space, defining the CD8+ T cell-high naive lymphoid subtype as the trajectory root based on its enrichment in young individuals. From this starting point, trajectories progressed through a central intermediate region of the cell composition embedding before branching towards extremes in each archetype, with increasing dominance of specific immune lineages and states with increasing pseudotime.

To robustly quantify the extent of skewing of the cell state repertoire towards few or single cell states, we computed Shannon entropy of sample-level cell type composition (Fig. 2b), where high entropy corresponds to 'normal' blood where cell types are evenly distributed. Entropy decreased progressively along the blood composition trajectories, correlating inversely with pseudotime and defining a subset of individuals with an outlier low entropy, with few cell states dominating their blood composition. We termed this loss of blood diversity 'lineage sweep', reflecting the dominance of one lineage (Fig. 2b). Although entropy decreased with age, age alone did not explain the most extreme decreases in entropy (Extended Data Fig. 3a).

Clonal haematopoiesis across blood archetypes

Clonal haematopoiesis of indeterminate potential (CHIP), marked by somatic mutations found in myeloid malignancies could influence mature blood cell states, blood archetypes and underlie outlier blood states. Across the CARDINAL UKB cohort, we identified 98 individuals harbouring CHIP at study enrolment, most frequently involving DNMT3A (n=71), TET2 (n=17), as well as ASXL1 and PPM1D (Fig. 4d). Multiple myeloid cell types were enriched in CHIP carriers, including S100+ CD14+ monocytes and type 2 conventional dendritic cells (cDC2), whereas lymphoid states such as naive B cells were depleted (Extended Data Fig. 6f).

However, after adjustment for age, sex and ancestry, several type I IFN states in addition to cDC2 were enriched in CHIP carriers (Fig. 4e). Consistently, CHIP carriers were enriched in the type I IFN archetype (Fig. 4f), suggesting myeloid bias to be a more general age-related trend and a link between the IFN-responsive inflammatory blood states and CHIP.

Archetypes resolve blood count abnormalities

We reasoned that the blood archetypes could facilitate diagnostics of clinical blood count abnormalities by revealing subtypes reflecting the mechanisms and cell types involved, aided by composition trajectories, clonality and GRN activity inference.

We integrated complete blood counts with the cell state fractions and archetypes and defined blood count abnormalities based on clinically used cutoffs (Fig. 5a). Common leukocyte abnormalities, including monocytosis, lymphocytosis and lymphopenia were enriched in distinct archetypes and composition subtypes (Fig. 5b-c, Extended Data Fig. 9a), similarly as a broader set of abnormalities across CBC parameters (Extended Data Fig. 9b). For example, both monocytosis and lymphopenia were associated with two distinct composition subtypes, type I IFN and monocyte-rich myeloid. Lymphopenia and monocytosis in the type I IFN-archetype manifested in largely non-overlapping samples, suggesting that some individuals are more prone to respond to interferon by an increase in circulating monocytes, while others respond with decreased lymphocytes (Extended Data Fig. 9c). Lymphocytosis could be grouped into CD5+ B cell-driven, CD8+ ZNF683 high TEMRA-driven, CD4 CTL-driven or adaptive NK-driven subtypes, with the CD4+ CTL-driven cases exhibiting increased clonality.

We next sought to define regulon activities underlying blood count abnormalities (Fig. 5d).

This approach revealed two types of monocytosis, myeloid bias-driven monocytosis with heightened RXRA and MAFB regulon activity, and type I IFN-driven monocytosis with increased ASCL2, HES1, ETV7 and IRF9 activity.

We found that blood abnormalities were more common in samples with lineage sweeps than those without (Extended Data Fig. 9d-e). However, lineage sweeps were found also in samples with no abnormalities in CBC, indicating that the scRNA-seq-derived cell states can reveal extreme blood skewing in the absence of blood count abnormalities. Moreover, not all cases of expansive blood abnormalities such as lymphocytosis manifested as lineage sweeps in scRNA-seq, indicating utility of scRNA-seq in distinguishing phenotypically skewed expansions.

Archetypes stratify infection and immunotherapy outcomes

Finally, to enable classification of archetypes and composition subtypes in external datasets, we developed ArcheTypist, a classification model based on the CellTypist framework 55 (Fig. 6a). Following label transfer of our cell state annotations to a dataset, ArcheTypist uses the matrix of cell type fractions across samples to classify each sample into blood archetypes or more fine-grained composition subtypes, as well as to project samples to a reference embedding using nearest neighbours in the cell state fraction space. ArcheTypist achieved high performance in a held-out test set split from the CARDINAL dataset both for archetypes (F1 score 0.83-0.87) and composition subtypes (F1 score 0.57-0.85), allowing subtyping blood composition in external single-cell cohorts (Extended Data Fig. 10).

Demonstrating the utility of mapping external samples to our reference atlas and archetypes, blood samples from COVID-19 positive donors 4 were enriched in the type I IFN archetype, particularly within a short duration since symptom onset, and depleted in the cytotoxic archetype (Fig. 6b-c, Extended Data Fig. 9f-g). In longitudinal blood samples from a COVID-19 human challenge study 5 , donors who remained stably in the naive lymphoid archetype experienced abortive or transient infections, whereas progression through the type I IFN archetype to the myeloid archetype was associated with sustained infection (Fig. 6d, Extended Data Fig. 9h). In pre-treatment blood samples from a cohort of anti-PD-1/anti-CTLA-4 checkpoint blockade cancer immunotherapy-treated melanoma patients 56 , the naive lymphoid archetype was associated with lower immune-related adverse events compared to the cytotoxic and myeloid archetypes (Fig. 6e). Taken together, these results show that blood archetypes provide a framework for translating variation in blood immune composition into clinically meaningful patient stratification across disease contexts.

Discussion

Based on thousands of blood transcriptomic profiles at cellular resolution, we propose that blood states can be grouped into archetypes comprising individuals with similar blood composition. We use this framework to contextualise axes of variation in human blood across the population, including phenotypic extremes and clonal expansion, and ultimately to explain variation in blood counts and disease-associated perturbations (Fig. 6f).

The archetype model enables placing individuals along trajectories towards increasingly extreme blood compositions. Originating from a CD8+ naive lymphoid composition dominating in young individuals, the blood composition of an individual may progress towards a naive CD4+ composition more common in females, a cytotoxic archetype associated with CMVinduced clonal expansion of cytotoxic lymphocytes 8 more common over age, or a myeloid archetype more frequent in males. In addition to demographics, blood archetypes were associated with risk factors for common diseases. The naive lymphoid archetype was linked to a more favourable cardiometabolic profile and may represent a healthier immune state. In contrast, the myeloid archetype may reflect ageing-associated myeloid bias 21, 22 , potentially exacerbated by cardiovascular risk factors. Progression towards archetypal extremes was accompanied by lineage sweep, a progressive reduction in blood compositional diversity driven by expansion of restricted cellular programmes, representing an important and previously underappreciated dimension of immune variation. We identify three broad routes towards such extremes. In the cytotoxic archetype, extreme states arise through adaptive clonal expansion of cytotoxic lymphocytes, consistent with chronic antigen exposure such as CMV⁸. In the CD5+ B cell archetype, they reflect premalignant lymphoid expansion accompanied by regulatory rewiring away from conventional B cell programmes, chromosomal alterations that link bulk mCA findings 11, 35, 36 to a specific cell state, and germline eQTLs detectable only within this premalignant state and not in common cell types. The eQTL finding suggests that somatic evolution can unmask latent germline regulatory effects. Myeloid clonal haematopoiesis, in turn, was associated with the type I IFN archetype, which may reflect the increased infection susceptibility 43, 44 or inflammatory priming 45 reported in CHIP carriers. Defining the regulatory programmes that sustain these outlier states may reveal ways to prevent progression towards dysfunctional ageing-associated states or malignancy.

We provide the cell2clone framework for studying clonality in emerging population-scale immune atlases 2, 3, [61] [62] [63] [64] [65] . This approach allowed us to link clonal expansion to lymphocyte subsets beyond those previously known to harbour clonal expansions. Clonal expansions in exhausted or atypical B cells raise the possibility that they may underlie monoclonal B cell lymphocytosis with a non-CLL CD5-phenotype, or represent the cell of origin of rare B cell malignancies carrying the i(17q) aberration 66, 67 . We envision that cell2clone unlocks a new dimension of biology in population-scale immune atlases by enabling analyses of genetic determinants of clonal expansion across diverse cohorts and guiding research into lymphoid clonal haematopoiesis.

Clinically, the archetype framework resolves blood count abnormalities that appear indistinguishable in CBC into biologically distinct subtypes. ArcheTypist places samples from focused studies in a population-scale context, in an analogous manner to how cellular reference atlases allow placing single cells in the context of cell type and state variation.

ArcheTypist linked progression through the type I IFN archetype to the myeloid archetype to sustained COVID-19 infection, and the naive lymphoid archetype to lower immune-related adverse events after checkpoint blockade in melanoma patients. These results point towards a next generation blood diagnostic measurement, CBC 2.0, integrating blood archetypes, cell states, clonality, regulatory programmes and somatic alterations.

Our study has limitations. The cross-sectional nature of the data prevents assessing whether a person's archetype is stable over time, warranting future longitudinal sampling. Moreover, the direction of causality of archetype associations with health records remains undefined. In addition, clonality and copy-number alterations inference from transcriptomes captures mainly large clones, potentially underestimating their frequencies.

Population-scale single-cell atlases make it possible to define the normal range of tissue composition, the recurrent subtypes within it, and the outliers at its edges. The archetype framework offers a general model for this task. Beyond blood, applying the same principles to emerging population-scale datasets of solid tissues can enable identification of tissue archetypes, trajectories, and pathological extremes across diverse human organs.

Methods

The CARDINAL dataset and cell annotations

The CARDINAL scRNA-seq dataset generation, including sample collection, preprocessing and cell annotation strategy including marker genes are described in Giacopuzzi et al. 17 Complete blood counts from the same samples were obtained using a Sysmex XN-1000 haematology analyser as described in Giacopuzzi et al. 17

Defining blood composition subtypes and archetypes

To assign samples into subgroups based on the composition of co-varying cell types, a sample-by-cell-type matrix with each feature representing the proportion of a given cell type (CT3 level) within the cells in the sample was generated. For easy exploration and analysis, the cell composition atlas was loaded into an AnnData 68 object, with cell types as "var_names", samples as "obs_names" and cell type fractions as the data matrix, enabling analysis using Scanpy 69 in a similar manner as a conventional single-cell object. Convenience functions to facilitate object building are available as part of our companion package ArcheTypist. Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed and neighbours and graph-based Leiden clusters of samples reflecting similar cell type compositions (blood composition subtypes) were defined using Scanpy with default parameters. To identify characteristic cell types for the archetypes, differences between cell type composition in each blood composition subtype compared to all others were tested using Mann-Whitney U-test on CT3 fractions out of total cells per donor, followed by Benjamini-Hochberg adjustment for multiple testing. Cell composition clusters enriched in similar cell types were merged to obtain blood subtypes termed archetypes. The archetypes and blood composition subtypes were visualised on a force-directed atlas embedding computed using the neighbours in the cell composition space using http://sc.tl/.draw_graph" in Scanpy.

Associations with biobank metadata

Associations between metrics derived from scRNA-seq, including archetypes, blood composition subtypes, cell state entropy and clonality, and UK Biobank clinical phenotypes were evaluated using sample-level measures treated as binary variables (archetype and blood composition subtype membership) or continuous variables (cell state entropy and clonality).

Diagnosis variables defined by recorded dates (for example, first reported disease events) were converted into binary "ever" indicators, with non-missing dates coded as cases.

Continuous phenotypes were analysed directly after numeric coercion. Variables with insufficient case counts or limited non-missing observations were excluded to ensure model stability.

For each immune metric-phenotype pair, we fitted linear regression models with the immune metric as the dependent variable and the phenotype of interest as the independent variable.

Models were adjusted for age, sex, and ethnic background where indicated. Categorical covariates were encoded using reference-level dummy variables. Observations with missing values in the outcome, predictor, or covariates were excluded on a per-model basis.

Associations between archetypes or blood composition subtypes and disease in G&H were tested using generalised additive models. Separate binomial models were fitted for each archetype or composition subtype, with membership in the selected subtype or archetype coded as the binary outcome and all remaining groups as the reference. Disease status was included as the predictor of interest, with adjustment for sex, ancestry, and age. Age was modelled as a smooth term using cubic regression splines, with sex-specific smooths where specified. Models were fitted using restricted maximum likelihood. Multiple-testing correction was performed using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure.

Gene regulatory network inference

For GRN inference using SCENIC 25 , the UKB cohort was first subsampled to ~280,000 cells, with including 20,000 cells from medium-abundance cell types (at least 1,000 cells in total) and preserving 5% of cells from abundant cell types (at least 20,000 cells in total) and 50% of cells from sparse cell types (less than 20,000 cells in total) at the CT3 level.

Cell-type-specific regulons were inferred using pySCENIC 70 , which infers TF-target gene relationships based on gene co-expression and enrichment of TF-binding motifs near target gene promoters. To reduce the impact of sparsity in single-cell expression data and improve computational tractability, cells were further aggregated into mini-metacells comprising 10-15 cells. After obtaining the regulons from SCENIC, the regulons were scored using http://scanpy.tl/.score_genes" in Scanpy and an object of patient-specific pseudobulks at the CT3 level comprising both the UKB and G&H cohorts was obtained. Regulons differentially active between cell types were identified using a Mann-Whitney U test between pseudobulks of one cell type and comparator cell types, requiring at least 20 cells for a pseudobulk to be included in the analysis. Regulons were correlated with cell type fraction out of all PBMCs using Spearman correlation, requiring at least 20 cells for a pseudobulk to be included in the analysis.

Cell composition trajectory inference

Cell composition trajectories were inferred using scFates. The cell type fraction-based PCA and neighbours was used as basis and the tree computed using function scf.tl.tree in scFates with the following parameters: method="ppt", Nodes=125, ppt_lambda=100, ppt_sigma=0.0125, ppt_nsteps=100. Results were projected on the force-directed atlas embedding. A root was selected based on the vicinity of a trajectory branch terminal node to the CD8+ naive lymphoid composition subtype, identified as the composition subtype highly enriched in young individuals. Pseudotime was computed using http://scfates.tl/.pseudotime in scFates.

Milestones were annotated based on their overlap with previously defined cell composition subtypes. Cell type fractions were correlated with pseudotime along a trajectory between a starting point, such as the naive lymphoid CD8+ milestone, and other terminal milestones using Spearman correlation.

Cell composition entropy

Shannon entropy was used to quantify the diversity of cell state composition within each donor.

Cell state fractions were first averaged across all samples from the same donor and normalised so that fractions summed to one. Shannon entropy was then calculated using "scipy.stats.entropy" in SciPy 71 on the resulting relative cell state distribution using a base-2 logarithm. To avoid undefined values arising from zero fractions, a small pseudocount (10⁻¹²) was applied before computation. Higher entropy values indicate a more even distribution of cell states, whereas lower values indicate dominance by a smaller number of cell states. Cell state entropy was calculated after subsampling each donor to 250 cells. Donors with < 250 total cells were excluded from the analysis, as entropy was found markedly reduced in these donors likely due to insufficient sampling. Lineage sweep was defined as cell state entropy < 4.5.

Adaptive lymphocyte clonality inference using cell2clone

We built the cell2clone workflow for identifying clonally expanded adaptive lymphocyte populations in scRNA-seq data without VDJ sequencing information. For each cell, expression of constant region genes was quantified as normalised expression values. For T cells, β-chain constant region usage was assessed using TRBC1 and TRBC2. For B cells, light chain usage was assessed using the κ-chain gene (IGKC) and λ-chain genes (IGLC), in which case normalised expression values were summed across all λ-chain genes. A cell was classified as κ (or TRBC1) if transcripts from the corresponding gene or gene set were higher than those of λ (or TRBC2) and vice versa. Cells in which neither gene set was detected were classified as ambiguous.

Clonality was quantified at the sample-by-cell-type level. We ran cell2clone across all B and T cell subtypes at the CT3 cell type level for all samples and ranked cell types by their median dominant clone size. For each sample and each annotated cell type, the number of cells classified into each state was counted after excluding ambiguous cells. Sample-cell type combinations with fewer than 20 classified cells were excluded from downstream analysis to reduce instability due to low counts. Clonality was summarised using several complementary measures, including the fraction of cells assigned to the first state and Shannon entropy as a measure of diversity within the two-state system. In addition, a median-centered directional log2 ratio of the counts of the two states was calculated to capture both magnitude and direction of imbalance, as well as absolute value of this as a directionless clonality metric. A clone size score was calculated to quantify the deviation of the largest clone fraction of each cell type from the expected value in a fully polyclonal cell population. The fraction assigned to the dominant clone was first constrained to lie within a small interval away from 0 and 1 to improve numerical stability. The expected clone fraction was then estimated either separately within each cell type (default) as the median largest clone fraction of that cell type, or globally across all cells as the overall median. For each cell type, the absolute deviation from the expected value was computed and normalised by the maximum possible deviation given the expected fraction, yielding a score bounded between 0 and 1. A score of 0 indicates that the observed clone fraction equals the expected value (i.e. fully polyclonal population), whereas a score of 1 corresponds to the largest possible deviation (i.e. fully monoclonal population).

To contextualise clonality within overall cellular composition, the proportion of the total sequenced cells in each sample represented by a given cell type was calculated using all cells from that sample, including ambiguous and unclassified cells. This separation ensured that clonality metrics reflected confidently classified cells, while abundance metrics reflected overall representation within the sampled cellular compartment.

To analyse whether clonal expansions in different cell types co-occur in the same individuals, graph-based clusters were derived based on the cell type-specific clone size score metric using Leiden clustering and the variation was visualised using a UMAP (Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection) embedding.

Permutation test for cell types harbouring clonal expansions

To identify cell types enriched for samples with unusually large clone size scores, a permutation-based enrichment test was performed. A reference cell type, such as naive B or T cells, was used to define a clone size score threshold corresponding to a specified percentile of its score distribution (99th percentile unless otherwise stated). For each test cell type, the observed proportion of samples with clone size scores exceeding this threshold was calculated. Statistical significance was assessed using a permutation test in which cell type labels were randomly permuted while clone size scores were held fixed, preserving the overall score distribution. For each permutation, the proportion of samples assigned to the test cell type that exceeded the reference threshold was recalculated, generating a null distribution of expected proportions under random assignment. One-sided empirical p values were computed as the proportion of permutations producing an equal or greater fraction of highscoring cells than observed. Cell types represented in fewer than 10 samples were excluded from analysis. P values were adjusted for multiple testing across cell types using the Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate procedure.

Assignment of single cells to putative clones

Clonal assignment was performed using a sample-aware clustering approach combined with receptor chain dominance criteria. Briefly, using a set of selected samples and cells restricted to an annotation hierarchy level (e.g. CT1="B" for B cells), PCA dimensionality reduction and k-neighbour graph construction were carried out using Scanpy, with T cell receptor and immunoglobulin genes excluded from highly variable gene selection to prevent clustering from being driven by antigen receptor expression. To avoid cross-sample aggregation, Leiden clustering was performed separately within each sample.

Cells were classified according to mutually exclusive receptor chain states (TRBC1 versus TRBC2 for T cells; κ versus λ for B cells) as described above. Within each sample-specific Leiden cluster, the proportion of classified cells belonging to each chain state was calculated.

Clusters exceeding a predefined minimum size and exhibiting dominance of a single chain state above a selected threshold (≥80% as default) were designated as putative clones; all other clusters were labeled polyclonal. Cells without confident chain assignment were excluded from purity calculations but retained in final labeling. This strategy identifies transcriptionally coherent, chain-dominant populations consistent with clonal expansion.

Sample-specific transcriptional segregation analysis

To assess whether clonal expansion is associated with unique cell states restricted to samples harbouring them, sample-specific segregation of transcriptional states was quantified within each cell type using silhouette analysis in the principal component (PC) space. Analyses were restricted to samples classified as having high clonality ("top") or low clonality ("bottom"), and to the relevant annotation hierarchy level (e.g. CT1="B" for B cells). For each cell type and clonality group separately, cells were represented using the first 30 PCs from the gene expression PCA embedding. Samples represented by fewer than 10 cells within a given cell type were excluded. To reduce the influence of unequal cell recovery between samples, a maximum of 300 cells was randomly selected per sample.

Sample identity was used as the cluster label, and a silhouette coefficient was calculated for each cell using Euclidean distances in PC space. For each cell type, sample-level silhouette scores were compared between the high-and lowclonality groups using a two-sided Mann-Whitney U test. Multiple testing across cell types was controlled using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure. Higher silhouette scores in the highclonality group were interpreted as increased sample-specific segregation of transcriptional states, whereas scores near zero indicated greater mixing of cells between samples.

Analysis of cell states associated with clonal haematopoiesis of indeterminate potential

Samples with clonal haematopoiesis of indeterminate potential (CHIP) were defined using the UKB data field 30105 "Clonal haematopoiesis of indeterminate potential (CHIP) number of variants" as having at least 1 CHIP mutation.

Associations between CHIP status and individual cell states were assessed using both univariable and multivariable analyses. Initially, each cell state was compared between CHIPpositive and CHIP-negative individuals. Group means were calculated and effect sizes were summarised as log2 fold changes, calculated from the ratio of group means after the addition of a small constant (10⁻⁹) to avoid division by zero. Statistical significance was evaluated using two-sided Welch's t-tests. P values were corrected for multiple testing across all cell states using the Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) procedure.

To adjust for demographic factors, a separate ordinary least squares linear regression model was fitted for each cell state. Cell state abundance was modelled as the dependent variable, with CHIP status included as the primary predictor and age, sex, and ethnic background included as covariates. For each model, the regression coefficient (β), standard error, t statistic, 95% confidence interval, and P value corresponding to CHIP status were extracted.

Standardised regression coefficients were calculated by scaling β according to the standard deviations of the outcome and CHIP status. Effect sizes were additionally expressed as the percentage change relative to the overall mean abundance of each cell state. P values from all regression models were adjusted for multiple testing using the Benjamini-Hochberg FDR procedure.

Gene-specific analyses were performed in an analogous manner. For each recurrently mutated CHIP gene, individuals carrying a mutation in the gene of interest were compared with other mutation carriers. For each cell state, group means, medians, and log2 fold changes were calculated, and statistical significance was assessed using two-sided Welch's t-tests. Archetype classification

To enable classification of samples into the archetypes and cell composition subtypes defined here in external datasets, we developed the ArcheTypist framework. The prediction consists of two stages. First, a conventional CellTypist 55 prediction is run on a dataset to transfer the cell state labels defined in our study. Second, an AnnData object is derived based on cell state fractions across samples. This object is then used to classify samples into archetypes and composition subtypes with two available methods, either using another prediction round based on CellTypist logistic regression using an ArcheTypist model where features are cell states instead of genes, or a nearest-neighbour method described below. In the CellTypist-based logistic regression method, the predicted labels at single-sample resolution are used as the predictions.

To assess the reproducibility of archetypes and blood composition subtypes, samples from the CARDINAL dataset with existing archetype and composition subtype annotations were randomly divided into training (80%) and test (20%) sets. ArcheTypist was trained on the training set using archetype assignments as class labels and subsequently applied to the heldout test set. Classification performance was evaluated using precision, recall, and F1 score for each archetype, together with macro-averaged and weighted-averaged summary statistics.

Performance metrics were calculated using the classification_report function from the Python package scikit-learn.

Mapping query to reference

Query samples were mapped onto a reference force-directed FA2 graph using similarity in the cell type fraction space. Reference and query AnnData objects were first restricted to their shared cell type features. Cell type fraction matrices were extracted from both objects and converted to dense arrays where required. For each query sample, the five nearest reference samples were identified using cosine distance on the shared cell type fraction profiles.

Neighbour weights were calculated as the inverse cosine distance, with a small numerical offset to avoid division by zero, and normalised to sum to one for each query sample. Query FA2 coordinates were then inferred as the weighted average of the FA2 coordinates of the corresponding nearest reference neighbours.

Mapping diagnostics were stored for each query sample, including the closest reference sample, the nearest-neighbour cosine distance, and mapping similarity, defined as one minus the cosine distance. Reference annotations for archetype and composition subtype were transferred to query samples using a weighted nearest-neighbour vote. For each annotation, neighbour weights were summed by label, and the label with the highest total weight was assigned to the query sample. The maximum summed weight was stored as the transfer confidence.

The CellTypist cell type prediction models for celltype 2 and celltype 3 labels are available in the CARDINAL data central repository (data will be released upon publication) and through the ArcheTypist package for celltype 3.
