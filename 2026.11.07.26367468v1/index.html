How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. Abstract

Excess mortality can be estimated from crude death counts or from age-standardised mortality rates (ASMRs). Although both approaches are widely used, their analytical relationship and the effects of methodological choices such as standard population, age-group definition, and baseline selection are not always explicitly considered. The analytical relationship between relative excess mortality based on death counts and ASMRs was derived and both approaches were applied to mortality in Germany during the COVID19 pandemic 2020-2023. Expected mortality was estimated by linear extrapolation of pre-pandemic trends. ASMR calculations were repeated using different standard populations, age-group definitions, open-ended age groups, and reference periods. Death-count-and ASMR-based P-scores were analytically equivalent when the age distribution of the standard population corresponded to that of the population under study. Empirically, both approaches yielded closely corresponding estimates for Germany. The effect of the standard population depended on the age distribution of excess mortality and was particularly pronounced in 2020, when excess mortality was concentrated at older ages. The definition of the open-ended age group had an additional effect: aggregating ages 80+ altered the pre-pandemic ASMR trend, and consequently changed the estimated excess mortality. Variation of the pre-pandemic reference period substantially affected P-scores, but death-count-and ASMR-based estimates changed in a closely corresponding manner. Relative excess mortality derived from death counts and ASMRs is closely related, and differences between the approaches can be explained by their underlying weighting and baseline assumptions. For trend-based excess mortality estimation, the standard population, age-group definition, and baseline specification are therefore integral methodological choices. Death counts provide a simple and transparent reference measure for analyses within a single population, whereas age-standardised estimates require careful consideration of these additional assumptions.

1 Introduction

1.1 Excess Mortality and its Quantification

Excess mortality is defined as the degree to which the observed number of deaths exceeds the number expected under a counterfactual baseline. It has been widely used to assess the overall mortality impact of the COVID-19 pandemic because it is based on all-cause mortality and is therefore independent of the classification and reporting of individual causes of death.

Different mortality measures can be used for the calculation of excess mortality. The most direct approach is based on crude death counts, i.e. the absolute number of deaths in a population during a specified period. Studies comparing multiple countries use death counts, as these values are publicly available from national statistic offices and the calculation is straightforward [1], [2], [3], [4].

Alternatively, mortality can be expressed as the Crude Mortality Rate (CMR), relating the number of deaths to the corresponding population size, or as an Age-Standardised Mortality Rate (ASMR), in which age-specific mortality rates are weighted according to a defined standard population. These approaches differ substantially in their data requirements and in the assumptions required for their calculation. A smaller number of authors use these measures to quantify excess mortality during the years of the COVID-19 pandemic [5], [6], [7], Regardless of the mortality measure used, expected mortality is commonly estimated by extrapolating mortality patterns observed during a pre-pandemic reference period which raises the question how to calculate the counterfactual number of deaths. In aging populations, death counts are rising continuously and extrapolating existing trends is a straightforward approach. Linear extrapolation makes the particularly simple assumption that the direction and magnitude of the observed trend keep the same and continue beyond the reference period.

Poisson regression is used in mortality surveillance systems such as EuroMOMO for modelling annual mortality trends [8]. For the German pre-pandemic mortality data considered here, however, the gradual increase in annual deaths results in only minimal curvature of the exponential trend implied by Poisson regression. Consequently, linear and Poisson regression yield very similar baseline estimates, with differences below 0.5% for all reference periods investigated [9].

Various national statistical offices used averages or median values of previous years rather than extrapolating an existing trend [10], [11]. In the presence of a rising mortality trend, such approaches result in a lower counterfactual baseline and consequently higher estimates of excess mortality.

More complex models implementing spline-based approaches, have been used by WHO and IHME to estimate the global mortality impact of the pandemic [1], [3]. While such models can account for nonlinear patterns within the observed period, extrapolation beyond the fitted data is also influenced by the estimated curvature. Consequently, relatively small differences in curvature near the end of the reference period may result in substantially different counterfactual trajectories and these results have been discussed intensively [12]. Linear extrapolation avoids curvature and therefore provides a simple and transparent reference model.

As age standardisation is intended to remove the influence of differences in population age structure, the effect of population ageing is removed from the mortality trend. This reveals the underlying longterm decline in age-specific mortality associated with increasing life expectancy, resulting in declining ASMR trends. Analogous to increasing trends in death counts, such declining trends can be approximated and extrapolated linearly as well [7].

This raises a fundamental methodological question: under which conditions does excess mortality derived from age-standardised mortality rates provide the same result as the substantially simpler calculation based directly on death counts? Conversely, when the two approaches differ, it is important to understand whether these differences reflect the underlying mortality pattern or arise from the choice of standard population and age-group aggregation.

The present study investigates this relationship analytically and empirically. The mathematical relationship between excess mortality derived from death counts and from ASMR is first established. The effects of standard population and age-group aggregation are then examined using mortality in Germany during the COVID-19 pandemic as a case study. Particular attention is given to the contrast between years in which excess mortality was concentrated in older age groups and years in which relative excess mortality was more broadly distributed across age groups.

4 Summary and Discussion

4.1 Relationship between Death-Count-and ASMR-Based Excess Mortality

The analytical and empirical results demonstrate a close relationship between excess mortality derived from death counts and from age-standardised mortality rates. Death-count-based relative excess mortality can be expressed as a weighted combination of age-specific excess mortality, with the expected numbers of deaths defining the weights. ASMR-based excess mortality modifies these weights according to the relationship between the age distribution of the standard population and that of the population under study. If both distributions are identical, both approaches yield the same relative excess mortality.

This relationship is reflected in the German data. Although annual death counts increased during the pre-pandemic period while ASMR decreased, both approaches produced similar P-scores for 2020-2023. Death counts therefore provide a comparatively simple and transparent measure of relative excess mortality when a single population is analysed over time. The analytical relationship clarifies under which conditions both approaches provide similar results and why they may differ.

4.2 Effects of the Standard Population and Age-Group Definition

.

The choice of standard population affects ASMR-based excess mortality through the weighting of agespecific mortality. The magnitude of this effect depends on the age distribution of excess mortality itself. This explains the larger sensitivity observed in 2020, when excess mortality was strongly concentrated in the oldest age group, compared with 2022, when elevated mortality was distributed much more broadly across adult age groups. The changing age pattern of excess mortality during the pandemic has also been demonstrated in the underlying age-stratified analysis.

The definition of the age groups introduces an additional and less obvious effect. Aggregating 80-84 and 85+ into a single 80+ group leaves changes in age composition within this group unaccounted for by standardisation. Because mortality differs substantially between 80-84 and 85+, such compositional changes affect the mortality rate of the aggregated group. In contrast, when both groups are standardised separately, their relative weights are fixed by the standard population.

This distinction is particularly relevant for trend-based excess mortality estimates. With DE2020 as the standard population, the aggregated and stratified calculations coincide when the age composition corresponds to that of 2020. In earlier years, however, the population aged 80+ was relatively younger. Consequently, aggregation changes the pre-pandemic ASMR trend and therefore the extrapolated expected mortality. The resulting difference in the P-score is thus not primarily caused by the observed mortality in the target year, but by a different baseline.

This finding highlights an interaction between standard population and age-group definition. A standard population can control changes in age structure only at the resolution provided by the selected age groups. For trend-based excess mortality estimation, the standard population should therefore not be regarded merely as a scaling convention; together with the age-group definition, it determines which demographic changes are removed by standardisation and which remain in the mortality trend.

4.3 Impact of Baseline Selection and Methodological Implications

Baseline selection had a substantial influence on the magnitude of estimated excess mortality. However, death-count-and ASMR-based P-scores responded in a closely corresponding manner when the reference period was varied. The 2013-2019 baseline produced estimates approximately in the middle of the range obtained from the alternative reference periods. This agrees with the previous sensitivity analysis, in which the 2013-2019 death-count trend yielded the median projected value and a slope close to the average of the investigated trends.

The 2015-2019 reference period provides a useful counterexample, producing markedly higher excess mortality estimates with both approaches.

Taken together, the results identify three closely related methodological choices affecting estimates of relative excess mortality: the baseline used to estimate expected mortality, the standard population used for age standardisation, and the age-group definition. Death-count-based analysis avoids the latter two choices and therefore provides a useful transparent reference measure for analyses within a single population. ASMR additionally controls for demographic change but introduces assumptions concerning standard population and age-group resolution that need to be reported and considered when interpreting trend-based excess mortality estimates.

The empirical analysis is limited to Germany and to the mortality patterns observed during 2020-2023. The magnitude of the effects identified here may differ in populations with other age structures or mortality patterns. Furthermore, the empirical calculations use linear baseline models, although the analytical relationships between death-count-and ASMR-based excess mortality are not restricted to the German pandemic period. Population estimates used as denominators for age-specific mortality rates are themselves subject to demographic estimation and census revisions, providing an additional source of uncertainty in age-standardised analyses.

5 Conclusions

Death-count-and ASMR-based estimates of relative excess mortality are analytically closely related and can yield equivalent results under defined conditions. The German data demonstrate that differences between the two approaches depend systematically on the standard population, the agegroup definition, and the baseline used to estimate expected mortality.
