To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 6 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Background: Motor imagery (MI) brain-computer interfaces (BCIs) are increasingly investigated for promoting motor recovery after stroke. However, it remains unclear how BCI control improves through repeated training in stroke patients and whether the timing of MI decoding systematically influences this trajectory.

.

1 Introduction

Motor imagery (MI) brain-computer interfaces (BCIs) are widely studied for upperlimb rehabilitation after stroke and are of particular interest for patients whose voluntary motor function is too limited to engage in conventional active practice [1].

Nevertheless, numerous experimental paradigms exist, making it difficult to disentangle the contribution of individual parameters and to conclude on best practices [2].

Consequently, protocol parameters are often fixed across participants even though the behavioral and neurophysiological characteristics relevant to BCI control may differ substantially between patients. This issue is particularly relevant after stroke. Lesion location and extent, severity of the motor deficit, cognitive comorbidity and stage of the recovery trajectory differ from one patient to another, and influence the individual response to a given BCI treatment.

These patient-specific factors can shape the neurophysiological expression of motor imagery, including the oscillatory frequency band in which the discriminant response emerges [3, 4] and how this response evolves over training. As a result, electrophysiology event-related responses within a single cohort can even go in opposite directions [5], so that a group-average effect may not describe the trajectory of an individual patient [6]. As a matter of fact, designing a BCI paradigm for stroke rehabilitation often implies choices inherited from local practices, technical and algorithmic constraints, as well as accommodations to patients' needs [7]. Frameworks for instruction and user training do exist for healthy volunteers, but no equivalent guidance covers rehabilitation after stroke [8, 9].

An important aspect of rehabilitation is the time spent practicing and repeating targeted motor tasks. Conventional therapies deliver around 32 repetitions per session/day, whereas animal models suggest around 500 repetitions are needed to induce cortical synaptic changes [10]. Accordingly, BCI protocols should be intensive in terms of number of MI repetitions/trials and number of consecutive sessions. At the same time, any BCI protocol should also avoid excessive fatigue and drowsiness in patients, knowing that the EEG setup already takes about 20% of the entire session [11].

Another crucial factor is the duration of the trial associated with the MI task, typically a trade-off between 3 [12] and 10 seconds or more [13]. Longer durations may account for changes in MI responses associated with motor system alterations after stroke [14, 15]. Shorter durations are mainly intended to shorten the overall session, made of several consecutive trials, and therefore diminish patients' fatigue.

Finally, most of the approaches in the BCI field have focused on univariate local features, such as EEG power spectra and even-related dynamics changes. However, stroke is a network-level disorder where both MI and recovery depends on the reorganization of intra-and interhemispheric interactions involving primary and associative brain areas [16] [17] [18]. Approaches based on functional connectivity [19] [20] [21] and covariance [22] between EEG signals are therefore more grounded candidates to provide richer network-based feedback in MI-BCI after stroke.

Motivated by the aforementioned considerations, we realized a functional electrical stimulation (FES)-controlled EEG network-based MI-BCI study involving 5 chronic stroke patients over 18 equally-spaced sessions spanning 6 weeks of training. Notably, we manipulated the time allocated for the MI task across sessions according to a singlecase experimental design (SCED) with randomized alternating treatment, so that patients served as their own control [23]. We evaluated whether this intense protocol can be tolerated by patients, whether their online BCI accuracy improves over the training, in association with specific brain activity and network changes, and whether the MI timing had a significant overall impact.

2 Methods

2.1 Experimental protocol

Five chronic BCI-naive stroke patients were included in the study. Inclusion criteria were chronic stroke (≥ 6 months after a first-ever lesion), residual distal upper-limb impairment (UE-FMA < 53) [24], and visible active finger flexion (MRC ≥ 2) [25].

Exclusion criteria included insufficient command comprehension (BDAE < 12) [26], fixed finger or wrist flexor spasticity (mAS = 4) [27], botulinum toxin injection in the forearm or hand within the previous 12 weeks, and any contraindication to FES or EEG recording Other eligibility criteria were reported in the Supplementary Methods (Methods S1), together with the full inclusion assessment battery tests (Methods S2.1). No screening for BCI aptitude was applied This study was part of a larger clinical trial (NCT06951035) that was approved by an independent French research ethics committee (CPP Sud-Méditerranée I, ID-RCB 2024-A00230-47) and authorised by the French National Agency for Medicines and Health Products Safety. All patients gave written informed consent in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

2.1.1 FES-controlled MI-BCI training

Upon enrollment, patients underwent 18 BCI training sessions over six weeks (spaced 1-2 days apart), followed by a three-month post-intervention assessment. Before each session, participants rated their mood, stress and sleep quality on a tactile visual support with a continuous scale from 0 to 100. Fatigue was rated on the same scale before, during and after each session. Spasticity was measured with the mAS scale on the thumb, the index, the other fingers and the wrist, before and after each session. Each segment was graded on the six-level modified Ashworth scale [27], with grade 1+ scored as 2 and higher grades shifted accordingly, and the four segment scores were summed into a global score ranging from 0 to 20. Upper-limb motor functions were measured with the FMA scale before the first and last session. All behavioral and clinical scales were retaken a final time at the follow-up visit. Each session was conducted in a quiet shielded controlled environment. Patients were comfortably seated in front of a screen placed horizontally on a height-adjustable table, with the paretic upper limb supported on a mobile armrest to ensure optimal positioning and minimize fatigue (Fig. 1).

FES was delivered through the FESIA Grasp system [28], positioned on the paretic forearm and calibrated before training to produce a standardized and functional hand opening, more details about FESIA calibration are provided in Methods S2.2.

Patients were instructed to imagine opening their paretic hand in a sustained manner. Specifically, patients were invited to focus on the internal sensation of movement, including muscle contraction and joint displacement (kinesthetic MI), and to visualize finger extension from a first-person perspective (visual MI). A target cup was placed on the table in front of the patients to help them perform the task in an ecological and functional way. Standardized instructions were given before each session and included a brief breathing exercise followed by guided MI. A detailed script was used during the first three sessions and an abbreviated version thereafter (Methods S3.1). Each session consisted of nine consecutive runs of 18 MI trials, corresponding to 162 MI trials in total. The first run was used for calibration and always triggered FES, whereas the subsequent eight testing comprised 144 closed-loop trials in which FES depended on BCI classification (Fig. 2). Each trial began with a 5 s Rest (baseline) phase cued by an open circle on the table-screen, followed by a MI phase, triggered by a red circle, that lasted between 4, 5 or 6 s.

In the final 3-second feedback phase, the FES was delivered only when the MI was correctly detected by the classifier. Random inter-trial intervals between 1 and 2 s were introduced to reduce temporal predictability and anticipation. This trial structure was intended to allow smooth transitions between phases and avoid overly long trials that could increase fatigue or reduce attentional engagement. At the end of each run, the experimenter debriefed with the patient through a discussion, detailed in Methods S3.2.

Online BCI performance was measured in terms accuracy, defined as the number of correctly classified Rest and MI phases over two times the total number of trials.

2.1.2 Single-case experimental design

To evaluate whether the timing of MI decoding affected BCI control, we implemented a randomized alternating-treatments single-case experimental design [23] (Fig. 2b).

Three MI classification timing (MIT) conditions were defined by shifting the onset of a fixed 3-s decoding window to 1 (condition A), 2 (condition B) or 3 (condition C) seconds after cue onset. Accordingly, the entire cue period lasted 4, 5, or 6 s so that classification always used the final 3 s of the MI phase. These conditions were assigned using a balanced randomization scheme, ensuring equal presentation across the 18 sessions (Fig. 2). Patients, but not experimenters, were blinded to the MI duration tested in each session.

2.2.1 EEG acquisition

EEG was recorded with a 64-channel BrainAmp system (Brain Products, Germany) with active electrodes and a 500 Hz sampling frequency, following a 10-20 montage and referenced to the mastoids (Supplementary Figures (Fig. S1)). Electrode impedance was lowered and maintained below 10 kΩ at the beginning of each session.

Bilateral forearm electromyography was recorded and monitored during the session to ensure that muscular contractions were not present during the MI phases. The following signal processing and statistical analysis were carried out with the MNE-Python library, while online feature extraction and decoder training used the HappyFeat software [29]. The online BCI pipeline and its interaction with the FES device was implemented using the OpenViBE software [30]. A notch filter at 50 Hz and its harmonics with a common average reference was applied on the signal. The frontotemporal electrodes FT9 and FT10, as well as the inion electrode Iz, were excluded from the analyses.

2.2.2 Feature extraction

To ensure comparability, all the EEG features used to control the BCI were calculated on the basis of a fixed 3-second window. During the Rest phase, this window corresponded to the first three seconds; during the MI phase, the position of the window depended on the SCED, i.e. 1 s after the cue for 4 s MI duration, 2 s after the cue for 5 s MI duration, and 3 s after the cue for 6 s MI duration (Fig. 2b).

On the segmented windows, we estimated the power spectral density (ϕ) over 0.25 s epochs shifted by 0.161 s. To get the most reliable estimate for such short-time windows, we used an autoregressive Burg model of order 24 with a spectral resolution of 2 Hz. The resulting estimates were eventually averaged to get one robust measure for the 3-second window. Functional connectivity was calculated on sliding 0.5 s epochs shifted by 0.32 s to cover the entire 3-second window. Specifically, we computed the imaginary coherence [31] to measure phase-synchronized interactions between brain signals while suppressing spurious zero-lag correlations due to volume conduction. This measure has been widely used in MI-BCI studies [17, 19, 20, 32]. The resulting brain connectivity network can be represented as a full weighted matrix where X ij is the cross-spectrum between the EEG signals recorded at electrodes i and j at frequency f and X ii , X jj are the corresponding autospectra.

Similarly to power spectra, we used a bivariate autoregressive Burg model of order 24 with a spectral resolution of 2 Hz to estimate the auto-and cross-spectra. The resulting connectivity matrices W were eventually averaged to get one robust network for the 3-second window.

Using the network neuroscience formalism, we calculated the node strength (s) as the sum of the weighted connections (or links) incident to a given EEG electrode i (i.e. a node of the network) where N is the total number of nodes.

By locally integrating information from the other nodes, node strengths make it possible to identify the most strongly connected electrodes in the EEG network, thus reducing complexity and favoring interpretability [19, 20].

2.2.3 Feature selection, classifier, and FES control

We adopted a semi-automatic procedure to select the most relevant brain activity (power spectrum, ϕ) and network (node strength connectivity, s) features.

Candidate features were first visualized on a r 2 channel-frequency map [33] computed between MI and Rest phases, for ϕ and s features separately. They were then ranked according to their magnitude, only considering the sensorimotor electrodes (FC, C, CP) (Fig. S1) within the alpha (8 -12 Hz) and beta range (13 -30 Hz). A regularized linear discriminant analysis (LDA) classifier [34] was separately trained on three different balanced combinations of power and network features, using the best one, two or three ϕ-s pairs. The combination achieving the highest classification accuracy was selected. This feature selection process was repeated at three different time points during the session to allow the classifier to adapt to possible non-stationarities arising from mental fatigue, mental strategy changes and signal drift (Fig. 2b). Specifically, the first classifier was trained on the initial calibration run and used for testing runs 1 -2. It was then retrained using data from testing runs 1 -2 and applied to testing runs 3 -5. Finally, the classifier was retrained on data from testing runs 3 -5 and applied to testing runs 6 -8.

2.3 Offline performance and neurophysiological measures

2.3.1 Offline BCI performance

Three offline metrics of MI-BCI skill were computed for each run [35]. Run-wise crossvalidated accuracy was computed using four-fold cross-validation within each run, with common spatial patterns and a linear discriminant analysis over 8 -30 Hz, ensuring a constant amount of training data throughout the protocol.

To complement this classifier-dependent measure, we quantified two classifier-free properties of the covariance distributions: their separation and their within-class consistency. Rest distinctiveness was the Riemannian distance between the imagery and rest covariance means divided by their mean dispersion, thereby measuring how distinctly MI-related activity departed from rest. Class stability was defined as the inverse dispersion of the imagery class around its own mean, capturing the reproducibility of MI-related activity across trials. Covariances were estimated on the unreferenced raw signal.

2.3.2 Event-related spectral dynamics

Relative power change was quantified separately for MI and Rest at each frequency bin as (X -R)/R × 100, where X denotes the EEG power computed over the same 3-s MI or Rest analysis window used for online classification. The reference R was a 1-s window, placed just after the rest period and 1 s before the cue to keep anticipation out of it [36]. Thus, negative values denote an event-related desynchronization (ERD), positive values an event-related synchronization (ERS).

First, to characterize the temporal dynamics of the ERD and ERS responses and their evolution across training, we identified the most discriminant ERD and ERS site in each session from the signed r 2 maps. The r 2 map of each session was tested by threshold-free cluster enhancement (TFCE) (E = 2/3, H = 2, 2000 sign flips) [37, 38] (Fig. S2. A). For each session, the significant channel-frequency bin with the largest negative value was taken as the ERD site, and the bin with the largest positive value as the ERS site; where the map held no significant bin of a given sign, the corresponding site was left undefined. Significance over time was then established at each site using the studentized bootstrap of Graimann et al. [39] (1000 resamples per window, α = 0.05), pooling the trials of the session. The analysis used overlapping bands whose width grew with frequency (2, 4 then 8 Hz), with the lower cutoff stepping by 1 Hz from 8 to 31 Hz (Fig. S2. B). To guard against noise, isolated significant windows were discarded.

Four metrics were then derived from each session's response. Amplitude was the area under the relative power change curve. Inter-trial variability was the standard deviation of that per-trial amplitude across the trials of the session. Duration was the fraction of significant windows, and latency, the time of the earliest of them, left undefined when the analysis period began with a significant window already present, as this would reflect anticipation rather than a genuine response to the cue. Each metric was computed over a specific window: amplitude over the 3-s rest and imagery windows; inter-trial variability over the imagery window; duration over the 3-s rest window and on the cued window (condition duration plus the 3-s imagery window); and latency over the cued window (Fig. S2. C).

Second, to characterize longitudinal changes in MI-Rest discriminability, session slopes were estimated from the signed r 2 maps of spectral power ϕ and node strength s over the alpha and beta bands. The analysis was restricted to sensorimotor electrodes.

Connected components were formed separately among channel-frequency bins of each sign, so that each cluster was homogeneous in direction; negative clusters corresponded to MI<Rest responses and positive clusters to MI>Rest responses.

2.3.3 Brain network properties

To assess how MI altered the brain network at the single connection level, we calculated the signed coefficient of determination r 2 between the EEG network link weights in MI and Rest windows, such that negative values denote MI decrements.

To measure the contribution of within-hemisphere segregation and betweenhemisphere integration, we averaged these values for the affected hemisphere (D AH ), the unaffected hemisphere (D UH ), and between hemispheres [17, 18]. The hemispheric balance was assessed by the laterality index [17, 19] : positive values indicating a stronger contrast on the lesioned side (toward coupling, or away from decoupling).

To characterize the topological specificity of these changes relative to the motor network, edges were further divided into three classes: inner motor edges, connecting two motor electrodes; outer motor edges, connecting a motor electrode to a non-motor electrode; and extramotor edges, connecting two non-motor electrodes as represented in Fig. S3. Longitudinal changes in signed r 2 were then estimated separately for each edge class. A negative slope denotes progressive MI decoupling relative to Rest.

2.4 Statistical analysis

The primary outcome was the longitudinal evolution of online BCI accuracy. Complementary outcomes included offline BCI performance, ERD/ERS dynamics, longitudinal r 2 clusters of power spectral density (ϕ) and node strength (s), network intensities, fatigue, and spasticity.

Session-level outcomes were analyzed using an analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) including three terms: session number, MIT, and fatigue reported on arrival, with patient-specific intercepts in the group-level analyses.

Session number represented the longitudinal trajectory across training. MIT was entered as a linear term corresponding to decoding-window onsets of 1, 2, or 3 s after cue onset. Fatigue was included as a nuisance covariate when it varied across sessions. The two effects of interest had different inferential status. MIT was experimentally randomized and was therefore tested by permuting the MIT assignments according to the randomization scheme implemented in the study, following the Draper-Stoneman principle [40], thereby preserving the design-based randomization inference. Session number was observational rather than randomized and was tested conditionally on the remaining model using Freedman-Lane permutation of the residuals from the reduced model [41, 42].

Inference was based on 2000 permutations of a heteroscedasticity-robust Wald statistic [43]. Permutations were restricted within each patient, so that observations were only exchanged among sessions from the same patient [44]. The observed configuration was included in the permutation distribution, yielding a minimum attainable p-value of 1/2001 ≈ 0.0005. Each 95% confidence interval was obtained by inversion of the corresponding permutation test.

Offline BCI metrics were analyzed as complementary outcomes to determine whether longitudinal changes persisted when performance was evaluated indepen- Multiple-comparison correction depended on the outcome family. For the six temporal ERD/ERS outcomes, the session and MIT effects were corrected separately using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure (p BH ) [45], within each patient and at the group level. For r 2 maps of spectral power and node strength, the session and MIT effects were tested separately, with family-wise error controlled at 5% using permutation-based threshold-free cluster enhancement. For edge-level network measures, the session and MIT effects were corrected separately using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure across the nine combinations of network subdivision (inner motor, outer motor, and extramotor) and hemispheric contribution (affected hemisphere, unaffected hemisphere, and interhemispheric), and across the three corresponding laterality indices.

3.1 Behavioral outcome and BCI performance

Five patients were included with no drop-outs. Their characteristics are given in Table 1. All patients were chronic, between 12 and 63 months after their first ever stroke. Three patients had residual fingers flexion without voluntary fingers extension (P1,P3,P4). One patient (P1) presented a non-fluent aphasia classified as global, its severity carried by expression, comprehension being sufficiently preserved to meet the inclusion threshold on command execution. Two protocol deviations occurred: session 18 of P1 delivered 6 s of MI instead of the 5 s of its assigned condition, and session 2 of P3 delivered 4 s instead of 5 s. Both were analyzed under the duration actually delivered.

At the group level, BCI control accuracy significantly improved across sessions, with the group mean increasing from 65.8% to 72.7% (ANCOVA, p BH = 0.0015, Fig. 3). This result was confirmed offline by a progressive run-wise cross-validated accuracy increment indicating greater task separability (ANCOVA, p < 0.001, Fig. S4a). Consistently, rest distinctiveness increased across sessions, indicating that MI-related activity became progressively more distinct from rest (ANCOVA, p < 0.001, Fig. S4b). In contrast, class stability did not change over training, suggesting that improved BCI performance was not accompanied by a systematic reduction in the trial-to-trial dispersion of MI-related activity (ANCOVA, p = 0.42, Fig. S4c).

Fatigue and spasticity changed significantly neither across sessions nor within sessions (ANCOVAs, p > 0.12, Fig. S5). Notably, we found no significant effects of the MI classification timing (MIT) on BCI accuracy, fatigue and spasticity (ANCOVAs, all p ≥ 0.22).

At individual level, BCI accuracy improved significantly in four of the five patients (ANCOVAs, p ≤ 0.034, Fig. 3). Offline, run-wise cross-validated accuracy increased in all five patients (ANCOVAs, all p ≤ 0.022, Fig. S4a), while increases in rest distinctiveness were only clearly observed in P2, P3, and P4. No patient showed a clear longitudinal change in class stability (ANCOVAs, all p ≥ 0.14, Fig. S4c). No significant changes were found for fatigue or spasticity, except for an improvement of the within-session fatigue across sessions in P5 (ANCOVAs, p = 0.01, Fig. S5). Notably, two patients (P1, P3) reported no fatigue at all across the entire BCI intervention, and P2 had no spasticity. In general, the MIT did not have significant effects, but patient P4 exhibited deteriorated BCI accuracies when considering longer MIT (ANCOVA, p = 0.004).

3.2 MI-related spectral and spatial responses

Across the total 90 sessions, we found 126 significant clusters in the signed-r 2 maps of the power spectra showing that MI values (ϕ) were in general lower than Rest (80 vs 46). Those values were predominantly observed in the beta band (95 vs 31 in alpha) and occurred more frequently over the unaffected hemisphere than the affected hemisphere (60 vs. 30) (Fig. S6). As for the node strength (s), we found 108 significant clusters in the respective signed-r 2 maps, also indicating MI decrements compared to Rest (77 vs 31), with a less pronounced beta predominance (64 vs. 44) (Fig. S7). The mean absolute value |r 2 | ranged from 0.11 to 0.36 across patients for power spectrum and from 0.06 to 0.23 for node-strength, with the highest values observed in P3.

While, MI power decreases were predominant and spatially reproducible, the balance between negative (MI<Rest) and positive (MI>Rest) responses varied substantially across patients. P2, P4, and P5 predominantly exhibited MI-related negative responses, whereas P1 showed more positive responses. P3 consistently exhibited both negative and positive responses, with a focal beta power decrease at Cz accompanied by predominantly alpha power increase over bilateral fronto-central electrodes.

The node strength values largely reproduced these patterns, although the positive responses were particularly sparse in P2, P4, and P5 (Fig. S7).

Spatial stability also differed markedly between patients. P3 showed the strongest spatial concentration, with beta negative responses localized at Cz in 15 of out 18 sessions. P5 showed a similarly concentrated distribution, although without convergence on a single electrode. P1 developed a stable positive response at C4 during the last sessions, whereas its negative response remained spatially distributed. P4 showed no clear spatial clustering and P2 had the most heterogeneous distribution particularly for positive responses (Fig. S6).

3.3 Evolution of ERD/ERS dynamics across sessions

The ERD/ERS temporal profiles corresponding to the strongest signed-r 2 values further revealed marked inter-individual differences. ERDs emerged rapidly in P2 and P3, whereas they developed progressively in P4 and P5. In P1, a brief cue-related ERD was followed by a sustained ERS. P3 also showed a sustained ERS following cue onset.

These profiles indicate substantial inter-individual variability in the temporal dynamics of MI-related brain activity (Fig. 4). Analyses of the amplitude, trial-wise standard deviation, duration and latency of ERD/ERS responses are presented in Table 2. In the MI classification windows, ERD amplitudes ranged from -14 to -56% (group mean -32%, SD 16), compared with +18 to +59% for ERS (group mean +32%, SD 19). ERD occupied 53 -95% of the cue window (group mean 74%), compared with 13 -75% for ERS (group mean 46%), and emerged earlier (590 vs 1100 ms after cue).

Across sessions, ERD became stronger (-0.70%/session, p BH = 0.018), less variable (-0.46, p BH = 0.006), and more sustained within the MI phase (+1.6%/session, p BH = 0.003), while its latency remained unchanged (p BH = 0.29). ERS amplitude (+1.6%/session, p BH = 0.018) and window occupancy (+1.9%/session, p BH = 0.018) also increased, without significant changes in variability or latency. The Rest phase remained largely stable at the group level, with no significant session-related changes (all p BH ≥ 0.09). Only P3 showed significant session-related changes during Rest, the window becoming progressively more occupied by an ERD (+3.4 pp/session, p BH = 0.012), with no change in amplitude (Fig. 4).

Finally, MIT had significant effects on the longitudinal reinforcement of ERD and ERS neither at the group nor at individual level (group p BH ≥ 0.59; individual p BH ≥ 0.18).

3.4 Topography of the reinforcement and network coupling

Longitudinal reinforcement was spatially localized. Across sessions, every patient exhibited at least one significantly reinforced |r 2 | cluster in the power spectra (ϕ), negative in four of them and positive in P1, while all but one showed at least one such cluster in node strength (s) (Fig. 5).

The cluster slopes ranged from +3.9 to +18.5% across sessions. Since the slopes were calculated relative to each cluster's polarity, positive slopes indicate that MI-Rest discriminability increased over time for both negative and positive responses (Fig. 5).

MIT had no significant effect in four of the five patients, whereas in P4 a longer MIT was associated with reduced MI-Rest discriminability for both spectral power over frontocentral electrodes (12-26 Hz, p = 0.013) and node strength over central midline electrodes (8-10 Hz, p = 0.026), consistent with the decline in online accuracy observed in this patient (Supplementary Tables, (Table S1)).

Network analysis further revealed a consistent group-level decrease of the functional connectivity contrast in sensorimotor networks (Fig. 6). Inner motor edges showed a progressive decrease of the within-hemisphere segregation in both the affected and the unaffected hemisphere (p BH ≤ 0.005), and a similar decrement for the betweenhemisphere integration (p BH = 0.002). The segregation decrement was larger in the affected hemisphere, as indicated by the laterality index, although this difference did not survive correction (p BH = 0.057). Decomposing the contrast showed that these effects were driven by longitudinal increases in Rest connectivity rather than by decreases during MI, the MI component remaining flat in all three scopes (uncorrected, Rest p ≤ 0.065 against MI p ≥ 0.47; Fig. S8).

No significant group effects were found for outer-motor edges (p BH ≥ 0.17), and among extra-motor edges only the segregation of the unaffected hemisphere increased (p BH = 0.006); the laterality index did not survive correction (p BH = 0.057).

At the individual level, the connectivity trajectories were quite heterogeneous.

P1 and P3 showed the most extensive decrements for inner motor edges, across all three scopes (P1, all p BH = 0.009; P3, all p BH ≤ 0.03), although the relative task contributions might differ: P1's effects were primarily driven by increases in the Rest phase, whereas P3's were predominantly driven by decreases during MI (Fig. S8). P2 showed a limited interhemispheric decrease for the outer motor edges (p BH = 0.040), while P4 showed a shift in the inner motor hemispheric balance (p BH = 0.015) and interhemispheric extramotor decoupling (p BH = 0.013). No significant longitudinal connectivity effects were reported for P5 (all p BH ≥ 0.18).

4 Discussion

4.1 Individual variability and BCI learning

This study identified three main features of repeated MI-BCI training in chronic stroke.

First, BCI control changed across sessions, with marked differences in the magnitude and neurophysiological expression of these trajectories between patients. Second, varying the onset of the MI decoding window produced no systematic benefit within the tested range. Third, longitudinal changes in BCI control were accompanied by changes in both local spectral activity and functional connectivity, but without meaningful short-term motor improvement. Together, these results support an individualized and longitudinal interpretation of BCI control rather than a model based on a fixed neural response or a single temporal parameter. Such heterogeneity is consistent with previous reports of individual differences in MI-related oscillatory responses and their evolution after stroke [3, 4], including trajectories that diverge within the same cohort, recovering and non-recovering patients showing opposite patterns [5].

All patients but one showed increasing BCI accuracy across sessions, and a similar longitudinal trajectory was reproduced offline using run-wise cross-validated accuracy. Classifier-independent covariance measures clarified the nature of this longitudinal change. MI and Rest became progressively more separable, whereas the global within-class stability of MI did not increase. Improved BCI control therefore reflected increasing differentiation between MI and Rest rather than convergence toward a more stereotyped MI state. This means that improved BCI control was associated primarily with increasing MI-rest separability rather than with convergence toward a more stereotyped global MI state. The convergence of online control, offline decodability, and neurophysiological changes supports an interpretation in terms of acquisition of BCI control, although the observational progression across sessions does not by itself establish learning as the causal mechanism. This distinction is relevant for restorative BCI rehabilitation because successful classification alone does not establish that a recovery-relevant neural state is being reinforced. The present results identify physiological changes associated with acquisition of BCI control, but do not establish whether these changes are themselves restorative. This is important because MI-BCI training is commonly framed around reinforcement of a canonical ERD response [13, 46, 47], yet we showed that the MI-related brain activity and connectivity responses strengthened by the BCI training were not uniform across patients.

In general, ERD is typically associated with sensorimotor activation [36], whereas the post-movement beta rebound has been interpreted as an idling state of primary motor cortex [48], and surrounding ERS as inhibition or disengagement of nonrecruited motor areas [49]. Beta rebounds may also reflect confidence in internal-model estimates rather than idling alone [50]. In P1, the effective feedback signal was associated with a beta rebound, whereas P3 received feedback associated with an ERD . Both showed longitudinal strengthening of ERS-related activity, possibly due to the proprioceptive feedback associated with passive movements and tactile stimulation [51, 52] (Fig. 4). These findings suggest that BCI learning in stroke rehabilitation should not be understood as the progressive stabilization of a single canonical MI pattern. Personalization should consequently extend beyond classifier calibration to the selection and longitudinal reassessment of the brain features used for feedback.

4.2 MI timing and temporal dynamics

Allowing the classifier to start the MI decoding longer after the cue did not systematically improve BCI accuracy or longitudinal neural outcomes. Although late classification may accommodate slower or altered MI responses after stroke [15, 53], our results indicate that this is unlikely to benefit all patients.

The ERD/ERS temporal profiles observed here illustrate why this distinction matters (Fig. 4). In P1, all the three possible MI classification windows captured a sustained beta rebound because the preceding ERD had already ended. More in general, a fixed decoding window may mismatch the temporal dynamics of an individual's MI response even when the underlying response is robust. This has implications for the interpretation of the MI timing effects. The absence of a group-level effect of MI timing does not necessarily imply that timing is irrelevant. Rather, its effect may be obscured by heterogeneous individual response dynamics. A MI classification timing that is appropriate for one participant may be suboptimal for another, particularly when the onsets and durations of ERD/ERS differ.

The timing of the decoding window should therefore be considered in relation to the temporal dynamics of the individual MI response rather than treated as a universally optimal parameter. Future protocols could align the decoding window with each patient's MI response profile and reassess this alignment as the response evolves across training.

4.3 Clinical and network changes during training

Despite longitudinal changes in BCI control, brain activity, and functional connectivity, the intervention was not accompanied by meaningful short-term motor improvement. Patients gained between 0 and 3 points on the UE-FMA, remaining below the reported clinically important difference of 4.25 to 7.25 points [54]. The present results therefore provide no evidence that the BCI protocol used in this pilot improved upper-limb motor function.

The BCI intervention was not systematically embedded within a standardized program of concurrent physiotherapy or occupational therapy. Such combined rehabilitation may be important for translating BCI-related neural changes into repeated task-specific motor practice. In addition, several participants had very severe distal impairment, and eighteen sessions may represent a relatively short exposure for detecting motor change in this population [6].

Functional connectivity provided complementary information to local spectral activity. Node strength also showed longitudinal changes over sensorimotor regions in four of five patients, indicating progressive changes in functional coupling between MI and Rest [20, 21, 55]. These effects should not be interpreted as a uniform increase or decrease in beneficial network engagement, because node strength quantified a task contrast rather than connectivity during MI alone.

At the group level, edge-level analysis showed a progressive decrease in withinhemisphere and interhemispheric coupling for connections within the motor network.

These patterns are compatible with the view of stroke as a network-level disorder in which reorganization can involve ipsilesional re-engagement, contralesional compensation, and interhemispheric redistribution [16-18, 32, 56-58].

However, these connectivity changes quantified the MI-Rest contrasts, so an overall decrease can result from connectivity decrease during MI, increase during Rest, or both. For example, in patients P1 and P4 connectivity decrements were predominantly modulated by Rest increases, consistent with evolving baseline network reorganization [4, 59]. Instead, P3 showed primarily pure MI-driven modulations, consistent with task-related reorganization [46, 47]. Analyzing MI and Rest separately is therefore essential when interpreting longitudinal connectivity changes and dedicated restingstate recordings would be required to determine whether changes observed during the reference period reflect broader changes in ongoing network organization.

Together, these findings suggest that BCI learning in stroke patients extends beyond the local spectral features. Spectral power captures local oscillatory modulation, whereas functional connectivity provides information about interactions across regions. The associated trajectories may therefore provide complementary markers of BCI learning, although whether they predict longer-term functional recovery remains to be elucidated.

4.4 Implications for personalized BCI rehabilitation

These converging findings support treating personalization as a longitudinal process.

During BCI training, the patient learns to generate a detectable signal, the decoder learns to recognize it, and the underlying brain activity and network organization may themselves change [60]. These two learning processes are coupled, and the underlying brain activity and network organization may themselves evolve across training. Online BCI accuracy alone cannot distinguish these processes [35].

Three aspects emerge from these results as relevant to longitudinal personalization.

First, the physiological feature used for feedback may need to be selected according to the patient's actual MI response rather than assuming a canonical ERD. Second, the timing of the decoding window may need to reflect the onset and duration of the individual's response and potentially adapt as training progresses. Third, brain connectivity measures may complement local spectral features when characterizing changes that are not captured by the classifier alone.

This perspective also reframes the interpretation of BCI learning. Improved classification does not necessarily indicate convergence toward a common neural mechanism, just as a stable clinical score does not imply an absence of neural adaptation. Instead, behavioral, spectral, and network trajectories may provide partially independent views of the learning process. Identifying which of these trajectories are associated with later functional recovery is therefore an important open question.

The present study does not establish which brain feature or trajectory is optimal or causally related to recovery. Its main contribution is instead to show that repeated BCI training can produce individual changes in the neural signals used for control that are not fully captured by a single group-level feature or fixed protocol parameter.

Larger controlled studies should determine whether these trajectories identify distinct BCI-learning phenotypes, how BCI parameters can be adapted during training, and eventually whether neural trajectories predict longer-term clinical recovery.
