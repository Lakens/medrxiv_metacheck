Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Metacheck does not yet analyze automatically whether sentences which include non-significant p-values are correct, but we recommend manually checking the sentences below for possible misinterpreted non-significant p values.

Text Sentence
P = .061 The proportional hazards test was borderline nonsignificant (P = .061).

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). Statcheck might still miss one-sided tests that are described in another sentence, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Abstract

Background: Banff histological diagnoses do not capture how a graft's injury has evolved over time. Repeated injury is common, but the consequences of repeated histological injury are currently not known, especially when observed in the absence of clinical signs.

Methods: In a single-center observational cohort of 1818 kidney transplantations performed between 2004 and 2021, with 5218 post-transplant biopsies of which 82% were pre-planned protocol biopsies, transitions between six Banff histological diagnoses were described as recurrent events, with landmark Aalen-Johansen estimates and unadjusted Andersen-Gill rate ratios. A multistate model, with states for a first and a second diagnosis of rejectionrelated injury (RRI, comprising TCMR, AMR and MVI DSA-/C4d-), estimated cause-specific hazards of death-censored graft failure, in all biopsies and in protocol biopsies only. Followup ran from day 90 to five years after transplantation.

Results: Each diagnosis was more frequent after an earlier episode of the same diagnosis, and several diagnoses also made a different diagnosis more or less frequent: TCMR was more frequent after AMR, MVI DSA-/C4d-and BKPyVAN, and AMR less frequent after MVI DSA- /C4d-. The hazard of graft failure was increased after a first RRI diagnosis (HR 2.18, 95% CI 1.35-3.51) and substantially further after a second (HR 9.31, 95% CI 5.84-14.85). The association persisted when both diagnoses were made on protocol biopsy, in the absence of clinical signs (HR 1.76, 95% CI 1.09-2.84 and HR 2.58, 95% CI 1.31-5.08), although the difference between a second and a first diagnosis was not statistically significant.

Conclusions: A prior Banff diagnosis strongly predicts that same diagnosis recurring, and in several cases also changes the expected incidence of a different diagnosis. A second diagnosis of rejection-related injury identifies a markedly higher-risk graft than a first, independently of baseline risk factors, and subclinical episodes detected on protocol biopsy also mark increased risk. This defines both an unmet therapeutic need and a high-risk population for trial enrichment.

INTRODUCTION

Kidney transplantation is the preferred treatment for end-stage kidney disease, and immunosuppressive therapy has substantially reduced the incidence of early allograft rejection. Nonetheless, active and chronic injury remains prevalent and continues to drive premature return to dialysis. 1, 2 Such injury is most often detected histologically and categorized according to the Banff classification. 3 A Banff diagnosis, however, is rendered from a single biopsy at a single moment. It is by construction mostly blind to the graft's diagnostic history. Yet alloimmune injury is rarely a single event: it persists, is suppressed, recurs, and sometimes changes phenotype, and the same histological picture may mean something quite different in a graft seeing it for the first time than in one seeing it again. 4, 5 Evidence that this longitudinal context matters is accumulating but narrow. One study of repeated T-cell mediated rejection (TCMR) found that approximately half of patients with an initial TCMR experienced persistent or recurrent rejection, often within months, and that a second TCMR diagnosis carried a significant additional hazard of graft failure on top of the first. 6 A recent multicenter study of early TCMR reported that, despite treatment, approximately one third of patients had rejection again on the first subsequent biopsy, and that these patients carried a substantially higher hazard of graft loss than those in whom rejection had resolved. 7 Meanwhile the natural history of other phenotypes is contested: DSA-negative C4d-negative microvascular inflammation (MVI DSA-/C4d-) has been reported to be less often repeated, 8 and BK polyomavirus nephropathy (BKPyVAN) is often regarded as manageable through reduction of immunosuppression without lasting consequence for the graft. 9, 10 Several questions therefore remain open. First, and most consequentially, the available evidence on repeated rejection rests on biopsies obtained because of graft dysfunction.

Whether repeated injury detected before dysfunction, in a patient who appears entirely well, carries comparable prognostic weight has been examined only in small early cohorts, 11 and has not been established in a contemporary adult population, because it requires a cohort with systematic sequential protocol biopsies. Second, existing work is largely confined to TCMR, leaving the recurrence behavior of antibody-mediated rejection (AMR), MVI DSA-/C4d- and BKPyVAN, and the transitions between phenotypes, largely unexplored.

We therefore addressed two questions in an observational cohort with a systematic protocol biopsy program, in which the majority of biopsies were obtained irrespective of graft function.

First, do prior Banff diagnoses predict which diagnosis follows, that is, do phenotypes persist or cluster, and at what rate? Second, does accumulating diagnoses of rejection-related injury carry risk beyond a first episode, and does that hold when the injury is detected on protocol biopsy in the absence of graft dysfunction?

METHODS

Study population and clinical data

The cohort included all adult kidney transplant recipients transplanted at University Hospitals

Kidney transplant biopsy evaluation

Post-transplantation indication biopsies were performed at the time of graft dysfunction, and pre-planned protocol biopsies were performed at 3, 12 and 24 months. The following, potentially co-existing, diagnoses were considered: TCMR, AMR, MVI DSA-/C4d-, BKPyVAN, Borderline changes (bTCMR) and Probable AMR (pAMR). 12 Diagnoses were defined according to the Banff 2022 criteria, 3 except for TCMR, from which isolated v cases were excluded. 13 For the graft failure analyses, we defined a composite exposure, rejection-related injury (RRI), comprising TCMR, AMR and MVI DSA-/C4d-, phenotypes of established or suspected alloimmune origin that are each managed by escalating immunosuppression. bTCMR, pAMR and BKPyVAN were excluded from the composite and analyzed separately. The rationale for the composite boundaries is given in the Supplementary Methods. Whether the composite behaves consistently across its three components is examined directly (Supplementary Figure S2). "No Rejection/No BKPyVAN" refers to a biopsy in which none of the above diagnoses was present.

We used the term "repeated diagnosis" to refer to a repeated observation of the same diagnosis in two separate biopsies. We did not distinguish persistence from recurrence, since no systematic post-treatment biopsies were available. This distinction is likewise unresolved in the largest available cohort. 7

Statistics

Characteristics of transplants and follow-up intervals were summarized as medians and IQRs or counts and proportions. In all analyses, follow-up ran from day 90 to five years after transplantation, the period of biopsy collection, and the diagnostic history covered all biopsies of the first five years. Among recipients with multiple biopsies, all chronologically ordered within-patient biopsy pairs with the later biopsy after day 90 were classified by diagnostic sequence. Biopsies with concomitant diagnoses contributed to each applicable sequence: a pair of biopsies both showing concomitant TCMR and AMR (Mixed Rejection) contributed to the sequences TCMR-TCMR, TCMR-AMR, AMR-TCMR and AMR-AMR. These sequences were visualized in Sankey plots. This gives a general, time-agnostic description of transitions between diagnoses.

Transitions between diagnoses were further analyzed as recurrent events, since each diagnosis can be observed repeatedly and together with other diagnoses. Landmark Aalen-Johansen estimates gave the cumulative incidence of transitions into each later diagnosis from day 90, among transplants with a given diagnosis by day 90, with graft failure and death as competing events. These groups overlap and should not be interpreted as stratified cumulative incidences. Andersen-Gill 14 models summarized each pair of earlier and later diagnosis in one number: the rate ratio of transitions into the later diagnosis, with the earlier diagnosis as the only time-dependent covariate. In a sensitivity analysis, steroid-treated and untreated earlier diagnoses were separated. All of these analyses are unadjusted for potential confounders and hence only describe the observed transitions.

A multistate model linked rejection-related injury to graft failure, 15 with states 1) no RRI, 2) first RRI, 3) second RRI, 4) graft failure and 5) death with functioning graft (Figure 1).

RESULTS

Study population and follow-up information

The complete cohort consisted of 1891 transplants. Characteristics are described in Table 1.

Seventy-seven transplants had no biopsies, while the remaining 1814 transplants had 6272 biopsies in total, of which 4565 (73%) were protocol biopsies. After excluding cases with missing values or 90 days or less of follow-up (N = 73) from the complete cohort, 1818 transplants were included in all analyses. During follow-up (median 4.75 years, IQR 3.26-4.75), 120 grafts failed and 160 patients died with a functioning graft.

The analysis cohort contributed 5218 biopsies and 5900 time intervals: 682 (12%) intervals preceding the first post-transplantation biopsy, 4277 (72%) following a protocol biopsy and 941 (16%) following an indication biopsy (Figure 2). Their distribution over time, and of the time-dependent covariates within them, is summarized in Supplementary Table S2.

Sequences of histological diagnoses

Of the 1818 analyzed transplants, 1562 (85.9%) had a biopsy after day 90 preceded by an earlier biopsy within five years, 309 (17.0%) had a diagnosis of rejection or of BKPyVAN in such a biopsy and in an earlier one, and 158 (8.7%) had such diagnoses in protocol biopsies specifically. Among these 1562 transplants, all sequences of diagnoses were counted and visualized (Figure 3, Supplementary Table S3). Among sequences between two diagnoses, repeated observation of the same diagnosis was more common than transition between different diagnoses. Heterogeneous transitions were still relatively common between TCMR and AMR and between TCMR and MVI DSA-/C4d-, while the sequence from MVI DSA-/C4d-to AMR was very rare (Figure 3).

Cumulative incidence curves from day 90 revealed heterogeneity in the timing of repeated diagnoses. Repeated BKPyVAN occurred mostly within weeks after day 90, whereas repeated AMR and MVI DSA-/C4d-also accrued around the one-year protocol biopsy, after which the curves flattened (Figure 4).

Andersen-Gill rate ratios, which summarize each pair of diagnoses in one number, confirmed the visual impressions from the Sankey plots and the cumulative incidences. The rate of transitions into every diagnosis was significantly increased after an earlier diagnosis of the same type (RR 1.49 for bTCMR to 23.67 for AMR). For every diagnosis except bTCMR, this was the largest rate ratio of any earlier diagnosis. Several transitions between different diagnoses were also more frequent: TCMR after AMR, MVI DSA-/C4d-and BKPyVAN, AMR after TCMR, bTCMR and pAMR, and bTCMR and pAMR after AMR. AMR was less frequent after MVI DSA-/C4d-(RR 0.20) and BKPyVAN after bTCMR (RR 0.42) (Figure 5, Supplementary Table S4). In the sensitivity analysis of steroid-treated earlier diagnoses, TCMR, AMR, pAMR and MVI DSA-/C4d-still occurred significantly more often after a prior diagnosis of the same type, though not to the same degree as in the main analysis, especially for AMR, pAMR and MVI DSA-/C4d-(Supplementary Figure S1, Supplementary Table S5).

Associations with graft failure

Repeated rejection-related injury and graft failure

From the one-year landmark to five years after transplantation, the cumulative incidence of graft failure was 4.3% without RRI, 7.1% after a first RRI diagnosis and 32.4% after a second (Figure 6). The hazard of graft failure was significantly increased after a first RRI diagnosis (HR 2.18, 95% CI 1.35-3.51) and markedly further after a second (HR 9.31, 95% CI 5.84-14.85). The effect of the second was significantly larger than that of the first (Figure 6, Table 2, Supplementary Tables S6 and S9). The proportional hazards test was borderline nonsignificant (P = .061). In a model allowing time-varying effects, the hazard ratio of a first RRI declined from 3.35 (95% CI 1.83-6.11) at one year to 0.70 (95% CI 0.21-2.28) at five years, whereas that of a second remained high, from 9.65 (95% CI 4.66-19.95) to 7.94 (95% CI 3.55-17.78) (Supplementary Figure S3).

Rejection-related injury detected on protocol biopsy

Restricting the exposure to diagnoses made in protocol biopsies, that is, to injury detected in the absence of graft dysfunction, the hazard of graft failure remained significantly increased after a first RRI diagnosis (HR 1.76, 95% CI 1.09-2.84) and after a second (HR 2.58, 95% CI 1.31-5.08), although the difference between first and second was not statistically significant (HR for second versus first 1.47, 95% CI 0.72-2.98) (Figure 6, Table 2, Supplementary Tables S6 and S9).

In the sensitivity analysis restricted to steroid-treated episodes, the conclusions were unchanged: both a first (HR 2.02, 95% CI 1.10-3.72) and a second RRI (HR 12.13, 95% CI 5.55-26.49) were associated with graft failure, the second with the larger hazard ratio (Supplementary Table S7).

Specific diagnoses

Examining diagnoses individually, the hazard of graft failure was significantly increased after each diagnosis except pAMR (HR 1.32, 95% CI 0.60-2.93). Each of the three components of the RRI composite was individually associated with an increased hazard of graft failure, indicating that the composite result is not attributable to a single constituent phenotype. The hazard of graft failure was also significantly increased by the majority of sequences of two diagnoses, and often more so than after a single diagnosis (Supplementary Figure S2, Supplementary Table S8).

DISCUSSION

In this cohort, a prior diagnosis was associated with a higher incidence of the same diagnosis later, and in some cases with a higher or lower incidence of a different phenotype, for instance more TCMR after AMR, MVI DSA-/C4d-or BKPyVAN and less AMR after MVI DSA-/C4d-.

The hazard of graft failure rose after a first episode of rejection-related injury and steeply after a second. Both episodes also carried risk when detected on protocol biopsy in patients without graft dysfunction, although the difference between them was not significant there.

While the Banff classification mostly does not take previous biopsies into account, these findings indicate that the diagnostic history of a graft carries prognostic information that a single biopsy cannot convey.

Diagnoses that caused graft dysfunction are likely more severe, and so more consequential for graft survival, than those detected on protocol biopsy. This fits the attenuation of the hazard ratio of a second episode from 9.31 to 2.58 when only protocol biopsies were counted, and may explain part of the excess of a second over a first episode. Detection bias is the alternative: transplants that accumulate diagnoses are those biopsied most often, and indication biopsies are prompted by graft dysfunction. However, graft dysfunction can often be explained by histological injury diagnosed in the subsequent biopsy, so we believe detection bias to have limited impact. The protocol-biopsy model also addresses this concern by construction, since ascertainment then does not depend on clinical presentation. Both associations persisted there (HR 1.76 and 2.58), although the excess of a second over a first episode was no longer significant.

TCMR was often followed by a biopsy without rejection. Despite this, its association with graft failure was similar to that of AMR and MVI DSA-/C4d-. Established therapies for TCMR therefore appear effective at halting visible activity without fully curbing the effect on graft survival. This effect was especially pronounced when TCMR followed a prior diagnosis, confirming earlier findings. 6, 7 AMR recurred more strongly than any other diagnosis (RR 23.67) and was also frequent after pAMR (RR 6.51), plausibly reflecting persistent sensitization and the absence of adequate options to eliminate HLA-DSA. 17, 18 External evidence points the same way: after AMR treatment, persistence or progression of humoral lesions on a follow-up biopsy identified grafts at markedly higher risk of loss, whereas their disappearance was associated with more favorable long-term survival. 19 A recent report likewise found an increased risk of AMR after pAMR, but also after MVI DSA-/C4d-, whereas in our study AMR was less frequent after MVI DSA-/C4d-(RR 0.20). 20 In our cohort we observed very little de novo occurrence of HLA-DSA after MVI DSA-/C4d-, which is the step that would be required for progression to AMR. MVI DSA-/C4d-has been described as more transient than AMR. 8 Here it nevertheless recurred strongly (RR 8.13), and its association with graft failure was similar to that of AMR. This agrees with studies comparing the two in survival models using time of biopsy as time zero 20, 21 and in joint longitudinal-survival models. 22 It differs from the weaker association reported by the same study that described the more transient course, 8 which used a landmarked survival model and would therefore be expected to yield smaller effect sizes with reduced power. 23 For MVI persisting across repeated biopsies, graft survival did not differ according to whether inflammation was still present on a third biopsy, 24 although that design conditions on two prior episodes and so does not speak to the contrast between a first and a second diagnosis.

While BKPyVAN is held responsible for 5% to 10% of graft failures, previous studies have not found an effect on the hazard of graft failure, 25 and BK polyomavirus can often be cleared by lowering immunosuppression. 10 We nonetheless found that the incidence of BKPyVAN was increased after prior BKPyVAN, and that BKPyVAN was associated with an increased hazard of graft failure, especially after prior TCMR, pAMR or BKPyVAN. An increased incidence of TCMR after BK polyomavirus has also been reported in an independent cohort, plausibly reflecting the reduction of immunosuppression used to control viral replication. 26 Repeated observation of BKPyVAN may mark more severe viral infection: earlier work associated repeated BKPyVAN with worsening eGFR trajectory 27 and higher plasma viral load and histological pvl score with slower viral clearance. 25 The principal strength of this study is the ability to analyze repeated diagnoses in protocol biopsies specifically, which allows the association to be assessed in the absence of a clinical indication. A second strength is the use of time-dependent covariates in recurrent-event and multistate models, which avoids immortal time bias arising from subgroups defined by timedependent variables, 28 handles correlated observations without discarding biopsies, and avoids the loss of power associated with landmarking. 23 The Aalen-Johansen curves add the absolute-risk view relevant to stratification and trial enrichment. Established prognostic tools for kidney allograft failure derive their histological input from a single biopsy at a single time point, 29 which is precisely the information that a diagnostic history extends. Several limitations apply. First, treatment was neither randomized nor recorded in detail. We could therefore not establish whether the excess risk of graft failure after a repeated diagnosis arises mainly from suboptimal drug exposure, through inadequate regimens, nonadherence or rapid metabolism, or from the absence of adequate therapeutic options. Either way, current standard practice did not fully eliminate that excess risk. Second, despite adjustment of the graft failure models for pre-transplant HLA-DSA and baseline recipient, donor and transplant characteristics, residual confounding by unmeasured heterogeneity in baseline and time-dependent alloimmune risk between patients may remain. Third, disease may have occurred without being captured in a biopsy, which would bias the estimated effects toward the null. Fourth, the recurrent-event estimates for diagnoses are unadjusted and purely descriptive: associations between different diagnoses partly reflect shared risk and biopsy intensity. Follow-up was also limited to the five-year biopsy period. Fifth, missing values were not imputed, but the small number of transplants excluded for missing data or insufficient follow-up (N=73) had baseline characteristics closely matching the analysis cohort (Table 1). Our center also contributed to a recent multicenter study of early TCMR, 7 so the two cohorts partly overlap for transplants performed between 2013 and 2019. Finally, this is a single-center study, and the protocol biopsy density that makes the central analysis possible is also the principal constraint on its generalizability: the diagnostic trajectories reconstructed here are more complete than those available in most transplant programs, and findings should be extrapolated to centers without systematic protocol biopsies with corresponding caution.

Transition rates into diagnoses were associated with earlier diagnoses, especially but not exclusively those of the same type. A second diagnosis of rejection-related injury was strongly associated with graft failure, and substantially more so than a first, and both remained associated with graft failure when identified on protocol biopsy in patients without graft dysfunction. Even when treated, an episode leaves the graft at a higher risk than before it.

More broadly, these observations indicate that alloimmune injury often unfolds along trajectories, and that longitudinal context is essential for its interpretation and for risk stratification.

This is a single center operating a systematic protocol biopsy program, in which protocol biopsies are obtained at pre-specified time points in the absence of graft dysfunction and indication biopsies are obtained at times of graft dysfunction. Patients who underwent another solid organ transplantation before kidney transplantation, and patients with a combined transplantation, were not included. Post-transplant clinical information consisted of anti-human leukocyte antigen donor-specific antibodies (HLA-DSA) and histological biopsy evaluation. Maintenance immunosuppression was mostly tacrolimus, mycophenolate and corticosteroids, with induction, usually basiliximab, reserved for recipients at increased immunological risk. From 2005, BK polyomavirus DNAemia led to mycophenolate reduction, and BKPyVAN to mycophenolate cessation, lower tacrolimus targets and cidofovir. Rejection treatment was at physician discretion, with steroid initiation recorded after each biopsy. Data collection and analysis were approved by the Ethics Committee of the University Hospitals Leuven (registration S64006). This paper adheres to the Declaration of Istanbul.

Aalen-Johansen estimates gave the cumulative incidence of graft failure from one year post-transplantation, by state at one year. A cause-specific Cox model for the transitions into graft failure, with the current RRI state as a time-dependent covariate, estimated the hazard ratios of a first and a second RRI. It was adjusted for baseline transplant characteristics and pre-transplant HLA-DSA, but not for potential mediators: time-dependent HLA-DSA (which also enters several diagnostic definitions), chronic lesion scores and the number of indication biopsies. Deviations from proportional hazards were assessed with the cox.zph function of the survival R package,16 and time-varying effects were modeled by an interaction with time since transplantation. A pre-specified secondary model counted only diagnoses made in protocol biopsies, thereby estimating the association in the absence of graft dysfunction and removing the dependence of exposure ascertainment on clinical presentation. In a sensitivity analysis, the exposure was restricted to steroid-treated episodes, with follow-up censored at the first RRI episode that was not steroid-treated. Exploratory cause-specific Cox models, with the same adjustment set and follow-up, evaluated each individual diagnosis and each ordered pair of diagnoses as time-dependent covariates. All analyses were conducted in R version 4.4.1. Full model specification, the counting-process construction, censoring rules and the complete adjustment set are given in the Supplementary Methods.
