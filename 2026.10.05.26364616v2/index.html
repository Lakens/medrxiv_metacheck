To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 2 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Metacheck does not yet analyze automatically whether sentences which include non-significant p-values are correct, but we recommend manually checking the sentences below for possible misinterpreted non-significant p values.

Text Sentence
p = 0.34 Risk at high temperatures did not differ consistently by parental income (interaction p = 0•34).
p = 0.053 Children of parents with only primary education had higher IRRs for both mean temperature and intraday variability (Wald test for interaction, p = 0•053 and 0•044).

For metascientific articles demonstrating the rate of misinterpretations of non-significant results is high, see:

Murphy S, Merz R, Reimann L, Fernández A (2025). “Nonsignificance misinterpreted as an effect’s absence in psychology: Prevalence and temporal analyses.” Royal Society Open Science, 12(3), 242167. doi:10.1098/rsos.242167.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. How StatCheck works

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Short-term climatic exposure conditions can trigger paediatric hospital admissions, but evidence remains limited on the health relevance of intraday temperature variability, humidity, and precipitation, and generally on which subgroups are most susceptible.

We conducted a nationwide case time-series study of Swedish children and adolescents aged 6-18 years, between 1987-2018 using linked register data. Associations between daily mean temperature, intraday temperature variability, relative humidity, and precipitation and inpatient hospitalisations were estimated with Poisson regression (individual fixed effects) combined with distributed lag non-linear models over lags 0-21 days. Outcomes included all-cause hospitalisation and cause-specific admissions. Effect modification was examined by sex, age group, socioeconomic indicators, region, and calendar periods.

Among 4,638,247 children and adolescents in the cohort, 1,070,702 (23•1%) experienced at least one hospitalisation, contributing 1,736,169 hospitalisation events. All-cause hospitalisation increased with higher mean temperature, with risk highest at hot extremes. Intraday temperature variability showed a U-shaped association, with higher risks at both lowest and highest exposure levels. Relative humidity showed no association with hospitalisation risk. Higher precipitation was associated with increased hospitalisation risk, but estimates at high rainfall levels were imprecise. Stratified analyses indicated modest differences by sex and socioeconomic indicators, but clearer heterogeneity by age and region, with the highest risks among younger children and in East Sweden; associations varied across calendar periods and were imprecise for most causespecific outcomes. Sensitivity analyses mostly showed that the associations were robust.

Acute exposure to hot temperatures, both low and high intraday temperature variability, and higher levels of precipitation were associated with an increased risk of all-cause hospitalisation among children in Sweden, with clearer variation by age group and region than by sex or socioeconomic position. These findings support targeting paediatric climate-health preparedness and early-warning systems toward younger children and higherrisk regions such as eastern Sweden and extending such systems beyond mean temperature to include intraday temperature variability, a distinct, currently overlooked driver of risk that heat-focused systems are not designed to capture.

Research in context Evidence before this study

We identified epidemiological studies examining climatic exposures and hospitalisation among children, published in any language from February 1, 2025, the final search date of the most recent systematic review, to June 26, 2026, in MEDLINE, Embase and Web of Science. Search terms combined paediatric populations and hospital admissions with climate exposures and distributed lag non-linear models (DLNM) (eg, (child OR paediatric*) AND (hospital* OR admission*) AND (temperature OR heat OR cold OR "diurnal temperature range" OR "temperature variability" OR intraday AND humidity OR precipitation OR rainfall) AND (DLNM OR "distributed lag" OR case-time series* OR case-crossover*). Previous studies have reported associations between ambient temperature and increased risk of paediatric morbidity, particularly for respiratory and infectious diseases. However, evidence is often limited to small study population, single healthcare settings or geographic areas, cause specific outcomes, aggregated data, and incomplete assessment of lagged effects and subgroup vulnerability. Analyses of different climatic exposures, such as temperature, humidity, and precipitation, remain rare.

Added value of this study

Our study used linked whole-population register data from more than 4•6 million school-aged children and adolescents aged 6-18 years in Sweden. This is one of the largest whole-population cohort studies of individuallevel climate-health associations in children internationally, and the first such study in the Nordic countries to examine the short-term associations between climatic exposures and hospitalisation risk for children. We found that daily mean temperature and intraday temperature variability were each associated with all-cause hospitalisation, with higher risks at hot temperature extremes and at both low and high levels of within-day temperature variability. Relative humidity showed no association, whereas higher precipitation was associated with increased hospitalisation risk, although estimates became statistically weaker and more imprecise at the highest rainfall levels. The study has several important strengths: it examines different climatic exposures within a single analytical framework; it assesses heterogeneity by age, sex, socioeconomic indicators (parental education and household income), region, calendar period, and parental medical history, showing that risk was highest among younger children and in East Sweden; it evaluates lag-specific and cause-specific associations, showing that heat-related risk was concentrated within the first week of exposure while cause-specific estimates were less precise; and it uses individual-level adjustment for time-invariant characteristics through a withinperson case time-series design, controlling for time-invariant individual characteristics and therefore strengthening causal interpretation.

Implications of all the available evidence

Paediatric hospitalisation risk rises in the days following temperature extremes and periods of high intraday temperature variability, with the largest effects among younger school-age children (6-13 years) and in East Sweden. This supports practical measures such as ensuring reliable access to hydration and shifting the timing of outdoor activity to cooler parts of the day, and, at the healthcare-system level, regionally targeted paediatric surge planning; for example, aligning staffing and emergency capacity with forecasted periods of extreme or highly variable temperature. The chief implication for scientific understanding is that within-day temperature variability represents a distinct dimension of climate-related risk not captured by mean or maximum temperature alone, suggesting heat-health frameworks and early-warning systems built on mean temperature capture only part of the risk. The U-shaped association, with increased risk under both very stable and highly variable conditions, indicates that temperature variability carries information beyond mean thermal load and warrants further investigation. Future research should test whether incorporating variability-metrics improves prediction of paediatric admissions and whether these patterns generalise beyond high-latitude settings.

Introduction

Climate change is raising global temperatures and leading to more frequent weather extremes, posing major public health risks for current and future generations. The mean global temperature in 2023-2025 exceeded 1•5°C above the pre-industrial baseline, and Europe is currently warming at more than twice the global average. 1 The health impacts are already evident, including 181,000 heat related deaths across Europe in the summers of 2022, 2023, and 2024. 2 Cold spells continue to account for substantial morbidity and mortality and are projected to persist as a recurrent hazard in northern regions for the coming decades. 3 Climate change also modifies other climatic exposures, including patterns of short-term temperature variability, humidity during warm periods, rainfall and floods, with potential impacts on population health. [4] [5] [6] School-aged children are more vulnerable than working-age adults to non-optimal (moderate departures from the climatic variable value at which risk is lowest) and extreme weather conditions owing to their developing physiological systems and longer time spent outdoors. 7, 8 These climatic factors exert both direct and indirect impacts on child health. 9 For example, extreme heat impairs thermoregulation and increases respiratory and gastrointestinal morbidity, while cold can trigger airway inflammation. 10, 11 Indirectly, extreme precipitation may limit access to care. 12 Children in lower-income households are likely to bear a disproportionate burden through greater exposure, via housing and neighbourhood conditions such as limited access to thermally regulated indoor spaces, and greater vulnerability at comparable levels of exposure. 13 This evidence, however, comes largely from single-site or single-city observational studies and selected populations, rather than wholepopulation data.

Climate-health associations remain limited for school-aged children, particularly in Sweden. 14 Most studies have focused on older adults and single climatic exposures and relied on aggregated outcomes. They rarely examine how vulnerability is shaped by developmental stages, school year, geographic location, and socioeconomic factors. 13, 15 As a result, public-health agencies, schools, and health services lack the evidence needed to decide which climatic conditions warrant preparedness for children, beyond heat alone, and which groups of children to prioritise. To overcome these limitations, we linked whole-population Swedish health and administrative registers for children aged 6-18 years to high-resolution climate data to quantify associations of non-optimal and extreme temperature (mean and intraday variability), relative humidity, and precipitation with risk of hospitalisation. We modelled non-linear, lagged effects using a within-person design and assessed effect modification by age, sex, socioeconomic indicators, region, and calendar period.

Methods

Study Design and Population

We conducted a whole-population case time-series study using linked data from six Swedish national registers: the Total Population Register (RTB), the Longitudinal Integrated Database for Health Insurance and Labour Market Studies (LISA), and the National Education Register (UREG) from Statistics Sweden (SCB); and the Medical Birth Register (MFR), National Patient Register (NPR), and Cause of Death Register from the Swedish National Board of Health and Welfare (Socialstyrelsen). Children were linked to their parents using parental identifiers recorded in the Medical Birth Register; parental education, income, and medical history were derived from LISA, RTB, and the National Patient Register, respectively.

We defined a whole-population cohort of children and adolescents (hereafter, for brevity, "children") aged 6-18 years in Sweden between 1 January 1987 and 31 December 2018 using the RTB (see Supplementary Methods for the rationale for this age range). Children entered the cohort from the start of the calendar year in which they turned six years, or from first registration in Sweden if later, and were followed until the end of the calendar year in which they turned 18 years, death, emigration/deregistration, or 31 December 2018, whichever occurred first. Because both cohort entry and exit were defined by calendar year rather than exact birthdate, the age distribution of the cohort was broadly consistent across the study period rather than skewed toward younger ages in earlier calendar years.

We applied a case time-series design. 16 Analyses were restricted to individuals who experienced the outcome, with each individual serving as their own control on non-event days within the same calendar month. Children could be admitted to hospital more than once during follow-up. Because admissions occurring close together often represent readmission or continuation of the same illness episode rather than a new, independent event, we applied a 28-day 'washout' period: any hospitalisation occurring within 28 days of a previous one was excluded, and only admissions separated by at least 28 days were retained as distinct events. Each retained event was then analysed within its own person-month-year stratum, so that comparisons were made only between days within the same individual and calendar month, which accounts for within-person clustering by design (see Supplementary Methods for implementation details). Climate exposure on the event day was compared with exposure on selected non-event days for the same individual, enabling estimation of short-term effects and yielding a within-person design that controls for all time-invariant confounders. The case time-series analysis rests on the assumptions that: i) time-varying confounding within each person-month-year comparison window is adequately controlled (via day-of-week and week-of-year); ii) the occurrence of an event does not alter the probability of a subsequent event; iii) exposure measurement error is non-differential with respect to event timing and iv) each child's registered parish or district approximates where they were actually located on the days of exposure, so that climate values assigned from that location approximate their true exposure. Data were accessed in the National Academic Infrastructure for Supercomputing in Sweden. The analysis was performed according to a prespecified protocol. The phenotypes and associated codes are available at [Supplementary Methods and github.com/shivangpandey31/climate-school-children-health-sweden]. The study is reported in agreement with the RECORD and STROBE statements for observational studies using routinely collected health data.

Variables of the study

Outcome Variable

The primary outcome was all-cause hospitalisation. As secondary outcomes, we included cause-specific hospitalisation for injury and poisoning, respiratory diseases, digestive diseases, and mental disorders, selected as the most frequent ICD-chapter groupings among hospitalisation events within the age range. Cause-specific outcomes were defined using the primary diagnosis code. Full details of the code list applied, and information on admission type, are provided in Supplementary Methods.

Exposure

Daily environmental exposures were derived by linking individuals' annual parish/district of residence (from SCB registers; see Supplementary Methods for parish size and population characteristics) to harmonised administrative boundaries, with all historical units reconciled to a consistent 2015 parish geography using shapefiles from the Swedish national mapping, cadastral and land registration authority (Lantmäteriet). Gridded climate data were obtained from the Swedish Meteorological and Hydrological Institute (SMHI) GridClim 17 (2•5×2•5 km; 1961-2018) and recast to parish-level, population-weighted daily means, which were then assigned to individuals. We examined four exposures: daily mean ambient temperature (DAT), daily mean relative humidity (RH), daily total precipitation (PR, converted to mm/day), and intraday temperature variability (ITV), defined from 6-hourly temperatures (00:00, 06:00, 12:00, 18:00) as the sum of absolute successive within-day changes (°C/day). See [Supplementary Methods] for more information on the climate data.

Effect Modifiers

We examined effect modification by sex (Female/Male) as recorded in the Total Population Register (legal sex); age group (6-9, 10-13, 14-18 years; aligned with the Swedish education system 18 ), parental education (highest completed level between both parents, measured one year before the child's birth: primary school, upper secondary, university); household disposable income (family disposable income, measured one year before the child's birth and divided into population quintiles); calendar period based on change in education policy and economic context over time (1987-1993, 1994-2005, 2006-2018); 19 geographic region (based on residence; NUTS1 regions: East Sweden , South Sweden, North Sweden); 20 and parental medical history defined as any parental diagnosis, from a pre-specified list of ICD codes recorded in the National Patient Register before the child's birth (full code list in Supplementary Methods). In addition to NUTS1 region, we examined spatial variation at the county level (län) which generally Swedish health care regions, also derived from each individual's registered residence on the population register. County-specific IRRs were estimated at selected values spanning the range of each exposure and are presented as maps (Figure 4; relative humidity in Supplementary Figure S14). See the Supplementary Methods for more information on the description of cohort.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical Model

We applied a case time-series analytical framework employing distributed lag non-linear models (DLNM) to estimate associations between climate exposures and hospitalisation outcomes. 21, 22 Incidence rate ratios (IRRs) were estimated across the full observed exposure distributions of temperature, intraday temperature variability, relative humidity, and precipitation, centred at the median of each variable.

For each exposure, we constructed a cross-basis using natural cubic spline functions for the exposure-response dimension. Internal knots were placed at the 10 th , 75 th and 90 th percentiles of the exposure distribution except for precipitation. There, boundary knots were set at the 37 th (around 0•2 mm/day) and 99 th (around 21•8 mm/day) percentiles with internal knots at 50 th , 75 th and 90 th percentiles to stabilize estimates in the tails of the distribution given the high frequency of zero-rain days and the long right tail of extreme precipitation values. The lag-response dimension was modelled within the same DLNM over 0-21 days. Cumulative exposure-response associations were obtained using . In practical terms, an IRR at a given exposure level reflects the cumulative change in hospitalisation risk associated with a single day at that exposure, relative to a single day at the median exposure level, summed over that day and the following 21 days of the lag window (i.e., combining the immediate effect with delayed contributions to risk).

We estimated the short-term effect of climatic exposures on daily hospitalisations using a fixed-effect Poisson regression with each stratum represented by individuals as person-month events. Effect modification was assessed formally by adding interaction terms between the cross-basis and each modifier to the main model and testing all interaction coefficients jointly with a Wald χ² test.

We conducted six sensitivity analyses. First, we repeated the analysis using alternative lag structures of 3, 7, 14, 21, and 28 days, to evaluate how the choice of exposure window influenced the estimated climatehospitalisation associations. Second, we compared DLNM specifications with and without boundary knots in the cross-basis, to evaluate sensitivity to tail modelling beyond extreme percentiles. Third, we excluded summer and Christmas breaks and, separately, stratified by school holiday status (see Supplementary), to assess potential bias from holiday-related changes in hospitalisation patterns. Fourth, we replicated the analysis using a timestratified case-crossover design, selecting control days falling on the same day of the week within the same calendar month as the event day, to assess consistency with the case time-series result. 23, 24 Fifth, we restricted the analysis to each child's first recorded hospitalisation during follow-up, to assess whether repeated admissions of the same child, which may not be independent of one another, influenced the results. Sixth, we compared hot (April-September) and cold (October-March) seasons, with spline knots recomputed separately within each season using the same percentile-based approach described above.

Python 3•11 was used to construct the data processing pipeline and creating figures using pandas (2•3•2) and matplotlib (3•9•2), while statistical analyses were carried out in R 4•3•1 using the dlnm (2•4•7), and gnm (1.1•5) packages.

Results

Descriptive statistics

Of the 4,638,247 children in the cohort, 1,070,702 (23•1%) experienced at least one inpatient hospitalisation during the study period (Table 1). In total, 2,029,276 hospitalisation events were identified, of which 1,736,169 remained after applying the 28-day washout. Among these retained events, 411,024 (23•7%) were for injury and poisoning, 191,997 (11•1%) for respiratory diseases, 149,150 (8•6%) for digestive diseases, and 107,890 (6•2%) for mental disorders. The remaining 876,108 events (50•5%) fell outside these cause-specific groupings; the largest share of these (233,304 events, 13•4%) were classified as symptoms and ill-defined conditions (ICD-9 780-799; ICD-10 R00-R99). Discussion

Our study has shown that high daily mean temperature, both low and high intraday temperature variability, and higher precipitation are associated with an increased risk of hospitalisation among 1•07 million Swedish children aged 6-18 years; while no association is observed for relative humidity.

Several mechanisms could plausibly link these exposures to paediatric hospitalisation, and the relevant pathway likely depends on the reason for admission. Injuries, about a quarter of admissions, showed the clearest association with heat and high intraday temperature variability, which is more plausibly behavioural than physiological: warm weather brings more outdoor activity, swimming, travel, and holiday activities, and with them more opportunity for injury. For medical conditions, high temperatures may act through impaired thermoregulation, dehydration, sleep disruption, and exacerbation of underlying conditions. 9 Injuries, however, accounted for about a quarter of admissions and showed the strongest associations with both mean temperature and intraday variability. At high intraday temperature variability, rapid within-day changes may challenge thermoregulation and leave less time to adapt clothing, activity, hydration, and movement between indoor and outdoor environments. The association at very low variability is harder to explain. Days with very low variability occurred mainly in winter, so this association may reflect other conditions that accompany such days rather than variability itself. Very stable days are meteorologically distinctive. In Sweden, they occur during winter inversions and overcast conditions, but also during warm, cloudy periods in July. In winter, children spend more time indoors, favouring respiratory infections; in summer, they spend more time outdoors and travelling, which increases exposure to injuries. The low-variability association may therefore reflect seasonal conditions rather than stability itself. It was driven mainly by respiratory and digestive admissions, consistent with greater infection spread during cold, stable winter conditions, whereas the high-variability association was driven by injuries. Higher precipitation may increase risk through infection-related pathways in children. 12 The timing of these associations pointed to short-term responses: risk at both high mean temperature (≥24°C, the 99th percentile) and high variability was concentrated within approximately the first week after exposure. Risk was instead lower on cold days, and this reduction occurred mainly in the first few days after exposure. deferred care-seeking during cold weather, when travel is difficult and non-urgent visits may be postponed, is a plausible contributor. This is consistent with the reduction being concentrated in symptoms and ill-defined conditions and in mental disorders rather than in injuries. These two groups may partly overlap, as admissions coded as symptoms in children are often related to mental health conditions. Because the reasons for hospital admission differ between children and adults, so may their associations with temperature. Findings from adult or all-age studies, which are dominated by adults, therefore cannot be generalised to children. Cause-specific patterns varied. Hospitalisation risk for injury and poisoning increased with both higher temperature and greater temperature variability, plausibly through increased outdoor activity, risk-taking behaviour, dehydration, fatigue, and reduced concentration during hot or rapidly changing weather. Associations for respiratory and digestive diseases were positive but less precise.

Associations differed more clearly by age and region than by sex or socioeconomic position. Younger children had higher risks at high mean temperature and high intraday temperature variability, possibly reflecting differences in thermoregulation, activity, and time spent outdoors. Heat-related risk was greatest in East Sweden and weakest in North Sweden, while high intraday temperature variability showed stronger associations in East and South Sweden. East Sweden includes Stockholm, with urban heat islands and a diverse population with varying healthcare-seeking patterns, and admission thresholds may differ between regions. These factors, together with differences in housing and local climate adaptation 7, 14 , may contribute to the regional patterns. Direct comparison with previous studies is difficult, as paediatric evidence has largely focused on single outcomes or settings, 11, [28] [29] [30] and few studies have examined multiple dimensions of susceptibility within one population. Differences by sex were small and, while there were modest differences by socioeconomic status, and those by socioeconomic position modest, with no consistent income gradient in heat-related risk, in contrast to settings with more unequal access to cooling and care, 31, 32 which may reflect Sweden having less social inequality and universal healthcare access. Associations differed across calendar periods, with little evidence of heat-related risk in 1987-1993 and clearer associations thereafter, although estimates for the earliest period were imprecise. These periods span substantial changes in Swedish society, including the economic crisis of the early 1990s, reforms to the education system, and a marked rise in wealth inequality from the 1990s onwards. Our design cannot identify which of these, if any, underlies the change, and period differences may also reflect changes in hospital admission practices and diagnostic coding over time. Heat-related risk nevertheless remained evident in the most recent period (IRR 1•16, 95% CI 1•06-1•26), indicating that it has not diminished despite three decades of social and health-system change.

Our study has several key strengths. The within-person case time-series design with individual fixed effects controls by construction for all time-invariant characteristics, and we additionally adjusted for time-varying factors including day-of-week and seasonality. Distributed lag non-linear models allowed us to capture nonlinear exposure-response relationships and effects distributed across a 21-day lag window. Exposure assignment was strengthened in two ways: historical administrative boundaries were harmonised to a consistent 2015 geography to improve comparability over time, and population-weighted parish-level exposures were used to reduce misclassification arising from spatial aggregation. Finally, the nationwide whole-population cohort minimised selection bias and loss to follow-up, and provided the statistical power needed for precise estimation and for stratified analyses across Sweden.

Our study also has potential limitations. First, exposure assignment used registered district/parish of residence assumes children lived where they were registered; any mismatch may introduce non-differential misclassification and bias estimates toward the null. Second, although we controlled for seasonality (week of year), residual time-varying confounders (eg, circulating pathogens, behavioural changes) cannot be excluded.

Third, estimates at the extremes particularly for heavy precipitation were imprecise, because such events were rare. Fourth, assessing compound exposures (notably temperature-humidity interactions to better capture heat stress) 4 would be valuable, but joint DLNM modelling is not currently computationally feasible at the whole population scale, even with high-performance computing resources. Scalable approaches that can model multiexposure, non-linear and lagged relationships such as machine learning or deep learning frameworks may offer a practical alternative for future work. 33, 34 Heat-health action plans in Sweden and elsewhere in Europe are designed mainly around adults, and particularly older adults. Sweden's framework consists of SMHI issued heat warnings, and Public Health Agency of Sweden (Folkhälsomyndigheten) provided guidance for municipal and regional heat-wave action plans. While young children are recognised as a vulnerable group, 35 our findings suggest two changes. Warnings could take account of within-day temperature variability as well as mean temperature, and thresholds and paediatric service capacity could be set by region and age group rather than applied uniformly across the country. Swedish preschools have been shown to reach high indoor as well as outdoor temperatures, with little capacity to manage heat, so advice that assumes indoor spaces are cooler may not hold; ensuring children have reliable access to water, and moving outdoor activity to cooler parts of the day, are safer measures. 7, 14 High daily mean temperature and both low and high intraday temperature variability were consistently associated with increased short-term hospitalisation risk among school-aged children in Sweden. Children's risk profiles differ from those established in adults and show no sign of diminishing over time. Climate-health preparedness that treats children as a distinct group, and that looks beyond mean temperature alone, is thus needed.
