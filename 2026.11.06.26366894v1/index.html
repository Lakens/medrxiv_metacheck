Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). Statcheck might still miss one-sided tests that are described in another sentence, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. Some differences come from the ground truth: it lacks p-values with a correction name (“p Bonf = .061”), and counts partial eta squared written with its subscript after it (“η 2 p = .05”), “p = .05”, and some table notes and figure legends (“† p < .10, *p < .05”), which this module leaves out, as non-significant p-values. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. Full text

Abstract

Manual localization of anatomical landmarks within human cochlea structures in 3D computed tomography (CT) images is labor-intensive. However, despite progress in various studies on automatic localization methods, it remains challenging to jointly achieve rapid, precise, and fully automated landmark localization. Therefore, we propose a deep learning-based localization framework, called MEDICO-YOLO, which bridges speed and precision. It can automatically and simultaneously localize 6 human cochlea landmarks (apex, basal point, round window center of both left and right cochleae) in the full-resolution 3D CT images. The main contributions are: 1) MEDICO-YOLO learns from few manually annotated 3D CT images; 2) Within the framework, a 2D CNN-based network was developed to specifically localize cochlea landmarks in 3D single-scale medical CT images; 3) A novel loss function was designed; 4) The localization model achieved human expert-level precision while ensuring rapid inference. This study employed anonymized 3D pre-operative CT images (0.3 mm, covering the full head or temporal bone region). The evaluation involved comparing four models that were trained using 4 different training set sizes (n=4, 8, 12, 16). Each of them was evaluated on a distinct test set (n=16). The results show that the MEDICO-YOLO framework achieved strong localization performance by learning from few annotated 3D CT images. Trained on only 8 CT volumes, the model already attained a mean localization error of 1.30 mm (approximately 4 voxels). When trained on 16 CT volumes, the model achieved a mean error of 0.71 mm and a successful detection rate (SDR@3.0 mm) of 97.92%. Furthermore, on an Apple M1 laptop, the model achieved a real-time speed (32 ms) for inferencing each 2D slice (608 x 608) and an average latency of 25.43 s for inferencing the full 3D CT image (608 x 608 x 200, including post-processing). The complete methodology for the MEDICO-YOLO framework is presented in this article.

INTRODUCTION

Understanding the precise spatial information of patient-specific anatomical structures in medical imaging is critical for individualized surgical planning and other medical applications. Relevant to the temporal bone region, different studies have focused on the segmentation of anatomical structures (1, 2). However, in cochlear implant surgery, segmenting the shape of a structure alone is often insufficient, localizing clinically relevant landmarks (e.g., cochlea apex, basal point and round window center) can also be crucial for performing minimally invasive surgical procedures (3). Whereas manually localizing cochlea landmarks remains challenging and labor-intensive due to the cochlea's minute size and significant inter-individual variation (4, 5).

Earlier studies employed atlas-based approaches for automatic localization or segmentation of anatomical structures within the temporal bone region (6) (7) (8). Such approaches employ predefined anatomical atlas constructed from annotated dataset as templates, aligning them with new patient scans via registration techniques, demonstrating precise localization performance. However, it is often sensitive to anatomical variability and can become computationally expensive when integrating multiatlas strategy or deformable registration technique.

Deep learning-based convolutional neural networks (CNNs) have been increasingly employed in various studies to perform automatic landmark detection in medical images (9, 10). More relevant to our domain, several studies have proposed CNN-based approaches for localizing anatomical structures within the temporal bone region. Margeta et al. (11) developed the web-based platform Nautilus, employing a 3D U-Net architecture for cochlea landmark detection and segmentation in pre-and postoperative images. For detection in pre-operative images, the 3D U-Net outputs a heatmap for each landmark, 3 heatmaps in total. Subsequently, the coordinates of landmarks are extracted by identifying peak responses on the predicted Gaussian probability maps. The network trained with 279 pre-and post-operative scans, achieved an average localization error of approximately 2 voxels on pre-operative CT images. However, this method requires a manual initialization via a user-defined 10 × 10 × 10 mm region of interest (ROI). Wang et al. (12) demonstrated an approach to train the algorithm using only a single annotated CT scan. They first employed a shallow 2D U-Net to segment the structure of interest (SOI) mask, enabling an initial alignment between the target image and reference image. Subsequently, they integrated 3D registration to refine the coordinates of landmarks. However, the accuracy of this method can be sensitive to the quality of the single annotated reference image, and the registrationbased optimization can become complex when large anatomical variation presents. Additionally, Stebani et al. (13) proposed a dual-headed 3D U-Net trained on 38 CT scans to achieve both temporal bone segmentation and landmark detection. In the detection component, they integrated a heatmap regression output layer into the network's head module and implemented its corresponding regression loss. This approach rapidly localizes the helicotrema, oval window and round window within a 128 × 128 × 128 voxel sub-volume, achieving an average localization error of 5.2 voxel units. Furthermore, Wang et al. (14) proposed a few-shot learning approach for automatic localization of several cochlea landmarks (apex, basal point and round window center), combining an optimized data pre-processing operation with a tailored multi-class classification 2D CNN model. A sliding window strategy was then employed at the localization process. Using only 5 annotated CT scans as training data, their algorithm achieved an average localization error of approximately 3 voxels on the input 3D CT volumes.

However, due to its two-stage design, a manual ROI initialization is required to accelerate the inference.

These works underscore that the landmarks within the temporal bone area can be localized automatically and precisely on 3D CT images. However, due to multi-stage strategies or the characteristic of the U-Net architecture in localization (which often outputs one heatmap with full input image size per landmark), some approaches necessitate a manually defined ROI to accelerate the detection process. Achieving both rapid and precise automatic landmark localization on CT images without pre-cropping ROI, still remains a challenge. Two-stage object detection algorithms such as R-CNN, Fast R-CNN, Faster R-CNN (15-17) first require filtering ROIs through a region proposal mechanism, then followed by the object classification and bounding box regression on the selected ROIs. Although these methods can achieve high detection precision, such two-stage detection design still slows down the inference speed. On the other hand, single-stage detector like center-based approach CenterNet (18) can predict directly the object's center with heatmap-based output. However, each class necessitates a separate heatmap to detect its center, increasing computational cost during inference.

Another single-stage detection algorithm, You Only Look Once (YOLO) (19), has gained more attention owing to its real-time inference capability, global context and end-to-end processing pipeline. YOLO employs a single grid-based CNN model to process the entire input image in a single forward pass. By treating object detection as regression rather than classification, it simultaneously predicts the class, confidence scores and bounding box coordinates of multiple presented objects at output, thereby reducing inference duration while retaining high detection accuracy.

Unlike conventional YOLO, which primarily detects multi-scale objects on 2D natural images, our clinical task focuses on localizing anatomical landmarks within the human cochlea structure: the apex (helicotrema), the basal point (center of the cochlea basal turn), and the round window center. The dataset in use is 3D medical CT images covering the entire head or temporal bone region, with consistent isotropic image resolution.

Therefore, we propose a deep learning-based, automated landmark localization framework, named MEDICO-YOLO (MEDical Image Centers Only YOLO), which is single-scale, 3D medical CT image-focused. The methodology of this framework encompasses 2D CNN-based network architecture, loss function, data generation, training configuration, and the 3D localization inference pipeline. It supports few-volume learning and localizes multiple cochlea landmarks simultaneously and fully automatically, attains both high precision and rapid inference. Furthermore, it requires no manual initialization thanks to its single-stage design.

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first automated cochlea landmark localization framework that achieves collectively all of the aforementioned features. Thus, the main contributions include: 1) To enable few-volume learning while solving the overfitting issue, a custom data generator was developed.

From a limited set of manually labeled 3D CT volumes, the data generator could generate a large and adjustable amount of 2D images and their corresponding ground truth label matrix for training and validation data. To enhance 3D spatial context on the generated 2D images, 3D augmentation was also integrated.

2) A 2D CNN-based network architecture was developed. Since the target cochlea structure appeared at a consistent scale on the studied dataset (isotropic image spacing of 0.3 mm), the neck module was omitted, and the backbone and head modules were adapted accordingly. Thus, the network outputs single-scale rather than multi-scale feature maps, thereby reducing the network size.

3) A novel loss function was designed from scratch, this included the implementation of customized individual loss components and an overall loss calculation mechanism that considers different cases. This loss function determined the model's learning direction, contributing to its high precision. It also led to a correlation between the confidence threshold and precision, where the model localizes more precisely with a higher confidence threshold.

4) A localization inference pipeline integrated DBSCAN clustering (20) was developed to directly process the input full-resolution 3D CT scan. Without any manual initialization, it simultaneously outputs the 3D coordinates of all 6 target landmarks on either a full-head or temporal bone 3D CT scan.

5) The overall MEDICO-YOLO framework was evaluated quantitatively and qualitatively on a distinct test set (n=16), including inference speed, localization precision, qualitative visualization, comparison with human experts in localization precision, and overall comparison with other related works. The results demonstrate that this framework achieved human expertlevel precision while maintaining rapid inference speed.

MATERIAL AND METHODS

Data Description

The dataset employed in this study (TABLE 1) consists of 36 anonymized preoperative 3D cone beam computed tomography (CBCT) scans, including 17 scans covering the human head and 19 scans covering the temporal bone region. The image shape for full head CT scan is 812 × 812 × 460, for temporal bone CT scan is 536 × 536 × 198. The dimension represents (X, Y, Z) in the axial view, where X and Y denote axial dimension, and Z denotes the number of axial slices. These scans were acquired from 36 patients (21 females and 15 males, all adults), using 2 different scanners, between 2010 to 2023. Each scan includes both left and right cochleae, with 0.3 mm isotropic image spacing and no gantry tilt. 21 of them were scanned at the ENT clinic or Neuroradiology department in Medizinische Hochschule Hannover with the institutional review board's generic ethics decision No. 1897-2013 from 2013-07-09 regarding publications with patient data. Other 15 scans were acquired from full-body donors (ethics vote No. A 2016-0083) with University of Rostock. The scans with strong motion artifacts were excluded, while scans showing strong metal artifact on the side of the head (due to the attachment of a mini-stereotactic metal frame) were included. Ground truth annotations were manually performed by an expert using 3D Slicer (21), and divided into training, validation and testing sets, with each group containing distinct CT scans. To assess few-volume learning performance, the model was trained on 4 distinct training sets containing 4, 8, 12 and 16 manually labeled CT volumes respectively. For testing, a separate test set comprising 16 different CT volumes was employed.

Network Architecture

Since clinicians or radiologists commonly analyze 3D CT scans with 2D multiplanar views, we accordingly redefined the 3D CT cochlea landmark localization task as 2D slice localization in the axial direction. Therefore, a 2D CNN-based network architecture was developed. Compared to 3D convolutions, this 2D convolution-based adjustment reduces the computational burden by eliminating convolution operations along the depth dimension. The backbone module of the network was inspired by the CSPDarknet53 (22), employing Mish (23) as activation function. Beyond the backbone module, several architectural modifications tailored to our task were implemented: (1) The input image was adjusted to a single-channel image with the shape of 1 x 608 x 608. (2) The conventional neck module was removed due to the consistent anatomical size of the cochlea structure and fixed isotropic voxel spacing of 0.3 mm across the studied dataset, which eliminated the need for multi-scale feature fusion.

(3) The head module was adjusted accordingly to the modification of the neck module, which outputs a single-scale feature map with an anchor-free design (24, 25), and (4) the shape of the output tensor was tailored to 13 x 13 x 2 x 9.

As FIGURE 1 illustrates, the input image is downsampled across 5 successive CSP stages. Each stage integrates CSPNet (26), which divides the input feature map into two branches: one branch processes through a series of residual blocks (27), while the other bypasses them, the two branches are then merged via channel concatenation. This design reduces the computation while keeping both spatial and semantic information. Following the backbone module, the feature map is directly connected to the head module, which consists of five 3 x 3 convolution layer and one 1 x 1 convolution layer, Leaky ReLU is employed as activation function. The output tensor has a shape of 13 x 13 x 2 x 9, corresponding to 13 x 13 grid cells that divide the input image, each cell contains two bounding boxes, where each box consists of 9 parameters: x, y, w, h, confidence, and a class label represented by onehot encoding (4 classes in total). The parameters (x, y, w, h) are defined relative to each grid cell.

As anatomically, the human cochlea structure has a dimension of approximately 10 mm x 8 mm x 4 mm, which corresponds to 33 x 27 x 13 voxels in 0.3 mm resolution CT scan (4). Thus, to ensure the output feature map contains a sufficient receptive field, and each cell approximately covers the dimension of the cochlea structure, the size of input image was determined to 608 x 608, and the grid size to 13 x 13. In this single-scale design, such suitable spatial configuration is particularly important since all spatial information is presented on a single scale feature map.

Loss Function

Beyond the network architecture, the definition of the loss function was equally crucial, as the network defines the feature extraction capacity while the loss function determines the learning objective. Thus, to localize cochlea landmarks, a novel loss function was designed. Unlike traditional YOLO family, we redesigned the loss function to directly predict the landmarks (x, y) coordinates rather than the bounding box, the (w, h) parameters were then omitted and thus eliminated the need of traditional loss metrics like intersection over union (IoU). Each cell was designed to predict 2 landmarks, which is consistent with the cochlea anatomy presented in the studied dataset. Each grid cell on the 2D axial CT slice can contain at most 2 cochlea landmarks (apex, basal point). In the loss function, the prediction with the minimum matching cost (1) comparing to the ground truth, is responsible for localizing the corresponding landmark.

Custom Data Generator

For data preparation, different operations were implemented. To address the issue of strong metal artifact presented by the frame attached on the 3D loaded CT volume, intensity thresholding was applied, where the threshold was determined adaptively by calculating the mean and standard deviation of the voxel intensities in the loaded volume. The voxel values exceeding this threshold were replaced with 0. The entire volume voxel values were then normalized to a range between 0 and 1. Due to the scarcity of manually annotated 3D CT images, and to prevent the potential overfitting (29) issue, a custom 2D data generator was implemented. This generator can produce a large, adjustable amount of training and validation data for the network, containing both 2D cochlea images and background-only images, along with their label matrix. To compensate for the lack of 3D spatial context information resulting from the 2D network design, 3D data augmentation operations were also incorporated into the generator.

For cochlea images, a 3D rigid rotation around the CT volume center was firstly applied: ±6° rotation about x-axis and y-axis, and a wider angular range rotation about the z-axis (within the axial plane) with higher sampling probability within ±30° range. Subsequently, the central axial 2D slice contains the ground truth landmark were extracted from the rotated 3D volume. A translation operation was additionally integrated. For non-rotated volumes, a 2D translation was applied on the extracted axial 2D slice. When involving 3D rotation, a 3D rigid transformation was firstly applied to the CT volume by randomly translating its rotation center within a defined translation range, then the central axial slice was extracted from the transformed volume while ensuring the presence of the landmarks on the extracted 2D slice. The same 3D augmentations were applied to background-only images. However, owing to the substantial imbalance between images containing landmarks and background, the amount of augmented background images was correspondingly reduced.

According to the different dimension of the input CT volume (covering full head or temporal bone region), the extracted 2D slices were cropped or padded to 608 x 608, while preserving at least the full temporal bone region. Furthermore, 2D augmentation operations including Gaussian blur and random sharpness were applied.

For the network's learning, the ground truth labels for training must align with the augmented images, meaning the labels must correspond to the transformed positions of the target landmarks in the augmented images. To achieve this, the corresponding geometric transformations were also applied to the original ground truth label matrix.

The label matrix was structured the same as the network's output format, with the shape of 13 x 13 x 2 x 9, which contains coordinates information (x,y,w,h), confidence score, and the class label of 4 classes (one-hot encoding). Thus, this custom data generator was able to flexibly produce augmented 2D images with their corresponding ground truth label matrix, for all 3 landmark classes (apex, basal point and round window center) and a background class.

Training Configuration

The complete framework was implemented using PyTorch (30) and trained from scratch on 4 NVIDIA Tesla V100 GPUs with 32GB RAM. All of the 4 GPUs were employed to accelerate the training process by integrating Distributed Data Parallel (DDP) via NCCL. To reduce memory consumption, automatic mixed precision (AMP) with FP16 precision was integrated. The training process was configured with 100 epochs, a batch size of 16, and an AdamW (31) optimizer with weight decay set to 0.005. The initial learning rate was set to 0.0001 and the cosine annealing scheduler was enabled.

To compare performances of different models, a total of 4 independent models were trained on 4 different training sets, each training set comprises 4, 8, 12 and 16 CT volumes respectively, and the corresponding validation sets is 1,2,3 and 4 CT volumes.

Localization Inference Pipeline

At inference, the entire 3D CT volume (containing the full head or the temporal bone region) was processed sequentially slice by slice along the axial direction. Each input 2D slice was cropped or padded (without resizing) to 608 x 608 while preserving at least the full temporal bone region. The trained model inferenced on each slice, generating a 13 x 13 x 2 x 9 output tensor. This tensor contains the landmark coordinates information, confidence score, and a class label with one-hot encoding format. Subsequently, the predicted tensors across each axial 2D slice were aggregated together based on their class and the confidence threshold and then clustered in 3D space using DBSCAN (20). The centroid of each cluster was then computed. These centroids were assigned to the left or right side based on their x or y coordinates, which correspond to the anatomical position of the left and right cochleae in the 3D CT scan. This final predicted output label was then compared with the ground truth label, and the localization error was measured using 3D Euclidean distance, in mm. The number of predicted output tensors can vary with the confidence threshold setting, which can potentially influence localization precision.

Thus, in a single forward pass, the proposed network automatically and simultaneously classifies and localizes a total of 6 cochlea landmarks in the input 3D CT image, for each cochlea (left/right cochleae): apex, basal point and round window center.

RESULTS

To investigate the localization performance, 16 CT volumes from the test set were used. All the CT scans had isotropic image spacing of 0.3 mm. The evaluation consisted of both quantitative and qualitative analyses. Metrics employed include the 3D Euclidean distance and the successful detection rate (SDR@3.0mm), the latter defined as the percentage of detected landmarks with the localization errors bellow 3.0 mm. The localization error was calculated by measuring the 3D Euclidean distance in mm, between the predicted landmark position and its ground truth position in 3D space. The missed landmark localizations were considered as undetected when calculating the SDR@3.0mm. Furthermore, the inference speed was evaluated based on the duration (ms) of single 2D slice inference and the latency (s) of end-to-end entire 3D volume inference, including post-processing. All experiments were performed on an Apple M1 MacBook Pro, 16 GB memory, with Metal Performance Shaders (MPS) enabled for GPU acceleration.

Few-Volume Learning Analysis

The few-volume learning characteristic of the MEDICO-YOLO was first evaluated by comparing 4 models trained on 4 different training set sizes (respectively 4, 8, 12, 16 CT volumes). The evaluation was contacted on the entire test set of 16 volumes, in total of 96 landmarks, the confidence threshold was set to 0.7 for all the inferences. FIGURE 3 and TABLE 2 demonstrate that the models trained with larger training set size achieve a higher SDR@3.0mm, and reduced dispersion and outliers. Especially, the model achieved high localization accuracy when trained only on 8 CT volumes, with a mean localization error of 1.30 mm (approximately 4 voxels). While training on 16 CT volumes, the model achieved peak localization performance among the evaluated training set sizes, with SDR@3.0mm of 97.9 %, and an overall mean localization error of 0.71 mm (approximately 2.4 voxels). Specifically, 0.85 mm for the apex, 0.67 mm for the basal point, and 0.60 mm for the round window center. This additionally indicates the localization performance varied across different anatomical landmarks, nevertheless, sub-millimeter precision was achieved for all landmarks. These findings demonstrate that the MEDICO-YOLO framework achieved promising localization performance from few annotated ground truth 3D CT scans. This few-volume learning capacity may benefit from the synergistic design of the entire framework, particularly the custom data generator with its data augmentation.

DISCUSSION

Overall Comparison with Related Works

To provide a general view of the field, we compared the proposed framework with the other relevant studies in terms of overall methodology (TABLE 4). Due to the differences in datasets and methods, this comparison was performed using the results reported in the respective literature, only the localization part was compared. Each method presents distinct advantages in its specific clinical scenario. 11) developed an approach that capable of localizing landmarks on both pre-and post-operative CT scans, thereby offering a more general usage scenario. Meanwhile, in this specific task of localizing the landmarks of both left and right cochleae on pre-operative 3D CT scans, the advantage of our approach is that it achieved a balance between the few-volume learning, a fully automated localization pipeline, the rapid inference speed, and high localization precision.

Overall, achieving such encouraging performance underscores the strength of the framework's synergistic and task-oriented design, which encompasses the network architecture, loss function, data generation, training configuration, and inference pipeline. Each component is tailored to the others, collectively forming an end-to-end localization framework.

Limitations and Future Work

This work has several limitations. First, since the data generator was designed for generating axial slices from the 3D CT scans, the model can only process the input 3D CT in the axial direction. Second, only the anatomical landmarks of the cochlea structure were localized, whereas additional structures are also interesting to involve for more comprehensive surgical plannings. Another limitation is that this study integrated exclusively pre-operative CT scan data, which restricts its utility in post-operative analysis. In future work, we aim to directly address these limitations by: (1) expanding the data generator to include further sagittal and coronal view slices, (2) extending the framework to localize landmarks of additional structures (e.g., ossicles, facial nerve) without largely modifying the design principle of the MEDICO-YOLO framework, and (3) involving post-operative CT scans through metal artifact reduction or registration techniques. This work can also be extended by integrating multi-modal imaging data.

CONCLUSION

In conclusion, both quantitative and qualitative experiments demonstrated that the proposed MEDICO-YOLO framework can localize multiple cochlea landmarks in 3D pre-operative CT scans fully automatically and with reliable localization performance, thereby supporting its practical integration into pre-operative surgical planning for cochlear implant surgery. A broader clinical application can also be envisaged with the planned expansion in future work.
