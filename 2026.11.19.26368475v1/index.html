Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. Abstract

The approach used in this project to call germline variants from short-read sequencing data forms the basis of a great deal of current human genetics, whether it be in population studies or in diagnostic testing. A full germline variant-calling workflow according to the GATK Best Practices was constructed and carried out using public whole-genome sequencing data from the reference sample NA12878. In order to comply with the limitations of a laptop (an Intel Core i3, 8 GB of RAM, Ubuntu via WSL2), the analysis was limited to chromosome 22. The reads for chromosome 22 were streamed directly from the 1000 Genomes high-coverage CRAM file (run ERR3239334), converted into paired FASTQ files, checked using FastQC and MultiQC, and then cleaned with fastp. The cleaned reads were aligned to a GRCh38 reference containing only chromosome 22 using BWA-MEM, after which they were sorted, duplicates were marked and the base quality scores were recalibrated before variants were called with GATK HaplotypeCaller. The GATK-recommended hard filters were applied separately to SNPs and indels, and the variants that passed these filters were annotated using the Ensembl Variant Effect Predictor (VEP) and summarised with a Python script.

From the 81,599 raw calls, 73,570 (90.2%) survived the filtering process, and the transition/transversion ratio for the SNPs that passed filtering was 2.04. Of the annotated variant alleles, 98.9% were already present in the catalogue, 734 affected the coding sequence in 270 genes, and no variant corresponded to a pathogenic entry in ClinVar. Examination using IGV verified the read support for the selected high-impact calls, including the variants that define the CYP2D6*3 and *4 alleles, consistent with the published genotype of NA12878. The main limitations were a reduced effective depth of 8.6x, resulting from an incomplete readcollation step which retained about 31% of the read pairs, and the use of a chromosome-only reference.

Introduction

Next-generation sequencing and variant calling

It is now routine, thanks to next-generation sequencing (NGS), to sequence an entire human genome in just a few days at a cost which twenty years ago would have been unimaginable [1].

Sequencing platforms of the type Illumina NovaSeq generate hundreds of millions of reads, each of which is only 100 to 150 bases long, and these individual reads by themselves are not significant. They must be examined, aligned to a reference genome and then compared with it before any biological questions can be addressed. The process of identifying the positions at which a sample differs from the reference is known as variant calling, and it is at the heart of most human genetics research, whether it is involved in population studies or in diagnostic testing [2].

Variant calling is not carried out by a single program but involves a series of steps, and an error at any one of them is passed on to the next stage. Bases of low quality may appear to be mutations, duplicated reads can increase the evidence for a false variant call, and reads that have been placed in the incorrect section of the genome can result in the detection of variants that are not actually there. In response to these issues, the Genome Analysis Toolkit (GATK), developed at the Broad Institute, provided a range of tools together with a recommended workflow, commonly referred to as the GATK Best Practices, which has since become a standard reference for germline analysis [3] [4] [5].

The project is concerned with germline variants, which are the inherited differences found in all the cells of an individual. Since humans have two copies of each autosome, a true germline variant is expected in about half of the reads that include it (in the case of heterozygosity) or in almost all of them (when homozygous). This simple expectation proved to be a useful way of checking the results at every stage of the work.

The reference sample NA12878

NA12878 is a woman with northern European ancestry from a family in Utah (belonging to the CEU population) whose cell line has been investigated by both the HapMap and the 1000 Genomes projects. Her DNA has been sequenced on almost all of the major platforms, and the Genome in a Bottle consortium used her genome to create one of the earliest high-confidence benchmark collections of SNPs and indels [6]. She took part in the 1000 Genomes Project [7], and her genome was subsequently resequenced to an average coverage of about 30x by the New York Genome Center as part of the expanded high-coverage 1000 Genomes dataset [8].

There is a practical benefit when working with a genome that has been so extensively studied: it is possible to compare some of the results, for example the overall variant profile or her pharmacogenetic genotypes, with previously published findings.

Why chromosome 22

The human genome consists of approximately 3.1 billion bases, and the amount of memory and storage required to process a 30x whole genome exceeds that which the laptop used in this case could offer. It was for this reason that the analysis was restricted to chromosome 22. In the GRCh38 assembly [9], this chromosome has a length of about 50.8 Mb, and a large part of its short arm is an unsequenced gap. Although the chromosome is small, chr22 is gene-dense and includes areas that are known to be problematic for short reads, such as the low-copy repeats in 22q11, the immunoglobulin lambda locus and the drug-metabolism gene CYP2D6 together with its neighbouring pseudogene CYP2D7. It thus provides both simple and difficult areas for a variant-calling pipeline.

Sequencing data and file formats

The FASTQ record produced by Illumina instruments contains the base sequence and a Phredscaled quality value for each base, defined by the equation Q = -10 log10(P) where P represents the probability that the base is incorrect; Q20 and Q30 thus relate to error rates of 1% and 0.1% respectively [1]. Once alignment has been carried out, the reads are stored in the Sequence Alignment/Map (SAM) format or in its compressed binary version, BAM. The CRAM format provides additional compression by recording only the differences between each read and the reference sequence, which is why a copy of the reference sequence is required in order to read a CRAM file. Samtools and BCFtools are the standard tools used for working with these alignment files and the variant call files (VCF/BCF) that are generated later [10].

Read quality control and trimming

FastQC evaluates the quality of the raw reads using various modules, including per-base quality, GC content, duplication level and adapter content, and assigns to each module a pass, warning or failure flag [11]. MultiQC gathers the results from FastQC and a number of other tools into a single report, which makes it simple to compare different samples or to observe the effect of a processing step [12]. Trimming involves the removal of the low-quality ends of the . reads and of any remaining adapter sequences. Trimmomatic carries out trimming using a sliding-window quality filter and includes an optional adapter-clipping step which requires a file containing the adapter sequences [13]. fastp combines quality filtering, adapter trimming and reporting into one fast program. With paired-end data it is able to detect adapters from the overlap between the two reads of a pair without being given the sequence, and it can also identify overrepresented sequences [14].

Read alignment

An aligner must be able to quickly process hundreds of millions of reads while allowing for mismatches as well as small insertions or deletions. BWA makes use of the Burrows-Wheeler Transform and an FM-index of the reference sequence in order to enable fast searching with only moderate memory requirements [15]. The BWA-MEM algorithm, which is based on maximal exact matches, is intended for reads that are 70 bp or longer and is the aligner recommended in the GATK Best Practices [5, 16]. A mapping quality (MAPQ) is assigned to each aligned read and this expresses the confidence level of the aligner regarding the read's position; reads that come from repeated or duplicated regions are given a low MAPQ since they match just as well in more than one location. GATK also requires read-group tags (which indicate the sample, library, platform and unit) so that the reads from different runs can be treated separately [5].

Post-alignment processing

The aligned reads are sorted according to their genomic coordinates and are indexed in such a way that various programs can go directly to any specific region; duplicate reads, which result from PCR amplification during library preparation or from optical effects on the flow cell, are identified by having the same starting position and are marked instead of being deleted, so that subsequent tools can ignore them while the file remains intact [4]. Base quality score recalibration (BQSR) then adjusts for the systematic errors in the quality values given by the sequencer by modelling the observed mismatch rate as a function of the reported quality, the position in the read (cycle) and the surrounding sequence context, while at the same time excluding sites of known variation so that true polymorphisms are not treated as errors [4, 5].

Variant discovery and filtering

Early variant callers looked at each position separately. The GATK HaplotypeCaller, on the other hand, first identifies active regions, then reassembles the reads in those regions into candidate haplotypes and employs a pair hidden Markov model to determine how well each read supports each haplotype. As a result of this local reassembly, indel calling in particular is more accurate [17]. Each call is recorded in a VCF file together with annotations that explain the evidence, including the quality by depth (QD), two measures of strand bias (FS and SOR), the root-mean-square mapping quality (MQ) and the rank-sum tests which compare the reads supporting the reference allele with those supporting the alternative allele.

Raw calls still give rise to false positives. The variant quality score recalibration (VQSR) develops a filtering model using training data, but in order to do so it requires a large number of variants, usually obtained from many samples or complete genomes. For a single sample or a small region, GATK suggests using hard filtering with fixed thresholds on the annotations, and this should be done separately for SNPs and indels [5]. A common way of checking the results is to look at the transition-to-transversion (Ti/Tv) ratio for the SNPs, which is approximately 2.0 to 2.1 for whole-genome data; a considerably lower value indicates that there are many false positives [4].

Functional annotation and interpretation

Nothing much can be said about the biology based just on a variant's position. The Ensembl Variant Effect Predictor (VEP) attaches each variant to the overlapping transcripts and designates Sequence Ontology consequence terms, for example missense, synonymous or intronic, along with an impact class which is classified as HIGH, MODERATE, LOW or MODIFIER [18]. It is important to take into account the population frequency, since a variant found in many healthy individuals is probably not going to cause a serious disease. The Genome Aggregation Database (gnomAD) gives allele frequencies from around 76,000 genomes [19]. AlphaMissense makes use of a deep-learning model to predict how likely it is that a missense change will be harmful [20], and ClinVar gathers the clinical interpretations submitted by testing laboratories [21]. The Integrative Genomics Viewer (IGV) and its web version igv.js are still the standard method of examining the reads associated with a call [22, 23].

Difficult regions and the CYP2D6 locus

Short reads have difficulty when the genome includes nearly identical copies of the same sequence. The gene CYP2D6, which is responsible for the metabolism of a large proportion of commonly used drugs, is located next to the pseudogene CYP2D7, with which it has about 94% sequence identity, and the region is frequently found to have deletions, duplications and also hybrid genes that combine CYP2D6 and CYP2D7 [24]. Because general-purpose genotyping tools are unable to resolve such structural variations, specialised tools like Cyrius have been developed in order to genotype CYP2D6 from whole-genome data [24]. The Genetic Testing Reference Material programme (GeT-RM) of the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention issues consensus CYP2D6 genotypes for the Coriell reference samples, including NA12878 [25].

Rationale for the present work

The pipelines that have been published generally assume that access to a computing cluster is available; for a person working on a personal computer, it is possible to carry out every step of the GATK workflow by limiting the analysis to a single chromosome of a well-characterised sample and at the same time be able to compare the results with existing published knowledge.

The drawbacks of this method, particularly those associated with the use of a chromosomeonly reference, are examined in the Limitations section.

Scope of this study

The project was based on an eight-week plan which included data retrieval, quality control and trimming, alignment, BAM clean-up and validation, base quality score recalibration, variant discovery using hard filtering, functional annotation, and the preparation of this manuscript.

This study outlines each of the steps taken, the reasons for the decisions made, the problems that occurred during the process and the way in which they were dealt with, as well as what the final set of variants shows.

Research objectives

The project had the following objectives: 1.

To set up a reproducible Conda-based environment for germline variant calling on a Windows laptop via WSL2.

2. To get the NA12878 sequencing reads for chromosome 22 in an efficient manner without having to download the whole genome.

3. To assess raw read quality and remove adapters, low-quality bases and overrepresented sequences. 4. To align the cleaned reads to the GRCh38 reference to generate a sorted, duplicatemarked and indexed BAM file.

5. To recalibrate the base quality scores and document the effect with before and after plots. 6. To call germline SNPs and indels using GATK HaplotypeCaller and apply the standard hard filters. 7. To annotate the passing variants with Ensembl VEP and then summarise them using a Python script. 8. To look at a selection of the variants in IGV and record the limitations of the approach.

Methods

Computing environment

All work was performed on a laptop (Intel Core i3-1115G4, 8 GB RAM) running Ubuntu via WSL2. Project files were stored on the D: drive (/mnt/d/NGS_Project/) as the system drive had very little free space. Software was installed from the Bioconda and conda-forge channels [26] into a Conda environment called variant-calling (Python 3.10). MultiQC was installed via pip as conda reported a dependency conflict and R was installed in a separate environment (r-plots) to avoid having to change the main environment. Table 1 lists the software versions and Figure 1 provides an overview of the workflow. The output contained 1,541,984 read pairs in each file (114 MB and 119 MB). As explained in the Results (Read retrieval), this was later found to be a random subset of about 31% of the chr22 read pairs.

Data source and read retrieval

Quality control and read cleaning

FastQC was run on both raw files and the reports were combined with MultiQC. A first round of trimming used Trimmomatic in paired-end mode with LEADING:3, TRAILING:3, SLIDINGWINDOW:4:15 and MINLEN:36. This command had no adapter-clipping step, and on review, adapters and overrepresented sequences were removed explicitly with fastp. fastp was therefore run on the original raw reads: FastQC and MultiQC were then repeated on the fastp output, so that the raw, Trimmomatic and fastp results could be compared in one report. The fastp files were used in all later steps.

Reference preparation and alignment

The total reference size was 3.1 GB. bwa index was run to create an index for the reference, which ran for approximately 49 minutes before being halted by the OS due to lack of memory.

All reads were from chr22, so we extracted chr22 from the reference using samtools faidx. This created a FASTA file containing only chr22 (49 MB) with the original GRCh38 co-ordinates.

This was indexed using samtools faidx. We also used GATK CreateSequenceDictionary and bwa index. The BWA index was created in 43 seconds. The clean reads were aligned to the reference (BWA-MEM) using 4 threads. Read groups were added using GATK AddOrReplaceReadGroups: bwa mem -t 4 chr22.fasta NA12878_chr22_R1_fastp.fastq.gz \ NA12878_chr22_R2_fastp.fastq.gz > NA12878_chr22_aligned.sam gatk AddOrReplaceReadGroups -I NA12878_chr22_aligned.sam -O NA12878_chr22_RG.bam \ -RGID NA12878 -RGLB lib1 -RGPL ILLUMINA -RGPU unit1 -RGSM NA12878

BAM processing and validation

The BAM file was sorted and indexed with samtools (sort -@ 4 and index). PCR and optical duplicates were marked with GATK MarkDuplicates (they were flagged rather than removed) and the output was indexed again. The number of reads with the duplicate flag was determined via samtools view -c -f 1024, coverage statistics for the chromosome were generated with samtools coverage and the alignments were checked in IGV-Web against the hg38 genome.

Base quality score recalibration

BQSR needs catalogues of known variants. The standard dbSNP 138 file for hg38 is an uncompressed 10.95 GB VCF that cannot be queried by region over the network, so it was not used. Instead, three compressed and indexed resources from the Broad Institute public bucket (gcp-public-data--broad-references/hg38/v0) were streamed for chr22 only with bcftools view -r chr22 (Table 3). The 1000 Genomes phase 1 high-confidence SNP set took the place of dbSNP as the SNP resource. BaseRecalibrator was run on the duplicate-marked BAM with the three resources and a 4 GB Java heap, and ApplyBQSR wrote the recalibrated BAM. A second BaseRecalibrator pass on the recalibrated BAM produced an 'after' table, and AnalyzeCovariates compared the two tables. Because AnalyzeCovariates draws its plots with R, R 4.5.3 with the packages ggplot2, gplots, reshape and gsalib was installed in the separate r-plots environment and added to the PATH for that single command.

Variant calling and hard filtering

Variants were called using GATK HaplotypeCaller on the recalibrated BAM (native pair HMM threads set to 4, 4 GB heap), resulting in a compressed raw VCF. SNPs were extracted using SelectVariants (--select-type-to-include SNP). Indels were selected together with mixed sites (where a SNP and an indel occur at the same position) using --select-type-to-include INDEL and MIXED. Without the MIXED option, 68 of these records were missing from both the SNP and indel sets. Each of the two sets was then filtered using VariantFiltration with the GATKrecommended starting thresholds as listed in Table 4. The two filtered VCFs were then combined using MergeVcfs. Variants that failed filtering were simply annotated with a failure in the FILTER column rather than being removed. Variant counts and Ti/Tv ratios were then derived from the resulting VCF using bcftools stats.

Functional annotation

Only variants with FILTER = PASS were annotated (bcftools view -f PASS). Ensembl names chromosomes without the 'chr' prefix, so chr22 was renamed to 22 with bcftools annotate -rename-chrs. The file was submitted to the Ensembl VEP web interface (release 2026-07) with these settings: human GRCh38.p14, GENCODE 50 transcript set, HGVS notation, AlphaMissense predictions, gnomAD genomes v4.1.1 allele frequencies, and phenotype and disease associations (ClinVar fields were present in the output). The results were downloaded in VCF and tab-separated (TSV) formats. For viewing in IGV, the annotated VCF was recompressed with bgzip and indexed with bcftools.

Summary statistics and visual inspection

Each variant allele generates one row in VEP per overlapping transcript, and hence, the original 780,634 rows in the TSV file were condensed by running a compact Python code snippet (pandas 2.3.3, matplotlib 3.10.9). Rows are summarised based on their genomic location, reference, and alternative alleles while keeping the most severe effect per allele. This ranking of severity is done on the basis of IMPACT (HIGH > MODERATE > LOW > MODIFIER), where the canonical transcript is preferred in case of equal impact in two rows.

A variant is classed as a known variant if it has an rsID. In the current VEP output, there are no values in the Existing_variation column; hence, rsIDs have been obtained from the gnomAD genomes annotation. The frequency of the alleles has been described as common (≥ 5%), low frequency (1-5%), rare (< 1%) or not in gnomAD. Significance from ClinVar has been derived on the basis of a pattern that does not include 'conflicting classifications of pathogenicity'.

The code further identified 63 variants as having either HIGH impact or being predicted by AlphaMissense to be pathogenic. Four such variants have been chosen to represent different categories and levels of confidence and were analysed in IGV-Web along with the recalibrated BAM and VCF.

Results

Read retrieval

Streaming just chromosome 22 brought the download down from 15.8 GB to 168 MB. The chr22 CRAM had 10,104,487 records and the final FASTQ files had 1,541,984 pairs (3.08 million reads). When the working directory was subsequently cleaned up there were still the 0020 to 0063 temp files from samtools collate present. Collate works by taking the reads and distributing them into 64 temp files by read name before processing them sequentially one at a time. The fact that only 20 of the 64 temp files had been processed (20/64 = 31%) is very close to the proportion of reads that were recovered (3.08 million of about 10.1 million records).

As collate assigns the pairs to its temporary files by read name rather than by position, the pairs that are retained form an effectively random sample spread right across the chromosome. This is what gives the even coverage that we see in IGV. All the subsequent results are internally consistent for this subset; the effective depth of 8.6x (see Alignment and BAM processing) is a result of this step and not of the original data which had been sequenced to around 30x. The issue was not spotted until after the analysis was complete and is listed as one of the limitations in the Limitations section.

Quality control

The raw reads were of high quality (Figure 2). Mean Phred scores stayed between about 28 and 30 along the whole 150 bp read with no drop at the 3′ end, duplication was 4.2-4.3%, GC content was 47%, and FastQC found adapter sequence in no more than about 0.13% of reads at any position. The only warning concerned per-sequence GC content, which is expected:

FastQC compares the reads with a whole-genome model, and chr22 is richer in GC than the genome average. Trimmomatic kept both reads for 1,528,266 of 1,541,984 pairs (99.11%); 10,703 forward-only reads (0.69%), 2,778 reverse-only reads (0.18%) and 237 pairs (0.02%) were dropped. The fastp results are summarised in Table 5. fastp detected a small amount of TruSeq adapter (about 0.27% of reads) and removed it. The overrepresented sequences it reported were simple repeats such as poly-A and AC dinucleotide runs, each in fewer than 0.15% of reads, and it estimated the insert size peak at about 268 bp. The trimmed reads passed every FastQC module except two expected warnings (Figure 3): GC content, for the reason given above, and sequence length distribution, because trimmed reads no longer all have the same length. The same pattern was seen for the fastp output in the combined six-sample MultiQC report. Since the raw data were already clean, the cleaning steps changed the read set only slightly, but they made sure that adapter removal was carried out and documented.

Alignment and BAM processing

BWA-MEM aligned the reads to chr22 in approximately 2.6 minutes to produce a 1.3 GB SAM file which became a 385 MB BAM file after read groups were added and 149 MB after sorting.

MarkDuplicates identified 104,691 of the 3,058,443 records (3.4%) as duplicates, including 7,512 optical duplicate pairs, and samtools confirmed the same number of flagged reads. The coverage statistics are in Table 6. About 27.5% of chr22 had no reads, which corresponds mostly to the unsequenced short arm and the centromeric gap at the start of the chromosome. A mean mapping quality of 57.4, close to the maximum of 60, shows that most reads were placed unambiguously. In IGV (Figure 4) the reads were stacked evenly on both strands, scattered single mismatches were rare, and columns of consistent mismatches marked likely true variants.

Base quality score recalibration

BaseRecalibrator used 2,881,860 reads after excluding duplicate-flagged and zero-MAPQ reads, and the recalibrated BAM (302 MB) contained exactly the same 3,058,443 records as its input. The data were already well calibrated: the overall reported quality was Q25.1 against an empirical Q25.0, and 97% of the 424 million observed bases had a reported quality of Q30 (empirical Q29). Smaller biases were present. Bases reported as Q20 behaved like Q17, quality in the middle of reads was about one point too optimistic with a dip of about 3.5 points near the start of reads, and a few sequence contexts (AC, CT and GC) were off by 1-2.5 points.

. After recalibration these deviations moved close to zero (Figure 5), with a slight positive overshoot in the middle of reads. The magnitude of the corrections is very much in keeping with the recent Illumina data (the quality scores are binned and generally good). Using the 1000 Genomes high-confidence SNPs rather than dbSNP meant that far fewer known sites were masked, so some real variants may have been counted as errors. Given that the corrections were so small the impact on the final calls is probably negligible.

Variant calling and hard filtering

HaplotypeCaller ran for 38.3 minutes and generated 81,599 variant records. bcftools reported 69,591 SNPs and 12,056 indels (with 637 multiallelic sites), so a few records are in both sets.

The SNP set contained 69,543 records and the indel+mixed set 12,056, which gives the full 81,599. After hard filtering, 73,570 variants (90.2%) passed (Table 7). The Ti/Tv ratio for the PASS SNPs was 2.04 (41,423 transitions and 20,294 transversions), which is in the expected range for whole-genome data and a good indicator that the PASS set is not skewed towards false positives. No variant failed the QUAL filter, as HaplotypeCaller by default does not output calls below a confidence of 30. The most common reason for failure was low mapping quality (MQ40), which is indicative of reads in repetitive/duplicated sequence, with strand bias (SOR3) being the next most common.

Functional annotation

VEP annotated all 73,570 PASS records, generating 780,634 transcript-level rows which the Python script then collapsed down to 74,061 variant alleles. The difference from the 73,570 is due to the multi-allelic sites where each alternative allele is individually counted. Table 8 contains the summary of the results. As expected for a whole genome, most variants lay outside coding sequence: 34,947 were intergenic and 26,597 intronic (Figures 6 and 7). Almost 99% of alleles already had an rsID and 89% were common in gnomAD (Figure 8); only 857 alleles (1.2%) were absent from gnomAD. This is the pattern expected for a healthy and extensively studied individual, and it supports the reliability of the PASS calls. ClinVar held records for 1,029 of the variants, almost all classified as benign or likely benign, with 15 of uncertain significance and nine labelled as drug response. None was pathogenic or likely pathogenic, which again fits a healthy reference individual. Two points call for caution. First, missense variants outnumbered synonymous ones (407 against 254), and the genes with the most coding variants included PKDREJ, GSTT4, the immunoglobulin lambda genes IGLV5-48 and IGLV4-69, CYP2D7, CYP2D6 and POTEH, several of which belong to families of very similar genes. Some of these calls may come from reads that belong to paralogous copies, a risk that the chr22-only reference increases. Second, the script reports the most severe consequence across all transcripts, so a variant that is intronic in the main transcript but coding in an alternative one is counted as coding. The HPS4 variant in the section on manual inspection of selected variants is one such case, so the count of 57 HIGH-impact alleles should be read as an upper limit.

Manual inspection of selected variants

Four variants from the candidate list were examined in IGV (Table 9). The RTCB variant had the strongest support (Figures 9 and 10). Ten of 17 reads carried the T allele, on both strands; the reads had high mapping quality and the variant lay in the middle of reads rather than at their ends. Its VCF record showed QUAL 283.64, QD 16.68, FS 1.957, SOR 0.956 and MQ 60, all well inside the filter limits, and an allele frequency of 0.5, consistent with a heterozygous call. The variant is very rare in gnomAD and AlphaMissense predicts it to be likely pathogenic, but a single heterozygous variant predicted to be damaging, in a healthy person, is a prediction and not a clinical finding. At the CYP2D6 splice acceptor site (Figure 11), 4 of 14 reads had the variant, again on both strands. IGV linked the position to the ClinVar entry NM_000106.6:c.506-1G>A (the splice defect that defines the CYP2D6*4 allele) and the frameshift that defines CYP2D6*3 (rs35742686) was also one of the HIGH-impact calls. GeT-RM lists NA12878 as having the consensus genotype *3/(*68)+*4 [25], so the presence of both defining variants is in accord with the published result. The allele fraction of 29% is less than the 50% we would expect at a heterozygous site. With only 14 reads, this could easily be due to chance and the presence of the extra CYP2D6-CYP2D7 hybrid copy (*68) and the high degree of sequence similarity between the two genes may also have an effect on the read counts. The hybrid itself cannot be resolved by short-read SNV calling, which is why tools such as Cyrius were developed [24]. The PRODH deletion (Figure 12) was present in all five reads covering the position and was called homozygous, but the depth was low, the variant is absent from gnomAD and it lies in.

the duplication-rich 22q11 region. In the RefSeq transcripts shown in IGV it falls just outside the coding exon, so the frameshift label probably comes from a different GENCODE transcript.

It is best treated as a low-confidence call that would need confirmation by another method. The HPS4 variant (Figure 13) was clearly heterozygous, with 4 of 7 reads on both strands. IGV named it NM_022081.6:c.706+151C>T, an intronic position in that transcript, while in an alternative transcript the same change creates a stop codon. This explains why VEP flagged it as HIGH impact even though it is common (gnomAD frequency 0.12) and probably of little functional consequence. Taken together, these checks showed that calls with good depth, balanced strand support and high mapping quality looked convincing in the reads, whereas calls in low-coverage or duplicated regions deserved caution even when they had passed the filters.

Discussion

A full germline variant-calling workflow was developed and executed on chromosome 22 of NA12878 using a laptop that had 8 GB of memory. Each step in the GATK Best Practices was carried out and verified. The reads for chr22 were obtained without having to download the entire genome, the quality of the reads was checked and the adapter sequences were removed using fastp, the reads were then aligned and transformed into an analysis-ready BAM file, the base qualities were recalibrated, and the 81,599 raw calls were reduced by hard filtering to 73,570 PASS variants with a Ti/Tv ratio of 2.04. When the variants were annotated with VEP, a variant profile typical of a healthy and well-studied genome was observed, and examination in IGV confirmed the read support for the variants that had been selected, including the CYP2D6*3 and *4 defining variants which correspond to the published genotype of NA12878.

On the way, a number of practical problems were dealt with, such as those concerning memory limitations when indexing, instability of the network connection, errors in pairing during FASTQ conversion, and a type of mixed variants which were lost during the filtering process.

Moreover, when the work was reviewed it became clear that only about 31% of the read pairs had been carried forward. Identifying and recording these issues was just as important a part of the project as actually running the tools.

Limitations

• Effective depth. The incomplete collate step kept a random ~31% of the chr22 read pairs, giving a mean depth of 8.6x instead of about 30x. Sensitivity, particularly for heterozygous variants, is therefore lower than the source data would allow.

• Chromosome-only reference. Reads that truly come from similar sequence on other chromosomes had nowhere else to align. This can inflate mapping quality and allow paralog-derived false positives to pass the MQ filter.

• Known sites for BQSR. The 1000 Genomes high-confidence SNPs replaced dbSNP 138, which was too large to download in this setting.

• Filtering. The hard-filter thresholds are GATK's generic starting values and were not tuned for this dataset, and mixed SNP-indel sites were filtered with the indel thresholds.

• Consequence counting. Reporting the most severe consequence across all transcripts overstates the number of HIGH-impact variants; counting with MANE Select transcripts only would be more conservative.

• No formal benchmark. The calls were not compared with the Genome in a Bottle truth set, so precision and recall were not measured.

Future scope

• Use the same analysis on the full chr22 reads (around 30x coverage depth) to demonstrate increased sensitivity; the same approach can be applied to the whole genome using computational clusters or the cloud.

• Compare hard filtering with VQSR and with machine learning approaches.

• Automate the workflow using Nextflow (e.g. the nf-core/sarek pipeline) or Snakemake.

This will make it fully reproducible.
