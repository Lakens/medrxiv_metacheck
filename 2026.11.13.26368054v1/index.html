Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). Statcheck might still miss one-sided tests that are described in another sentence, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. Abstract

Insurance-related differences in post-acute care after critical illness have mostly been studied within single conditions or settings. We described Medicaid-private differences in discharge destination and length of stay among working-age adults in two non-equivalent settings: 20,511 patients whose first hospitalization included an ICU stay at one Massachusetts academic medical center (2008)(2009)(2010)(2011)(2012)(2013)(2014)(2015)(2016)(2017)(2018)(2019)(2020)(2021)(2022), and 603,512 Texas hospitalizations with ICU or coronary care unit accommodation charges in 325 acute care hospitals (2017-2019). Adjusted for admission-time characteristics, Medicaid was associated in both settings with more skilled nursing facility discharge (5.2 and 4.3 percentage points), less inpatient rehabilitation (-2.0 and -2.6 points), and longer stays. Long-term acute care and total facility use differed in direction. Destination differences persisted with further adjustment and in a common 2017-2019 period; length-of-stay differences narrowed. Texas Medicaid rules limiting adult freestanding rehabilitation and inpatient days are consistent with, but cannot establish, a role for benefit design.

Introduction

Survivors of critical illness often leave the hospital with new physical, cognitive, and mental health impairments (Needham et al., 2012). Where they go next -home, home with home health care, a skilled nursing facility (SNF), an inpatient rehabilitation facility (IRF), or a long-term acute care hospital (LTCH) -determines the setting and intensity of services they receive after discharge. IRFs provide intensive hospital-level rehabilitation, LTCHs extended hospital care for prolonged critical illness (Kahn, Benson, et al., 2010), and SNFs skilled nursing and therapy at lower intensity. Which setting a patient enters depends on clinical need and also on coverage, authorization, and provider acceptance.

Insurance has been associated with post-acute care in several populations. Among working-age stroke survivors, Medicaid was associated with more institutional post-acute care but, among institutional users, with less IRF and more SNF care (Skolarus et al., 2012), and a national stroke registry found Medicaid patients more likely than privately insured patients to be discharged to an SNF and less likely to an IRF (Medford-Davis et al., 2016). Among workingage stroke patients with Medicaid in 2010, IRF discharge was less common in states whose Medicaid programs did not cover IRF care, a group that included Texas (Skolarus et al., 2014).

Among mechanically ventilated patients in Pennsylvania, Medicaid was associated with lower LTCH use than commercial insurance (Lane-Fall et al., 2012). Findings differ across conditions (Kane et al., 2014), and Medicaid expansion has been associated with more rehabilitation after injury (Zogg et al., 2019). In the database used here, two recent conference abstracts examined insurance and ICU length of stay among patients with stroke (Shah, 2025) and discharge to inpatient rehabilitation among ICU patients with ischemic stroke treated with thrombolysis (Shah & Strobel, 2026); both concern a single condition and a single outcome and have not been peer reviewed. Insurance may also be related to length of stay: authorization and bed availability are commonly documented reasons for delayed discharge (Sorensen et al., 2020), and among injured patients discharged to facilities, those enrolled in Medicaid retroactively during the admission had longer stays than those with established Medicaid coverage (Haddad et al., 2025). In Massachusetts, non-peer-reviewed hospital association and state task force reports describe payer authorization, including by commercial insurers, as a source of delayed transitions (Massachusetts Health & Hospital Association, 2025; Massachusetts TACPAC Task Force, 2025).

Two gaps remain. First, most studies concern a single diagnosis or a single post-acute setting, so they cannot show whether insurance is related to whether patients receive facilitybased care, which facility they receive, or both. Second, single-site or single-state studies cannot show which associations recur across settings that differ in their data, populations, and Medicaid programs.

We therefore described Medicaid-private differences in the full distribution of discharge destinations and in length of stay among working-age survivors of intensive care in two nonequivalent settings: one academic medical center in Massachusetts, a state that expanded Medicaid, and acute care hospitals in Texas, a state that did not (Kaiser Family Foundation & Georgetown University Center for Children and Families, 2019). We asked which associations were similar across settings and which differed. We then compared the differences with documented Medicaid benefit rules in each state as a way to generate hypotheses, not to estimate policy effects.

New Contribution

The study adds three things to prior work. First, it reports every discharge destination separately in a broad, all-diagnosis population of working-age survivors of intensive care, which shows that the most consistent insurance difference concerns the type of post-acute care (nursing facility rather than inpatient rehabilitation) more than the amount of facility-based care; a single facility composite would have obscured offsetting differences. Earlier analyses of the same database were limited to stroke and to one outcome (Shah, 2025; Shah & Strobel, 2026). Second, it pairs a clinically detailed single-center cohort with a large discharge dataset from 319 Texas hospitals analyzed within hospitals, so that the main associations do not rest on one institution's case mix or referral network. Third, it documents the Texas Medicaid benefit rules for adult IRF care and inpatient days in force during the study years, including the programs to which they applied, and shows that the Texas pattern is consistent with them, extending stroke-specific evidence on IRF coverage (Skolarus et al., 2014) to critical illness and to LTCH care. The comparison across settings is descriptive and generates hypotheses about benefit design.

Conceptual Framework

We used the behavioral model of health services use, in which use reflects need, predisposing characteristics, and enabling resources (Andersen, 1995). After critical illness, need is reflected in comorbidity, acute severity, and organ support; predisposing characteristics include age, sex, and race and ethnicity; and insurance is an enabling resource. We distinguish three ways insurance may be related to post-acute care. Through benefit design, a payer may not cover a setting for adults or may limit inpatient days. Through administration, prior authorization and network rules may delay or prevent placement. Through provider acceptance, facilities may decline a payer's rates. Other enabling factors, such as caregiver support, housing, and substance use, are correlated with insurance and may independently limit discharge home. These channels suggest patterns, not tests. Provider acceptance and social circumstances would be expected to produce less discharge home and more facility use with Medicaid in both settings. Benefit design would be expected to make the type of facility track a state's rules, with less use of settings that its Medicaid program covers narrowly for adults and substitution toward settings it covers. Administrative and placement delays would be expected to lengthen hospital stays, particularly among patients discharged to facilities. Because insurance is recorded at admission and several clinical measures accrue during the stay and may themselves reflect care processes, the framework also orders covariates in time (Figure 1; Supplementary Table S2), which determined the estimand and the sequence of models.

Method

Design and data sources

This was a retrospective, two-setting cohort study with separate data sources and analyses; the settings were not pooled. Setting 1 is a single academic medical center in Boston, Massachusetts. Setting 2 is acute care hospitals in Texas that reported to the state discharge data system. Reporting follows the STROBE and RECORD guidelines (von Elm et al., 2007; Benchimol et al., 2015); the checklist is in Supplementary Table S13.

Setting 1 (Massachusetts).

We used MIMIC-IV version 3.1 (Johnson, Bulgarelli, Shen, et al., 2023; Johnson, Bulgarelli, Pollard, et al., 2024), de-identified electronic health records for patients admitted to the emergency department or ICUs of the medical center from 2008 to 2022, accessed through PhysioNet (Pollard et al., 2026) under a credentialed data use agreement.

Severity and treatment variables came from the MIMIC Code Repository derived tables (Johnson et al., 2018). For each patient we selected the first hospitalization that included an ICU stay, before applying other criteria; the unit of analysis is the patient.

Setting 2 (Texas).

We used the Texas Hospital Inpatient Discharge Public Use Data File (PUDF), which contains discharges reported by state-licensed hospitals; reporting exemptions for rural hospitals were repealed beginning with 2015 data (Texas Department of State Health Services, 2020 , 2021). The primary period was 2017-2019; 2020 was analyzed separately, after removing outpatient records that the state identified in the second-quarter 2020 file. The PUDF has no patient identifier, so the unit of analysis is the hospitalization. Eligible stays were hospitalizations with ICU or coronary care unit (CCU) accommodation charges, recorded under revenue centers 020X and 021X (Texas Department of State Health Services, 2020). The ICU category also includes intermediate (step-down), burn, trauma, and psychiatric intensive care charges, which the file reports as a single amount, so stays with any of these charges enter the cohort and the Texas cohort is a billing-based construct rather than a validated clinical ICU cohort. We therefore report results separately for stays with ICU charges only and CCU charges only, and after excluding stays in mental health and substance use diagnostic categories and in psychiatric-designated hospitals. The specialty-unit field cannot serve as an independent check: the ICU or CCU was coded as the first specialty unit for 99% of eligible stays.

Study population

In both settings we included adults aged 18-64 years who survived to discharge, were not discharged to hospice, had Medicaid or private insurance, and had a classifiable discharge destination; Setting 1 also required a valid interval between final ICU exit and discharge. Texas stays at hospitals with masked identifiers or at specialty hospitals without an acute care indicator were excluded. On Texas records with HIV or substance use diagnoses, the state reports age in coarsened groups (18-44 and 45-64 years) and masks sex; in the primary models these records keep their coarsened age group as its own category and an unknown sex category, and sensitivity analysis S9 excludes them. Sample flows are shown in Supplementary Figure S1 and, for Texas by payer, Supplementary Table S1.

Exposure

In Setting 1 the exposure was admission-recorded insurance (Medicaid or private). In Setting 2 it was the expected primary payer on the claim: Medicaid, or private (commercial preferred provider, point-of-service, exclusive provider, indemnity, health maintenance organization, Blue Cross Blue Shield, or other commercial plans); Medicare Advantage claims were classified as Medicare (Supplementary Table S3). Both are administrative labels. Neither source identifies Medicaid eligibility group, managed care program or plan, or dual eligibility, and some Texas Medicaid managed care claims may be filed under commercial health maintenance organization codes.

Outcomes

The author set the outcomes and their priority before the Texas analyses were run; the hierarchy was not prospectively registered. The primary outcomes were discharge to an SNF or nursing facility, discharge to an IRF, and hospital length of stay (LOS). Secondary outcomes were discharge home, with home health care, to an LTCH, to other destinations (against medical advice, transfer to another acute or psychiatric hospital, custodial care, and other), and to any post-acute facility (SNF, IRF, or LTCH). Destinations are harmonized administrative categories built from different source fields (Supplementary Table S3), not necessarily identical clinical settings; the Texas nursing facility category combines Medicare-certified SNFs and Medicaidonly nursing facilities, which we also report separately. LOS was measured in both settings as the whole hospital stay in calendar days (discharge date minus admission date, minimum 1), the definition available in Texas. In Setting 1 only, we also measured days from final ICU exit to hospital discharge (post-ICU days), treating negative intervals of up to 4 hours as zero. Neither LOS measure identifies time medically ready for discharge, placement delay, or avoidable days.

Covariates and estimand

Admission-time covariates in Setting 1 were age (linear and quadratic), sex, race and ethnicity, admission type, and admission period; in Setting 2 they were age group, sex, race, ethnicity, admission type and source, emergency department use, and discharge quarter.

Comorbidity was measured with the Charlson index without age points (Charlson et al., 1987; Quan et al., 2005). Its timing differs by source: MIMIC-IV diagnosis codes are assigned at discharge and carry no present-on-admission indicator, so the Setting 1 index may include conditions that arose during the stay; in Setting 2 we computed the index from all diagnosis codes and, as a check, only from codes flagged as present on admission or exempt from that reporting. Organ support and severity measures were, in Setting 1, the first-day Sequential Organ Failure Assessment score (Vincent et al., 1996), invasive ventilation, vasopressor use, ICU length of stay, and first ICU type, and in Setting 2, invasive ventilation, ventilation for 96 hours or more, and the All Patient Refined Diagnosis Related Group (APR-DRG) severity of illness, risk of mortality, and major diagnostic category, which are assigned at discharge from all of the stay's codes. Diagnosis-coded social-needs proxies (housing or economic circumstances, substance use disorder, serious mental illness; Supplementary Table S3) are coded during the encounter and do not measure baseline social need. Race and ethnicity, recorded administratively, were treated as social constructs.

Estimand. For each outcome, the target quantity is the difference between Medicaid and privately insured patients (Setting 1) or stays (Setting 2) in the probability of each destination, or in mean LOS, standardized to the covariate distribution of the analytic sample -in Texas, among stays in the same hospital. It is a descriptive association, not the causal effect of insurance. Figure 1 orders the covariates in time. Only the admission-time characteristics are clearly measured before insurance could influence care, so the admission-time model (M0) is the primary descriptive comparison. We then added, in temporal order, comorbidity (M1); organ support (M2); in Texas, discharge-assigned severity of illness, risk of mortality, and major diagnostic category, and in Massachusetts, first-day severity and the ICU course (M3); and diagnosis-coded social-needs proxies (M4). Because later covariates may lie on pathways from insurance to the outcome or be coded differently by payer, M1-M4 are conditional, postadmission-adjusted associations and do not show whether severity explains a payer difference.

We report M3, the covariate set of the earlier single-center analysis, alongside M0 throughout.

Statistical analysis

Setting 1. Destination was modeled with multinomial logistic regression, and adjusted probabilities were obtained by marginal standardization (g-computation): each patient's probabilities were predicted with insurance set to Medicaid and to private and then averaged over the cohort (Muller & MacLehose, 2014). We report adjusted probability differences and ratios, with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) from 500 patient-level bootstrap replicates (fixed seed; all replicates converged for the primary models). Differences were computed from unrounded probabilities. Post-ICU days were modeled with a two-part model (Deb & Norton, 2018).

Robustness analyses included inverse probability weighting with stabilized, truncated weights (Austin & Stuart, 2015), with diagnostics in Supplementary Table S10, and the sequential models fitted as linear probability models with heteroskedasticity-robust errors. Setting 2. Each destination was modeled separately with a logistic model that included a fixed effect for every hospital, so that comparisons are within hospitals and every predicted probability lies between 0 and 1. Adjusted probabilities were obtained by marginal standardization over all analytic stays (Muller & MacLehose, 2014), and we report adjusted probability differences and ratios with delta-method 95% CIs from a hospital-clustered sandwich variance; hospitals in which no stay had a given outcome contribute equal predicted probabilities under both payers. Because separate binary models need not sum to 1 across destinations, we also report a multinomial model without hospital fixed effects (Supplementary Table S6). We assessed calibration by deciles of predicted probability overall and by payer, the distribution of fitted probabilities by payer and across hospitals, and influence by approximate leave-onehospital-out estimates and by excluding the 10 largest hospitals. Linear models with hospital and discharge-quarter fixed effects and hospital-clustered (CR1) standard errors were used for LOS, for comparison with the logistic destination models, and for the sensitivity analyses. We also report the hospitals that identify the within-hospital contrast and estimates restricted to hospitals with at least 11 stays of each payer and Medicaid shares of 5%-95%.

Sensitivity to unmeasured confounding. For SNF, IRF, and LTCH discharge we computed E-values (VanderWeele & Ding, 2017) for the adjusted probability ratio (Medicaid vs private, private as reference; ratios below 1 were inverted) and for the confidence limit closest to 1, from the Massachusetts multinomial model and the Texas fixed-effects logistic model; Texas conditional risk ratios from a Poisson model with hospital fixed effects are shown for comparison (Supplementary Table S20). An E-value is the minimum strength of association, on the risk ratio scale, that an unmeasured confounder would need with both insurance and the outcome, conditional on the measured covariates, to explain away the estimate; it does not address selection, misclassification of exposure or outcome, or model misspecification.

Missing data.

In Setting 1, no covariate values were missing in the analytic cohort; 265 patients without a recorded discharge destination were excluded (Supplementary Figure S1). In Setting 2, no values were imputed: unknown race, ethnicity, admission type, and admission source were modeled as explicit unknown categories, ungroupable APR-DRG records as their own severity and risk category, coarsened age as its own age category, and masked sex as unknown; fewer than 11 stays without a length of stay were excluded. Missing and masked values by payer are shown in Supplementary Table S9.

Texas sensitivity analyses repeated the linear model (S1) excluding discharges against medical advice and transfers to acute hospitals; (S2) restricting to stays with ICU or CCU as the first coded specialty unit (99% of stays, so not a meaningfully stricter definition); (S3) restricting to invasively ventilated stays, a selected high-acuity population rather than a validation sample; (S4) adding the social-needs proxies; (S5) in 2020 and in 2017-2020 combined; (S6) without APR-DRG covariates; (S7) excluding privately insured stays with Medicaid as a secondary payer; (S8) excluding transfers in from other facilities; (S9) excluding records with coarsened age and masked sex; ( S10) trimming LOS at the 99th percentile; and (S11) excluding pregnancy and childbirth stays.

We also estimated models in teaching and non-teaching hospitals and, as a secondary analysis, among adults aged 65 years or older comparing Medicaid with Medicare as the primary payer. Analyses other than the three primary outcomes are secondary, and we emphasize direction, magnitude, and consistency rather than P values.

Comparison of settings.

We re-estimated the Setting 1 associations using the Texas outcome definitions (linear probability models and whole-stay LOS) with a covariate set restricted to measures available in both sources (age group, sex, race and ethnicity, admission type, Charlson index, invasive ventilation, and period), for all patients and for invasively ventilated patients. To align calendar time, we also repeated the Setting 1 standardized models (M0 and M3) among patients whose MIMIC-IV anchor year group was 2017-2019 and compared them with the Texas 2017-2019 estimates; because MIMIC-IV shifts dates and reports only a 3-year anchor year group for each patient, this subset approximates admissions in those years. Between-setting differences have variance equal to the sum of the two squared standard errors, each recovered from its 95% CI as (upper -lower)/3.92 (robust or bootstrap in Setting 1; hospital-clustered in Texas), treating the samples as independent. These contrasts describe differences between data sources that also differ in unit of analysis, hospital mix, covariates, and measurement; they do not isolate state policy.

Benefit rules.

We abstracted adult Medicaid coverage rules for IRF care, LTCH care, and inpatient day and spending limits, with the programs and age groups to which each applied, from state provider manuals, regulations, and state reports dated within or near each setting's study years (Supplementary Table S12). These rules were not linked to individual patients, because neither data source identifies eligibility group, managed care program, or plan, so the payer field does not identify exposure to any rule.

Results

Study samples

In Setting 1, 20,511 patients aged 18-64 years met the criteria, of whom 6,934 (33.8%) had Medicaid. In Setting 2, of 9,339,506 discharges in 2017-2019, 2,479,142 were adult stays with ICU or CCU charges and 1,222,806 were among patients aged 18-64 years; of these, 645,595 had Medicaid or private insurance, most exclusions being stays with Medicare (disability-related), self-pay, or charity coverage. Of these 645,595 stays, we excluded 22,364 ending in death, 10,618 discharged to hospice, 119 with the patient still in hospital, 19 with an invalid discharge status, 318 at hospitals with masked identifiers, 8,644 at specialty hospitals without an acute care indicator, and fewer than 11 without a length of stay, accounting for all 42,083 excluded stays (Supplementary Table S1 gives each step by payer). The analytic sample included 603,512 stays in 325 hospitals, of which 151,862 (25.2%) were covered by Medicaid; 319 hospitals had more than one stay and contribute to within-hospital estimates. Within-hospital contrasts were identified by 302 hospitals that treated both payer groups, which accounted for 99.7% of stays; 266 hospitals had at least 11 stays of each payer (99.3% of stays), and the hospital Medicaid share had a median of 24.2% (interquartile range, 11.7%-38.1%). Compared with privately insured patients in Texas, Medicaid patients were younger (aged 55-64 years, 26.9% vs 38.7%), more often Black (26.1% vs 14.1%) or Hispanic (36.0% vs 23.9%), more often admitted as emergencies (76.8% vs 64.0%), more often had major or extreme severity of illness (59.0% vs 47.7%) and invasive ventilation (10.0% vs 7.0%), and more often had coded substance use disorder (19.3% vs 10.9%) or serious mental illness (11.3% vs 3.3%; Table 1). The same differences in age, race and ethnicity, admission type, and social-needs codes were present in Massachusetts.

Discharge destination

In Setting 1, adjusted for admission-time characteristics (M0), Medicaid was associated with a higher probability of SNF discharge (adjusted probability difference, 5.2 percentage points [pp]; 95% CI, 4.4 to 6.0) and of LTCH discharge (2.1 pp; 1.5 to 2.7), and with lower probabilities of discharge home (-6.3 pp; -7.8 to -4.9), with home health (-3.5 pp; -4.8 to -2.2), and to an IRF (-2.0 pp; -2.7 to -1.2; Table 2). With the full covariate set (M3), the corresponding differences were SNF 4.6 pp (3.8 to 5.4; adjusted probabilities 8.8% vs 4.3%), LTCH 1.4 pp (0.8 to 1.9), home -4.8 pp (-6.1 to -3.5), home health -3.3 pp (-4.5 to -2.0), and IRF -1.7 pp (-2.5 to -0.9).

In Setting 2, crude SNF discharge was more than twice as common with Medicaid (6.2% vs 2.6%), whereas IRF (1.4% vs 4.0%) and LTCH discharge (0.5% vs 1.7%) were much less common (Table 2). Within hospitals and adjusted for admission-time characteristics (M0), Medicaid was associated with more SNF or nursing facility discharge (4.3 pp; 95% CI, 3.8 to 4.8), more discharge with home health (4.1 pp; 3.5 to 4.7), less IRF (-2.6 pp; -2.9 to -2.4) and LTCH discharge (-1.3 pp; -1.4 to -1.2), and less discharge home (-6.5 pp; -7.8 to -5.3). With the full covariate set (M3), the differences were SNF 3.2 (2.7 to 3.6), IRF -2.7 (-3.0 to -2.4), and LTCH -1.6 (-1.7 to -1.4) pp. Most of the SNF difference was in Medicare-certified SNFs (3.0 pp in the linear M3 model; 2.4 to 3.5). Because the IRF and LTCH differences offset the SNF difference, total facility discharge differed little by payer (M0, 0.0 pp; -0.7 to 0.6; M3, -1.4 pp; -1.9 to -0.8). All fitted probabilities from the fixed-effects logistic model lie between 0 and 1, whereas the linear models produced fitted values below zero for 27.6% of stays for IRF and 31.9% for LTCH. The two models nonetheless gave similar adjusted probability differences (M3 linear model: SNF 3.4, IRF -3.0, LTCH -2.0 pp), and the logistic model's predicted probabilities tracked observed rates across deciles of predicted risk in both payer groups (Supplementary Tables S17-S18).

Length of stay

Medicaid was associated with longer stays in both settings. Adjusted for admission-time characteristics, whole-stay LOS was 1.91 days longer in Setting 1 and 1.41 days longer within Texas hospitals; with the full covariate set, the differences were 1.29 days (95% CI, 0.98 to 1.61) and 0.66 days (0.54 to 0.78; ratio of geometric means 1.04). In Setting 1, Medicaid was also associated with 1.03 more post-ICU days in the two-part model (95% CI, 0.80 to 1.21). In both settings the LOS difference was larger among patients discharged to a post-acute facility than among those discharged home (Setting 1, 2.30 vs 0.65 post-ICU days; Setting 2, 2.25 [1.42 to 3.08] vs 0.63 [0.54 to 0.72] whole-stay days); because destination may itself depend on insurance, this stratified comparison is descriptive.

Sequential adjustment

In the linear models, the destination associations changed modestly as covariates were added in temporal order (the SNF difference narrowed and the IRF difference widened slightly), whereas the LOS associations narrowed more (Table 5 and Supplementary Table S5). In Texas, the SNF difference was 4.1 pp with admission-time characteristics only (M0), 3.4 pp in M3, and 3.1 pp with social-needs proxies (M4), and the IRF difference was -2.6 pp in M0 and -3.0 pp in M3; the total facility difference was near zero in M0 (0.1 pp) and -1.6 pp in M3. In Massachusetts, the SNF difference was 5.0 pp in M0 and 4.4 pp in M3, and the IRF difference -2.0 and -1.9 pp. The LOS difference fell from 1.41 to 0.66 days in Texas and from 1.91 to 1.29 days in Massachusetts (post-ICU days, 1.48 to 1.12), mostly when comorbidity, organ support, and severity were added, so part of the longer stay with Medicaid reflects measured severity. In Texas, present-on-admission indicators were reported on 99.8% of Medicaid and 99.8% of private stays, and computing the Charlson index only from present-on-admission codes changed the estimates little (fixed-effects logistic model, M1p vs M1: SNF 3.7 vs 3.6 pp; IRF -2.9 vs -2.9 pp; M3p: SNF 3.2, IRF -2.7 pp; Supplementary Table S17). These comparisons show how the estimates depend on the covariate set; small changes across models do not show that unmeasured need, severity, or access mechanisms are unimportant.

Robustness

Texas estimates were similar across sensitivity analyses (Table 4). In the linear models, the SNF difference ranged from 3.1 to 3.7 pp and the IRF difference from -3.2 to -2.8 pp across S1, S2, and S4-S11, and the LOS difference from 0.6 to 0.8 days (0.2 days with LOS trimmed at the 99th percentile). In the fixed-effects logistic model (M3), the SNF and IRF differences were 3.2 and -2.7 pp among stays with ICU charges only, 2.9 and -1.5 pp among stays with CCU charges only, and 3.2 and -2.7 pp after excluding mental health and substance use diagnostic categories and psychiatric-designated hospitals (Supplementary Table S19). Without hospital fixed effects, the SNF difference was 3.0 pp and the LOS difference 0.79 days, close to the within-hospital estimates (Supplementary Table S4). No single hospital drove the results: leaving out one hospital at a time, the linear-model SNF difference ranged from 3.21 to 3.43 pp and the IRF difference from -3.06 to -2.95 pp; restricting to hospitals with good payer overlap gave SNF 3.4 and IRF -3.0 pp, and excluding the 10 largest hospitals gave fixed-effects logistic differences of 3.3 and -2.7 pp (Supplementary Tables S7 and S18). Among invasively ventilated stays (S3; 46,681 stays in 267 hospitals), a selected high-acuity population, the differences were larger: SNF 8.7 pp (7.4 to 10.1), IRF -8.9 pp (-9.9 to -7.9), total facility discharge -11.0 pp (-12.6 to -9.4), and LOS 3.5 days (2.8 to 4.3). For the adjusted probability ratios (Medicaid vs private) in M3, E-values (point estimate and confidence limit) were, in Massachusetts, 3.56 (confidence limit, 3.12) for SNF (ratio 2.07; 1.86 to 2.30), 1.77 (confidence limit, 1.49) for IRF (0.81; 0.74 to 0.89), and 2.13 (confidence limit, 1.81) for LTCH (1.39; 1.25 to 1.59); in Texas, 3.81 (confidence limit, 3.40) for SNF (2.19; 1.99 to 2.42), 5.39 (confidence limit, 4.55) for IRF (0.34; 0.29 to 0.39), and 10.16 (confidence limit, 8.05) for LTCH (0.19; 0.15 to 0.23; Supplementary Table S20). These values describe sensitivity to unmeasured confounding under the E-value assumptions, conditional on the measured covariates; they do not show that residual confounding is absent. Results among adults aged 65 years or older, a secondary and atypical comparison of Medicaid-primary with Medicare-primary stays, are in Supplementary Table S11.

Comparison of the two settings

With harmonized definitions (Table 3 and Figure 2), both settings showed more SNF discharge, less IRF discharge, less discharge home, and longer whole-stay LOS with Medicaid.

The SNF difference was somewhat larger in Massachusetts (4.4 vs 3.4 pp; difference between settings 1.1; 95% CI, 0.1 to 2.0 pp), and the IRF differences were similar (-2.6 vs -3.0 pp; 0.4; 95% CI, -0.5 to 1.3 pp). The settings differed in total facility discharge (3.3 vs -1.6 pp; 4.9; 95% CI, 3.6 to 6.2 pp), reflecting the opposite LTCH associations, and the LOS difference was larger in Massachusetts (1.25 vs 0.66 days; 0.59; 95% CI, 0.19 to 0.99 days). Among ventilated patients, the SNF and IRF differences were larger in Texas (SNF 8.7 vs 5.3 pp, difference -3.5; 95% CI, -5.4 to -1.5 pp; IRF -8.9 vs -5.2 pp, 3.6; 95% CI, 1.8 to 5.5 pp), as was the LOS difference (3.5 vs 1.6 days). Restricting Setting 1 to the 2017-2019 anchor year group (4,018 patients, 1,394 with Medicaid) gave admission-time SNF and IRF differences of 5.2 pp; 3.5 to 7.0 and -1.6 pp; -3.4 to 0.1, compared with 4.3 and -2.6 pp in Texas (differences between settings, 0.9 pp; -0.9 to 2.8 and 1.1 pp; -0.7 to 2.8), and an LTCH difference of 3.1 pp; 1.8 to 4.5, compared with -1.3 pp in Texas (Table 3). The subset estimates were less precise (the IRF interval included zero; 459 and 457 of 500 bootstrap replicates converged for M0 and M3), and the whole-stay LOS difference in the subset was larger than in the full cohort (M3, 2.24 days; 1.31 to 3.17).

Discussion

In a single academic medical center in Massachusetts and 325 acute care hospitals in Texas, Medicaid, compared with private insurance, was associated with a similar pattern among working-age survivors of intensive care: more discharge to nursing facilities, less discharge to inpatient rehabilitation or home without services, and longer hospital stays. It was present with admission-time adjustment alone and, in Texas, within hospitals, in a bounded-probability model, across charge-defined subgroups, and without dependence on any single hospital.

Destination differences changed modestly but persisted as covariates measured during the stay were added, whereas the LOS differences narrowed by a third to a half with adjustment for comorbidity and severity. Whether Medicaid patients received more facility-based care differed between settings, because LTCH use was higher with Medicaid in Massachusetts and lower in Texas.

The consistent finding -nursing facility rather than rehabilitation -extends results from working-age and registry stroke populations (Skolarus et al., 2012; Medford-Davis et al., 2016) to a broad, all-diagnosis critical illness population. Its appearance in two data sources, and within Texas hospitals, makes it unlikely to reflect one institution's practices or the sorting of Medicaid patients to particular hospitals. It is compatible with the provider-acceptance, administrative, and social channels of the framework, which could operate in both states. The data cannot show whether the SNF placements were clinically appropriate, whether patients were eligible for or offered IRF care, or how patients fared after discharge.

The differences between settings are consistent with the benefit channel, but the design cannot attribute them to it. During the Texas study years, state regulation limited Medicaid payment for inpatient hospital care to the first 30 days of each spell of illness, with a new spell beginning only after 60 consecutive days out of an acute care facility (1 Tex. Admin. Code § 354.1072). The December 2018 and August 2019 editions of the state provider manual defined inpatient rehabilitation as a benefit when provided as part of a general acute care admission, or with prior authorization for clients aged 20 years or younger in a freestanding rehabilitation facility, made all rehabilitation subject to the spell-of-illness limit, and set a $200,000 annual inpatient limit for adults aged 21 years or older (Texas Medicaid & Healthcare Partnership, 2018 , 2019; 1 Tex. Admin. Code § 354.1149). These rules did not apply uniformly: a state evaluation describes the spell-of-illness limit as applying to adults in fee-for-service Medicaid, STAR+PLUS (the managed care program for adults with disabilities), and STAR Health, but not in STAR (the program for pregnant women and low-income parents), and the $200,000 limit as applying only in fee-for-service Medicaid and STAR Health (Texas Health and Human Services Commission, 2024). Texas was also among the states without Medicaid IRF coverage in 2010 (Skolarus et al., 2014). We could not obtain a 2017 manual, the manual does not address LTCH stays, and the payer field does not identify program, so which stays were subject to which rule is unknown. The very low Texas ratios for IRF and LTCH discharge, their concentration among ventilated patients, and the relative increases in SNF and home health discharge are consistent with these rules. In Massachusetts, whose Medicaid regulations provide for chronic disease and rehabilitation hospital services (MassHealth, 2015), the IRF difference was smaller and LTCH use was higher with Medicaid; the regulation does not show that all enrollees had equivalent access to these settings. The Texas LTCH finding also matches evidence from Pennsylvania (Lane-Fall et al., 2012), where these rules did not apply. The contrast therefore motivates a hypothesis -that adult Medicaid benefit design shapes the type of post-acute care after critical illness -that requires designs using policy variation to test.

Eligibility composition also differs. During the study period, Texas Medicaid covered parents only up to 17% of the federal poverty level and did not cover other nondisabled adults, whereas Massachusetts covered adults up to 138% (Kaiser Family Foundation & Georgetown University Center for Children and Families, 2019). Working-age Texas Medicaid patients are therefore more likely to qualify through disability, pregnancy, or extreme poverty, and may have functional and social needs that administrative data do not capture. This could contribute to the larger Texas differences, although greater need alone would be expected to increase, not decrease, IRF and LTCH use, and excluding pregnancy stays did not change the results.

Longer stays with Medicaid in both settings, concentrated among patients discharged to facilities, are compatible with documented delays in authorization and placement (Sorensen et al., 2020; Haddad et al., 2025) and with Massachusetts reports of payer-related transition delays (Massachusetts Health & Hospital Association, 2025; Massachusetts TACPAC Task Force, 2025), but LOS also reflects severity, complications, and the ICU course -adjustment for these narrowed the differences -and neither data source records when patients were medically ready for discharge.

Policy implications

The findings have three implications for research and practice, none of which assumes that a shift between settings is harmful. First, studies of post-acute access should report destinations separately; in Texas, a single facility composite showed little difference by payer while concealing large, offsetting SNF, IRF, and LTCH differences. Second, adult Medicaid coverage of IRF and LTCH care and inpatient day limits are explicit, documented benefit choices that are consistent with the largest differences here; they merit direct evaluation with designs that exploit changes in these rules, linked to clinical eligibility, program enrollment, and post-discharge outcomes. Third, hospitals and Medicaid plans could track destination and LOS by payer alongside referral, authorization, and acceptance data to identify which channel operates.

Limitations

This study has limitations. The settings are not equivalent in unit of analysis, hospitals, covariates, or measurement, and the Massachusetts common-period subset only approximates 2017-2019, so differences between them cannot be attributed to state policy. Insurance was measured from administrative records, which may misclassify coverage, program, plan type, and dual eligibility; in Texas, some Medicaid managed care claims may be coded as commercial.

Benefit rules could not be linked to individual stays. The Texas cohort is defined by billing categories that include intermediate, burn, trauma, and psychiatric intensive care charges, and repeated stays by the same patient cannot be identified, so hospital-clustered errors do not capture dependence among stays by one patient at different hospitals. Discharge destination fields can misclassify LTCH transfers (Kahn & Iwashyna, 2010), and destinations are harmonized administrative categories. Covariates measured after admission may be affected by care processes, and Massachusetts comorbidity codes lack present-on-admission indicators.

Administrative data lack pre-admission function, caregiver support, and preferences, and the diagnosis-coded social-needs proxies may be incompletely and differentially coded. We did not observe whether IRF or LTCH care was requested, denied, or declined, or any post-discharge outcomes. Finally, Setting 1 is a single academic center.

Conclusion

Among working-age survivors of intensive care at a Massachusetts academic medical center and Texas hospitalizations with ICU or coronary care unit accommodation charges, Medicaid was consistently associated with nursing facility rather than rehabilitation discharge and with longer hospital stays, whereas overall facility use differed between settings.
