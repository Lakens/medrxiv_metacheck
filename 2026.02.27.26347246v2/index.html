To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 1 non-significant p value that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Metacheck does not yet analyze automatically whether sentences which include non-significant p-values are correct, but we recommend manually checking the sentences below for possible misinterpreted non-significant p values.

p ≥ 0.53 For all other predictors, there was no evidence of differences between subgroups (all adjusted p ≥ 0.53).

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). Statcheck might still miss one-sided tests that are described in another sentence, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). How Non-Significant P Value Check works

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Introduction: Atmospheric conditions under climate change constitute an increasing health burden and raise pressure on health care systems. Intensive care units (ICUs) are particularly vulnerable due to their low buffer capacity and high utilization rates. This study aimed to examine the association between atmospheric exposures and ICU admissions for intensive care relevant diseases across Germany. Methods: We analyzed daily time-series data on ICU admissions from AOK health insurances and atmospheric data in the period 2009 to 2023. Using principal component analysis, we clustered Germany into eleven human-bioclimatic regions. For each atmospheric predictor, the optimal time lag was selected, and disease-specific predictor sets were defined while accounting for multiple testing. In each region, we fitted quasi-Poisson generalized additive models to estimate diseasespecific ICU admission risks associated with atmospheric exposures. Models were stratified by age (<60, ≥60 years) and sex. Model performance was assessed using (weighted) explained deviance (WED). Results: Over the 15-year study period, on average 2,526 ICU admissions per 100,000 AOK insured persons per year were recorded in Germany (44% women, 74.3% aged ≥60 years). Pressure-related variables were most frequently associated with ICU admissions (p ≤ 0.05). Sea-level pressure demonstrated a significant association in 29.3% of the models. Heat-related variables, including tropical nights (18.3%) and summer days (17.8%), were significantly associated with ICU admissions in Germany. Among air pollutants, ozone showed the highest proportion of significant associations (15.6%). Significant associations for heat-related atmospheric variables were observed more frequently among men and persons ≥60 years. Mean model performance across all models was 10% WED. The models for ICU admissions due to myocardial infarction (NSTEMI) in the East German Lowlands demonstrated the best model performance (WED: 49%). Discussion: Atmospheric factors were associated with ICU admissions, and these associations differed by region, disease, and age-sex group. Pressure-and heat-related factors were the most relevant atmospheric factors. These findings indicate that atmospheric factors have the potential to adversely impact intensive health care preparedness and delivery.

1 Introduction

Urbanization, economic development, and industrial growth have contributed to increasing levels of air pollution and changes of the atmospheric environment over recent decades. At the same time, climate change is expected to increase the frequency, intensity, and duration of atmospheric hazards, including heat waves and other extreme weather events [1, 2]. These environmental changes represent important stressors for human health and are associated with substantial increases in morbidity and mortality [3, 4]. A growing body of evidence indicates that there is an influence on the occurrence and exacerbation of a wide range of diseases, including respiratory, cardiovascular, gastrointestinal, neurological, and trauma-related diseases [5]. As many of these diseases frequently require hospital-based treatment, climate change-related health impacts are expected to place an increasing burden on health care systems. Vulnerable populations, particularly older adults, children, and individuals with pre-existing medical conditions, are disproportionately affected by atmospheric hazards and extreme weather events [4, 6]. Consequently, a comprehensive understanding of atmosphere-health relationships is essential for quantifying future health care demands and supporting the development of climate-resilient health care systems and infrastructure [7].

Numerous studies have reported associations between atmospheric stressors and increased rates of mortality and hospital admissions [2, 8]. However, considerably less attention has been paid to the potential implications for intensive care medicine, despite intensive care units (ICUs) representing one of the most resource-intensive and capacity-limited sectors of health care. Patients requiring intensive care are often characterized by severe acute illnesses, multiple comorbidities, and heightened physiological vulnerability, making them particularly susceptible to environmental stressors. Moreover, ongoing climate change may translate into higher demand for critical care services, potentially exacerbating existing capacity constraints. In Germany, the average ICU utilization rate of 81.6% leaves limited reserve capacity to accommodate sudden increases in demand associated with atmospheric conditions [9]. This challenge is further intensified by the persistent shortage of specialized healthcare personnel [10]. Understanding how atmospheric factors are associated with ICU admissions has therefore a substantial clinical and public health relevance, as it may help identify vulnerable patient groups, anticipate future health care demands, and support strategic resource planning in intensive care.

Despite growing evidence on climate-related health effects, nationwide assessments of atmospheric influences on ICU admissions remain limited, particularly regarding regional and disease-specific differences. This study addresses this gap by linking region-specific daily ICU admissions with atmospheric data across Germany. The objectives are to analyze atmosphere-ICU associations across Germany, quantify regional heterogeneity, and identify diseases and age-sex-specific subgroups with heightened susceptibility to atmospheric conditions.

2 Materials and Methods

2.1 Study sample and health data

Daily time-series data of intensive care admissions derived from anonymized, nationwide routine data from the German health care system was collected for the years 2009-2023. The effects of the Covid-19 pandemic on ICU admissions were observed, particularly in respiratory diseases (see Supplementary Figure S1). However, the impact on intensive care as a whole was negligible (Figure 2). Moreover, a long-term downward trend in admissions and health care utilization has been observed [11]. Consequently, the pandemic years were not considered separately. The 15-year health data was provided by the AOK Research Institute (WIdO), derived from statutory health insurance data of eleven regional AOK insurances. The study sample was stratified by sex and two age groups (<60 and ≥60 years old). The choice for only two age groups was motivated by the need to standardize the health insurance-based subsample of about 30% of the population to the total population of Germany in a follow-up study. Thus, four subgroups were analyzed: female ≥60 years (FE), male ≥60 years (ME), female <60 years (FY), and male <60 years (MY). Repeated admissions of one individual were not accounted for. All admissions were treated as independent admissions. The operationalization of intensive care required a broader definition, given that not all hospitals have dedicated ICU or fulfil the billing criteria for complex intensive care treatment. Therefore, the operation and procedure (OPS) code for intensive care complex treatment, the OPS code for intensive care patient monitoring, or the department code of ICU was used as a selection criterion (Supplementary, Table S1& S2). The study included admissions of the 13 most common diseases in the ICU, accounting for 46% of all ICU admissions [5, 12, 13]. The diseases were classified using the codes of the International Statistical Classification Of Diseases And Related Health Problems, 10th revision, German Modification (ICD-10-GM) (Supplementary, Table S3). They were grouped into the following categories: cardiovascular diseases (heart failure, non-ST elevation myocardial infarction (NSTEMI), ST elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI), tachycardia, pulmonary artery embolism), respiratory diseases (pneumonia, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD)), neurological diseases (ischemic insult, transient ischemic attack, non subdural hemorrhage, epilepsy), internal diseases (sepsis) and traumatic diseases (trauma). For a few disease categories, the term "ICU admissions" was defined more broadly than the ICU definition given above. For cardiovascular diseases, continuous rhythm monitoring is essential. Therefore, admissions treated in settings such as chest pain units were also classified as ICU admissions. Without this adjustment, a considerable proportion of cardiovascular admissions requiring intensive monitoring might not have been captured. Similarly, for respiratory diseases, admissions receiving ventilation outside of an ICU were included to ensure that all admissions requiring intensive care-level treatment were represented (Supplementary Table S3). For neurological diseases (except epilepsy), admissions were categorized separately according to whether treatment was provided in an ICU or in a specialized stroke unit (Supplementary, Table S4), resulting in the consideration of 16 outcome types in total. Prior to statistical model building, the daily health data was analyzed regarding erroneous values within the ICD-10-GM coding system, internal validity as well as with respect to completeness, homogeneity, variability, outliers and trends [14, 15].

2.2 Human-bioclimatic Regionalization

To account for regional differences and to define internally homogeneous exposure clusters, Germany was divided into human-bioclimatic regions (HBC-regions). The regionalization was based on seasonally resolved climatic variables, which are related to human health. Data was derived from the ERA5-Land reanalysis dataset provided by the Copernicus Climate Data Store [16]. For the classification of the HBC-regions, a spatial principal component analysis was used to identify the optimal number of regions, and the grid boxes were assigned to the determined number of HBCregions by means of a k-means cluster analysis. The regionalization identified six main regions and, given the presence of still some noticeable intra-regional variations, three further subregions using the aforementioned methods were built. Overall, the regionalization resulted in eleven distinct, bioclimatic consistent HBC-regions, characterizing the different human-bioclimatic conditions in Germany on regional scales (Figure 1). The eleven HBC-regions were mapped to their corresponding ZIP codes, allowing the daily health data to be assigned to the respective regions.

2.3 Atmospheric data

For assessing the atmospheric impact on intensive care, daily data of multiple atmospheric variables and indices was collected. Daily meteorological data was obtained from the quality-controlled ERA5 reanalysis data of the European Centre for Medium-Range Weather Forecasts (ECMWF), and air pollution data from the Copernicus Atmosphere Monitoring Service (CAMS) [16] [17] [18] [19]. In total, 31 atmospheric variables and indices were extracted (Supplementary, Table S5). Averaging atmospheric exposures within the HBC-regions inevitably led to a reduction in the spatial resolution of the atmospheric data as well as to a smoothing of extremes. This limitation particularly affected the representation of urban heat island effects as well as the small-scale variability of air quality variables. Thus, regional exposure characterization can be regarded as background exposure, giving an overall conservative exposure assignment.

2.4 Statistical analysis

To investigate the associations between daily ICU admissions and atmospheric data, we calculated generalized additive models (GAMs) with quasi-Poisson distribution to account for overdispersion [20, 21]. GAMs are particularly useful for time series analyses that investigate the relationship between climate effects and health, and allow nonparametric adjustments for nonlinear effects [20, [22] [23] [24]. In each HBC-region the ICU outcomes stratified according to sex and age were analyzed. In total, 704 different models (11 regions x 16 ICU outcome types x 4 subgroups) were created to establish the regional associations between the daily ICU admissions and atmospheric factors.

To determine the optimal lag in the exposure-outcome models, for all regions, outcome types and subgroups bivariate predictor-response models were built using no lag up to ten days lag for meteorological predictors and up to seven days for air quality predictors [23, 25]. The optimal lag for each atmospheric predictor was selected based on the relative risk (RR) and 95% confidence interval (CI) [26]. Subsequently, for each region, outcome type and subgroup GAMs were established using all predictor variables with their specific lags. Predictor variables remained in the models if the pvalue was significant (α ≤ 0.05) after Bonferroni correction. The correction for multiple testing was applied separately within each region-, outcome-, and subgroup-specific model [27, 28]. In order to harmonize predictor sets according to outcome types and to facilitate interpretation, the final predictor set per outcome type consisted of all predictors which were at least significant in one GAM out of 44 in total (11 regions x 4 subgroups). The resulting outcome type-specific predictor sets were subsequently used to fit the final GAMs for each combination of outcome type, subgroup, and HBCregion.

For nonlinear associations, thin plate regression splines with shrinkage were used as the smoothing basis [29, 30]. Models were fitted using restricted maximum likelihood (REML), which provides robust estimation of smoothing parameters and is particularly suitable for models with overdispersed count data [30]. The shrinkage approach (select = TRUE) additionally allowed terms with little explanatory value to be penalized towards zero, thereby reducing the influence of irrelevant predictors [30]. Next to atmospheric predictors, the models controlled for seasonality effects through sinusoidal and cosinusoidal components, time trend as well as weekday versus weekend [26, [31] [32] [33] [34]. Seasonality and time trend were represented in the models using thin plate regression splines.

Collinearity among the atmospheric predictors was assessed using Spearman's rank correlation coefficient. Predictor pairs with an absolute correlation coefficient of |ρ| ≥ 0.7 were considered as collinear. In cases of collinearity, the predictor showing the stronger statistical association with the respective outcome, as indicated by the lower p-value in the regression analysis, was retained, while the less strongly associated predictor was excluded from the model [35].

Explained deviance (ED) was used to check model performance. To summarize subgroup-specific performance for each region and outcome type, weighted explained deviance (WED) was calculated by weighting the EDs of the subgroups according to their proportion in the case numbers of a region and outcome. Differences between subgroups were assessed descriptively by comparing the WED, ED and the proportion of atmospheric predictors with a significant association (p ≤ 0.05). These comparisons are therefore exploratory and do not allow conclusions on statistically significant differences between subgroups. For the regional outcome type with the highest WED, predictor-specific log-RRs from the corresponding subgroup-specific models were compared assuming independence between subgroups. Heterogeneity across the four subgroups was assessed using Cochran's Q test and quantified by I².

The resulting p-values were adjusted across predictors using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure [36]. For predictors exhibiting significant heterogeneity (adjusted p < 0.05), pairwise z-tests were performed, comparing sexes within age groups and age groups within sexes. Results are reported as ratios of RRs with corresponding 95% CIs and p-values.

The main statistical analyses were performed using the package mgcv in R (Version 4.5.1) and RStudio (Version 2025.05.1.513) [20, 23]. The study was conducted in accordance to the Declaration of Helsinki and the reporting follows the RECORD guidelines for studies using routine health data [37].

3 Results

3.1 Health data

The sample comprised on average 2,526 standardized ICU admissions per 100,000 AOK insured persons per year over the 15-year study period (a total of 9,970,548 ICU admissions) (Figure 2, Supplementary: Standardized ICU admissions per outcome type, Figure S1). 44% of the admissions were female and 74.3% of the admitted persons were ≥60 years old. ICU admissions were most frequent in the ME subgroup and least frequent in the FY subgroup. Compared with the FY subgroup, the ME subgroup exhibited approximately three times as many ventilation days. In contrast, obesity was more common among people younger than 60 years than among those aged ≥60 years. The highest proportion of obese people was observed in the FY subgroup (Table 1). Trauma accounted for the highest number of ICU admissions among all outcome types. In the ≥60 year subgroups, ischemic stroke represented one of the most frequent outcome types, whereas pneumonia was more prevalent in the <60 year subgroups (Table 1). An almost continuous decrease in ICU admissions was observed across all HBC-regions (except 6.3) from 2017 onwards. Over the entire study period, HBC-region 6.1 (Mecklenburg Lake District in northeastern Germany) consistently showed the highest ICU admission rate per 100,000 AOK insured people (Figure 2).

3.2 Atmospheric data

A comparison of regional atmospheric characteristics revealed pronounced differences across the eleven HBC-regions (Supplementary Figures S2-S8). Distinct regional patterns emerged, particularly regarding cold exposure, heat exposure, and air pollution. The most cold-exposed regions were observed in HBC-regions 5.2 (Thuringian-Franconian Low Mountain Range) and 3.1 (Alpine Foothills & Southern Low Mountain Ranges). HBC-region 5.2 exhibited the highest number of ice days (average: 27 days/year; daily Tmax < 0°C) and the lowest mean wind chill index (WCI, mean ± standard deviation: 5.4 ± 7.4°C). Similarly, HBC-region 3.1 showed pronounced cold exposure, with the highest number of frost days (average: 95.2 days/year; daily Tmin < 0°C) and an absence of tropical nights (daily Tmin > 20°C). In contrast, HBC-region 2.2 (southwest Germany) represented the most heat-exposed region, with the highest number of tropical nights (average: 1.5 nights/year, maximum: 6 nights in 2018), summer days (average: 43.5 days/year; daily Tmax > 25°C), and hot days (average: 9.6 days/year; daily Tmax > 30°C), as well as the highest mean wet-bulb globe temperature (WBGT: 13.6 ± 7.5°C). The highest daily mean temperature was observed in HBCregion 1.2 (10.7 ± 6.8°C). Overall, the air quality in Germany improved over the study period. Only ozone levels continued to rise in all regions over the 15 years (Supplementary, Figure S2). Even though the air pollution exposure on the chosen regional scale does only reflect average background conditions, distinct air pollution characteristics could be noticed. Elevated air pollutant concentrations were primarily observed in Lower Rhine and southwest Germany (HBC-regions 1.2, 2.2). The Lower Rhine region exhibited the highest daily mean concentrations of nitrogen dioxide (NO₂) (8.2 ± 3.7ppbv) and particulate matter (PM10) (19.5 ± 6.6µg/m³), while southwest Germany showed the highest daily mean PM2.5 concentration (13.5 ± 6.5µg/m³). In contrast, the highest daily mean ozone concentration was observed in HBC-region 5.2 (36.8 ± 12.4ppbv). Northern regions exhibited the greatest exposure to atmospheric pressure variability, with HBC-regions 4.1 and 6.3 experiencing the highest frequency of day-to-day pressure changes exceeding 10hPa (approximately 15 days per year). Likewise, the greatest number of wind days was recorded in north-eastern Germany, particularly in HBC-regions 6.2, 4.1, and 1.1, with up to 18 wind days annually. Conversely, the highest mean sea-level pressure (SLP) was observed in southern Germany, namely in HBC-regions 3.1 (1017.4hPa) and 2.1 (1017.2hPa).

3.3 Health-atmosphere associations

The outcome type-specific predictor sets included between six and 18 atmospheric predictors (Table 2). Across all models, the three pressure-related variables (SLP, SLPD, wind day) showed most frequently statistically significant associations (p ≤ 0.05) with ICU admissions. SLP was significantly associated with ICU admissions in 29.3% of all models (206/704 models), wind day in 24.9% (175/704 models) and sea-level pressure difference (SLPD) in 20.2% (142/704 models). Heat-related predictors followed, in particular tropical nights (18.3% of models) and summer days (17.8%). Among air pollutants, ozone showed the highest proportion of significant associations (15.6% of models). Cold wave day was the only atmospheric predictor with no significant association in any model. Table 3 summarizes the significant associations between atmospheric predictors and ICU admissions. All outcome-specific results are provided in the supplement and show that the atmospheric factors act on specific outcomes. Pressure-and cold-related predictors were predominantly associated with respiratory outcomes, whereas heat-related variables were mainly associated with neurological outcomes. Cardiovascular outcomes were associated with a broad range of atmospheric factors (Supplementary, Table S6).

3.3.1 Sex-and age-specific differences

Outcome-specific analyses showed differences between sexes (Supplementary Table S7), detailed analysis including statistical effect sizes are given in the Supplementary Tables S8-S23. The proportion of significant associations per atmospheric factor was almost the same for both sexes (differences < 2% for 21 of 30 atmospheric factors, Supplementary Table S7). SLP showed the highest proportion in both sexes (female 27.3%, male 31.2%). Sex differences were most pronounced for SLPD (female 22.7% vs. male 17.6%) and wet-bulb globe temperature (female 9.1% vs. male 13.1%). Heat-related predictors were consistently more frequently significant in males, with tropical nights being the exception (men: 17.0%; women: 19.9%). Conversely, cold stress wave indices, particulate matter (PM10) and ozone were more often significant in females. Outcome-specific analyses revealed further sex differences (Supplementary Table S24). For ischemic stroke treated in stroke units, neither cold wave duration nor heat stress wave days were significantly associated with ICU admissions in women, whereas both were significant in 13.6% of the models for men. For trauma, PM10 was significantly associated with ICU admissions in 22.7% of the models for women but in none of the models for men.

Overall, a larger proportion of models for persons aged ≥60 years showed significant associations with atmospheric factors. Age-stratified analyses showed that the proportion of significant associations differed mainly for heat-related and wind-related factors (Supplementary Table S7).

Heat-related indices were more frequently significant in people aged ≥60 years (e.g., heat wave day: 10.8% vs. 3.7%; summer day: 20.7% vs. 14.8%), as well as for atmospheric pressure predictors (SLP: 31.8% vs. 26.7%; SLPD: 20.7% vs. 19.6%). In contrast, wind days (<60: 29.0% vs. ≥60: 20.7%) and cold wave indices (cold wave: <60 6.2% vs. ≥60 2.3%) were more often significant in people aged <60 years. An examination of outcome-specific differences revealed a significant association between tropical nights and neurological outcomes, particularly among people <60 years (e.g., ischemic stroke: 40.9% vs. 36.4%, non subdural hemorrhage: 45.5% vs. 22.7%), whereas hot days (Tmax >30 °C) were significantly associated among persons ≥60 years (Supplementary Table S24). Cold-related predictors, such as cold waves, exhibited a significant association with respiratory outcomes more often in persons <60 years than in those ≥60 years (pneumonia: 59.1% vs. 9.1%, COPD: 31.8% vs. 18.2%).

3.3.2 Best-fitting regional outcome type based on weighted explained deviance (WED)

Model performance was evaluated and compared across outcome types and HBC-regions using the WED. Averaged across all outcome types and HBC-regions, the WED was 10%. When averaged across all regions, WED ranged from 2% for epilepsy to 34% for NSTEMI (Table 4). NSTEMI was the best-performing outcome type in every HBC-region except the north-east (HBC-region 6.1), where trauma performed marginally better (WED: 11% for trauma vs. 10% for NSTEMI; Supplementary Table S25). At the level of individual regions, the highest WED was observed for NSTEMI in HBC-region 6.2 (WED = 49%). At subgroup level for NSTEMI in HBC-region 6.2, a particularly good model fit was observed in the older subgroups (FE and ME: ED = 56%). An overview of the ED values by region, outcome type, and subgroup are provided in Supplementary Table S26.

To illustrate the associations between atmospheric factors and daily ICU admissions in more detail, we focus on the four subgroup-specific NSTEMI models for eastern Germany (HBC-region 6.2). The results (including lags, effect sizes and p-values) for all other models are given in the Supplementary (Tables S8-S23). SLP was significantly associated with NSTEMI admissions (p ≤ 0.05) in all subgroup models except for female <60 years. A 10hPa lower SLP was associated with a 1.1% to 1.6% higher risk of ICU admissions (FE: RR 1.011, 95% CI 1.003 -1.019; FY: RR 1.000, 95% CI 0.999 -1.000; ME: RR 1.012, 95% CI 1.005 -1.021; MY: RR 1.016, 95% CI 1.003 -1.028; Supplementary Figure S9). Other pressure-related variables, such as wind days, showed no collinearity with SLP and no relevant association (Figure 3). From the heat-related variables only heat waves showed a statistically significant association in males ≥60 years (RR 0.941, 95% CI 0.895 -0.989, also see Figure 3 and Supplementary Figures S10, S11), corresponding to a 6% lower risk of ICU admissions on heat wave days. In contrast, cold-related variables such as frost days were associated with higher risk for ICU admissions in older persons, estimates in younger persons were similar but nonsignificant and less precise (FE: RR 1.022, 95% CI 1.001 -1.043; ME: 1.024, 95% CI 1.004 -1.044; FY: RR 1.038, 95% CI 0.982 -1.098; MY: RR 1.022, 95% CI 0.987 -1.058; Figure 3, WCI: Supplementary Figure S12). No air pollutant was significantly associated with NSTEMI admissions (p ≤ 0.05), and the effect estimates were close to zero (Figure 3, Supplementary, Figure S13). Tests for heterogeneity between subgroups indicated differences only for SLP (Cochran's Q = 14.2, adjusted p = 0.016; I² = 79%). For all other predictors, there was no evidence of differences between subgroups (all adjusted p ≥ 0.53). The heterogeneity for SLP was driven by a weaker association in women <60 years compared with men <60 years (ratio of RRs 0.985, 95% CI 0.972 -0.998, p = 0.05) and women ≥60 years (ratio of RRs 0.989, 95%CI 0.980 -0.998, p = 0.05). In summary, lower SLP and cold-related conditions were associated with higher risk for NSTEMIrelated ICU admissions in eastern Germany, whereas heat and air pollution showed little or no association.

4 Discussion

The present study indicates that atmospheric factors are associated with intensive care admissions, with pronounced differences by atmospheric predictor, outcome, region, age and sex. Pressurerelated variables were most frequently significantly associated with ICU admissions, followed by heat-related predictors. Associations were dependent on outcome types and no atmospheric factor was associated with all outcome types. This suggests that diseases are influenced by different atmospheric exposures. The relevance of atmospheric pressure for an increased risk of cardiovascular and neurological diseases was also reported in earlier studies showing for example that drops in atmospheric pressure are related with stroke incidence [38] [39] [40]. Heat-related predictors, particularly tropical nights and summer days, were significantly associated with ICU admissions in a considerable proportion of models and were mainly linked to neurological outcomes. This is consistent with previous research. He et al. [41] reported that higher nighttime temperatures were associated with an increased stroke risk, and other studies have linked temperature to mortality and cardiopulmonary diseases [4, 42]. The relevance of both day-and nighttime heat for ICU admissions suggests that insufficient nocturnal cooling may contribute to thermal stress. In contrast, heat and cold (stress) wave related indices were less frequently associated with ICU admissions. Although earlier studies have reported associations between heat waves and various diseases, inconsistent definitions of spell-based indices and differences in the health outcomes studied make comparisons difficult [12, 43]. Thermo-physiological indices, which combine temperature, humidity and radiation, played an important role in many models. This supports the assumption that other thermal stress variables than temperature alone are relevant for ICU admissions in Germany. Also the German Weather Service uses perceived temperature to assess thermal stress and issues warnings to health authorities [44].

Beyond purely temperature-based predictors, thermo-physiological indices should therefore be considered in practical applications to describe thermal stress more accurately. Among the air pollutants, ozone showed the highest proportion of significant associations with ICU admissions, whereas all other pollutants were significantly associated in fewer than 10 % of models. A possible explanation is the decline in concentrations of these pollutants over the 15-year study period, which reduced exposure and thus the ability to detect associations, whereas ozone exposure has steadily increased in Germany. Therefore, future studies and health adaptation tools should consider daily ozone levels to reduce health consequences and support the planning of health care capacities [45]. However, it also must be noted, that in the present study air pollution exposure was described by regional averages. This could have led to an exposure underestimation in certain areas such as larger urban areas and industrial hubs.

In the sex-stratified analyses, a larger proportion of models for males showed significant associations with atmospheric factors than for females. However, since models were fitted separately for each subgroup and comparisons are based on the proportion of significant results, these differences do not necessarily reflect true differences in susceptibility. Although slightly more ICU admissions occurred among men (56 %), which may have contributed to higher statistical power, this is unlikely to be the sole explanation. Heat-related factors were more frequently significantly associated with ICU admissions in men, whereas women showed more significant associations with air pollutants, particularly PM10 and ozone, which is in line with literature describing a higher vulnerability of women to air pollution [46]. The lower number of significant heat associations in women could be related to behavioral and occupational factors, such as higher health awareness, greater use of preventive care or lower occupational heat exposure [47, 48]. As these factors were not assessed in this study, this explanation remains speculative. Age-specific analyses showed more significant associations with atmospheric factors in people aged ≥60 years. Gronlund et al. [49] reported increased hospital admissions and emergency room visits among older people during extreme heat and heatwaves. The higher statistical power of the models for elderly may partly be explained by the high proportion of older adults admitted to ICUs. Nevertheless, heat-related factors were also significantly associated with ICU admissions in younger age groups, indicating that heat is relevant across all ages.

Overall, the best model performance based on WED was observed for myocardial infarction (NSTEMI) models. As NSTEMI was among the outcomes with the most frequent ICU admissions, the large number of admissions may have allowed higher statistical power. In eastern Germany (HBC-region 6.2), atmospheric pressure was significantly associated with NSTEMI ICU admissions in most subgroup models. A 10hPa drop in atmospheric pressure as well as cold-related events were associated with an increase in ICU admission risk. In this context, a sudden atmospheric pressure drop may act directly on the cardiovascular system or serve as a proxy for the associated changes of the weather conditions. For cold-related associations, increased blood viscosity and arterial pressure have been discussed as possible mechanisms, although neither was assessed in this study [50]. No clear association was found between heat and NSTEMI, which may be explained by less heat exposure in this region. The apparently protective effect of heat waves in older men was only marginally significant (upper confidence limit close to 1). It should therefore be interpreted with caution. Fine particulate matter has previously been linked to vascular damage and myocardial infarction [51]. The absence of such an association in these models may be due to the use of regional averages, which cannot capture small-scale variations in air pollutant concentrations and may therefore obscure localized exposure effects. Although the effects of the atmospheric factors were relatively small and their public health relevance at the population level is therefore limited, even small changes in ICU admissions can be detrimental to health care preparedness and delivery [52].

A strength of this study is the use of routine data, which reflect real-world healthcare provision and provide a large number of cases, enabling disease-specific and subgroup analyses [14]. Changes in ICD-10-GM coding were checked and incorporated into the case selection, and the ICU-relevant diseases were defined a priori. Furthermore, the consideration of different HBC-regions in Germany made it possible to capture regional differences in atmospheric-health associations across the whole country.

Several limitations should be considered. AOK data are claims data, and coding biases due to financial incentives may have affected data validity [14]. Also, the sample consisted exclusively of AOK insured individuals. The age and sex structure differs from that of the German population, which may limit external validity. However, AOK insured people account for almost one third of the German population [53]. In a follow-up study, which will assess the prediction suitability of the models, the results will be age-sex standardized to the German population. Thus, subgroup models were fitted separately in the present study, taking the loss that differences in the number of ICU admissions may have led to differences in statistical power. Regarding atmospheric predictors, the use of regional averages for exposure did not allow to capture small-scale variations, particularly relevant for air pollutants and the urban heat islands. Furthermore, Bonferroni correction was applied within each model but not across the large number of region-, outcome-and subgroup-specific models, so some significant associations may be chance findings. Collinear predictors (|ρ| ≥ 0.7) were handled by retaining the predictor with the lower p-value in the regional regression analysis. As a result, retained predictors may be favored, which should be considered when interpreting the proportion of significant models and comparing predictors. Also, as for any epidemiological time series study, the present study does not per se allow causal interpretation. Models were adjusted for seasonality, time trend and weekday, but not for other time-varying factors such as public holidays or influenza epidemics, which may be particularly relevant for respiratory outcomes. The study period of 15 years included the COVID-19 pandemic, as the impact of the pandemic on the intensive care was negligible and the data shown a long-term downward trend in admissions, suggesting a lasting change in health care structures [11]. Furthermore, given that intensive care constitutes critical infrastructure that is less affected by the postponement of elective procedures than general hospital admissions, the pandemic period was not analyzed separately [54].

In conclusion, this study indicates that atmospheric factors, most noticeably pressure-related ones, are associated with intensive care admissions in Germany. The study also extends previous research that focused mainly on mortality and general hospitalization. Associations differed by region, outcome, age and sex. Although the observed effects were small, they may be relevant for critical health care infrastructure such as intensive care and may inform regional ICU capacity planning. In a follow-up study, the identified associations will be used to assess short-term predictability of ICU admissions with the scope to develop a practically relevant tool.
