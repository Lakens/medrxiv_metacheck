Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. Abstract

Background: Following stroke, motor and cognitive impairments often limit functional independence. Personalized rehabilitation requires comprehensive clinical assessments, which are costly and time-consuming. Imaging scans are routinely collected, but their predictive value in determining post-stroke function remains unclear.

Objectives: To determine the ability of clinical assessments, lesion features with medical chart information, and a combined clinical and biomedical model to predict kinematic outcomes from a robot-based tracking task in chronic stroke individuals.

Methods: Eighteen participants (70±6 years) completed a robot-based tracking task and a battery of motor and cognitive assessments. Three elastic net regression models were developed: 1) clinical assessments only; 2) lesion metrics and chart information only; and 3) a combined model. Models were evaluated using leave-one-subject-out cross-validation, bootstrapping, and permutation testing with false discovery rate correction. Robot-based outcomes included tracking error, normalized distance, velocity error response, and delay.

Results: The clinical assessment model significantly predicted tracking error (R²=0.45), delay (R²=0.22), velocity error response (R²=0.18), and normalized distance (R²=0.12). The combined model significantly predicted tracking error (R²=0.37), delay (R²=0.19), and velocity error response (R²=0.11). The imaging model did not significantly predict any outcome. Box and Blocks Test and Color Trails 1 were stable features. The right dorsal attention network was a stable predictor in several combined models.

Conclusion: Clinical assessments outperformed lesion features in predicting tracking performance after chronic stroke. Attention-and motor-related measures were the strongest predictors, highlighting the importance of cognitive-motor interactions in function. A larger sample and more granular lesion features may improve prediction from imaging biomarkers.

1. Introduction

One in four people over the age of 25 will have a stroke during their life, resulting in approximately 94 million people living with stroke (Feigin et al., 2025). Following stroke, many individuals experience motor and cognitive impairments, which can be detrimental to their quality of life. Specifically, recent studies report post-stroke cognitive impairment at a rate of 60% (El Husseini et al., 2023) and motor impairment at a rate of 61.7% (Clery et al., 2020). High-intensity rehabilitation is essential for increasing functional recovery and independence; however, in lowand middle-income countries (LMIC), access to rehabilitation services is limited ("Global Inequalities in Stroke Rehabilitation: System Capacity, Workforce, and Access in Low-and Middle-Income Countries | Current Physical Medicine and Rehabilitation Reports | Springer Nature Link," 2026). This need is particularly important as LMICs account for 89% of the global disability adjusted life years due stroke (Feigin et al., 2025). Improving equitable and effective delivery of post-stroke rehabilitation services in low-resource environments requires optimal allocation of rehabilitation services, which may be facilitated by predictive models of rehabilitation needs.

Depending on the size and location of the lesion, a person diagnosed with stroke may develop various motor and cognitive impairments. Imaging information is crucial for predicting recovery as function is influenced by the location of neuroanatomic injury (Jo et al., 2023).

Structural brain imaging is routine procedure in post-stroke care, typically performed using Computerized Tomography (CT) or Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI). Although MRI-scans offer superior tissue contrast, they are expensive and not widely available in LMICs (Murali et al., 2024). CT-scans, however, are more affordable and more widely available in both low and highresource settings.

While several studies have investigated the relationship between post-stroke function and neural lesion features using CT-scans, these studies focused on specific regions of interests, rather than exploring the whole brain (Ernst et al., 2018). Focusing on highly specific brain regions does not consider that the brain is deeply interconnected. For example, the premotor cortex (motor domain) relays information between the motor cortex (motor domain) and cognitive processing areas, such as the prefrontal cortex (cognitive domain). Therefore, any disruption in these networks due to stroke may lead to deficits in both motor and cognitive performance. Although few studies have examined how often these deficits co-occur, the available evidence suggests that cognition influences motor performance, and that low or moderate cognitive function can mask otherwise strong motor performance (Bui et al., 2022; Lin et al., 2021). Although information from the acute phase is valuable in clinical decision making, it does not directly inform therapeutic strategies administered in the chronic phase of rehabilitation, when long-term recovery of motor and cognitive abilities occurs. Given that the critical time window for post-stroke recovery extends beyond 12 months, well into the chronic phase of stroke, ongoing assessment throughout post-stroke rehabilitation care is essential (Ballester et al., 2019).

Traditionally, stroke rehabilitation relies on "impairment-level treatments," which strongly emphasizes motor performance (Shelton FN and Reding MJ, 2001). Administering comprehensive clinical assessment, however, is challenging even in high-resource environments and presents significant challenges in LMICs due to the extensive time required to complete them (~2-3 hours) and the limited number of experts trained to perform them. While simple self-report measures can be fast and require minimal training to administer, literature supports that stroke patients may not be cognizant of their own disabilities, limiting the efficacy of self-report measures (Hartman-Maeir et al., 2003).

More recently, rehabilitation robots have been proposed as a viable tool for administering clinical assessment, particularly in low-resource environments, with robot systems potentially showing greater sensitivity than standard clinical assessments (Ekechukwu et al., 2020; Logan et al., 2018). Moreover, robot-assisted therapy can deliver real-time feedback, supporting datadriven therapeutic adjustments. Through robot-assisted therapy, personalized neurorehabilitation strategies can be developed from a patient's current "presenting characteristics" (Jadhav AP et al., 2020). Specifically, robot-assisted therapy can target both motor and cognitive processes, with one study reporting improvements in targeted cognitive domains (Aprile et al., 2020). Despite this preliminary evidence for cognitive recovery, initial robotic intervention strategies are typically based on performance in clinical motor assessments, without considering cognitive domains such as executive function, attention, and working memory. Additionally, because cognitive impairments can mask true motor ability, patients with moderate cognitive impairment and mild motor impairment may be misclassified as having more significant motor impairment (Bui et al., 2022; Lin et al., 2021). This misclassification can distort a patient's functional profile and lead to suboptimal therapeutic decisions. Thus, relying solely on limited clinical assessment scores assumes a simplistic and unrealistic view of post-stroke recovery, especially if there is not sufficient time or staffing to deliver a comprehensive assessment battery. Furthermore, examining impairment without considering the source, namely brain damage in the form of a lesion, ignores the patient's true capacity for recovery.

Predicting chronic outcomes is essential, as therapists design interventions based on individual needs, which are often defined by observable motor and cognitive performance.

Discovering biomarkers, from non-invasive and accessible technology, that can predict functional recovery in stroke patients is a priority. Literature supports that combining biomarkers (such as imaging) and functional measures to predict recovery is an ideal model [17]. However, existing integrated prognostic models are limited. For example, PREP2 incorporated clinical and imaging measures to predict upper limb recovery, but it relied primarily on measures of overall disability and motor outcome rather than detailed assessments of motor and cognitive performance [17]. To address this gap, we developed models using routinely available information from the medical chart, CT-scan derived lesion metrics, and chronic motor and cognitive assessments to predict kinematic metrics from a complex robot-based task in a retrospective pilot cohort of medically complex patients in the chronic stage of stroke. We compared the performance of these models to one another in order to understand the relative value of different types of clinical information in predicting post-stroke performance.

2.2.1. Regions of Interest

The seven main brain networks will be considered. These networks, defined in Table I, encompass areas across the whole brain and are involved in several distinct functions that require human operation. Many of these brain regions are lateralized, with the right-and leftbrain regions covering specific functions. Because sensorimotor control is strongly lateralized, SMN lesion metrics were expressed relative to lesion side as ipsilesional and contralesional measures. In contrast, association networks were retained as left-and right-hemisphere features to preserve potential hemisphere-specific effects related to attention, salience processing, and cognition. Specifically, the right DAN and SAN can affect reaction time and cause neglect, which may lead to global deficits (Menon and Uddin, 2010; Shulman et al., 2010). Damage to the left DAN also causes some issues with neglect and attention that may lead to global deficits, so it was also included (Vossel et al., 2014). Although other networks were lateralized by function, the selected features were more relevant to the task. Hemorrhagic and subcortical strokes were included as a feature due to them being linked to work stroke outcomes (Balk et al., 2026; Liew SL et al., 2022).

2.4. Robot Assessment

Following the clinical battery, participants completed three robotic assessments: spatial span, n-back, and trajectory tracking. These assessments were initially developed to assess motor and cognitive function in individuals with HIV, stroke, and both HIV and stroke (Bui et al., 2022 (Bui et al., , 2021)). Participants completed the robotic assessments using THEAbot, which is a 1-DoF robot for upper limb rehabilitation developed by Recupero Robotics, LLC. THEAbot is reconfigurable with three positions that cover shoulder, elbow, and forearm. For assessment purposes, participants use the robot in position 1 where the robot is positioned to the side of the user with the workspace parallel to the sagittal plane. This movement simulates reach-to-grasp movements and involves flexion/extension of the shoulder and elbow (Meng et al., 2025).

For the purposes of this paper, we focus on the trajectory tracking task, which requires the user to follow the same path for 15 trials lasting 15 seconds each. Performance on continuous tracking tasks, quantified with kinematic outcome measures, have proven effective for identifying post-stroke motor impairment (Feng and Winters, 2009; Jones and Donaldson, 1981). Continuous tracking tasks, however, also involve cognitive domains. Preview tracking tasks in particular have shown correlations with cognitive assessments related to executive function and attention in addition to motor assessments related to motor impairment and motor activity (Bui et al., 2022 (Bui et al., , 2021)). Continuous preview tracking tasks allow for constant action monitoring and better imitate human behavior compared to discrete tasks (Pereira et al., 2017).

Such tasks are highly relevant to activities of daily living, e.g. driving, which require motor and cognitive domains (Jones and Donaldson, 1981). Trajectory tracking, therefore, is ideal to study multi-network interaction in the brain because it involves a complex task with both cognitive and motor inputs, and it is highly relevant to activities of daily living.

2.5. Predictive Modeling Procedure

To predict kinematic outcomes and simulation model parameters, we utilized elastic net regression, a form of penalized regression that combines L1 and L2 penalties, encouraging sparsity by setting some coefficients to zero while also accounting for co-linearity in predictors through a bias-variance trade-off ("Regularization and Variable Selection via the Elastic Net on JSTOR," n.d.). Two parameters, λ and α, define the model, where λ is the penalty strength parameter and α is the ratio between L1 and L2 penalties (α = 0 for ridge regression; α = 1 for lasso regression).

2.5.1. Outcome Variables

A total of six outcome variables were identified: three kinematic metrics and three simulation model parameter metrics. The kinematic metrics included root mean square tracking error (RMSE), normalized distance (ND), and spectral arc length (SPARC). RMSE represents the square root of the mean squared tracking error where a higher value corresponds to worse performance (Bui et al., 2021). ND represents the total distance traveled divided by the distance required to exactly follow the target; a value of one represents moving as much as the task requires while values above and below one represents moving more and less, respectively (Bui et al., 2021). SPARC is a measure of smoothness defined as the arc length of the Fourier magnitude spectrum of the speed profile; a more negative value corresponds to less smooth movement (Balasubramanian et al., 2015).

For simulation model parameters, we fit a feedback control simulation model, which is defined in Appendix B and in our previous work (Waters et al., 2025; Waters and Johnson, 2025). From this simulation model, we identified parameters including Kv, Delay, and Bias. Kv represents the gain on error in velocity; a greater value corresponds to a larger response to velocity error. Delay represents the average time shift that maximizes cross-correlation between the participant's trajectory and the target's trajectory. A higher value corresponds to greater delay in response. Bias represents the average median value of human position where the median position for the target is zero. The absolute value of Bias was used such that a bias to either side is treated the same.

2.5.2. Predictor Features

Three different feature matrices were constructed for the three different model types. For the M1 Clinical Assessment Model, we identified four candidate predictors: CT1, MoCA-EF, BBT, and Neuro-QoL Satisfaction. For the M2 Imaging + Chart Model, we identified four features from the chart and ten features derived from the imaging analysis. For chart features, diabetes, aphasia, and hemorrhagic stroke were collected and treated as binary variables while age was a continuous variable. These features were selected because they had a good distribution in the available population, were available in the medical chart, and had evidence of a relationship to the selected task (Balk et al., 2026) (Xu et al., 2021) (Lau et al., 2019). For image derived features, we selected total lesion volume in the subcortical region as well as lesion volume in specific brain networks. For the M3 Combined Model, we used all 18 features from both previous models.

2.5.3. Model Tuning and Assessment

The three model types (M1 Clinical Assessment Model; M2 Imaging + Chart Model; M3 Combined Model) were fit to each of the six outcome metrics for a total of 18 models. To improve interpretability, all outcome variables were sign-oriented such that a more positive value indicated worse performance; all candidate predictor features were oriented such that a more positive value corresponded to expected worse outcome. When fitting the model, we required all coefficients to be positive, ensuring that all predictor-outcome relationships were biologically justifiable, e.g., greater lesion volume leading to more deficit. Prior to fitting each model, we zscore normalized the continuous candidate predictors in the feature matrix.

Model performance was leave-one-subject-out (LOSO) cross-validated and assessed with the coefficient of determination (R² ). Within each cross-validation fold, hyperparameters were tuned using LOSO cross-validation to select the best λ and α values. We restricted α values to favor Lasso regression (0.5 to 0.9 with step size 0.1) in order to encourage feature sparsity. The set of parameters (λ, α) with the lowest cross-validation error were selected as the best performing model within each fold. Each regression model was then bootstrapped (n = 10,000) to generate the 95% confidence interval around the observed R² . Finally, permutation testing (n = 10,000) was performed on each model to test the likelihood that a given crossvalidated R² could be achieved at random with a null model. Significance p-values from permutation tests were adjusted for multiple comparisons across models using the Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) procedure; all reported p-values are FDR-adjusted. All analyses were carried out using Python scikit-learn library.

4. Discussion

In this paper, we used medical chart information, lesion metrics, and motor and cognitive assessment metrics to predict kinematic metrics from a motor tracking task. While some studies have assessed the predictive value of lesion metrics on behavioral outcomes, these studies primarily predict a dichotomized outcome (good vs. severe) or overall disability status. To our knowledge, this study is the first study to evaluate a biomarker model, clinical model, and combined model as predictors of continuous kinematic markers from a robotic assessment.

4.1. Clinical Assessments Outperform Neural Lesion Features

Research-standard motor and cognitive assessments outperformed the imaging model and performed similarly to the combined model. Specifically, the clinical assessment model had significant results in predicting Kv, Delay, ND, and RMSE, whereas the combined model only had significant results in Kv, Delay, and RMSE. These results contrast with prior reports that combined biomarker model outperforms individual models; however, prior studies rely heavily on overall measurements of disability (similarly to assessment performed in treatment settings), classification models, and binary outcomes (Brzus et al., 2025; Stinear et al., 2017). Furthermore, the similar performance of the combined and clinical model suggests that the clinical features may capture most of the variance relevant to the robotic task and are the gold standard for understanding motor and cognitive behavior post-stroke.

Unlike the clinical assessment model, the chart model, which included neuroimaging features and co-morbidities, did not significantly predict any outcome variable. One explanation for the poor performance in the chart/imaging model is the small sample size. Stroke-related lesions are highly heterogeneous. In the small, diverse cohort presented here, we noted that many lesion networks were unevenly distributed (Figure C.2) leading to network feature representation in only a subset of the cohort. Given the small sample size, we limited the lesion mapping to the seven main brain networks. Imaging features at this level of granularity, however, represent general function and may may not directly measure the specific processes involved in the robotic task. For example, the trajectory tracking task required visual processing, sustained attention, motor planning, error correction, and motor control. Although certain regions within the networks deal with these functions, the entire network encompasses task irrelevant regions. In contrast, CT1 and BBT directly assess relevant processes like sustained attention and motor speed. Finally, structural lesion mapping does not consider neuroplasticity. Non-contrast structural imaging cannot capture plastic changes in the brain, as neuroplasticity usually involves synaptic reorganization (Patel et al., 2025). Overtime, the relationship between lesion location and behavior may become less direct as patients recover and plasticity occurs. Our cohort was well into the chronic phase of stroke (7 years ± 8 years post stroke) and therefore had already experienced plasticity. In the acute and sub-acute phases, however, it may be possible to more directly predict performance.

Overall, these findings suggest that targeted clinical assessment metrics were sufficient to predict some kinematic outcomes on a preview tracking task and that medical chart data did not add predictive value. It is possible that neuroimaging information would be useful in poststroke prediction for larger sample sizes and with more granular features, but we note that the structural imaging approach described here will not account for neuroplasticity. Nevertheless, when combined with up-to-date clinical assessments, structural lesion mapping could help to better understand chronic post-stroke impairment.

4.2. Cognition, Particularly Attention, is Predictive of Preview Tracking Performance

Motor performance is task relevant, as demonstrated through the consistent selection of BBT across significant models. The consistent selection of CT1 across significant models (Kv, RMSE, ND, and Delay), however, validates that the robotic task is not purely motor, but rather, depends on sustained visual attention and processing speed. These results support previous work from our lab that showed that MoCA-EF was related to RMSE and ND in the trajectory tracking task, while CT1 was related to ND (Bui et al., 2022 (Bui et al., , 2021)). MoCA is highly variable to aphasia and MoCA-EF has low granularity being scored out of five points, which may explain why it was not significant in this study (Benaim et al., 2015; Chiti and Pantoni, 2014). We also note that the task explored here is almost twice as fast as the task presented in our previous work. The faster speed may explain why attention rather than executive emerged as a significant predictor.

Interestingly, the RMSE models showed similar sized coefficients for CT1 and BBT while ND models showed larger coefficients for BBT relative to CT1. The Delay and Kv models, however, showed greater coefficients for CT1 relative to BBT. Furthermore, the Kv model showed Neuro-QoL Satisfaction as a small but stable predictor. Together these results suggest that the model-derived parameters may be more sensitive to cognitive deficits than traditional kinematic metrics. While RMSE and ND represent overall task performance, past research has shown that these metrics do not distinguish chronic stroke participants with moderate motor impairment and low cognitive impairment from those with low motor impairment and moderate cognitive impairment (Bui et al., 2022). In other words, cognitive impairment can mask motor ability when assessed with standard kinematic metrics. The model parameters, Delay and Kv, however, help define motor control and serve as proxies for specific elements of tracking behavior, i.e. timing of response and response to error. These metrics, therefore, may help to better characterize tracking performance, reporting the source of error, e.g. delay or insufficient response.

Although the chart model was unable to predict robotic outcomes independently, stable selected features support the importance of attention in the selected task. Specifically, right DAN emerged as a stable feature in the significant combined models, with a selection frequency of 94% for RMSE and 100% for Delay. Outcomes such as Delay and RMSE reflect movement accuracy and timing, and literature supports the right DAN's role in relevant domains like spatial attention and visuospatial processing (Menon and Uddin, 2010; Shulman et al., 2010). These findings support that a combined model may allow for emergence of neuroimaging features, although these features alone may not be enough to produce a significant prediction model at this sample size and feature dimensionality.

4.3. Clinical Implications

In this study, we demonstrated that research-standard assessments outperform neuroimaging and medical chart data in predicting kinematic outcomes in a chronic stroke population. However, chronic assessments of behavior are not acquired frequently as many stroke patients are discharged at that point (Shahid et al., 2023). Even in the acute phase, we found that many patients in our study did not have motor or cognitive assessments: two patients had a MoCA, seven had an NIHSS (National Institute of Health Stroke Severity Scale), and none had a Barthel Index (measurement of functional independence). Thus, building a predictive model of chronic behavior using research-standard assessments may not be realistic.

CT imaging is routinely collected post-stroke, even in low-resource environments.

Information from these scans may be used to understand recovery and design better acute and chronic therapeutic interventions. Specifically, motor and cognitive interventions early on may lead to better rehabilitation outcomes . Despite neuroplastic reorganization, lesion location can limit recovery. For example, brainstem strokes, although small, are the most devastating type of stroke and hardest to recover . Thus, knowing lesion location early on may be valuable to understanding recovery potential.

Traditionally, robot-assisted therapies and conventional therapies have targeted motor impairment, but cognitive impairment is closely linked to motor performance post-stroke (Bui et al., 2022; Lin et al., 2021). This complex interaction between motor and cognitive impairments necessitates comprehensive motor and cognitive assessment to identify optimal candidate and treatment strategies. Quantitative robotic assessments can improve post-stroke assessment by providing combined motor and cognitive assessments, automatic data collection, and quick, objective functional measures.

5. Conclusion

This preliminary pilot study highlights the value of motor and cognitive assessments combined with neuroimaging. We demonstrate that cognitive function, particularly attention, along with motor activity are important predictors of post-stroke tracking performance. With a larger dataset, it may be possible to include more features, such as more task-specific networks or smaller, targeted regions of interest. The findings in this paper, although conservative, provide a promising foundation for using objective, affordable robotic technology to better characterize post-stroke impairment. robot systems and enAblegames™ for implementing the robotic assessments and software architecture.
