Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

This module retrieves information from repositories.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Introduction

Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus (T2DM) is a major global public health problem affecting over 500 million people worldwide (Duncan et al., 2021). Beyond its well-recognized microvascular and macrovascular complications, increasing evidence suggests that T2DM is associated with accelerated cognitive decline, mild cognitive impairment, and dementia (Bao et al., 2025).

Individuals with T2DM have an estimated 1.5-2 times greater risk of developing cognitive impairment compared to non-diabetic populations (Biessels et al., 2006).

Several mechanisms have been proposed to explain diabetes-associated cognitive dysfunction, including chronic hyperglycemia, insulin resistance, neuroinflammation, oxidative stress, endothelial dysfunction, cerebral microvascular disease, mitochondrial dysfunction, and neurodegenerative processes such as amyloid-β accumulation and tau hyperphosphorylation (Bao et al., 2025; Biessels et al., 2006). Consequently, numerous pharmacological and nonpharmacological interventions have been investigated to improve cognitive outcomes among individuals with T2DM (Allen et al., 2013; An et al., 2025; Gupta et al., 2011) These interventions include antidiabetic medications (e.g., metformin, GLP-1 receptor agonists, SGLT2 inhibitors) (Khan et al., 2026; Osman et al., 2025; Youssef et al., 2023) nutritional supplements (e.g., zinc, omega-3 fatty acids, vitamins) (Ghaedi et al., 2024; Jafarnejad et al., 2019)physical exercise, dietary modification, cognitive training, and multidomain lifestyle interventions (Margara-Escudero et al., 2025; Marlon E Cerf, 2021; Omolade Komolafe & Anthonia Okafor, 2024). While multiple systematic reviews and meta-analyses have synthesized evidence from randomized controlled trials (RCTs), findings remain fragmented and vary in methodological quality.

An umbrella review is therefore needed to comprehensively evaluate and compare evidence from existing systematic reviews and meta-analyses. It is also important in examining the biological mechanisms through which interventions may improve cognitive outcomes in adults with T2DM.

Rationale

Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus (T2DM)-induced cognitive impairment is a recognized complication among T2DM patients. Cognitive impairment adversely affects diabetic self-care, treatment adherence, quality of life, and the risk of dementia. Although numerous randomized controlled trials have evaluated pharmacological and non-pharmacological interventions to improve cognitive function, the evidence has been synthesized in multiple systematic reviews with varying scope, quality, and conclusions. This fragmentation makes it difficult to identify the most effective interventions and their overall strength of evidence.

Most existing systematic reviews focus on individual interventions, such as antidiabetic drugs, nutritional supplements, exercise, or lifestyle modification, rather than comparing evidence across intervention types. In addition, relatively few reviews comprehensively examine the biological mechanisms through which these interventions influence cognition, including improvements in glycemic control, insulin sensitivity, oxidative stress, neuroinflammation, and vascular function. A broader synthesis is therefore needed to integrate both clinical effectiveness and mechanistic evidence.

An umbrella review offers the highest level of evidence synthesis by integrating findings from systematic reviews and meta-analyses of randomized controlled trials. It enables comparison of intervention effectiveness, assessment of the quality and consistency of evidence, and identification of research gaps. The findings will inform clinical practice, guide future research, and support the development of evidence-based strategies to prevent or reduce cognitive impairment among adults with T2DM.

Study Design

Umbrella review of systematic reviews and meta-analyses of randomized controlled trials.

The review was conducted according to the recommendations of the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) Manual for Evidence Synthesis and reported following the Preferred Reporting Items for Overviews of Reviews (PRIOR) statement (Pollock et al., 2019).

Eligibility Criteria

This umbrella review reviewed systematic reviews and meta-analyses (including network metaanalyses) that contain randomized controlled trials. The review focused on adults (≥18 years) diagnosed with Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus with any form of cognitive decline, including mild cognitive impairment, subjective cognitive decline, diabetes-associated cognitive dysfunction, or early dementia.

Selection Process

All records were imported into Rayyan and duplicates removed. Two independent reviewers screened the titles, abstracts and full texts. The disagreements were resolved by through discussion and consultation with a third reviewer. The study selection process is presented using PRISMA flow diagram (figure 1).

Data Collection

The data extraction process was guided by a standardized form developed specifically for this purpose. Two (NIH, GA) independent reviewers extracted all relevant information from each included systematic review. The extracted included bibliographic information (author, year, and country), the type of review, the number of randomized controlled trials (RCTs) included, and the total sample size. Furthermore, comprehensive descriptive data on population characteristics, the nature of the intervention and its comparator, as well as the specific cognitive outcomes measured were extracted. To facilitate a robust synthesis, we also captured quantitative data such as effect sizes, alongside qualitative insights like mechanistic findings, the conclusions drawn by the review authors, and the results of their methodological quality assessment.

Data items

The primary outcome for which data were sought was cognitive performance assessed using validated instruments such as MMSE, MoCA, and ADAS-Cog. Secondary outcomes for which data were sought included mechanisms (glycemic control, insulin sensitivity, inflammatory biomarkers, oxidative stress biomarkers, neurotrophic factors, neuroimaging outcomes, and cerebral blood flow). All results compatible with each outcome domain in each study were sought, including all measures and time points reported.

Other variable included: bibliographic information (author, year, country), type of review, number of RCTs included, total sample size, population characteristics, comparator, cognitive outcomes measured, effect size, mechanistic findings, conclusions, and methodological quality assessment results. Where information was missing or unclear, this was noted and assumptions were documented.

Study Risk of Bias Assessment

Methodological quality of the included reviews was appraised using the AMSTAR 2 (A Measurement Tool to Assess Systematic Reviews 2) to evaluate the rigor by two (NIH, GA) reviewers. Discrepancies were resolved by consensus with a third reviewer. This was used to categorize each review into one of four confidence ratings: high, moderate, low, or critically low.

At the same time, the certainty of the evidence for the key findings was assessed. Where the included reviews had already reported risk of bias assessments (Cochrane RoB, RoB 2, NOS, PEDrp, JBI) these were extracted directly. Where such assessments were not reported, the certainty of the evidence was independently evaluated by the review team using established GRADE principles.

Effect measures

For each outcome, the effect measures used in the synthesis or presentation of results were as follows: dementia incidence and cognitive decline were expressed as risk ratios (RR) or odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals; cognitive performance (MMSE, MoCA, ADAS-Cog, and domain-specific tests) was expressed as standardized mean differences (SMD) or mean differences (MD) with 95% confidence intervals; and mechanistic outcomes (HbA1c, fasting glucose, inflammatory biomarkers, etc.) were expressed as MD or SMD as reported in the included reviews.

Synthesis methods

This umbrella review employed a narrative synthesis approach as the primary method of evidence synthesis, given the anticipated heterogeneity in interventions, outcomes, and populations. Where included reviews reported comparable outcomes and comparators, we additionally performed exploratory second-order meta-analysis (meta-meta-analysis) to generate pooled effect estimates. This approach was exploratory and intended to summarize the range of effect estimates rather than to provide definitive pooled estimates. When multiple reviews reported the same the same comparison, we selected the review with the highest AMSTAR 2 rating or, where equal, the most comprehensive review (largest number of primary studies).

Overlap among primary studies included in the 11 reviews was assessed using the Corrected Covered Area (CCA) method. The CCA was calculated as follows: CCA = (N -r) / (r × c -r), where N is the total number of primary study occurrences across all reviews, r is the number of primary studies, and c is the number of reviews. The calculated CCA was 2.11%, indicating slight overlap. For the exploratory meta-meta-analysis, we used a hierarchical random-effects model to account for within-review and between-review variance. When multiple reviews reported the same comparison, we selected the review with the highest AMSTAR 2 rating or, where equal, the most comprehensive review (largest number of primary studies).

Results of individual studies and syntheses were tabulated in tables 1-8 and displayed using forest plots and PRISMA flow diagram (Figure 2). A graphical abstract (Figure 1) and mechanistic pathway diagram (Figure 3) were also produced.

For the exploratory meta-meta-analysis, random-effects models were fitted using the meta for package (version 4.0-1) in R (version 4.3.2). Restricted maximum likelihood (REML) was used to estimate between-study variance (τ²). Heterogeneity was quantified using the I² statistic and Cochran's Q test. Subgroup analyses were conducted using mixed-effects models. SUCRA rankings were derived from network meta-analytic estimates reported in the original reviews; where SUCRA values were not reported, we did not generate them de novo.

Subgroup analyses were conducted using mixed-effect models. They were performed for intervention category, cognitive severity, age, diabetes duration, sex, and follow-up duration.

Sensitivity analyses were conducted as reported in the reviews to assess the robustness of the synthesized results. Where sensitivity analyses were reported by the original authors, these were extracted and summarized.

Risk of bias due to missing results in the synthesis (arising from reporting biases) was assessed.

Where included reviews reported publication bias assessments (funnel plots, Egger's test, Beggs test), these were extracted directly. The presence of publication bias was noted for each intervention category where reported.

The certainty of the evidence for the key findings was assessed. Where the included reviews had already reported GRADE assessments, these were extracted directly. Where such assessments were not reported, the certainty of evidence was independently evaluated by the review team using established GRADE principles. Two reviewers (HN and GA) independently applied GRADE principles; discrepancies were resolved by consensus with a third reviewer. Ratings ranged from very low certainty to high certainty across outcomes and reviews. Certainty was downgraded for risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and publication bias.

3.0 Results

The PRISMA flow chart (fig 2) illustrates the systematic literature search and screening process for this umbrella review. A total of 334 records were initially identified through database searching, consisting 78 records from Medline, 78 from Scopus, and 178 from Science Direct.

After the removal of duplicate records (n = 158), 256 unique records were screened based on their titles and abstracts. In the initial screening phase, 218 records were excluded as they did not meet the predefined eligibility criteria, leaving 38 articles for full-text screening. Of these, 27 articles were excluded for the following reasons: studies did not specifically evaluate cognitive outcomes in T2DM populations (n = 9), intervention categories did not match the predefined framework (n = 7), insufficient data for extraction (n = 6), or duplication of data from included systematic reviews (n = 5). Finally, 11 systematic reviews met all inclusion criteria and were included in the data extraction and final qualitative synthesis of this umbrella review. This high attrition rate during full text screening reflects the application of rigorous methodological criteria to ensure that only high-quality, relevant evidence was synthesized across the five intervention categories examined in this umbrella review.

Lifestyle interventions, particularly exercise, work through synergistic mechanisms that together enhance brain health and cognitive function. Multi-component exercise combines effects from both aerobic and resistance training. Aerobic exercise improves mitochondrial function, enhances GLUT-4 translocation through AMPK activation, and upregulates BDNF expression through β-hydroxybutyrate-mediated neurotrophic factor transcription. On the other hand, resistance training improves muscle mass, glycogen storage, and activates PI3K/Akt pathways while elevating IGF-1 levels (Sun et al., 2025; Gao et al., 2026). This blend produces synergistic effects that optimize glucose metabolism, reduce insulin resistance, promote hippocampal neuroplasticity, and improve cerebral perfusion while reducing neuroinflammation (Li et al., 2025a). Mind-body exercises such as Tai Chi and yoga enhance cognitive function by enhancing autonomic nervous system function, improving intrinsic hippocampal activity, increasing hippocampal volume, and normalizing aberrant hyperactivity within the hippocampus (Gao et al., 2026).

Intranasal insulin directly bypasses the blood-brain barrier to deliver insulin to the brain via olfactory and trigeminal pathways. This produces region-specific changes in cerebral blood flow that reflect neuronal activity through neurovascular coupling. Intranasal insulin also modulates neurotransmitter release, ion channel trafficking, and synaptic plasticity to improve memory and executive function (Nijssen et al., 2023). Chinese herbal medicine exerts neuroprotection through multiple interconnected pathways, including reduction of oxidative stress via enhanced antioxidant enzyme activity (SOD, GSH-Px), inhibition of PI3K/Akt-mediated inflammation, and promotion of vascular renewal to regulate cerebral microcirculation. Additionally, these herbal formulations regulate Wnt/β-catenin signaling to improve hippocampal neuronal structure, upregulate BDNF expression, and increase anti-apoptotic proteins such as Bcl-2/Bcl-xl, collectively protecting against neuronal apoptosis and cognitive decline in patients with T2DM and mild cognitive impairment (Liu & Guo, 2022).

These varied mechanisms, while distinct in their primary targets, have shared pathways involving inflammation reduction, neurotrophic support, metabolic improvement, and cerebrovascular protection.

In the included reviews, Vitamin D was the only nutritional supplement with proposed neuroprotective mechanisms in T2DM, though evidence for clinical benefit remains inconclusive. Vitamin D crosses the blood-brain barrier and can be synthesized in situ by microglia during inflammation. Vitamin D exerts neurotrophic effects through stimulation of nerve growth factors and neurotrophin synthesis. It also modulates neurotransmitter production including acetylcholine, dopamine, serotonin, and GABA (da Silva et al., 2024).

The active metabolite 1,25(OH)₂D₃, confers its neuroprotection against excitotoxic insults through maintenance of intraneuronal calcium homeostasis, inhibiting inducible nitric oxide synthase to prevent apoptosis, and decreases L-type voltage-gated calcium channel activity (da Silva et al., 2024). Furthermore, vitamin D induces local immunosuppression by reducing microglia activation. It also controls Nrf2 and Klotho expression thus increasing antioxidant enzymes (catalase, GPx, SOD), and upregulating vitamin D receptor expression (da Silva et al., 2024) . Despite these reasonable mechanistic pathways, the clinical evidence for vitamin D supplementation in T2DM patients remains critically low quality, with high-dose (50,000 IU/week) conferring no additional cognitive benefit over low-dose (5,000 IU/week) in the only identified RCT (da Silva et al., 2024). The limited evidence, and a high risk of bias in observational studies with inadequate control for critical confounders like physical activity, smoking status, diabetes duration, and sun exposure limits precludes definitive conclusions regarding the cognitive benefits of vitamin D supplementation in patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus. Consequently, well-designed RCTs with rigorous methodology are urgently needed to determine whether the mechanistic pathways identified in preclinical and observational research translate into clinically meaningful cognitive protection in this population (da Silva et al., 2024).

Overall, the mechanistic evidence supports a use of several approaches to cognitive therapy in T2DM, with antidiabetic medications and multi-component exercise demonstrating the most robust mechanistic rationale and clinical evidence, while the role of nutritional supplements remains uncertain. 469 470

We examined evidence from twelve systematic reviews with over 213,604 participants. Our results demonstrate that both pharmacological and non-pharmacological interventions can effectively improve cognitive outcomes among T2DM patients. However, the quality of evidence varies considerably across intervention categories, and our synthesis reveals critical distinctions regarding effect size, patient selection, and implementation strategies that have direct implications for clinical practice.

Pharmacological Interventions: Comparative Effectiveness and Clinical Context

The strongest current evidence supports the use of specific antidiabetic drugs for cognitive improvement. SGLT2 inhibitors showed the strongest effect (OR = 0.44, SUCRA = 94.5%), followed closely by GLP-1 receptor agonists (RR/OR = 0.60, SUCRA = 92.1%). These findings are similar to the previous mechanistic studies demonstrating that these agents cross the bloodbrain barrier and possess ant inflammatory effects (Tseng et al., 2025; Wu et al., 2025). GLP-1 receptor agonists also inhibit amyloid-β aggregation and activate BDNF/TrkB signaling pathways (Wan et al., 2026). Also, Thiazolidinediones demonstrated consistent protective effects with no heterogeneity (RR = 0.78, I² = 0%), supporting their role through PPAR-γ activation in the hippocampus (E. K. Hui et al., 2025).

From a clinical point of view, the effect sizes observed for SGLT2 inhibitors and GLP-1 receptor agonists are not only statistically significant but clinically meaningful. Based on the pooled OR of 0.44 for SGLT2 inhibitors and assuming a baseline risk of 15% for clinically significant cognitive decline over 2-3 years (derived from the control group event rates in the included observational studies), the estimated number needed to treat (NNT) is approximately 12 (95% CI: 9-18). This calculation should be interpreted with caution given the observational nature of most included data and the absence of standardized definitions for clinically significant cognitive decline. The NNT is provided for illustrative purposes only and requires validation in prospective trials with pre-specified cognitive endpoints. This therefore compares well with established treatments for other diabetes complications. In subgroup analyses, adjustment for HbA1c reduction did not substantially reduce the cognitive effects of SGLT2 inhibitors and GLP-1 receptor agonists. However, formal mediation analysis was not performed in any included review. Therefore, while these findings are suggestive of direct neuroprotective actions beyond glycaemic control, they should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating rather than conclusive.

Future studies with formal intervention analysis are needed to separate the relative contributions of glycaemic and non-glycaemic mechanisms. This has important implications for clinical decision-making, as it implies that patients with well-controlled glycemia may still derive cognitive benefit from these agents. However, prescribing decisions must balance cognitive benefits against safety profiles. SGLT2 inhibitors carry risks of genital mycotic infections, volume depletion, and rare cases of euglycemic diabetic ketoacidosis, while GLP-1 receptor agonists are associated with gastrointestinal side effects that may limit tolerability in up to 15-20% of patients (Filippatos et al., 2014; Pelletier et al., 2021; Wikarek et al., 2024; Zhou et al., 2022) . The cognitive benefits of these agents should therefore be considered alongside their cardiovascular, renal, and metabolic profiles, with shared decision-making that incorporates patient preferences and comorbidity burden.

In contrast, metformin, sulfonylureas, insulin, and DPP-4 inhibitors showed varying or nonsignificant effects on cognitive outcomes. The heterogeneity observed for these agents (I² = 49.8% to 98.4%) suggests that their cognitive benefits may depend on patient-specific factors or study design characteristics (Z. Li, Lu, et al., 2024) . The lack of significant findings for insulin is particularly notable given its theoretical benefits. This may reflect the challenges of peripheral insulin administration in achieving adequate central nervous system penetration (E. K. Hui et al., 2025). Intranasal insulin, which bypasses the blood-brain barrier, has shown more promising results in smaller trials (Nijssen et al., 2023), suggesting that delivery route is a critical determinant of efficacy. For metformin, the heterogeneity may reflect its opposing actions improving systemic insulin sensitivity while potentially increasing amyloid-β production through AMPK-dependent pathways in certain contexts. These findings emphasize that clinicians should not assume all glucose-lowering agents confer cognitive protection and should prioritize agents with demonstrated cognitive benefits when this outcome is a clinical priority.

Lifestyle Interventions: Dose-Response, Adherence, and Synergistic Combinations

Among lifestyle interventions, exercise showed significant cognitive benefits across multiple modalities. Multi-component exercise being the most effective (SMD = 1.04, SUCRA = 78.1%), followed by aerobic exercise (SMD = 1.30) and mind-body exercise (SMD = 0.93). Resistance exercise alone showed no significant effect (SMD = 0.46), suggesting that combining different exercise types produces synergistic benefits (Gao et al., 2026; Y. Sun et al., 2025). These benefits were achieved through improved insulin sensitivity, enhanced cerebral perfusion, and increased neurotrophic factor expression (S.-J. Li et al., 2025). The optimal schedule appears to be at least three exercise sessions per week for six months or longer.

The dose-response relationship for exercise deserves particular attention. The synthesis of included reviews shows a threshold effect, with benefits emerging at approximately 150 minutes

Subgroup Analyses: Toward Personalized Intervention Selection

Subgroup analyses showed significant patient characteristics that impact treatment response.

Patients with lower baseline cognitive function (MMSE < 23) derived greater benefit from GLP-1 receptor agonists (Cohen's d = -3.00, p < 0.001). concerning age and gender, older adults (≥60 years) and females showed greater responses to these agents (Wan et al., 2026). Longer intervention duration (≥24 weeks for medications, ≥6 months for exercise) was consistently associated with greater cognitive improvements. These findings underscore the need for individualized intervention selection based on individual patient characteristics.

The effect modification by baseline cognitive function has substantial clinical relevance. T2DM patients with established mild cognitive impairment (MCI) may gain disproportionately greater benefits from GLP-1 receptor agonists than those with normal cognition, suggesting a possible "ceiling effect" or greater biological reserve in healthier brains. This finding is in line with recent trials of GLP-1 receptor agonists in Alzheimer's disease, which showed modest but statistically significant cognitive stabilization in early-stage patients (Jayakrishnan & Arjuman Parveen Shaik, 2025). In clinical practice, this meanns that cognitive screening in T2DM patients aged over 65 years is more than just prognostic tool. It could also help choose the appropriate therapy, with GLP-1 receptor agonists being ideal for those who show cognitive concerns on screening.

Sex differences in sensitivity to GLP-1 receptor agonists are significant, and they may represent differential expression of GLP-1 receptors in the brain, variances in body composition that alter drug distribution, or disparities in inflammatory and hormonal profiles.

Women in the included studies had higher baseline BDNF levels and greater BDNF increases following treatment, which could help explain this conclusion. This highlights the importance of sex-specific analyzes in future trials, as well as individualized therapy recommendations. In exercise programs, baseline fitness level was a significant mediator, with inactive participants exhibiting greater cognitive benefits than those already achieving activity requirements (SMD difference = 0.52).

The biggest cognitive benefits were shown in patients who went from sedentary to active, emphasizing the necessity of focusing exercise therapies on the least active patients. APOE ε4 carrier status did not significantly affect responses to pharmaceutical or lifestyle interventions, indicating that neuroprotective mechanisms engaged by these interventions may function independently of Alzheimer's disease genetic risk.

Mechanistic Convergence and Potential Synergy

The mechanistic evidence shows that various interventions enhance cognition through convergent pathways. The main ways that antidiabetic drugs work are through neurotrophic support, amyloid reduction, and anti-inflammatory mechanisms (Z. Li, Lin, et al., 2024; Tseng et al., 2025) . Exercise interventions augment mitochondrial function, glucose metabolism, and synaptic plasticity (Gao et al., 2026; Sun et al., 2025). Intranasal insulin sshowed region-specific cerebral blood flow changes and improved memory function through direct central nervous system delivery (Nijssen et al., 2023) (Nijssen et al., 2023). Chinese herbal medicine demonstrated potential through antioxidant and anti-inflammatory effects, though evidence quality was critically low (Liu & Guo, 2022).

The mechanistic evidence supports convergent pathways through which antidiabetic medications and exercise improve cognition in T2DM. GLP-1 receptor agonists and SGLT2 inhibitors share anti-inflammatory and neurotrophic mechanisms (Figure 2), which may explain their comparable cognitive benefits despite different primary targets. Exercise interventions enhance mitochondrial function, glucose metabolism, and synaptic plasticity, providing a complementary mechanism that may be additive to pharmacological effects. However, no included review directly compared combination therapy versus monotherapy, representing a critical evidence gap.

Evidence Gaps and Future Research Priorities

A number of significant gaps in the evidence were found. Our inclusion criteria were not met by any systematic reviews of multidomain methods or cognitive training therapies. This is a serious drawback because combination treatments might be more beneficial than individual treatments.

In a similar vein, there was little evidence to support the use of nutritional supplements; despite apparent molecular pathways, vitamin D did not demonstrate any cognitive benefits (da Silva et al., 2024). These gaps emphasize the need for more studies in these fields.

Methodological Limitations and Quality Considerations

The included reviews' methodological quality differed significantly. On AMSTAR 2, three reviews received high confidence ratings, five received moderate confidence, and four had severely low scores. For antidiabetic drugs, the GRADE certainty of evidence was high; for Chinese herbal medicine and vitamin D supplements, it was very low. The strength of our conclusions is limited by high heterogeneity (I² > 75%) across many outcomes and publication bias in some reviews. These methodological drawbacks highlight the necessity of better study designs and standardized outcome measures in subsequent research (Gao et al., 2026; Liu & Guo, 2022).

Several methodological concerns are worth highlighting. First, there is significant variation in cognitive evaluation tools between research, with over 15 different batteries requiring complex comparisons and meta-analyses. The Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) appears to be more sensitive to diabetes-related cognitive impairments than the MMSE, but both remain widely employed.

We recommend that future trials use a core outcome set for cognition in T2DM, which includes at least the MoCA for global screening, as well as domain-specific assessments for executive function (e.g., Trail Making Test B), memory (e.g., Rey Auditory Verbal Learning Test), and processing speed. Second, the majority of the studies examined did not have long-term follow-up beyond two years, raising concerns regarding the long-term viability of cognitive advantages and potential secular changes. Third, the lack of established criteria for clinically relevant cognitive deterioration, such as the minimal clinically important difference (MCID), reduces the interpretability of continuous outcome measurements. Developing MCIDs for cognitive outcomes in T2DM populations should be a focus in the field.

Publication bias was an issue for several intervention categories, with funnel plot asymmetry indicating that smaller trials with poor results may go unpublished. This is especially troublesome in herbal medicine and supplement trials, where industry funding and publication bias may be more prevalent. Pre-registration of trials and publication of negative results should be heavily promoted by journal rules and funding criteria. Furthermore, the majority of research from high-income nations limits their applicability to low-and middle-income settings, where the T2DM burden is quickly increasing and healthcare resources may differ significantly.

Clinical Practice Implications and Future Directions

Despite these limitations, our findings have important implications for clinical practice. These findings suggest that doctors treating T2DM patients, particularly those with cognitive issues or known risk factors, should prioritize SGLT2 inhibitors or GLP-1 receptor agonists when selecting diabetes drugs. The findings imply that cognitive advantage, in addition to cardiovascular and renal protection, may be an important consideration in shared decisionmaking. For non-pharmacological therapy, we advocate structured multi-component exercise regimens, with special emphasis on supervised programs and efforts to increase long-term adherence.

Cognitive screening, particularly in elderly patients or those with diabetes for more than ten years, should become standard practice in diabetes care to allow for early intervention and tailored medication selection. We also emphasize the importance of a well-structured clinical process. We propose that patients with T2DM and cognitive impairment undergo a multifactorial examination, which includes MoCA-based cognitive screening, vascular risk factor assessment, and evaluation of modifiable lifestyle factors.

We recommend a step-by-step approach for individuals with MCI (MoCA < 23): start prescribing exercise and optimizing diabetes treatment to include agents that improve cognitive function; reevaluate cognitive function at 6 months; and if there is no improvement, consider further evaluation for other causes of cognitive impairment and possible referral to cognitive specialists. As additional data becomes available, intranasal insulin and multi-domain therapies may prove to be beneficial choices.

Note that the intensity of exercise appears more important than volume for cognitive outcomes. Specifically, moderate-intensity aerobic exercise (60-75% of maximum heart rate) showing superior effects to low-intensity interventions. For multi-component programs, adding cognitive or coordination elements such as dual-task walking or dance-based exercise had additional benefits beyond standard combined aerobic-resistance programs. This is likely through common neural pathways involved in motor planning and executive function. Although with the convincing efficacy data, implementation of exercise interventions in routine diabetes care faces significant barriers. In the included studies, the mean adherence rates were 68-74% over six months, reducing to 45-55% at one year. This is similar with real-world data showing that fewer than 3 in 10 of adults with T2DM meet recommended physical activity guidelines. Supervised programs had larger effects than unsupervised interventions (SMD difference = 0.41, p = 0.02), underscoring the need for direct support in the initial adoption. Two included reviews conducted cost-effectiveness analyses of exercise programs which utilized structured supervision together with behavioral support. Although these programs were expensive upfront, they proved cost-effective over five-year periods when caregiving burden and cognitive decline costs were factored in These findings suggest that healthcare systems should invest in supervised exercise infrastructure, including diabetes-specific programs with cognitive outcome monitoring, as part of routine T2DM management.

Three gaps were rated as high priority. No systematic reviews examined combination therapy of pharmacological plus lifestyle interventions. No systematic reviews examined cognitive training or multidomain interventions. Head-to-head comparative trials of pharmacological agents are also urgently needed. Current evidence for drug comparisons relies heavily on indirect comparisons. Four gaps were rated as medium or low priority. Nutritional supplements beyond vitamin D need further study. Long-term follow-up beyond two years remains limited in most trials. Low-and middle-income country populations are underrepresented in current evidence. Vitamin D in deficient populations was rated low priority.

Future

studies should focus on rigorous evaluation of combination approaches (pharmacologic plus lifestyle), head-to-head comparative trials of pharmacologic agents with cognitive outcomes as primary endpoints, and studies in a variety of populations, including low-resource settings and underrepresented minorities. Clinical trials should be accompanied with mechanistic research employing sophisticated neuroimaging and biomarkers to clarify mechanisms of action and find response predictors. To capture real-world impact, cognitive performance measures should be combined with patient-centered outcomes such as caregiver burden, functional independence, and quality of life. SGLT2 inhibitors and GLP-1 receptor agonists showed the strongest cognitive benefits. Multi-component exercise was the most effective lifestyle intervention. However, heterogeneity exceeded 75% for many outcomes, limiting precision of pooled estimates. Most included RCTs had short follow-up durations, limiting long-term conclusions. Predominance of Asian studies may limit generalizability to African and Latin American populations. Publication bias was present in several reviews, particularly herbal medicine and supplement reviews. Residual overlap in the exploratory meta-meta-analysis may bias pooled estimates. This synthesis lacked individual participant data, precluding patient-level subgroup analyses. The absence of reviews on cognitive training and multidomain interventions represents an evidence gap. Moving forward, a tailored strategy based on patient characteristics is needed. Methodically addressing identified research priorities may advance cognitive protection in T2DM. This includes combination therapy trials, head-to-head drug comparisons, and long-term follow-up studies. Greater representation from low-and middle-income countries is essential. Such efforts may move us closer to effective, evidence-based cognitive protection for the growing T2DM population.
