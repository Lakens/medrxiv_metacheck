Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Metacheck does not yet analyze automatically whether sentences which include non-significant p-values are correct, but we recommend manually checking the sentences below for possible misinterpreted non-significant p values.

Text Sentence
p = 0.76 The paired difference was +0.020 (95% bootstrap CI, -0.103 to +0.144, p = 0.76), with no statistically detectable difference in macro-F1 between the models.

For metascientific articles demonstrating the rate of misinterpretations of non-significant results is high, see:

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. Abstract

Deep learning models can predict melanoma immunotherapy response but rarely explain predictions biologically. Concept bottleneck models offer interpretable predictions, but have not been applied to multiplex immunofluorescence (mIF), and whether their learned concepts reproduce tissue measurements remains unclear. We developed CB-COMIL, which routes mIF predictions through four supervised concepts (immune:tumour ratio, CD8/SOX10 balance, CD68 relative signal and PD-L1 evasion index), decomposing each prediction exactly into concept contributions. Developed on 211 slides and evaluated on 42 slides from an independent, differently acquired test cohort, CB-COMIL achieved macro-F1 0.69 versus 0.67 for an opaque comparator (difference 0.020, 95% CI -0.103 to 0.144). In 41 slides with complete measurements, learned concepts correlated with measured counterparts (Spearman ρ 0.61-0.76), yet substituting measured values reduced macro-F1 from 0.68 to 0.48. CB-COMIL makes predictions interpretable without detectable performance loss, and our evaluation shows that concepts can faithfully determine predictions without reproducing the corresponding biological measurements.

Introduction

Melanoma is among the most aggressive skin cancers, with an estimated 331,722 new cases and 58,667 deaths worldwide in 2022, and its incidence continues to rise [1]. Immune checkpoint blockade (ICB), a form of cancer immunotherapy, has transformed melanoma treatment by enabling the immune system to recognise and eliminate cancer cells [2]. ICB is administered either as monotherapy, typically targeting programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1) with agents such as nivolumab, or as combination therapy targeting multiple immune checkpoints, such as nivolumab with the anti-CTLA-4 antibody ipilimumab. Yet roughly 60% of patients do not respond to monotherapy, and even in combination around 50% fail to benefit, while still enduring debilitating toxicity and substantial personal and healthcare costs [3]. Identifying likely non-responders before treatment is therefore critical, both to spare patients futile toxicity and to redirect them towards alternative therapies. This need has motivated the development of computational models, among which image-based models that capture complex patterns within the tumour microenvironment (TME) are emerging as particularly promising.

Among tissue imaging modalities, multiplex immunofluorescence (mIF) provides a basis for characterising the tumour microenvironment by visualising multiple immune and tumour markers within the same tissue section. By simultaneously profiling multiple biomarkers, mIF enables quantification of marker co-expression and spatial cellular relationships associated with immunotherapy response, providing information beyond that available from single-marker tissue imaging [4]. Several tumour and immune features associated with checkpoint blockade response can be characterised using mIF, including immune and tumour composition, CD8 T cell infiltration, PD-L1 expression, and macrophage composition [5, 6, 7, 8]. The relationship between macrophage composition and response depends on macrophage phenotype and treatment context [8].

A model that expressed its predictions in terms of such biologically grounded features would provide a more clinically interpretable account of its decision than one expressed only through latent image features. To analyse whole-slide mIF images and capture their high-dimensional, non-linear relationships, deep learning-based computational frameworks have recently emerged [9, 10, 11]. However, despite their predictive performance, these models typically do not expose the biological factors underlying individual predictions, limiting clinical scrutiny of their decisions. This opacity matters most where the stakes are highest. For a decision as consequential as recommending or withholding immunotherapy, predictive accuracy alone is insufficient: the prediction should be interpretable by the pathologist, and its underlying reasoning should reflect the factors that informed the model's decision [12]. The central question therefore shifts from whether a model performs well to whether it can be trusted to support clinical decision-making. This requires evaluating AI models not only by their predictive performance, but also by whether their reasoning is transparent and biologically meaningful.

The conventional approach to interpretability relies on post-hoc explanations, whereby a black-box model is first trained and subsequently interpreted using a separate method, such as gradient-or perturbation-based attribution (e.g., Layer-wise Relevance Propagation (LRP) [13], Class Activation Mapping (CAM) [14], Local Interpretable Model-agnostic Explanations (LIME) [15], or SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) [16]). These explanations are produced after training and are not constrained by the training objective, so their faithfulness, the degree to which they reflect the model's actual computation rather than merely appearing plausible, varies and is rarely verified. For example, some saliency based attribution methods produce visually similar maps even after the model's learned parameters are randomised [17]. Attention weights are also widely used to explain 2 predictions, but their faithfulness varies across architectures and tasks. In a comparative evaluation, layerwise relevance propagation, integrated gradients and perturbation methods performed better than attention weights on tests of faithfulness [18].

Faithfulness alone, however, does not provide biological interpretability. Even when an attribution map faithfully identifies regions that influence a prediction, it expresses relevance primarily at the level of image regions or features rather than through explicit, biologically defined concepts. Consequently, such maps can reveal where the model relies on the image, but provide limited insight into which biological characteristics of those regions drive the decision. These concerns have prompted growing interest, particularly in high stakes clinical applications, in models that are interpretable by construction, where the intermediate representation driving the prediction can be examined and directly intervened upon before the final decision is formed [19]. In medical imaging, such self-explainable models include prototype-based and concept-based approaches [20]. Prototype-based methods explain predictions by comparing regions of a new tissue image with representative patterns learned from previously observed cases, allowing users to inspect the visual similarities underlying a decision. Both approaches offer useful insight into model behaviour, though they address different questions: prototype similarity shows which learned representative patterns a region of the image resembles, whereas a concept-based account quantifies how much each predefined biological property contributed. We therefore adopt a concept-based approach, which expresses predictions through biologically defined concepts whose individual contributions can be examined and directly intervened upon.

Concept bottleneck models (CBMs) are a representative approach of this paradigm [21]. Rather than mapping an input directly to an outcome, CBMs first predict a small set of human-specified concepts and then base the final prediction on those concepts. Consequently, the concepts are not post-hoc approximations of the model's reasoning but constitute the decision-making process itself, providing explanations that are inherently faithful to the underlying prediction.

In computational pathology, self-interpretable multi-instance learning (SI-MIL) introduces interpretable structure into a deep model by pairing a MIL branch with an interpretable branch that predicts linearly from hand-crafted morphometric and spatial features, with the deep branch guiding which patches the interpretable branch sees [22]. To our knowledge, however, concept bottleneck learning has not yet been applied to predicting immunotherapy response from mIF images, where routing the prediction through biologically meaningful concepts could make the decision interpretable in immunological terms. More fundamentally, it remains unclear whether biologically defined concepts that faithfully govern a model's prediction also faithfully represent the biological quantities they are intended to encode. This distinction between decision faithfulness and biological fidelity is particularly important in biomedical applications, where an interpretable representation may otherwise be mistaken for a biological measurement. It also remains unknown whether imposing such a biologically grounded concept bottleneck can preserve the predictive performance and generalisation of a strong black box model.

In this work, we propose CB-COMIL (Fig. 1), a concept bottleneck extension of our previously developed framework, Channel Optimisation with Multi-Instance Learning (COMIL) [9], to predict immunotherapy response in melanoma patients from whole-slide mIF images. The model is trained on 211 whole-slide mIF images from the Melanoma Institute Australia (MIA), each comprising seven channels: five protein biomarker channels (CD8, CD16, CD68, PD-L1 and SOX10), together with a nuclear stain (DAPI) and an autofluorescence (AF) channel. We define four concepts representing different aspects of the tumour immune microenvironment: immune signal relative to tumour signal (immune:tumour ratio), cytotoxic T-cell signal relative to melanoma-associated signal (CD8/SOX10 3 balance), macrophage signal relative to the CD8 and CD68 compartment (CD68 relative signal), and PD-L1 signal relative to CD8 signal (PD-L1 evasion index). These concepts were specified a priori for their biological relevance to checkpoint blockade, their measurable counterparts in the mIF panel, and their ability to provide a compact representation of the tumour immune microenvironment. CB-COMIL achieves predictive performance comparable to an opaque model while making each prediction attributable to four concepts supervised by measurable tissue quantities, and we evaluate both how much each concept contributes and how closely it corresponds to its measured counterpart. Evaluation is performed on an independent test cohort of 42 slides acquired with a different staining protocol and scanner, so the model is assessed under a measurable acquisition shift. This cohort offers a valuable opportunity for independent evaluation, particularly given the limited availability of mIF datasets with treatment response annotations and the high cost of generating such datasets. Specifically, our contributions are threefold: (1) we develop an interpretable-byconstruction extension of COMIL whose macro-F1 did not differ detectably from that of the opaque model (0.69 versus 0.67; 95% CI for the difference -0.103 to +0.144); (2) we show that the model design allows each prediction logit to be decomposed exactly into concept contributions and a bias, and we use this decomposition to attribute individual predictions and quantify each concept's influence through directional interventions; and (3) we provide empirical evidence that decision faithfulness does not necessarily imply biological fidelity: learned concept activations can govern model predictions while remaining imperfect proxies for their corresponding biological measurements, as shown by reduced predictive performance when these activations are replaced with measured values.

Results

We evaluate CB-COMIL through four linked questions. First, does attention identify the image regions the model relies on, and can those regions alone preserve predictive performance? We test this with paired deletion and insertion analyses [23], using Integrated Gradients (IG) [24] and random rankings as benchmarks. Second, how strongly does each concept influence the prediction? We examine directional interventions, patient-level prediction decompositions and joint concept decision spaces to assess how the four learned concepts influence model decisions. Third, how closely do these concepts reflect their intended biology? We compare them with independent measurements and use corrective interventions to test whether measured values can replace learned activations. Finally, we assess whether imposing this interpretable structure preserves predictive performance on the independent test cohort, comparing CB-COMIL with the opaque backbone and feature-engineered interpretable baselines, and ablating individual components of the framework. Together, these analyses test whether interpretability by construction can be achieved without a detectable loss in performance, and what the resulting concepts do and do not represent.

Attention identifies influential patches but does not establish sufficiency

We evaluated whether patches highlighted by CB-COMIL's attention contribute to its predictions using deletion and insertion experiments [23] on the independent test cohort, comparing attention rankings with IG and a random-ranking control (Fig. 2; Supplementary Table S1).

Deleting patches in descending order of attention reduced macro-F1 faster than random deletion (area under the deletion curve 0.56 versus 0.68) and changed 25.9% of predictions, compared with 0.5% for random ranking, with the same pattern for specificity, non-responder F1 and AUPRC. The largest separation was in specificity (0.51 versus 0.89), indicating that non-responder predictions were most sensitive to removal of the highest-ranked patches. IG reduced macro-F1 even faster (0.45) and changed 52.9% of predictions, with most of the decline occurring within the first 10% of patches deleted, whereas attention declined more gradually (Fig. 2a). Attention therefore identified patches that influence the prediction, but IG ranked them more effectively on most metrics.

Insertion showed no comparable advantage. Measured biological concepts do not reproduce the predictive utility of learned concepts

Having characterised the concepts' contributions to prediction, we next examined their correspondence with the intended biological quantities. We therefore assessed biological fidelity through two complementary analyses: comparing learned concept activations with independently measured mIF quantities and performing corrective interventions to determine whether measured values could replace learned activations in the prediction head.

Across the 41 patients in the independent test cohort with complete mIF data, all four learned concepts showed measurable correspondence with their independently quantified biological counterparts (Figure 5a-d). Spearman correlations were ρ = 0.61 for immune-tumour ratio, 0.66 for CD8/SOX10 balance, 0.76 for CD68 relative signal, and 0.72 for PD-L1 evasion. These positive correlations indicate that patients with higher measured values generally had higher learned activations, but do not establish numerical agreement, as Spearman correlation is unchanged by a constant shift or rescaling of the learned values. The deviations from the identity lines show that the learned activations do not reproduce the measured quantities exactly, so the concepts retain measurable biological alignment without functioning as direct measurements of their corresponding biomarkers.

We tested whether the measured biological quantities could substitute for the learned concepts in predicting treatment response. Following the concept intervention framework of Koh et al. [21], we replaced all four learned activations with their independently measured mIF counterparts while keeping the trained prediction head unchanged. This substitution reduced macro-F1 from 0.68 to 0.48 among the 41 patients in the independent test cohort with complete mIF measurements (Figure 5e). Because the head was trained on learned activations, we also evaluated an oracle condition in which logistic regression was refitted using the measured concepts. Refitting yielded a macro-F1 of 0.54, partially recovering performance but remaining numerically below the learned-concept model.

To examine whether restricting the measured inputs to four summaries limited performance, we additionally evaluated an L 1 -regularised logistic regression using the full 21-feature handcrafted mIF panel (Table 2). This model achieved a macro-F1 of 0.59 on the independent test cohort, compared with 0.54 for the model using the four measured concepts and 0.68 for CB-COMIL (Supplementary Table S4). Expanding the measured feature set therefore yielded higher performance in this comparison, but did not match the learned-concept model. Both measured-feature models also performed worse on the test cohort than in cross-validation, whereas CB-COMIL did not (Supplementary Table S4), suggesting that staining-related shift in measured intensities may contribute to this gap.

These findings support the interpretation of CB-COMIL's learned concepts as biologically aligned representations that explicitly contribute to its predictions. Their moderate to strong positive correlations with the corresponding measured biological targets indicate that higher measured values generally correspond to higher learned activations, but do not establish numerical equivalence. The reduction in predictive performance following concept substitution further indicates that the learned activations are not directly interchangeable with measured quantities and should not be interpreted as calibrated biomarker measurements.

Discussion

The value of AI predictions in cancer care extends beyond accuracy to understanding why a model favours treatment response or non-response for an individual patient. CB-COMIL addresses this need by expressing melanoma immunotherapy response predictions from mIF images through four learned representations of the tumour immune microenvironment: immune:tumour ratio, CD8/SOX10 balance, CD68 relative signal, and PD-L1 evasion. These representations, termed concepts, are trained to align with corresponding mIF-derived biological measurements. On the independent test cohort, CB-COMIL achieved comparable observed predictive performance to opaque COMIL across four random initialisations, while making individual predictions interpretable through the contributions of learned concepts. All four learned concepts showed moderate to strong positive correlations with their corresponding mIF-derived measurements in the independent test cohort (Figure 5), supporting their use as biologically aligned representations for examining the model's predictions.

While the learned concepts provide a biologically informed basis for examining predictions, attentionbased explanations offer a different perspective by identifying influential image regions. Removing the patches that attention ranked highest lowered macro-F1 more quickly than removing random patches (Fig. 2a), indicating that these patches influence the prediction. Retaining only the top-ranked patches, however, recovered performance no faster than retaining a random subset (Fig. 2b): even a small random fraction of the slide restored near-full performance, suggesting that multiple subsets of tissue can contain enough information to recover performance at the cohort level. Attention maps can therefore guide inspection of tissue regions that may inform the prediction, but do not establish which biological characteristics determine the prediction.

In contrast, CB-COMIL makes the contribution of each learned concept to the final prediction explicit. By constraining predictions to four learned concepts and a linear decision head, each response logit can be decomposed exactly into four signed concept contributions and a constant bias, without an alternative prediction pathway. Because the decision pathway is restricted to these concepts, the explanations reflect the model's actual decision mechanism rather than a retrospective approximation. Concept interventions quantified the relative influence of each concept, with immune:tumour ratio and CD8/SOX10 balance exerting the greatest influence over the evaluated range (Table 1).

Patient-level decompositions could support clinical interpretation by revealing the biologically aligned learned representations underlying individual predictions, rather than providing a probability alone (Figure 3). In T19, a responder incorrectly predicted as a non-responder, low learned immune:tumour and CD8/SOX10 activations drove the prediction towards non-response, with no substantial opposing contribution from the other concepts. The same pattern was observed among the eleven missed responders, who had lower learned immune:tumour activations than correctly classified responders (median -0.94 versus +0.30), contributing to their misclassification as non-responders (Supplementary Table S2). Conversely, the model incorrectly classified T29, a 13 clinical non-responder, as a responder. T29's learned scores for the immune to tumour ratio and CD8/SOX10 balance were as high as those of correctly classified responders, helping to explain the model's prediction. Understanding why T29 did not respond to treatment would require separate biological evidence. The decomposition illustrates the transparency of the decision process and the distinction between explaining a model's prediction and explaining an individual patient's treatment outcome. Further investigation could examine factors beyond the four concepts, including immune cell organisation and functional state. Decompositions of this kind could allow clinicians to see which tissue features drove a prediction and to judge when it warrants caution.

The usefulness of these explanations also depends on the choice of concepts. Leave-one-concept-out ablations showed that removing any single concept produced only modest changes in predictive performance, suggesting that the model does not rely critically on any one concept (Supplementary Table S6). Notably, removing CD68 relative signal produced the largest observed decrease in performance, despite its low weight in the prediction head (Table 1). This suggests that concept supervision may influence the shared representation beyond a concept's direct contribution to the prediction. We nevertheless retained all four, because they were selected a priori for their biological relevance to checkpoint blockade, their measurable counterparts in the mIF panel, and their ability to represent immune-tumour relationships compactly. Together, they provide a compact, biologically motivated basis for interpreting model predictions, although their individual contributions may overlap.

Importantly, biological correspondence did not necessarily translate into predictive importance. CD68 relative signal had the highest observed correlation with its measured counterpart (ρ = 0.76) (Figure 5), yet showed the smallest intervention effect (Table 1) and generally the smallest contributions to individual predictions (Fig. 3). Its limited influence could reflect information shared with other concepts or limitations of the selected summary, rather than a lack of biological relevance. Likewise, the positive prediction weight for learned PD-L1 evasion describes its contribution when the other concepts are held fixed (Table 1); it does not establish that immune evasion promotes treatment response. These contrasts show that a concept's contribution to the model's prediction does not directly establish its biological role in treatment response.

Correlation with a measured quantity does not make a learned concept a substitute for it. Replacing the learned activations with their measured counterparts in the trained prediction head reduced macro-F1 from 0.68 to 0.48, while refitting the prediction head on measured concepts achieved 0.54 (Figure 5e). A model trained on the full panel of 21 measured mIF features achieved a macro-F1 of 0.59 on the independent test cohort. Neither intermediate nor late fusion of measured features with image embeddings exceeded the macro-F1 of opaque COMIL (Supplementary Table S4). These findings suggest that learned concepts may retain predictive information not captured by the selected measured summaries, while adding those measurements to image embeddings provided no improvement in the evaluated fusion configurations. Two mechanisms may contribute: concept leakage under joint training, in which the response objective encodes information beyond the measured targets in the learned concepts [25, 26], and staining-related shift in measured intensities between cohorts. Thus, although the learned concepts support interpretation of the model's predictions, their numerical values should not be treated as calibrated biological measurements or substitutes for laboratory assay results.

For precision oncology, identifying patients unlikely to respond to immunotherapy could help prioritise further clinical assessment and consideration of alternative treatment strategies. At the evaluated threshold, CB-COMIL correctly identified more non-responders than opaque COMIL (17/19 versus 13/19), but fewer responders (12/23 versus 15/23). CB-COMIL predicted non-14 response for 28 patients, 11 of whom responded to immunotherapy. The proportion of non-response predictions that were correct was therefore similar for CB-COMIL and opaque COMIL (61% versus 62%, respectively), despite a numerically higher AUPRC of CB-COMIL (0.773 versus 0.702). The ablation results showed a similar distinction between performance across thresholds and performance at a selected threshold, with comparable AUPRC values yielding markedly different recall for responders and non-responders. At the evaluated threshold, the number of responders misclassified as non-responders argues against using the model to withhold immunotherapy. Clinical use would therefore require assessment of calibration and selection of a decision threshold that accounts for the clinical consequences of prediction errors.

The independent test cohort comprised 42 patients, of whom 41 had complete biological measurements. This cohort provides a valuable independent evaluation, as mIF datasets are costly and technically demanding to generate, and comparable public cohorts with treatment response annotations are scarce. Notably, the independent test cohort was acquired using different imaging instrumentation and staining protocols and exhibited statistically significant differences in marker signal distributions relative to the development cohort, including within responder and non-responder groups (two-sided Mann-Whitney U test; Supplementary Fig. S7). These differences provide quantitative evidence of domain shift, potentially reflecting variation in both acquisition conditions and case mix between cohorts, and allow CB-COMIL to be evaluated under conditions different from those used for model development. Patient-level bootstrap confidence intervals and evaluations across four random seeds were used to assess sampling uncertainty and sensitivity to training variability, respectively (Supplementary Table S9). Nevertheless, validation in larger independent cohorts remains necessary to establish broader generalisability.

The concept targets are derived from slide-level mean marker intensities, capturing relative marker signals rather than the spatial organisation of cells within the tumour microenvironment. These intensity-based ratios provide biologically motivated supervision at the same level as the aggregated slide representation used to predict the concepts. Spatial features, including the proximity of CD8 T cells to tumour cells, have also been associated with checkpoint blockade response [6]. Future work could extend the concept set to include cell densities, immune cell phenotypes and spatially defined tumour-immune interactions.

CB-COMIL makes melanoma immunotherapy response predictions from mIF whole-slide images inspectable through four predefined, biologically supervised concepts. On the independent test cohort, it made each concept's contribution explicit while achieving comparable observed macro-F1 to the opaque comparator. The learned concepts correlated with their measured biological targets, but substituting those targets into the trained prediction head reduced performance. Together, these findings show the value of assessing both how each concept contributes to a prediction and how closely its learned values correspond to tissue measurements.

Methods

Dataset

The model is trained using 211 slide-level labelled whole-slide mIF images of melanoma tissue sourced from the MIA. A separate set of 42 images was processed using a different staining sequence and imaging instrument, introducing potential technical distribution shifts in fluorescence signals and providing an opportunity to assess model generalisability across different acquisition conditions.

No patient contributed slides to both the development and test cohorts. Individual images reach 15 up to 24,000 × 24,000 pixels at a resolution of 0.5 µm per pixel. Each image comprises five protein biomarker channels (CD8, CD16, CD68, PD-L1 and SOX10), together with a nuclear stain (DAPI) and an autofluorescence (AF) channel. All patients received anti-PD-1 monotherapy or combined anti-PD-1 and anti-CTLA-4 therapy. Tissue samples were labelled according to 6-month progression-free survival, with non-responsive cases defined as the negative class (91 training and 19 test samples) and responsive cases as the positive class (120 training and 23 test samples). Biopsies were collected mainly within one year before treatment (89%), with a limited number taken more than a year before therapy. Full acquisition details appear in [7, 27].

Participants and specimens

Patients with unresectable stage III or stage IV melanoma who had received or were eligible for anti-PD-1 monotherapy (PD-1) or combined anti-PD-1 and anti-CTLA-4 immunotherapy (IPI+PD-1) were eligible for inclusion. Melanoma subtypes included cutaneous, mucosal, acral, subungual and melanoma of unknown primary. Patients with uveal melanoma, although included in prospective biomarker testing within the broader PIP-PREDICT programme, were excluded from the present analysis because of its distinct biological and clinical characteristics. Prior adjuvant or neoadjuvant systemic therapy was permitted.

Pretreatment formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tumour specimens were obtained from the Melanoma Institute Australia Biobank. Specimens containing fewer than 100 melanoma cells were excluded. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Sydney Local Health District Human Research Ethics Committee (2020/ETH00426).

Response classification

Treatment response was defined using progression-free survival (PFS) measured from immunotherapy initiation. Patients who experienced disease progression within six months of treatment initiation (PFS < 6 months) were classified as non-responders, whereas those with PFS ≥ 6 months were classified as responders.

Concept-target data

The four concept targets used to supervise the bottleneck are not annotations but quantities measured directly from the same mIF panel that produced the images, so each is independently re-measurable in a pathology laboratory. For slides with complete HALO measurements, the seven channels were quantified to slide-level mean intensities in HALO (Indica Labs) imageanalysis software, yielding one mean intensity per channel: the five biomarkers on their respective Opal fluorophores (CD8 Opal570 Intensity, CD68 Opal520 Intensity, CD16 Opal620 Intensity, PDL1 Opal650 Intensity, SOX10 Opal690 Intensity), the nuclear channel (DAPI Intensity), and autofluorescence (Autofluorescence Intensity). From these HALO intensities we first formed per-marker signal-to-noise ratios by normalising each marker to autofluorescence (marker SNR = marker/AF). The four concepts were then computed as composite ratios of these SNRs following their biological definitions (Table 2): the immune-tumour ratio (CD8 + CD16)/SOX10, the CD8/SOX10 balance, CD68 relative signal CD68/(CD8 + CD68), and the PD-L1 evasion index PD-L1/CD8. The slide-level concept values serve as supervision targets during training, rather than as model inputs. CB-COMIL learns to predict these concepts from KRONOS image features, allowing response 16 predictions without requiring measured concept values at inference. Concept targets were available for 181 training and 41 test slides with a complete quantified mIF panel. The remaining 30 training slides lacked concept targets and contributed to the response and instance-level losses, but not to the concept loss. The single test slide with missing intensities was evaluated using image-derived features alone. Because the ratios are right-skewed, each concept was log(1 + x)-stabilised and then z-scored; to prevent leakage, the standardisation statistics were estimated on the complete-mIF training slides of each fold only and applied unchanged to validation and test, with the final retrained model using statistics from all complete training slides.

Concept Bottleneck COMIL (CB-COMIL)

Concept Bottleneck COMIL (CB-COMIL) is built on our previously developed Channel Optimisation with Multi Instance Learning (COMIL) framework for whole-slide multiplex immunofluorescence [9], which aggregates per-marker patch features through a gated channel transform and gated-attention pooling to a slide-level prediction. We extend COMIL by incorporating marker-aware KRONOS features and replacing its conventional classifier with a concept bottleneck that routes predictions through four predefined immune concepts. These modifications retain marker-specific information and make the contribution of each learned concept to the final prediction explicit. The following sections describe the preprocessing, feature extraction, aggregation, concept bottleneck, training objective and reference models.

Preprocessing

Whole-slide seven-channel mIF images were stored as multi-resolution pyramidal TIFFs. Every channel was resolved to its marker by name from per-slide metadata before any pixel was read. A tissue foreground mask was computed from the union of the DAPI, CD8, and PD-L1 channels [9]. Following COMIL, each slide was then partitioned at a resolution of 0.5 µm/px (≈ 20×) into non-overlapping 224 × 224 px patches, corresponding to an approximately 112 µm field of view. A patch was retained if at least 25% of its pixels fell within the tissue mask.

Feature extraction and aggregation

The retained 224-px patches were encoded with the pretrained KRONOS [28] pathology foundation model after a two-stage normalisation: per-slide, per-channel intensity rescaling to [0, 1] using the 0.5th/99.5th percentiles (computed once per slide over a pixel sample), followed by KRONOS's fixed per-marker mean/std standardisation. We adopted a marker-aware configuration, in which each of the six retained channels (DAPI, SOX10, CD8, CD68, CD16, and PD-L1), with autofluorescence (AF) excluded, was encoded independently as a single-channel input, yielding six 384-dimensional per-marker feature representations per patch. KRONOS identifies each input channel by a marker token: the five biomarkers and DAPI were mapped to their native KRONOS tokens, while SOX10 was mapped to the S100 melanocyte-lineage token as the closest available proxy. AF, having no corresponding token and representing background signal rather than a stained marker, was excluded, leaving the six channels above.

Patch-level features were aggregated to a slide-level prediction using COMIL [9]. Each whole-slide mIF image with C channels is represented as a bag of N p patches, where each patch is a C × 384 tensor of per-marker KRONOS features. Following COMIL, aggregation proceeds in three stages: a 17 gated channel transformation (GCT) [29] that recalibrates the marker channels according to their multiplex context, attention-based pooling that weights patches by their relevance to the slide-level label and collapses the bag to a slide embedding, and an instance-level clustering objective that learns class-discriminative patches during training.

Gated channel transformation

The GCT comprises three components: global context embedding, channel normalisation, and gating adaptation. CB-COMIL operates on 384-dimensional (D=384) per-marker KRONOS features. For patch n, let f n,c ∈ R 384 denote the feature vector of channel c ∈ {1, . . . , C}.

Global context embedding. Each channel's global context is embedded as its L 2 feature magnitude under a learnable embedding weight α c , where ϵ is a small constant for numerical stability and α c adjusts the significance of each channel; a low α c removes channel c from the subsequent normalisation, allowing the module to adapt to each marker's characteristics.

Channel normalisation.

The channel embeddings are normalised across the C channels by L 2 normalisation, so that each channel embedding reflects its relative contribution among all the markers.

This lets the model up-or down-weight individual markers according to each patch's overall multiplex context rather than treating channels independently.

Concept bottleneck head

To enable interpretability by construction, the response prediction is routed through a small set of named, biologically meaningful concepts. These were pre-specified from the established immunobiology of immune-checkpoint blockade (ICB) response and from the quantities measurable on the mIF panel, before any association with clinical outcome was examined. This design choice follows the principle that concept bottlenecks are defined by human-specified concepts [21]; selecting concepts based on outcome associations would reintroduce the opacity that the bottleneck is designed to remove and risk overfitting to the development cohort.

The four CB-COMIL concepts were selected according to four criteria. First, each concept is an intensity-based proxy computed from slide-level mean marker intensities and maps to a canonical biological axis of immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) response. The immune-tumour ratio approximates the abundance of immune signal relative to tumour signal, reflecting the degree of immune infiltration. The CD8/SOX10 balance approximates cytotoxic T-cell signal relative to melanoma-lineage signal, indicating whether the effector population that checkpoint blockade reactivates is present in proportion to the tumour. CD68 relative signal approximates the share of macrophage signal within the CD8 and CD68 compartment; macrophages can suppress cytotoxic activity, although their association with response depends on phenotype [8]. The PD-L1 evasion index approximates PD-L1 signal relative to CD8 signal, a proxy for the balance between checkpoint-associated and cytotoxic signals. Second, each concept has a measurable biological counterpart that can be independently quantified from the same mIF panel. This provides an external biological reference for evaluating the learned concept representation. Third, each concept is defined as a composite ratio rather than a single raw marker measurement, enabling representation of interpretable biological relationships rather than isolated marker abundance. Fourth, the concept set is intentionally restricted to four quantities, so that the bottleneck remains a genuine biological abstraction rather than becoming 19 another high-dimensional latent representation. Consequently, every prediction must be expressed through these four predefined biological axes.

These four concepts are realised architecturally as a bottleneck layer. Following feature aggregation, the conventional classifier head is replaced with a two-layer prediction head comprising a concept layer and a linear response layer. The D-dimensional slide embedding S is first projected onto K = 4 interpretable concepts, which are subsequently mapped to the response logit through a linear layer, where W con ∈ R K×D , b con ∈ R K , θ ∈ R K and b ∈ R are learnable parameters.

Because the response layer is linear, the prediction logit decomposes exactly into a baseline bias b and K additive signed contributions, θ k ĉk , each corresponding to a learned concept.

Concept targets. The measured concept values used to supervise ĉ are right-skewed ratios, and were therefore log(1+x)-stabilised and then z-scored so that the four concepts contribute comparably to the concept loss. To prevent information leakage from held-out slides, the standardisation statistics were estimated on the complete-mIF training slides of each fold only and applied unchanged to validation and test; the final retrained model used statistics from all complete training slides.

Training objective

CB-COMIL is trained end-to-end with a three-term loss, where L bal BCE is a class-balanced binary cross-entropy (BCE) on the response, L inst CE is COMIL's instance-clustering loss weighted by a fixed µ, and the third term supervises the bottleneck towards the standardised true concepts c * with weight λ.

This concept term is gated by a completeness indicator ⊮[s complete]: only slides with a complete mIF panel contribute to it, whereas every slide, including the 30 training and 1 test cases without quantified mIF, contributes fully to the response and instance terms. Missing-modality slides therefore still train the backbone and response head but exert no pull on the concept layer, so the bottleneck is never supervised by imputed or fabricated biology. The instance weight µ weights instance-clustering loss, whereas the concept weight λ controls the strength of concept supervision. Both weights were selected by five-fold cross-validation on the development cohort (see Training).

Training. Models were trained per bag (batch size 1) for 50 epochs. For CB-COMIL, the objective combined the balanced BCE with the concept-supervision term λ∥ĉ -c * ∥ 2 2 , applied only to complete-mIF slides, and the instance-clustering loss with B = 8. The instance-loss weight µ was selected over {0, 0.1, 0.2, 0.3, 0.5, 0.7}, yielding µ = 0.3.

The concept-supervision weight λ was evaluated on the development cohort by five-fold crossvalidation over {0, 0.3, 0.5, 0.7, 0.8} (Supplementary Fig. S8). Cross-validation macro-F1 varied little across non-zero values (0.668-0.686), and all values lay within one fold standard deviation of the unsupervised setting (λ = 0: 0.682 ± 0.061). Concept fidelity, measured as the mean per-concept R² between learned and standardised measured concepts on complete-mIF validation slides, increased with λ, from 0.40 ± 0.18 at λ = 0.3 to 0.52 ± 0.07 at λ = 0.8. R² was used here because it penalises scale and offset error, whereas the rank correlation reported for the independent test cohort assesses ordering only. We selected the smallest non-zero weight (λ = 0.3) to introduce explicit concept supervision while limiting its contribution to the training objective. 20 Following COMIL, the embedding weight α was initialised to 1 and the gating weights and biases to 0. Optimisation used Adam (learning rate 10 -3 , weight decay 10 -4 ). Model selection used stratified five-fold cross-validation. The epoch selected within each fold was recorded, and the five selected epochs were averaged and rounded to obtain a single retraining epoch. The final model was then retrained on all 211 development whole-slide mIF images for this number of epochs and evaluated once on the 42-slide independent test cohort. All results reported in the main text use a fixed seed of 42. To assess robustness to initialisation, we repeated training with three further seeds (0, 1, 2) under identical configuration, with no per-seed tuning and the retraining epoch recomputed by the same procedure, and report the resulting macro-F1 and concept fidelity in Supplementary Table S9.

Point estimates on the independent test cohort are reported with 95% percentile bootstrap confidence intervals (B = 10,000, patients resampled with replacement). Pairwise model comparisons use a paired bootstrap on the macro-F1 difference, with significance declared when the 95% confidence interval of the difference excludes zero. The ORACLE (L 2 -regularised logistic model fit on the standardised true concepts) and full-panel (L 1 -regularised logistic model fit on the 21-feature mIF panel) comparisons are restricted to the 41 complete-mIF test patients.

Relationship to the original COMIL.

CB-COMIL preserves COMIL's core design-the global-context channel gate, the attention mechanism, and instance-level clustering-but differs in four respects dictated by the encoder and task. First, the two models construct their per-marker inputs differently. Because ResNet101 requires a three-channel input, COMIL formed a pseudo-RGB image for each biomarker by stacking it with the DAPI and AF channels to supply morphological context; CB-COMIL instead exploits the marker-aware design of KRONOS, which encodes a single-channel input identified by a marker token, so each marker is encoded individually without auxiliary stacking. Second, because KRONOS features are 384-dimensional, we omit COMIL's per-instance projection (2048 → 1024) and perform the gating, pooling, and aggregation in the native 384-dimensional space. Third, CB-COMIL uses a single, class-agnostic attention distribution rather than the per-class attention of the original COMIL. The present model operates on 384-dimensional KRONOS features coupled to a concept bottleneck head, where a single patch-importance map aligns directly with the concept-level interpretation reducing the attention parameter count. Fourth, the slide-level (BCE) and instance-clustering (cross-entropy) losses are combined using a fixed weight of 0.3 for the instance term, rather than the adaptive loss (ADL) used in the original COMIL [9]. This modification is particularly important for CB-COMIL, which introduces an additional concept supervision objective controlled by λ. Fixing the instance loss contribution avoids simultaneous dynamic reweighting of multiple objectives, allowing λ to provide an explicit and interpretable control over the tradeoff between predictive performance and concept fidelity.

Attention attribution and faithfulness evaluation

We evaluated the faithfulness of patch-level attention using complementary deletion and insertion experiments on the independent test cohort (n = 42). Three patch-ranking strategies were compared: the attention weights produced by CB-COMIL, Integrated Gradients (IG), and a random ordering. IG provided an independent gradient-based attribution reference, while random ranking controlled for changes in predictive performance caused by the perturbation procedure itself.

21

Integrated Gradients. Attribution methods based solely on the local gradient can be affected by gradient saturation, whereby the gradient approaches zero even for features that contributed substantially to the model's prediction. IG addresses this limitation by integrating gradients along a straight-line path from a reference input x ′ (the zero vector in our implementation) to the observed input x. For a differentiable model F : X → R, the attribution to feature i is where (x i -x ′ i ) represents the difference between the observed and reference feature values, and the integral captures the model's average sensitivity to that feature along the integration path.

IG satisfies the completeness property, such that the feature attributions collectively account for the change in model output between the reference and observed inputs, up to numerical integration error.

IG was computed with respect to the response logit, using 50 integration steps. Attributions were computed on the patch-level KRONOS features and summed over all feature dimensions and marker channels of each patch to give a single patch score, and patches were ranked by absolute score.

Deletion and insertion experiments.

We assessed two complementary properties of the attribution rankings, necessity and sufficiency, using deletion and insertion metrics [23]. Necessity was evaluated through deletion, in which patches were progressively removed in descending order of their attribution scores. Predictive performance was recalculated after each perturbation. A faster decline in performance indicates greater dependence on the highest-ranked patches.

Sufficiency was evaluated through insertion, in which patches were progressively retained in descending order of attribution, and predictive performance was assessed as additional patches were introduced. Patches were removed from the bag rather than masked in the image, and the attention weights were renormalised over the remaining patches. Rankings were computed once on the full bag and not updated after each step. Fractions from 0 to 0.9 were evaluated in steps of 0.05 for deletion, and from 0 to 1.0 for insertion, and areas under the curves were normalised by the evaluated fraction range. The random control was repeated over 10 random permutations, and its curves and areas are reported as the mean across permutations. Faster recovery of predictive performance provides stronger evidence that the highest-ranked patches retain information required for prediction.

Both experiments were performed using attention, IG, and random patch rankings. The random control underwent the same perturbation procedure as the attribution-based rankings.

Faithfulness metrics. Deletion and insertion curves were summarised using the area under the curve (AUC) for macro-F1, non-responder specificity, non-responder F1, and area under the precision-recall curve (AUPRC). Lower deletion AUC indicates greater sensitivity to the removal of highly ranked patches, whereas higher insertion AUC indicates faster recovery of predictive performance.

We additionally evaluated prediction flip rate to quantify changes in predicted class following patch perturbation. Together, these metrics allowed us to assess whether highly ranked patches were 22 influential in the model's predictions and whether retaining them was sufficient to recover predictive performance.
