What we found

We found 2 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Metacheck does not yet analyze automatically whether sentences which include non-significant p-values are correct, but we recommend manually checking the sentences below for possible misinterpreted non-significant p values.

p>0.05 Viable cells were recovered from all groups, with mean viability of 86% in HOPE 20h, 81% in HOPE 4h, and 77% in SCS 4h, and no statistically significant differences between groups (p>0.05) (Fig 5B-E).

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Background: Hypothermic oxygenated perfusion (HOPE) may extend donor heart preservation beyond conventional storage durations. We evaluated early graft function and myocardial cellular and metabolic integrity following porcine heart transplantation after 20 hours of HOPE.

Methods: Ten donor hearts underwent various preservation conditions: static cold storage (SCS) for 4 hours (n=2), HOPE for 4 hours (n=3), and HOPE for 20 hours (n=5), followed by heart transplantation (HT) in recipient pigs. Graft function was monitored over 3 hours by serial echocardiography, invasive hemodynamics, optical flow analysis, and vasoactive support scoring; myocardial tissue was analyzed by histopathology, flow cytometry, mitochondrial respirometry, transcriptomics, and proteomics.

Results: Ejection fraction, right atrial pressure and systolic blood pressure did not differ between groups, and no graft met criteria for primary graft dysfunction. LV contractility was within normal range with comparable vasoactive support among all groups. Preservation-associated weight gain was greatest after HOPE 20h, and histopathology showed comparable findings with edema present in all cohorts, without fibrosis, thrombosis, or necrosis. Cardiomyocyte viability was preserved across all groups. Maximal oxygen consumption rate was elevated in HOPE 20h versus HOPE 4h and SCS 4h, while basal and non-mitochondrial respiration were similar. Transcriptomics and proteomics revealed limited differences in gene and pathway expression among groups.

Conclusions: HOPE preserves donor heart viability, mitochondrial integrity, and ventricular function after 20 hours of preservation, without evidence of primary graft dysfunction, supporting extended preservation for heart transplantation. Edema, recovery of function, and metabolic responses warrant further investigation with longer-term monitoring.

Introduction

Heart transplantation (HT) provides the greatest clinical improvement and quality-of-life benefit for patients with end-stage heart failure beyond medical therapy and mechanical circulatory support (MCS), yet the supply of suitable donor hearts remains insufficient. [1] [2] [3] [4] Expanded donation criteria have increased the donor pool, but at the cost of more high-risk grafts, a risk that must be weighed against waitlist morbidity and mortality. [5] [6] [7] Geographic reach is also limited by the maximum tolerated ischemic time of roughly 4-6 hours for static cold storage (SCS), the current clinical standard, beyond which early graft dysfunction, rejection, and poor outcomes become more likely. [8] [9] [10] Improved preservation technology could extend this window and enable recovery of high-risk grafts. [11] [12] [13] Hypothermic machine perfusion (HOPE) has emerged as a novel storage strategy that may safely extend the tolerated storage time. 14 HOPE supplies continuous oxygen and nutrient delivery to the heart while maintaining a cold, arrested state using a solution composed of donor blood and cardioplegia perfused through an aortic root cannula. The 'XVIVO Heart Assist Transport' (XVIVO Perfusion, Gothenburg, Sweden) is among the HOPE devices currently undergoing clinical trials. [15] [16] [17] Early trials suggest that HOPE may lower incidence of graft failure through reduced oxidative stress and more robust mitochondrial protection. [18] [19] [20] However, the cellular and molecular changes during and after non-ischemic heart preservation remain incompletely characterized. 12, 19, 20 Our group previously demonstrated recovery of porcine hearts following up to 48 hours of HOPE preservation using an ex-vivo normothermic machine perfusion (NMP) circuit. Hearts achieved reanimation with coordinated cardiac contraction and conservation of cardiomyocyte viability. 19 Complementary transcriptomic and metabolomic analyses supported the maintenance of molecular programs associated with myocardial viability and metabolic stability during extended preservation, including limited activation of inflammatory and injuryrelated pathways. We also independently identified myocardial edema as a consistent feature of HOPE preservation following both brain death (DBD) and circulatory death (DCD) procurement, with more pronounced interstitial edema following prolonged HOPE. 19 Large animal models have demonstrated that the upper limit for HOPE may far exceed the 4-6 hour constraints of SCS while maintaining satisfactory outcomes. Thomassen et al. recently demonstrated successful porcine DBD and DCD heart transplantation following 24 hours HOPE, with preserved biventricular function, adequate cardiac output, and no primary graft dysfunction during a two-hour post-bypass observation period. 21 Building on these findings, we provide the first functional assessment of integrated cellular, molecular, and bioenergetic characterization of heart allografts following extended HOPE, combining assessment of myocardial cell viability, transcriptomic and proteomic responses, and mitochondrial respiratory function with evaluation of early graft performance after 20 hours of preservation.

Materials and Methods

Animal Model and Study Design.

Orthotopic heart transplantation (bicaval technique) was performed in 4month-old female Yorkshire pigs (60-65 kg). Donor hearts were procured by direct procurement, as previously described, and preserved by static cold storage (SCS; HTK solution, 4h, n=2) or hypothermic oxygenated perfusion (HOPE; XVIVO Heart Assist Transport) for 4h (n=3) or 20h (n=5) before implantation. 19, 20 The protocol was approved by the Columbia University Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (AC-AACA3703). Anesthesia, procurement, cardioplegia, and implantation details are in the Supplementary Methods.

Recipient Implant and Postoperative Monitoring.

After native cardiectomy, donor hearts were implanted using bicaval technique. Animals were maintained on full CPB for 1 hour after reperfusion, then weaned over 30 minutes, with CPB discontinued at 1.5 hours. The chest was left open and animals were monitored for 3 hours after separation from CPB, with hemodynamics and vasoactive support recorded every 15 minutes, and arterial blood gases and serum chemistries hourly. Anesthetic, hemodynamic, arrhythmia, and ventilator management are described in the Supplementary Methods.

Clinical Endpoints.

The primary outcome was primary graft dysfunction (PGD), defined as: (1) failure to separate from cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) with adequate hemodynamics after a 1.5-hour rest and weaning protocol; (2) LVEF <40% with persistently insufficient hemodynamics despite high-dose inotropes; or (3) right atrial pressure (RAP) >15 mmHg. Graft function was assessed by echocardiography (donor baseline; 0.5h, 1.5h, and 2.5h off CPB), LV contractility by solid-state pressure catheter and a composite vasoactive-inotropic drip score (1 and 2 h off CPB), and epicardial motion by optical flow analysis of video (baseline; 1.5h and 2.5h off CPB; Supplementary Methods).

Tissue Collection.

At the conclusion of the monitoring period, pigs were exsanguinated and hearts were excised warm without inducing cardiac arrest. Explanted allografts were systematically biopsied from the LV free wall and interventricular septum (IVS) for histology, flow cytometry, mitochondrial respirometry, RNA sequencing, and protein expression analysis.

Histology, Flow Cytometry, and Mitochondrial Respirometry.

LV and IVS tissue were fixed and stained with hematoxylin and eosin, Masson's trichrome, and TUNEL for histological analysis. IVS tissue was enzymatically dissociated for flow cytometric assessment of cardiomyocyte viability (Calcein-AM + /DRAQ5 + ). LV free wall was sectioned into 150 µm precision-cut slices using a vibratome and processed for extracellular flux (Seahorse) mitochondrial respirometry using a modified Mito Stress Test (FCCP and rotenone/antimycin A, without oligomycin). Detailed protocols in the Supplementary Methods.

Bulk RNA-seq Differential Expression and Gene Set Enrichment Analysis.

Bulk RNA sequencing of IVS tissue was performed on HOPE 20h (n=4) and clinical-control (SCS 4h + HOPE 4h; n=4) samples. Differential expression was assessed with PyDESeq2 (Benjamini-Hochberg FDR <0.05, |log₂FC| >1), and genes ranked by Wald statistic were analyzed by GSEA against the MSigDB Hallmark, GO Biological Process, and Reactome collections (positive NES = enrichment in HOPE 20h; FDR <0.05). Full library preparation, sequencing, ortholog mapping, and analysis details are in the Supplementary Methods.

Proteomics. Proteomic profiling of IVS tissue was performed by data-independent acquisition (diaPASEF) LC-MS/MS and DIA-NN. Differentially abundant proteins between HOPE 20h (n=4) and clinical controls (n=4) were identified with DIA-Analyst, and pre-ranked GSEA of the protein-level statistic used the same gene set collections as the RNA analysis (FDR <0.05). Results reflect relative protein abundance and pathway enrichment. Sample preparation, instrument settings, and search parameters are in the Supplementary Methods.

Integrated Transcriptomics and Proteomics.

RNA and protein results were matched by gene and pathway identifier. Directional concordance used a descriptive threshold of |log₂FC| >0.25 in both layers (Fig 8A), and the 50 genes with the lowest minimum FDR (<0.05 in at least one layer) were shown as a paired RNA-protein heatmap (Fig 8B, Supp. Table 3). Matched pathways were classified by whether FDR <0.05 was reached in the RNA data, the protein data, or both (Fig 8C). Selection and ranking details are in the Supplementary Methods.

Statistical Analysis

Analyses were performed in GraphPad Prism. Continuous variables were compared across the three groups by one-or two-way ANOVA with Tukey's post hoc test, or by Kruskal-Wallis test. Repeated blood gas measurements were analyzed with a mixed-effects model and Tukey's test. Cardiomyocyte viability used Welch's t-tests for pairwise comparisons and Brown-Forsythe/Welch ANOVA with Dunnett's test for the three-group comparison. Data are mean ± SEM unless stated otherwise; p<0.05 was significant. Omics statistics are described above and in the Supplementary Methods.

Results

Early Graft Function is Retained after 20-hour HOPE.

Ten porcine donor hearts were procured and preserved by SCS for 4 h (n=2), HOPE for 4 h (n=3), or HOPE for 20 h (n=5), then transplanted into recipient pigs. Graft function was assessed during a 3-hour observation period after separation from CPB (Supp. Video 1). No graft met the criteria for primary graft dysfunction with elevated right atrial pressure (RAP) in the HOPE 20h group above other groups, though not statistically significant (Fig 1A). Systolic blood pressure, arterial lactate, pH, creatinine, and hematocrit did not differ significantly between SCS 4h, HOPE 4h, and HOPE 20h hearts during the monitoring period with vasoactive-inotropic support (combined drip score) only differing between SCS 4h and HOPE 4h groups (p<0.05) (Fig 1B-G). Recipient aortic cross clamp duration and CPB times did not differ between groups (Fig. 1H-J). Echocardiographic ejection fraction, fractional area change, and LVEDD did not differ significantly between groups at any timepoint, including compared to donor baseline function prior to heart procurement (Fig. 2A-D and Supp. Video 2). Following reperfusion, myocardial edema was observed in all groups with significant increase in interventricular wall diameter observed in the HOPE 20h group from 1 to 2 hours (Fig. 2D-E).

Left Ventricular Contractility and Epicardial Motion Remain Within Normal Range After 20-hour HOPE

Contractile function was assessed by LV pressure-derived contractility at 1 and 2 hours off CPB, and by optical flow analysis of epicardial motion at baseline and at 1.5 and 2.5 hours off CPB (Fig 3 and Supp. Video 3). LV contractility did not differ between groups at 1 hour off CPB. At 2 hours, contractility was significantly lower in HOPE 20h than in HOPE 4h (p<0.05), with no significant difference between SCS 4h and either HOPE group. All groups showed an increase in contractility from 1 to 2 hours off CPB, and all values remained within the normal range for juvenile Yorkshire pig (Fig. 3A-B). 22 Peak epicardial contraction velocity did not differ significantly between groups or timepoints, including baseline (48.2 ± 17.0 mm/s), with all groups remaining comparable to baseline through 2.5 hours off CPB (Fig. 3C-D). Similarly, there was no significant difference in peak contraction acceleration between groups, with baseline acceleration 396.8 ± 153.7 mm/s 2 . All groups exhibited a mild, but not statistically significant, increase in peak contraction acceleration from 1.5h to 2.5h, with the HOPE 20h group showing the least increase (1.5h, 505.7 ± 281 mm/s 2 ; 2.5h, 522.5 ± 239.2 mm/s 2 ) (Fig. 3E).

Extended HOPE is Accompanied by Greater Weight Gain and Edema.

To characterize edema and structural injury after extended HOPE, we assessed gross tissue morphology, histopathology, pathways related to vascular permeability, and heart weight gain (Fig 4). All hearts had preserved gross morphology without visible necrosis (Fig 4A). GSEA of vascular permeability and edema pathways showed significant enrichment of gap junction trafficking and endothelial cell migration at the RNA level and of cell-cell junction organization at the protein level, whereas endothelial barrier, aquaporin-mediated transport, and VEGFR2mediated permeability pathways were not significant (Fig 4B). Blinded histopathology showed interstitial edema in all samples, most pronounced in HOPE 20h. Coagulative myocyte necrosis was focal; acute inflammation and hemorrhage were confined to one HOPE 20h animal, and no fibrosis or thrombosis was observed. TUNEL staining showed rare apoptotic nuclei without differences between groups (Fig 4C, Supplemental Fig 1). HOPE coronary flow (mL/min) tracing over time during individual preservations with mean and +/-1 SD demonstrates steady flow decline across all HOPE groups (Fig 4D). Percent heart weight gain following preservation, measured before implantation, was minimal in SCS 4h, intermediate in HOPE 4h, and most pronounced in HOPE 20h, which differed significantly from SCS 4h (p<0.05) but not from HOPE 4h (Fig 4E). Because coronary flow declined progressively during HOPE preservation, we examined the left anterior descending (LAD) coronary artery for vessel wall layer swelling that may contribute to coronary resistance. Alcian blue-stained sections showed a non-significant trend toward greater tunica intima thickness relative to tunica media after extended HOPE (Fig 4F-G).

Myocardial Cell Viability Is Retained After Extended HOPE.

To assess whether extended HOPE preserved cellular viability, interventricular septal biopsies collected at the end of the post-implantation observation period were dissociated and analyzed by flow cytometry for viable (Calcein-AM⁺/DRAQ5⁺) cells (Fig 5A). Viable cells were recovered from all groups, with mean viability of 86% in HOPE 20h, 81% in HOPE 4h, and 77% in SCS 4h, and no statistically significant differences between groups (p>0.05) (Fig 5B-E). Thus, neither 4-hour nor 20-hour HOPE reduced cardiomyocyte viability relative to the clinical-standard SCS 4h group, despite the longer preservation time and different preservation conditions.

Extended HOPE is Associated with Preserved Bioenergetics with Elevated Maximal Oxygen Consumption Rate, Spare Aerobic Respiratory Capacity and Substrate Level Phosphorylation.

LV free wall tissue sections underwent extracellular flux Mito Stress Test to quantify OCR and ECAR (Fig 6A, 6E). Because of the small group sizes, SCS 4h and HOPE 4h hearts were pooled as a clinical-standard comparator (n=4) and compared with HOPE 20h (n=4) (Fig 6B, 6F). Basal oxygen consumption rate (OCR) did not differ between the pooled clinical group and HOPE 20h (Fig. 6C). Maximal OCR was significantly higher in HOPE 20h (p<0.01), while non-mitochondrial OCR remained low and did not differ significantly between groups. Spare respiratory capacity was also significantly higher in HOPE 20h (p<0.01) (Fig. 6D). Extracellular acidification rate (ECAR) provides a measurement to track substrate-level phosphorylation indirectly by measuring proton pumping. ECAR tracing followed a similar pattern: basal ECAR did not differ between groups, compensatory ECAR was significantly higher in HOPE 20h than the pooled clinical group (p<0.05), and non-mitochondrial ECAR did not differ significantly between groups (Fig. 6G).

Transcriptomic and Proteomic Profiling Identifies Distinct Molecular Responses to Extended Hypothermic Oxygenated Heart Preservation.

To characterize molecular responses to extended preservation, bulk RNA sequencing and proteomics were performed on interventricular septal biopsies collected at the end of the observation period, comparing HOPE 20h with pooled clinical controls (SCS 4h and HOPE 4h). RNA sequencing identified 68 differentially expressed genes (DEGs; FDR <0.05, |log₂FC| >1; Supplemental Table 1) reflecting inflammation, cellular stress response, and metabolism (Fig 7A-B). Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) showed positive enrichment of cellular stress response, RNA processing, and extracellular matrix remodeling and negative enrichment of fatty acid metabolism (Fig 7C; Supp. Figs 3 4 5 6). In order to understand whether specific genes drove GSEA results, leading edge analysis was performed and when controlling for pathway redundancy, leading edge genes were consistently present in functionally distinct pathway clusters; informing potential central drivers of cellular response to extended preservation, these genes may highlight future directions (Supp. Fig 7). Proteomics identified 39 differentially abundant proteins (29 higher and 10 lower in HOPE 20h; Fig 7D-E, Supp. Table 2), with less concordance to the transcriptomic findings, possibly reflecting a lag between transcript and protein changes within the short observation period. Proteomic GSEA showed positive enrichment of neutrophil migration and mitochondrial translation and negative enrichment of coagulation, complement cascade, and platelet degranulation (Fig 7F; Supp. Fig 8).

Integrated Multi-Omics Analysis Describes Heterogeneous RNA-Protein Concordance and Predominant Transcriptomic Pathway Enrichment.

To relate transcriptional changes to protein abundance, we integrated the bulk RNA-seq and DIA-MS datasets by matching genes and pathways. Of 656 matched RNA-protein pairs exceeding a descriptive threshold of |log₂FC| >0.25 in both layers, 419 (64%) were directionally concordant and 237 (36%) discordant; 4,966 pairs did not meet the threshold (Fig 8A). At the gene level, only one concordant pair, LIPE decreased in HOPE 20h, reached FDR <0.05 in both layers (Fig 8B, Supp. Table 3), indicating limited RNA-protein coordination. At the pathway level (Fig 8C), the pathways meeting FDR <0.05 in at least one layer were those identified by transcriptomics, with strong positive enrichment of RNA processing and negative enrichment of fatty acid β-oxidation. These pathways did not reach FDR <0.05 in the proteomic data, but most showed the same direction of change. We also scored curated cardiac cell-type marker panels in the bulk RNA-seq data (Supp. Fig 9A). SCS 4h and HOPE 4h samples did not separate by cell-type signature (Supp. Fig 9B). Relative to pooled controls, HOPE 20h showed higher signature scores for B cells, T cells, macrophages, fibroblasts, and vascular smooth muscle cells and lower scores for endothelial cells and pericytes (Supp. Fig 9C). These shifts are directionally consistent with the inflammatory and vascular-permeability signals described above (Fig 4B).

Discussion

In this study, 20-hour hypothermic oxygenated perfusion supported successful porcine heart transplantation: all hearts were weaned from cardiopulmonary bypass, and none met study-defined criteria for primary graft dysfunction during the three-hour observation period. Ejection fraction and systemic hemodynamics did not differ between groups, and LV contractility, although lower after HOPE 20h than HOPE 4h at two hours, remained within the normal range. Extended HOPE showed cell viability comparable to clinical controls and preserved mitochondrial respiratory capacity, with distinct transcriptomic and proteomic changes in inflammatory signaling, RNA processing, and energy metabolism and limited RNA-protein concordance. It was also accompanied by heart weight gain and interstitial edema. Edema remains one of the main barriers to clinical translation and a priority for mitigation and longer postoperative monitoring studies.

Retained myocardial cell viability after 20h HOPE complements the observed early graft recovery and extends our previous benchtop findings into in vivo heart transplant model. The elevated maximal oxygen consumption rate (OCR) observed in the HOPE 20h group likely reflects a compensatory mitochondrial response to the cumulative stress of prolonged ischemia and reperfusion, rather than a marker of more robust tissue health. 23, 24 In separate work applying this same Seahorse respirometry assay to grossly necrotic, non-viable myocardium, we did not observe this compensatory elevation in OCR or ECAR; rather, energy production in severely injured tissue was uniformly and substantially depressed, with no comparable rise in any parameter (Supplemental Fig. 2). This suggests that the elevated maximal OCR seen in HOPE 20h hearts represent still-viable myocardium actively performing oxidative metabolism to compensate for and repair ischemicreperfusion injury, rather than a failing or irreversibly injured tissue bed.

Our data, and others, suggest that hearts preserved with HOPE for up to 20 hours retain sufficient function to undergo transplantation. 20 However, the intermediate and long-term consequences of the observed diastolic dysfunction and impaired lusitropy in the setting of pronounced myocardial edema are unknown. Importantly, edema and diastolic dysfunction were also observed in our SCS cohort and are frequently observed in current practice. Though some amount of edema and diastolic dysfunction are inherent to both heart transplantation, and native to a porcine model, it is highly likely HOPE is a separate contributing factor to interstitial edema. 24, 25 The continuous perfusion of the graft with preservation solution may contribute to the edema and weight gain observed after HOPE preservations. Additionally, we observed a reduction in perfusion rate as preservation time elapsed. Therefore, we hypothesize there is a component of coronary vasoconstriction and/or myocardial stiffness that contributes to increased vascular resistance, resulting in reduced flow. Mitigating this edemaassociated diastolic impairment by targeting the underlying mechanisms represents an important and clinically actionable next step for future studies aimed at optimizing and extending the HOPE preservation platform.

Several limitations of this study warrant discussion. The small, unequal cohorts, particularly the SCS 4h group (n=2), compared with HOPE 4h (n=3) and HOPE 20h (n=5), limit statistical power, and nonsignificant differences should not be interpreted as equivalence. Larger experimental cohorts are needed to strengthen these comparisons. Additionally, this study did not include long-term survival data; animals were not recovered to an intensive care setting, and the longer-term functional trajectory and recovery potential of hearts preserved for 20 hours with HOPE remains presently unknown. Supplemental Materials and Methods:

Our in vivo large animal model of HT was carried out using female 4 month-old Yorkshire pigs weighing approximately 60-65kg. All hearts were harvested using a standard, clinically accepted procurement technique with bicaval implantation following donation after brain death (DBD). Hearts were stored in SCS for 4 hours or HOPE for 4 or 20 hours before implant. Due to institutional policy, donor pigs were not subject to any procedure to inflict brain death. The protocol was approved by Columbia's Institute for Comparative Medicine (ICM) under protocol number AC-AACA3703 and all procedures were performed at Columbia University Medical Center.

Preoperative Donor and Recipient Preparation.

Prior to transplant procedure, three Yorkshire pigs 60-70kg were sedated and 5mL of blood is withdrawn for blood typing. Eldon Blood Typing Kit (Eldon Biologicals Home Kit 2511) was used to determine blood type for donor-recipient matching. All animals were made NPO at least 8 hours prior to surgery. Both the donor and recipient pig were sedated using 4.5 mg/kg of Telazol and 1.0 mg/kg of Propofol with maintenance by administering 1%-5% inhaled isoflurane. Pigs were orally intubated and mechanically ventilated with a tidal volume of 10 mL/kg/min, a rate of 10-15 breaths per minute was maintained for the remainder of the procedure. A water-based veterinary ointment was applied directly to the pig's corneas for lubrication. IV catheters were placed within the bilateral auricular veins. Muscle paralysis of the donor was achieved using 0.2 mg/kg of vecuronium. Fentanyl infusion at 25-100 μg/h given for analgesia. Monitoring equipment for electrocardiogram monitoring, non-invasive blood pressure, and pulse oximetry were placed. Mechanical ventilation with a tidal volume of 10 mL/kg/min, a rate of 10-15 breaths per minute was maintained for the remainder of the procedure. Heart rate (60-100 bpm) and systolic blood pressure (90-130 mmHg) were maintained. Pigs were positioned supine, scrubbed with a Providine-iodine 8.3% with isopropyl alcohol 72.5% antiseptic solution, and draped for median sternotomy in standard fashion.

Donor Cardiectomy.

Once an appropriate plane of anesthesia was reached, a median sternotomy was performed. A sternal retractor was placed, the thymus was excised, and a pericardial well was created with traction stitches. A baseline functional assessment of the donor heart was performed. Echocardiography, using long and short axis views from the epicardial surface, was used to assess baseline function. Once preliminary assessment was obtained, the heart was prepared for procurement. Heparin was administered (350 units/kg). The right atrium is cannulated with a dual-stage 30-36Fr venous cannula, clamped, and connected to a cell saver. The proximal ascending aorta is cannulated with a 9 Fr antegrade cardioplegia cannula. After sufficient cardiac and great vessel dissection, the right atrial cannula was opened. The left atrial appendage was also opened widely; the ascending aorta was cross-clamped, and cold cardioplegia was delivered. Cold saline slush was used topically to cool the heart once an arrest was confirmed. After monitoring for LV distention and adequate root pressure throughout the period of cardioplegia delivery, the heart was excised in standard fashion. Care was taken to harvest sufficient aortic length to facilitate loading into the XVIVO Heart Assist Transport. Prior to initiating HOPE, the cardioplegia cannulation site was closed and reinforced, the left atrial appendage was closed with running suture, a 5F sheath was secured within the appendage suture line for placement of the solid-state catheter, and a silastic shunt is placed across the mitral valve and sutured in place to ensure adequate LV venting.

Cardioplegic Conditions.

2 liters of histidine-tryptophan-ketoglutarate (HTK) was used for cardioplegic arrest for hearts allocated to the SCS group. SCS hearts were submerged in HTK solution throughout preservation and stored at 4-6 ˚C. The hearts allocated to the HOPE group were arrested using XVIVO Heart Solution. XVIVO Heart Solution was also used to prime the perfusion box with 350cc of packed RBCs. One of the 1000cc priming bags of XVIVO Heart Solution was supplemented with 3ml of XVIVO Heart Solution Supplement, 20 IU Insulin, 50mg imipenem, 5000 IU unfractionated heparin and 10mmol of potassium. 100mEq of sodium bicarbonate is added to preservation box. HOPE hearts stored for 4 hours were arrested with 1L XHS. HOPE hearts stored for 20 hours were arrested with 2L XHS. Preservations were set at standard XVIVO recommendations: aortic root pressure of 20 mmHg and temperature of 8 ˚C.

Recipient Implant.

Recipient pigs were anesthetized and prepped in similar fashion to the donor as described above. Femoral vein central line insertion and arterial catheterization was performed using ultrasound guidance and Seldinger's technique. All inotropes and vasopressors were administered centrally. Urethral catheterization in female swine is challenging due to anatomical conformation. Suprapubic access provides a reliable and simple alternative for direct bladder cannulation and Foley placement prior to recipient operation 30 . A 5cm inferior laparotomy was made, and the bladder was eviscerated. A catheter is inserted directly to the inferior, dependent portion of the bladder. The skin was closed and the catheter was secured to the skin. Median sternotomy was performed in standard fashion, the thymus was removed, and a pericardial well is created. Heparin was given with confirmation of ACT > 350. Aortic and bicaval cannulas for CPB were inserted, and CPB was initiated. The distal ascending aorta was cross clamped, and recipient cardiectomy was performed in standard fashion. The left hemi-azygous vein was ligated. The donor heart was removed from SCS or HOPE preservation and brought onto the field. Standard bicaval implantation was performed. Prior to completion of the left atrial anastomosis, a solid-state pressure catheter was inserted through the previously placed sheath in the left atrial appendage into the left atrium and advanced beyond the mitral valve orifice into the left ventricle. Following completion of the final anastomosis and removal of the aortic cross-clamp, the heart was reperfused while pigs remained on cardiopulmonary bypass. Defibrillation was required to restore sinus rhythm in most cases, typically necessitating 15-30 minutes of intermittent defibrillation attempts with 10-30J using internal paddles. During the reperfusion period; spontaneous return of sinus rhythm without defibrillation occurred in one 20-hour HOPE experiment. Animals were maintained on full cardiopulmonary bypass support for one hour following reperfusion before initiating a slow wean, with flow reduced in a stepwise fashion at approximately 500-1000 mL/min decrements. Inotropic and vasopressor support was titrated as needed during weaning as described above. In our initial two experiments, pigs were cooled to 34°C during bypass; in later experiments, active cooling was discontinued to reduce shivering, which seemed to limit lactatemia once bypass was initiated. Vecuronium was administered for neuromuscular blockade to further suppress shivering once CPB was initiated. The chest was left open throughout the experiment. Animals were monitored for a 3-hour terminal observation period. Vitals were recorded at 15-minute intervals throughout the monitoring period. Concurrent infusion rates of epinephrine, dobutamine, vasopressin, norepinephrine, amiodarone, and any additional vasoactive infusions were recorded at each time point. Right atrial pressure, urine output, arterial blood gas, and serum chemistries were assessed at hourly intervals. Short axis epicardial echocardiography was performed hourly to assess graft function. Total 'ischemic time' was calculated as the interval between donor aortic cross-clamp application and removal of the recipient aortic cross-clamp.

Veterinary Intraoperative Management

Anesthetic Maintenance: Animals were maintained on a continuous rate infusion (CRI) of fentanyl and propofol with low-concentration isoflurane 2-5%. Ketamine CRI was sometimes added, particularly when hemodynamic support was needed while minimizing isoflurane and opioid requirements. During induced hypothermia on cardiopulmonary bypass, CRI rates was reduced proportionally to account for decreased metabolic demand.

Immunosuppression: Methylprednisolone 1 g by IV was administered as a bolus at the start of the procedure. If mycophenolate mofetil was indicated per protocol, it was administered via orogastric gavage using a largebore rubber catheter.

Hemodynamic Support: Epinephrine and norepinephrine were prepared in advance as the primary vasopressors. Initial management of hypotension consisted of reducing isoflurane while increasing propofol CRI and optimizing volume status. Vasopressors were titrated if hypotension persisted or if myocardial contractility was impaired.

Anticoagulation and Reversal: Heparin was administered immediately prior to cannulation for cardiopulmonary bypass, with activated clotting time (ACT) monitored by the perfusion team targeting 350+ seconds. Additional heparin boluses were given as needed to maintain therapeutic anticoagulation. Upon separation from bypass, protamine was dosed based on the total cumulative heparin administered and infused slowly to minimize adverse hemodynamic effects.

Temperature Management: Intentional hypothermia was induced by the perfusion team during bypass, with core body temperature permitted to decrease to approximately 34°C (94°F). Active warming devices were discontinued upon initiation of bypass and restarted during the rewarming phase prior to separation. Temperature decline during this period was expected and required no intervention. In latter experiments, cooling by CPB was terminated, lactate elevation was not observed.

Antiarrhythmic Therapy: Lidocaine IV bolus (1mg/kg) was given for acute arrhythmia management. Amiodarone was used for antiarrhythmic prophylaxis. It must be diluted to 2 mg/mL in 5% dextrose in sterile water. A loading dose of 2 mg/kg is infused over approximately 20 minutes, followed by a maintenance infusion of 1 mg/kg/hr.

Neuromuscular Blockade:

Vecuronium was administered when neuromuscular blockade was desired, particularly to suppress shivering during hypothermia. A CRI of 4-8 mL/hr was typical, with supplemental boluses of 2-6 mL given for breakthrough muscular activity after confirming adequate anesthetic depth. When the animal is paralyzed and fully supported on bypass, anesthetic depth was confirmed with firm pressure applied between the digits of a forelimb.

Ventilator Management: Mechanical ventilation was maintained throughout the procedure, including during cardiopulmonary bypass, with positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP) set at 4 cmH₂O. Tidal volume and respiratory rate was reduced during bypass to minimize barotrauma while preventing atelectasis.

Clinical Endpoints Methods.

Cardiac graft function was serially assessed using echocardiography, invasive hemodynamic monitoring, and a composite vasoactive support score. Visual ejection fraction (EF), fractional area change, and left ventricular end diastolic diameter (LVEDD) were measured by echocardiography at donor baseline (prior to cardiectomy) and at 0.5, 1.5, and 2.5 hours following separation from cardiopulmonary bypass by parasternal short axis, via direct epicardial contact. Left ventricular contractility was assessed via the left ventricular solid-state pressure catheter at 1 and 2 hours off bypass. Central venous pressure and systolic blood pressure were recorded at 1 and 2 hours following separation from bypass. A combined drip score, reflecting cumulative vasoactive and inotropic support, was calculated at 1 and 2 hours off bypass by adding epinephrine and levophed dosing. Statistical comparisons between groups at each timepoint were performed using ANOVA. Hemodynamics Optical Flow: Quantified Epicardial Motion. High-definition video of cardiac contraction was obtained at baseline, prior to donor cardiectomy, and after implant throughout the monitoring period (1.5h and 2.5h off CPB). Once obtained, the videos were stabilized and analyzed using a custom Python pipeline to quantify epicardial motion and estimate contractile function 29 . Videos were converted to grayscale, and frame-frame pixel displacement in a standardized area at interventricular groove that includes both the right ventricle (RV) and the left ventricle (LV) was estimated using the Farnebäck dense optical flow algorithm in OpenCV 30 . Motion magnitude was averaged and converted from pixels/frame to mm/s using frame rate metadata and a calibration factor in the field of view. Raw velocity was smoothed through a Savitzy-Golay filter to obtain average velocity magnitudes throughout the cardiac cycle. To isolate systolic function, velocity values during each ventricular contraction were plotted, and the peak velocities were identified and averaged to obtain mean peak velocity (MPV) of each group. Furthermore, velocity plots were mathematically differentiated to obtain mean peak acceleration (MPA) of the epicardium during contraction. Statistical significance was calculated with one-way ANOVA.

Tissue Collection.

At the conclusion of the three-hour post-wean monitoring period, blood and tissue collection was performed immediately. Hearts were explanted while still warm and beating and immediately processed for downstream analyses, including flow cytometry, histology, mitochondrial respirometry (Seahorse assay) and RNA isolation and sequencing. The explanted heart was systematically biopsied using a 8mm biopsy punch across left ventricular (LV) free wall and interventricular septum (IVS).

Histological Analysis.

Myocardial tissue obtained from LV and IVS regions was fixed in 10% formalin (4% formaldehyde) for < 24 hours, then transferred to 70% ethanol and embedded in paraffin. Next, tissue blocks were sliced using a microtome and 10 µm-tick sections were stained with Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E) and Masson's trichrome for histological evaluation. Additional 5 µm-tick blank slices were generated for chromogenic terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase dUTP nick-end labeling (TUNEL) with HRP-DAB system. Histological specimen preparation and staining were performed in the Molecular Pathology Shared Resource of the Herbert Irving Comprehensive Cancer Center at Columbia University.

Flow cytometry.

Myocardial biopsies of 100 mg were collected from the IVS region, washed immediately in PBS 1x (Corning) and minced into 2 mm 3 using a scalpel blade in sterile conditions under the biosafety cabinet. Tissues were then transferred into 15 ml tubes containing a warm enzymatic solution consisting of DMEM Base (Gibco, 10313-021) with 450 U/ml Collagenase I (Worthington, LS004196), 60 U/ml DNase I (Millipore, DN25) and 60 U/ml hyaluronidase (Worthington, LS002592) and incubated in a rotating mixer in agitation at 65/70 rpm at 37 °C for 1 hour. The digestion was stopped by the addition of 8 ml of HBB buffer, consisting of 2% FBS (Sigma-Aldrich, 12306C) and 0.2% BSA (Prometheus, 25-529) in Hanks′ Balanced Salt solution (Gibco, 14175-095), and the single cells suspension was filtered using a 100 µm cell strainer to filter out undigested material and centrifuged at 350g for 5 min at 4 °C. Next, the pellet was resuspended in ACK lysing buffer (Gibco, A10492) to lyse red blood cells, incubated for 5 minutes at room temperature, washed with 9 ml of DMEM Base and centrifuged at 350g for 5 min at 4 °C. After one wash with PBS 1x, cells were incubated for 10 minutes at 4°C in 1 ml of Blocking Solution, consisting of 0.5% BSA in PBS. Then, 2 ml of PBS 1x were added to the tube and the cellular solution was split equally into three FACS tubes. Cells were centrifuged at 500g for 5 min at 4°C and the pellet from each FACS tube was resuspended in either 300 µl of Calcein AM (1:500) (BD Pharmingen, 564061) and DRAQ5 (1:2000) (Biolegend, 424101) in PBS, or only DRAQ5 (1:2000) in PBS (Calcein-negative control), or PBS only (unstained control). Samples were incubated for 30 minutes protected from light at 4°C, then washed with PBS 1x, centrifuged at 500g for 5 min at 4°C and finally resuspended in 300 µl of PBS. Cells were analyzed using the NovoCyte Penteon Flow Cytometer Systems 5 Lasers (Agilent) and data analysis was performed with the NovoExpress flow cytometry software. First, single particles were isolated from duplets by plotting Forward Scatter Area (FSC-A) against Forward Scatter Height (FSC-H) and gating only the events that localized along the diagonal line. Next, nucleated events were identified by DRAQ5 positivity, and, among them, metabolically active cells were identified by Calcein-AM positive staining. Calcein-AM is a cell-permeant probe that emits a fluorescent signal upon cleavage by cellular esterases and is retained within cells with intact membranes. Calcein-AM signal is proportional to cell viability and metabolic activity, and it normally decreases in low viable cell samples. 31 Unstained and Calcein-AM negative controls have been used to exclude false positive background signals.

Extracellular Flux (Seahorse) Mitochondrial Respirometry

Precision-Cut Myocardial Slice Preparation: Left ventricular (LV) free wall biopsies were obtained using a full thickness 8 mm punch and washed in 1× PBS. Biopsy cores were embedded in 3% (w/v) low-gellingtemperature agarose prepared in Tyrode's solution and mounted on a vibrating microtome fitted with a metal blade. Tissue was sectioned at 150 µm thickness using an advance speed of 1 and oscillation setting of 6 24 , 30 . Slices were collected and maintained in cardioplegia solution on ice until further processing. Individual precision-cut slices were further trimmed to 3 mm diameter mini-slices using a biopsy punch, transferred to a wash buffer containing 2% P/S, 5ug/ml gentamycin, 1.25 ug/mL amphotericin B in Advanced DMEM and washed twice for 5 minutes each. Finally, slices were incubated overnight in Advanced DMEM containing 10% FBS, 1% L-glutamine, 2% P/S, 5 ug/ml gentamycin, 1.25 ug/mL amphotericin B.

Seahorse Microplate Preparation:

Agilent Seahorse XFe96 sensor cartridges were hydrated with 200 µL of tissue-culture-grade deionized water per well and incubated overnight in a non-CO₂ incubator at 37°C. On the day of the assay, hydration water was replaced with 200 µL of XF Calibrant Solution per well. XF96 cell culture microplates were coated with Corning Cell-Tak tissue adhesive (3.75 µg per well, diluted in deionized water with 0.1 M sodium bicarbonate) for a minimum of 20 minutes to achieve a surface coverage of 5 µg/cm², then washed and allowed to dry prior to slice loading. nIndividual 3 mm × 150 µm myocardial mini-slices were transferred into Cell-Tak-coated wells containing 20 µL PBS to maintain slice flatness. PBS was removed, and slices were allowed to adhere to the well surface for 1-2 minutes. Warmed complete Seahorse XF DMEM assay medium (supplemented with 10 mM glucose, 1 mM pyruvate, and 2 mM L-glutamine) was then added to each well to a final volume of 180 µL, taking care to avoid slice detachment. 31 Four wells per plate containing assay medium without tissue served as background. The loaded microplate was incubated for 45-60 minutes in a non-CO₂ incubator at 37°C prior to the assay to allow temperature and pH equilibration.

Mito Stress Test: Mitochondrial function was assessed using a modified Seahorse XF Cell Mito Stress Test (Agilent) on an XFe96/XF Pro Analyzer. Oligomycin was omitted from the standard protocol, as precision-cut myocardial slices have been reported not to respond reliably to oligomycin dosing 32 , 33 . Carbonyl cyanide-4 (trifluoromethoxy)phenylhydrazone (FCCP, Port A) and a combination of rotenone and antimycin A (Rot/AA, Port B) were sequentially injected. FCCP was loaded to a final well concentration of 5 µM and Rot/AA to a final well concentration of 2 µM. 24 The sensor cartridge was calibrated for 15 minutes prior to loading the tissue culture microplate. Oxygen consumption rate (OCR) and extracellular acidification rate (ECAR) were measured over 8 mix-wait-measure cycles (3 minutes mix, 2 minutes wait, 3 minutes measure per cycle) 32 , 24 . Basal OCR and ECAR were recorded prior to FCCP injection; maximal OCR and ECAR were recorded following FCCP-induced uncoupling; and non-mitochondrial OCR was recorded following Rot/AA administration. OCR and ECAR values were normalized to individual slice volume, calculated from slice diameter (3 mm) and thickness (150 µm). Raw data were exported and analyzed using Seahorse Analytics software, Microsoft Excel, and GraphPad Prism.

RNA Extraction and Bulk RNA Sequencing

IVS tissue biopsies of approximately 200 mg were homogenized in 1 mL of TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, Cat. N. 15596026) and stored at -80°C until RNA extraction.

Extraction: RNA extraction and sequencing were performed by GENEWIZ® NGS Services from Azenta Life Sciences (South Plainfield, NJ, USA). Total RNA was extracted using Qiagen Rneasy Plus Universal Mini kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) following manufacturer's instructions.

Library Preparation:

RNA samples were quantified using Qubit 4.0 Fluorometer (ThermoFisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) and RNA integrity was checked using TapeStation (Agilent Technologies, Palo Alto, CA, USA). ERCC RNA Spike-In Mix (Cat: #4456740) from ThermoFisher Scientific was added to normalized total RNA prior to library preparation following manufacturer's protocol. RNA sequencing libraries were prepared using the NEBNext Ultra II RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina and NEBNext Poly(A) mRNA Magnetic Isolation Module (New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA, USA), according to manufacturer's recommendations. Briefly, mRNAs were initially enriched with Oligod(T) beads. Enriched mRNAs were fragmented for 15 minutes at 94 °C. First strand and second strand cDNA were subsequently synthesized. cDNA fragments were end-repaired and adenylated at 3'ends, and universal adapters were ligated to cDNA fragments, followed by index addition and library enrichment by PCR with limited cycles. The sequencing library was validated on the Agilent TapeStation, and quantified by using Qubit 4.0 Fluorometer as well as by quantitative PCR (KAPA Biosystems, Wilmington, MA, USA).

Sequencing:

The sequencing libraries were multiplexed and clustered onto a flowcell. After clustering, the flowcell was loaded on the Illumina NovaSeq instrument according to manufacturer's instructions. The samples were sequenced using a 2x150bp Paired End (PE) configuration. Image analysis and base calling were conducted by the NovaSeq Control Software. Raw sequence data (.bcl files) generated from Illumina NovaSeq was converted into fastq files and de-multiplexed using Illumina bcl2fastq 2.20 software. One mismatch was allowed for index sequence identification.

Data Analysis:

Differential gene expression analysis was performed using PyDESeq2, a Python implementation of the DESeq2 statistical framework. 34 Raw counts were normalized using the median-of-ratios method to account for differences in sequencing depth and RNA composition. Differential expression was modeled using negative binomial generalized linear models with gene-specific dispersion estimation. Preservation condition was included as the explanatory variable, with pooled clinical controls serving as the reference group. Differential expression was evaluated using Wald tests of the null hypothesis that the log₂ fold change between preservation conditions was zero. Cook's distance-based outlier handling and model refitting were enabled where supported by the available biological replicates. Resulting P values were adjusted for multiple comparisons using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure to control the false discovery rate (FDR). Genes with FDR <0.05 were considered statistically significant, with an additional absolute log₂ fold-change threshold >1 applied to identify differentially expressed genes exhibiting changes greater than twofold. Positive log₂ fold changes indicated increased expression in HOPE20h relative to clinical controls. Preranked gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was performed using GSEApy to identify coordinated transcriptional differences in predefined biological pathways. 35 Genes were ranked according to their signed Wald test statistics obtained from PyDESeq2, thereby incorporating both the direction and statistical strength of differential expression. All eligible genes with finite ranking statistics were included, irrespective of individual gene-level statistical significance or fold-change magnitude. Enrichment analyses were performed using MSigDB Hallmark 2020 and Gene Ontology Biological Process 2023 gene set collections. GSEA was performed using 1,000 permutations, a minimum gene set size of 10, a maximum gene set size of 500, and a fixed random seed of 42. Enrichment scores were calculated according to the distribution of pathway-associated genes within the ranked dataset and normalized to generate normalized enrichment scores (NES). Statistical significance was assessed using permutation-derived nominal P values and FDR-adjusted q-values, with FDR <0.05 defining significant pathway enrichment. Positive NES values indicated relative enrichment in HOPE20h, whereas negative NES values indicated relative enrichment in pooled clinical preservation controls. Pathway enrichment was interpreted as evidence of coordinated transcriptional differences rather than direct functional activation or inhibition of the corresponding biological processes.

Cell-Type-Associated Transcriptional Signature Analysis

To investigate preservation-associated alterations in cardiac cell-type transcriptional signatures, bulk RNA sequencing data from porcine myocardial samples subjected to static cold storage for 4 hours (SCS4h; n=2), hypothermic oxygenated perfusion for 4 hours (HOPE4h; n=2), or extended hypothermic oxygenated perfusion for 20 hours (HOPE20h; n=4) were analyzed using a custom Python-based pipeline. Raw gene-level counts were normalized using the median-of-ratios method, with size factors calculated from genes exhibiting nonzero counts across all samples. Normalized expression values were subsequently log₂-transformed following the addition of a pseudocount of one. To characterize cell-type-associated transcriptional activity, curated marker gene panels were assembled for cardiomyocytes, fibroblasts, endothelial cells, macrophages/monocytes, T lymphocytes, B lymphocytes, pericytes, and vascular smooth muscle cells (Supplemental Figure 10A). [36] [37] [38] [39] Candidate marker genes were mapped to porcine Ensemble gene identifiers using MyGene.info (Sus scrofa, NCBI taxonomy ID: 9823). Only exact gene-symbol matches with unambiguous gene-level assignments were retained. Marker coverage was assessed for each cell population, and signatures containing fewer than three successfully mapped, variable genes were excluded. To evaluate relative cell-type-associated transcriptional activity, log₂-transformed expression values were standardized independently for each gene across all eight biological samples using z-score normalization. For each sample, cell-type-associated transcriptional signature scores were calculated as the arithmetic mean of the standardized expression values of all successfully mapped marker genes within the corresponding cell-type panel. Importantly, differences in signature scores cannot distinguish altered cellular abundance from transcriptional regulation within existing cell populations. The limited number of biological replicates constrained the resolution and statistical power of exact permutation testing, particularly in comparisons involving two control samples.

Data Analysis:

Raw diaPASEF files were searched against the UniProt Pig proteome database (UP000008227) using DIA-NN (v2.6.0) with default settings for library-free analysis 39 . Search was performed with trypsin specificity, allowing up to one missed cleavage. Carbamidomethylation of cysteine (+57.021 Da) was set as a fixed modification, while protein N-terminal acetylation (+42.010 Da), methionine oxidation (+15.995 Da), and deamidation of N/Q (+0.984 Da) were included as variable modifications. The false discovery rate (FDR) was controlled at 1% at both precursor and protein levels. DIA-NN output files were further analyzed for differential abundance using DIA-Analyst platform (v0.11.1), without additional normalization. Missing values were imputed using the Perseus-type imputation method, and FDR was corrected with t-statistics based method. Proteins with adjusted p-value < 0.05 and absolute log2 fold change >1 were considered differentially abundant between groups. Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was performed on quantitative proteomic data to identify biological pathways associated with extended hypothermic oxygenated heart preservation (HOPE20h) relative to pooled clinical preservation controls (SCS4h and HOPE4h). Preranked GSEA was conducted using GSEApy, with proteins ranked according to a signed statistical significance metric incorporating both the magnitude and direction of differential protein abundance. 44 For each protein, the ranking statistic was calculated as: Ranking statistic = sign(log₂FC) × [-log₁₀(P value)], where log₂FC represented the relative protein abundance in HOPE20h compared with pooled clinical controls, and P represented the nominal P value obtained from differential protein abundance analysis. When nominal P values were unavailable, log₂ fold changes were used as the alternative ranking metric. Proteins with nonfinite ranking statistics were excluded, and duplicate gene symbols were removed to retain a single entry per gene. Preranked enrichment analysis was performed using MSigDB Hallmark 2020, Reactome 2022, and Gene Ontology Biological Process 2023 gene set collections. Gene sets were evaluated using 1,000 permutations, a minimum gene set size of 5, a maximum gene set size of 500, and a fixed random seed of 42. All eligible quantified proteins were included in the ranked dataset irrespective of their individual statistical significance. Enrichment scores were calculated based on the distribution of pathway-associated proteins within the ranked dataset and normalized to generate normalized enrichment scores (NES). Statistical significance was assessed using permutation-derived nominal P values and false discovery rate (FDR)-adjusted q-values. Pathways with FDR <0.05 were considered significantly enriched. Positive NES values indicated relative enrichment of pathway-associated proteins in HOPE20h, whereas negative NES values indicated relative enrichment in pooled clinical controls.

Integrated Transcriptomic-Proteomics

RNA-protein directional concordance was evaluated using matched gene-protein pairs. A descriptive absolute log₂ fold-change threshold of 0.25 was applied independently to RNA and protein measurements. 45 Pairs exceeding this threshold in both omics layers were classified as concordantly increased, concordantly decreased, RNA-increased/protein-decreased, or RNA-decreased/protein-increased according to their respective fold-change directions. Pairs failing to exceed the threshold in either layer were classified as below the directional threshold. Directional classifications were descriptive and independent of statistical significance, which was assessed separately using an FDR threshold of <0.05.

GSEA Leading-Edge Analysis and Pathway Redundancy Mapping

Leading-edge analysis was performed using a custom Python pipeline to identify genes contributing to pathway enrichment and characterize redundancy among enriched biological pathways. Leading-edge gene annotations, normalized enrichment scores (NES), and false discovery rate (FDR) q-values were obtained from the preceding gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA). Leading-edge genes were extracted from the GSEA results as the subset of pathway-associated genes contributing to the enrichment score at its maximum deviation from zero. These genes represent the principal contributors to the observed enrichment signal within each pathway. To assess redundancy among enriched biological pathways, pairwise overlap of leading-edge gene sets was quantified using the Jaccard similarity coefficient defined as the number of shared leading-edge genes divided by the total number of unique genes represented across both pathways. Gene-pathway relationships were additionally represented using binary membership matrices, indicating whether individual genes belonged to the leading-edge subset of each pathway. Membership heatmaps included up to 30 genes ranked by their frequency of occurrence across the top 25 significantly enriched pathways by minimum FDR. These matrices represented pathway membership rather than gene-expression magnitude. FDR in this analysis is pathway-level GSEA FDR, not gene-level DESeq2 FDR. A gene appearing in many leading edges is a recurring contributor to pathway enrichment, not automatically a significant DEG or a causal regulator. The Jaccard threshold (≥ 0.25) and gene-prioritization metrics were considered exploratory analytical criteria rather than independently validated statistical significance thresholds.

Proteomics

Tissue biopsied from the IVS weighting approximately 200 mg was placed in cryotubes, snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at -80°C until use.

Tissue Lysis and Protein Extraction:

Samples were pulverized and suspended in lysis buffer containing 1% SDC, 2% SDS, and 1× protease inhibitor in 100 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8.0), followed by homogenization using PreOmics' BeatBox for 10 min. The tubes were then placed on a magnetic rack, and the solubilized lysate was transferred to 1.5 mL Eppendorf tubes. The lysates were centrifuged at 14,000 rpm for 5 min, and the supernatants were transferred to fresh 1.5 mL Eppendorf tubes. The samples were incubated at 60 °C for 20 min with constant agitation at 1,400 rpm, followed by incubation at room temperature for 5 min and water-bath sonication for 2 min. Protein concentrations were determined using the Pierce BCA Protein Assay Kit. A total of 10 µg of protein from each lysate was used for subsequent processing. Proteins were reduced and alkylated using 10 mM tris(2-carboxyethyl)phosphine (TCEP) and 40 mM 2-chloroacetamide at 45 °C for 10 min with constant agitation at 1,400 rpm. Following reduction and alkylation, the samples were incubated at room temperature for 5 min and sonicated in a water bath for 2 min. Lysate cleanup was performed using SP3 magnetic beads, followed by overnight digestion at 37 °C with Lys-C/Trypsin (Promega) at an enzyme-toprotein ratio of 1:100. 37 Digestion was terminated by adding TFA to a final concentration of 1% (v/v). The resulting peptide mixtures were clarified by centrifugation at 14,000g for 10 min and desalted using a C18 plate. 41 The eluted peptides were dried using a SpeedVac centrifuge and resuspended in 3% acetonitrile / 0.1% formic acid. An aliquot corresponding to 200 ng of peptides from each sample was subjected to LC-MS/MS analysis.

Liquid Chromatography -Tandem Mass Spectrometry (LC-MS/MS):

Peptides were separated over a 40-min gradient at a flow rate of 400 nl/min on a reverse-phase C18 column with an integrated CaptiveSpray emitter (25 cm × 75 µm, 1.6 µm; IonOpticks). Mobile phase A consisted of 0.1% formic acid in water, and mobile phase B consisted of 0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile. The gradient was programmed as follows: 2% to 25% B over 30 min, 25% to 40% B over 5 min, then increased to 95% B before column re-equilibration. The timsTOF Pro2 was operated in diaPASEF model. 38 Data were acquired using 60 variable isolation windows covering m/z 350-1,250. Collision energy was linearly ramped with ion mobility, ranging from 59 eV at 1/K₀ = 1.45 Vs cm⁻² to 20 eV at 1/K₀ = 0.6 Vs cm⁻².
