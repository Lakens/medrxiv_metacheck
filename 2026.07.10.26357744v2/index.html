How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Abstract

Question: Which plasma proteins associated with major psychiatric disorders have credible genetic evidence of a causal role? Findings: In this proteogenomic study, 1,685 of 2,923 plasma proteins measured in 52,615 UK Biobank participants were associated with at least one psychiatric disorder. Of these, 26 proteins (32 protein-disorder pairs) were supported by Mendelian randomisation and colocalisation, with 7 proteins (9 pairs) meeting credible causal criteria after independent genetic validation.

Meaning: Multi-layer genetic appraisal narrowed 1,685 protein associations to a short priority list, led by CD40, an immune receptor targeted by drugs in clinical development, for schizophrenia and bipolar disorder.

Introduction

Treatment options for major psychiatric disorders have changed little in decades, and many patients with depression or schizophrenia do not respond adequately to existing drugs [1, 2]. Drug targets with human genetic support are more likely to succeed in clinical development [3, 4], and circulating proteins are attractive candidates because they are measurable in blood and often pharmacologically tractable [5].

Large-scale plasma proteomics now measures thousands of proteins in population cohorts [6]. Associations with psychiatric disorders are, however, difficult to interpret as protein levels are strongly influenced by adiposity, smoking, socioeconomic factors, and illness itself, so an association may reflect confounding or a consequence of the disorder rather than a cause [7, 8]. Mendelian randomisation (MR), which uses genetic variants that influence protein levels as instruments, can address confounding and reverse causation, and genetic colocalisation tests whether a protein and a disorder share a causal gene [9, 10]. Proteome-wide MR studies have used these methods to nominate candidate proteins for psychiatric disorders variant [11, 12].

Two aspects of this evidence base limit what it can support. First, most findings rest on a single cis-acting variant, which precludes tests of heterogeneity and pleiotropy [13]. Second, many signals map to the major histocompatibility complex (MHC), where extensive linkage disequilibrium prevents attribution of an association to a specific gene [14], or rely on trans-acting variants that may act through other proteins. Most studies have relied on MR using plasma protein quantitative trait loci (pQTLs), with or without colocalisation [15, 16], and findings are rarely corroborated by independent genetic evidence from other molecular layers, tissues, or classes of variant [17].

We asked which plasma proteins associated with major psychiatric disorders are plausibly causal. Starting from candidate proteins prioritised through cross-sectional and longitudinal screening of 2,923 plasma proteins in UK Biobank, we conducted two-sample MR and colocalisation using common-variant pQTLs across four major psychiatric disorders: anxiety, depression, bipolar disorder, and schizophrenia. Every genetically supported finding was then evaluated using a rule-based appraisal framework that integrates instrument validity with independent validation across three genetic layers: MR with colocalisation using expression quantitative trait loci (eQTLs) in blood to tests whether genetic regulation of the encoding gene has a concordant effect and so guards against artifacts of protein measurement [18]; the same approach in brain cortex, a tissue directly relevant to psychiatric disorders [19]; and MR using rare variants, which are distinct from the common-variant instruments and, being in weak linkage disequilibrium with nearby variants, are less prone to confounding by neighbouring genes [20]. To our knowledge, this is the first study of major psychiatric disorders to systematically evaluate multi-layer genetic evidence across plasma proteins within a uniformly applied appraisal framework.

Methods

Study design

This study combined cross-sectional and longitudinal analyses of individual-level UK Biobank (UKB) data with genetic analyses using publicly available summary statistics (Figure 1). UKB has ethical approval from the NHS National Research Ethics Service North West (16/NW/0274). UKB data were accessed under application 26999. This work adhered to the STROBE and STROBE-MR reporting guidelines [21, 22]. A detailed description of the study methods is provided in eMethods in Supplement1.

Participants, proteomics, and outcomes in UK Biobank UKB is a prospective population-based cohort of approximately 500,000 adults aged 40-69 years recruited in 2006-2010 [23, 24]. Plasma levels of 2,923 proteins were measured using the Olink Explore 3072 platform in Figure 1. Study overview and analytical pipeline for identifying plausibly causal protein-disorder markers (1) We examined associations between 2,920 plasma proteins and four psychiatric disorders in 52,615 UKB participants, both cross-sectionally at baseline (prevalent cases) and longitudinally at follow-up (incident cases), using logistic and Cox proportional hazards regression, respectively. A total of 1,685 proteins were involved in protein-disorder associations that were significant after multiple testing correction (FDR/LFSR < 0.001; * FDR/LFSR <0.05 for incident bipolar and psychotic disorders).

(2) For protein-disorder associations identified in step (1), we performed two-sample MR and genetic colocalisation using common-variant blood pQTLs (cis-only, cis and trans, or trans-only) as genetic instruments for protein levels. A total of 32 protein-disorder markers across 26 proteins met all of the following criteria: MR FDR <0.05, genetic colocalisation PP.H4 ≥0.8, no evidence of heterogeneity or horizontal pleiotropy (where testable), passing Steiger filtering, and no evidence of reverse causation.

(3) For proteins identified in step (2), we performed validation two-sample MR using rare or ultrarare variant blood pQTLs (cis-only), MR and genetic colocalisation using brain cortex eQTLs (cis-only), and MR and genetic colocalisation using blood eQTLs (cis-only). A validation analysis was considered significant if the MR P value was <0.05 and, for eQTL-based analyses, genetic colocalisation PP.H4 was ≥0.8.

(4) Protein-disorder markers identified in step (2) were appraised in three steps. First, each marker was assigned a tier based on whether its common-variant instruments (pQTLs) could securely attribute the MR signal to the protein: Tier A (secure attribution; cis-acting instruments outside the MHC region) or Tier B (unresolved attribution; trans-acting instruments and/or instruments within the MHC region). Second, results from each validation analysis were compared with those of the primary analysis and scored as follows: +2 (significant and directionally concordant for the same disorder), +1 (significant and directionally concordant for a different disorder), 0* (significant and directionally discordant for the same disorder), 0 (all other results), or NT (not testable). Scores were summed across validation layers, with the number of layers tested also reported. Third, markers were classified into evidence bands based on their instrument tier and validation scores, with "Promising" markers having supporting evidence for a plausible causal protein-disorder association and all other markers classified as "Uncertain". approximately 54,000 participants through the UKB Pharma Proteomics Project (UKB-PPP) [25, 26] (eTable1 in Supplement2). Participants missing >60% of proteins and proteins missing in >30% of participants were excluded.

Remaining missing protein values were imputed using random forests [27]. Psychiatric outcomes included depression, anxiety, bipolar disorder, and psychotic disorders, identified through linked health records, death registry, and self-report. Prevalent cases had diagnoses recorded before or at baseline assessment, reflecting lifetime rather than current illness. Incident cases were first diagnosed during follow-up (eTable2 in Supplement2).

Cross-sectional and longitudinal proteomic associations in UK Biobank

Associations of plasma protein levels with prevalent and incident disorders were estimated using logistic and Cox proportional hazards regression, respectively. Odds ratios (ORs) and hazard ratios (HRs), with 95% confidence intervals (CIs), were estimated per unit increase in normalised protein levels. Models were minimally adjusted for age, sex, and blood collection characteristics, and fully adjusted additionally for ethnicity, education, deprivation, body mass index, smoking, alcohol use, and physical activity (eTable2 in Supplement2).

Multiple testing was addressed using Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) <0.001 and Empirical Bayes local false sign rate (LFSR) <0.001, the latter included to improve the true positive rate for rare outcomes [28] [29] [30].

For outcomes with frequency <1%, thresholds of 0.05 were also considered.

To assess specificity of the protein-disorder associations, selected non-psychiatric conditions served as external controls. Rheumatoid arthritis was the positive control, given its well-established inflammatory biology and expected immune-related proteomic signals [31]. Senile cataract and refraction and accommodation disorders were negative controls, as localised ocular conditions expected to show minimal or specific proteomic associations.

To identify over-represented biological pathways among proteins associated with psychiatric disorders, functional enrichment analyses were performed.

Genetic causal inference using MR and colocalisation

Proteins associated with psychiatric disorders in the UKB were carried forward for genetic analyses. We performed two-sample bi-directional Mendelian randomisation (MR), using common-variant protein quantitative trait loci (pQTLs) as instrumental variables, to estimate the causal effect of genetically proxied protein levels on psychiatric outcomes [9, 32, 33]. Complementary pairwise conditional and colocalisation (PWCoCo) analyses evaluated shared causal variants at each locus, quantified by the posterior probability (PP.H4) [34] [35] [36]. Independent genome-wide significant cis-and trans-pQTLs (p ≤5×10 -8 ; r² <0.001 within 10,000kb, F-statistics ≥10) [37] were selected from UKB European-ancestry GWAS (N=34,557) [25]. Outcome summary statistics were extracted from the largest available GWAS for anxiety (Cases=122,341, Controls=729,881), depression (Cases=294,322, Controls=741,438), bipolar disorder (Cases=41,917, Controls=371,549), and schizophrenia (Cases=76,755, Controls=243,649) [38] [39] [40] [41].

Estimates were derived using the Wald ratio [42] or inverse-variance weighted (IVW) method [43]. Steiger filtering Cochran's Q (heterogeneity), and MR-Egger intercept (directional pleiotropy) tests were performed where sufficient variants were available [44, 45]. Multiple testing was addressed using FDR correction [28]. In reverse MR, multiple testing was addressed using Bonferroni correction to account for the increased potential of pleiotropic influences when considering psychiatric exposures.

Protein-disorder pairs were considered potentially causal if they passed FDR (<0.05) and Steiger filtering, colocalised (PP.H4U≥U0.8), passed the heterogeneity and pleiotropy tests (where testable), and showed no evidence of reverse causation.

Genetic validation

For each protein identified in the primary genetic analyses, we performed three validation analyses across four psychiatric disorders, each using cis-acting instruments: MR with colocalisation using expression quantitative trait loci (eQTLs) from blood (eQTLGen) [46] and brain cortex (MetaBrain) [19], and MR using rare (minor allele frequency [MAF] <1%) and ultrarare (MAF <0.1%) pQTLs identified in UKB exome-wide association study (ExWAS; N=49,736) [47],with psychiatric outcomes from a separate UKB ExWAS (N=454,787) [48]. A validation result was considered significant if the MR p-value was <0.05 and, for eQTL-based analyses, genetic colocalisation PP.H4 was ≥0.8.

Appraisal framework

The appraisal framework (Table 1) assessed the causal plausibility of findings from the primary genetic analyses.

MR and colocalisation identify a shared genetic signal but do not establish that the protein causes the disorder. The signal may act through a neighbouring gene, particularly in the major histocompatibility complex (MHC), or through trans-acting variants, and single-variant findings cannot be tested for pleiotropy. We therefore assessed how likely each instrument was to attribute the genetic effect on the disorder to the protein, and whether validation analyses corroborated the findings.

Cross-sectional and longitudinal proteomic associations in UK Biobank

Baseline data on 2,920 plasma protein analytes (eTable1 in Supplement2) were measured in 52,615 UK Biobank participants, median age of 58 years (range 40-70), 54% women, and 94% White ethnicity (eTable3 in Supplement2). At baseline, 6,085 participants (11.6%) had a lifetime diagnosis of at least one of the four psychiatric disorders, and 4,753 incident psychiatric diagnoses were recorded over a median follow-up of 13.2 years (interquartile range 12.4-14.0) (eTable4 in Supplement2).

After full covariate adjustment, we identified 830 protein-psychiatric disorder associations involving 574 proteins, with depression showing the broadest proteomic signature (424 proteins), followed by bipolar disorder (323), anxiety (45), and psychotic disorder (38). Adjusting beyond age, sex, and blood collection characteristics reduced associated proteins from 1,379 to 574, driven primarily by body mass index, smoking, and deprivation (eTable6 in Supplement2, eFigures2-5 in Supplement1). Across primary and sensitivity analyses, 1,685 proteins were associated with at least one psychiatric disorder (full results in eTable7 in Supplement2).

Plasma proteomics also captured disease-relevant signals in the negative and positive control conditions (eTable8 in Supplement2). Rheumatoid arthritis was associated with 779 proteins, dominated by immune markers. Senile cataract was associated with only 30 proteins, led by the lens protein β-crystallin B2 (CRYBB2), the sole protein associated with refraction and accommodation disorders.

Genetic causal inference using MR and colocalisation

In primary genetic analyses, we identified 125 protein-psychiatric disorder pairs (90 plasma proteins) with MR estimates surviving multiple-testing correction at FDR <0.05. Of these, 32 pairs (26 proteins) showed genetic colocalisation (PP.H4 ≥0.8), passed Steiger filtering, and showed absence of heterogeneity and horizontal pleiotropy (where testable). 22 of these 26 proteins were instrumented by a single genetic variant. In reverse-direction analyses, genetic liability to depression showed causal effects on six plasma proteins after Bonferroni correction (N tests=6,764; p<7.4×10 -6 ), none overlapping with the 26 primary proteins. Findings remained largely consistent in sensitivity analyses using depression and bipolar disorder GWAS excluding UKB participants. Genetic instruments are detailed in eTables11-12 (Supplement2), and primary MR and colocalisation results in eTables13-15 (Supplement2).

In validation genetic analyses of the 26 primary proteins across the four psychiatric disorders, five protein-disorder pairs showed supportive causal evidence in two-sample MR using rare or ultrarare genetic variants, while 10 pairs received additional support from brain and blood gene expression (eQTL) MR and colocalisation analyses.

Discovery, replication, and target characterisation

Four of the nine promising pairs (CD40, ADAM22, CDHR1, and PDIA3, each for schizophrenia) replicated a prior proteome-wide MR and colocalisation study with closely matching estimates. CD40 for bipolar disorder has not been reported in a disorder-specific MR and colocalisation analysis but was implicated in cross-disorder studies [54, 55]. No prior MR and colocalisation reports were found for SEMA3F (depression), DSG3 (bipolar disorder), or DDR1 (anxiety or depression) (eTables27-28 in Supplement2).

Among the promising targets, human Mendelian phenotypes link ADAM22 to developmental and epileptic encephalopathy and CD40 to monogenic immune syndromes, supporting neurodevelopmental and immune processes in psychosis (eTable22 in Supplement2). Mouse knockout phenotypes link DDR1 to hyperactivity, supporting a role in behavioural regulation, and SEMA3F to behavioural and neurological abnormalities, including altered locomotor activity, impaired exploration, and abnormal sensory responses (eTable23 in Supplement2).

Of the 26 primary proteins, 17 are potentially or strongly druggable, and five have approved or clinical-stage drugs: CD40, LTB, and CX3CL1 (Phase II autoimmune trials), AGER (Phase III Alzheimer's disease trial), and MYL3 (approved for cardiovascular indications) (eTables24-26 in Supplement2).

Discussion

In this proteogenomic study, we combined cross-sectional and longitudinal population proteomics with causal inference approaches using common and rare genetic variation to systematically prioritise candidate causal proteins for major psychiatric disorders. More than half of the 2,923 proteins examined in UKB were associated with at least one psychiatric disorder, but only 26 proteins (32 protein-disorder pairs) met both MR and colocalisation criteria. Further genetic validation narrowed these to nine high-confidence pairs across seven proteins (CD40, ADAM22, CDHR1, PDIA3, SEMA3F, DSG3 and DDR1), led by the CD40 for schizophrenia and bipolar disorder.

The contrast between widespread proteomic associations and few genetically supported candidates reflects how strongly plasma protein levels are influenced by socioeconomic, behavioural, and health-related factors [8], complicating efforts to separate causal drivers from confounding or downstream effects [56]. Nevertheless, UKB control analyses for rheumatoid arthritis, senile cataract, and disorders of refraction and accommodation confirmed that plasma proteomics can still recover biologically meaningful disease signals despite these external influences. A key strength of our study is systematically validating genetic findings across molecular layers (protein abundance and gene expression), tissues (blood and brain), and variant classes (common and rare), overcoming the single-layer focus of previous proteome-wide psychiatric studies [15, 16, 53, 57, 58].

Among the prioritised proteins, CD40 is a tumour necrosis factor receptor superfamily member, expressed on antigen-presenting cells, regulates adaptive immune activation, and implicated in neuroinflammation [59]. CD40 showed consistent support across analyses, with colocalised effects of circulating blood protein levels on schizophrenia and bipolar disorder, based on two independent cis variants, and effects of brain cortex gene expression, alongside concordant rare-variant evidence for depression. These results align with prior MR studies and further implicate immune mechanisms in psychiatric disorders [60, 61]. Although agonist and antagonist CD40targeting antibodies have entered clinical trials for cancer and autoimmune disease, the required direction of therapeutic modulation for psychiatric disorders remains unclear. Circulating CD40 largely comprises a soluble ectodomain shed from the cell surface, which can limit membrane-bound CD40 signalling [62]. Therefore, although higher genetically predicted circulating CD40 was associated with lower disorder risk, this could reflect greater receptor expression, increased shedding, or both, rather than increased CD40 signalling. Experimental work is needed to address these possibilities.

The remaining six prioritised proteins are plausible causal candidates, each with distinct biological support and caveats. ADAM22 (schizophrenia) and SEMA3F (depression) function in synaptic and axon-guidance pathways, with ADAM22 acting as the receptor for LGI1 in trans-synaptic signalling [63] and SEMA3F regulating limbic circuit development [64]. PDIA3 (schizophrenia) has independent transcriptomic, clinical, and experimental links to cognitive impairment [65]. DDR1 (anxiety and depression), a collagen-activated receptor tyrosine kinase implicated in oligodendrocyte signalling and blood-brain-barrier homeostasis [66] [67] [68]. However, its cis-acting variant instrument resides within the MHC region, where extensive linkage disequilibrium complicates causal attribution.

For DSG3 (bipolar disorder), low bulk brain expression warrants cautious interpretation of its brain eQTL signal, although bulk data can mask cell-type-specific expression and peripheral mechanisms remain possible [69]. Finally, CDHR1 (schizophrenia), although primarily characterised by its role in retinal function, has also been associated with traumatic brain injury [70]. Overall, these candidates represent prioritised hypotheses for experimental followup.

Limitations

Although applied uniformly using explicit rules, our appraisal framework relies on analytical judgements, and alternative thresholds could reclassify borderline findings. Second, most genetic instruments were single variants, precluding heterogeneity and pleiotropy sensitivity analyses, a limitation we sought to address through genetic validation analyses. Third, plasma protein levels may not reflect brain biology, and eQTL data were limited to bulk cortex, with several proteins lacking brain instruments. However, bidirectional peripheral-central immune and neurovascular communication [71, 72] and shared blood-brain molecular regulation [73] support the relevance of blood-based findings to brain phenotypes. Fourth, the anxiety, depression, and bipolar disorder GWAS included UKB participants, and rare-variant analyses were conducted within the UKB, meaning sample overlap could bias estimates toward phenotypic associations. Fifth, in UKB analyses, diagnoses relied on healthcare records and selfreport, risking misclassification. Bipolar disorder and schizophrenia cases were few, limiting statistical power, while the predominantly middle-aged, European-ancestry cohort limits generalisability. Finally, two candidate proteins meeting primary genetic criteria, AGER (schizophrenia) and PBXIP1 (bipolar disorder), showed directionally opposite effects in validation analyses, and were not prioritised, although such discordance could reflect distinct regulatory mechanisms rather than a lack of causal biology.

Conclusions

Few plasma protein associations with major psychiatric disorders showed credible genetic support for a causal role.

Of seven prioritised proteins, CD40 had the strongest evidence, specifically for schizophrenia and bipolar disorder, warranting follow-up to clarify therapeutic directionality. While plasma proteomics can nominate causal candidates, triangulation across molecular layers, tissues, and variant classes is essential to establish causal support.
