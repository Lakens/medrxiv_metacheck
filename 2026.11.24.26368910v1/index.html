Reporting p values imprecisely (e.g., p < .05) reduces transparency, reproducibility, and re-use (e.g., in p value meta-analyses). Best practice is to report exact p-values with three decimal places (e.g., p = .032) unless p values are smaller than 0.001, in which case you can use p < .001.

To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 11 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Background: The Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9) is widely used to measure depressive symptoms, but its performance in autistic adults remains understudied. In autism-focused telehealth care, it is unclear whether PHQ-9 scores can be interpreted similarly for adults who meet autism criteria and those who do not, or across demographic subgroups of autistic adults.

Objective: This study evaluated PHQ-9 reliability, internal structure, and associations with anxiety to inform score interpretation in psychotherapy with autistic adults. Total-score comparability and item 9 endorsement were compared between adults who met autism criteria and adults assessed in the same service who did not. An exploratory aim was to assess score comparability across subgroups of diagnosed adults.

Methods: This retrospective psychometric study used the first eligible check-in with paired PHQ-9 and Generalized Anxiety Disorder-7 (GAD-7) scores in 2 nonoverlapping cohorts. The original cohort included 3,471 clients diagnosed with autism and 484 who completed the same service's assessment without meeting diagnostic criteria. The replication cohort included 7,406 and 1,194 clients, respectively. Ordinal factor models assessed structure and whether item responses were comparable across groups at the same severity. Graded response models compared expected totals at matched latent severity across all 56 sets of 3 anchor items from items 1-8. Logistic models compared item 9 endorsement (response ≥1) at matched Patient Health Questionnaire-8 (PHQ-8) scores.

Results: Across anchor sets, diagnosed-minus-comparison differences in expected PHQ-9 totals, averaged over latent severity, ranged from -0.46 to 0.35 points in the original cohort and -0.47 to 0.49 points in replication. Model-specific 95% interval endpoints spanned -0.80 to 0.59 and -0.68 to 0.63 points, respectively. The largest upper bounds for the average absolute difference . on the 0-27 scale were 0.502 and 0.524 points, respectively. Cronbach's alpha ranged from 0.84 to 0.86. The correlated two-factor model fit better than the one-factor model, though absolute fit remained limited. PHQ-9 and GAD-7 correlations ranged from 0.69 to 0.74. PHQ-9 totals discriminated weakly against recorded depressive-disorder codes (area under the curve 0.60-0.65). In replication, scalar invariance was rejected in the primary comparison, with item-level differences of 0.10 points or less, and in 6 of 7 subgroup contrasts. At PHQ-8=5, the odds ratios for item 9 endorsement were 1.42 (95% CI 0.77-2.61) in the original cohort and 0.66 (95% CI 0.49-0.88) in replication. The joint between-cohort test yielded χ²(2)=5.78, P=.06.

Conclusions: The PHQ-9 total appears useful as a group-level summary of depressive symptoms at this service. Average modeled differences were small relative to within-group variation. Item 9 estimates at PHQ-8=5 pointed in opposite directions across cohorts. Comparability with nonautistic adults, diagnostic cutoffs, sensitivity to change, and applicability to other settings and to adults with higher support or different communication needs require further study.

Introduction

Depression and suicidality are common in autistic adults [1, 2]. A meta-analysis of 36 studies of autistic and possibly autistic people without co-occurring intellectual disability estimated pooled prevalences of 34.2% for suicidal ideation and 24.3% for suicide attempts and behaviors [3]. The Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9) is widely used to assess depressive symptoms and inform clinical care [4]. Sleep disruption, fatigue, and difficulty concentrating are symptoms assessed by the PHQ-9 that can also occur in the context of autism or autistic burnout [5, 6]. How respondents understand and answer particular items may also depend on characteristics associated with autism like the literal interpretation of wording, interoceptive differences, or difficulty identifying and describing emotions (alexithymia) [7] [8] [9] [10] [11]. These considerations motivate evaluating how the PHQ-9 performs in autistic adults and whether its items function similarly across subgroups defined by characteristics like gender or age.

Emerging research on depression measurement in autistic youth and adults [11, 12] provides a foundation for evaluating how the PHQ-9 performs in routine telehealth psychotherapy. A feasibility study examined agreement between screening and diagnostic instruments in autistic adults [13]. In community samples, a single PHQ-9 total score summarized depressive symptoms in autistic adults even though the items reflected two symptom domains [14]. Among university students, most items functioned similarly for selfreported autistic and self-reported nonautistic respondents; the exception was item 9, which asks about thoughts of death or self-harm [15]. Work with the Beck Depression Inventory-II in autistic adults suggests that item-level differences may have little effect on expected total scores [16], but whether the same holds for the PHQ-9 is unknown. If item-level differences offset one another, total scores may look comparable even when responses to particular symptoms differ; if they do not, two people with similar depression could fall on opposite sides of a screening cutoff.

Conclusions about item bias also depend on which items are assumed to function equivalently, so a finding is more credible when it holds across alternative assumptions.

Elevated rates of depression and suicidality among autistic adults make it important to evaluate the tools used to monitor depressive symptoms in the settings where they receive care.

Telehealth use in mental health care has grown [17, 18], and autistic adults have reported positive experiences with remote care [19] [20] [21] [22] [23]. Because symptom questionnaires are routinely administered electronically in telehealth [24] [25] [26] [27], autistic adults may complete the PHQ-9 repeatedly over the course of treatment, with scores used to track changes in depressive symptoms. Large general-population studies have found similar PHQ-9 scores across administration modes [28]. To our knowledge, however, no studies have evaluated the psychometric performance of the electronically administered PHQ-9 among autistic adults receiving routine telehealth psychotherapy. We addressed this gap in an autism-focused telehealth service where adults who met autism criteria and adults who did not completed the PHQ-9 as part of the same routine care workflow.

The aim of this study was to determine whether adults at the same estimated level of depression severity had similar expected PHQ-9 total scores regardless of autism assessment outcome. An exploratory aim was to examine whether PHQ-9 items and total scores functioned similarly across subgroups of clients diagnosed with autism. Additional analyses examined how sensitive the estimated group difference was to the choice of anchor items, evaluated the PHQ-9's internal structure and reliability, and assessed associations with anxiety and wellbeing. Item 9 was examined separately because it showed noninvariance in prior work with autistic adults, and because suicidal ideation and suicide attempts are common in this population [1] [2] [3]. The analysis tested whether clients with the same PHQ-8 score differed in their likelihood of endorsing thoughts of death or self-harm.

Methods

Study Design and Setting

This retrospective psychometric study used deidentified electronic health record data from a national telehealth service providing autism assessments and neurodivergent-affirming psychotherapy. Licensed psychologists conducted structured autism assessments [29]. Licensed master's-level clinicians delivered psychotherapy using the service's autism-adapted care model [30], a flexible cognitive behavioral approach incorporating skills from dialectical behavior therapy and acceptance and commitment therapy. Clinicians used explicit, concrete language and adapted the pace and delivery to clients' sensory needs and communication preferences.

In service-wide records of completed appointments, clients who received assessments had addresses in all 50 US states and the District of Columbia. Clients who participated in psychotherapy had addresses in 38 states. At the first completed appointment, 92.2% of assessment clients and 96.2% of psychotherapy clients used in-network insurance. Self-pay accounted for 6.2% and 2.8%, respectively. Clients in the original and replication cohorts had addresses in 28 and 35 US states, respectively.

The PHQ-9 and the Generalized Anxiety Disorder-7 (GAD-7) were administered together electronically as part of routine care. Completion was encouraged and optional. Before the October 2025 rollout of the measurement-informed care workflow, a check-in was requested before each psychotherapy appointment. After the rollout, a check-in was requested at intake and approximately monthly if none had been completed in the preceding 4 weeks. The original cohort spans both schedules. The replication cohort was observed under the later schedule. The baseline analytic file included one PHQ-9 administration per client, defined as the first available PHQ-9 during the observation window. A separate descriptive item 9 follow-up used the second eligible paired administration when available.

Positionality

The organization also maintains clinical and research advisory structures that include autism specialists and autistic advocates or community members who provide input on research priorities and designs. Measures

Depressive symptoms were measured with the PHQ-9 [4]. The PHQ-9 includes nine items rated from 0 to 3 for the preceding 2 weeks, with total scores ranging from 0 to 27. Higher scores indicate greater depressive symptom severity. Conventional severity bands were used descriptively: minimal (0-4), mild (5-9), moderate (10-14), moderately severe (15) (16) (17) (18) (19), and severe (20-27) [4]. Anxiety symptoms were measured with the GAD-7 [31]. The GAD-7 includes seven items rated from 0 to 3, with total scores ranging from 0 to 21. Higher scores indicate greater anxiety symptom severity. Conventional severity bands were used descriptively: minimal (0-4), mild (5-9), moderate (10) (11) (12) (13) (14), and severe (15-21) [31]. Two single-item routinecare ratings were also collected: stress and emotional functioning (observed range 1-7) and overall wellbeing (observed range 0-10). Data Analytic Plan

This psychometric evaluation combined classical test theory, factor analysis, and item response theory. Classical test theory was used to estimate internal consistency and the standard error of measurement (SEM). Factor analysis was used to examine the structure of depressive symptoms, estimate general-factor saturation, assess the distinction between depression and anxiety, and test measurement invariance by evaluating whether item parameters could be held equal across groups. Item response theory was used to assess measurement precision across severity, check local dependence, screen for differential item functioning (DIF), and estimate whether item-level differences accumulated into differences in expected PHQ-9 total scores at matched severity (differential test functioning). Because these comparisons require placing both groups on a common scale, a subset of items (anchors) was constrained to have equal parameters across groups, and the remaining items were free to differ. Item 9 endorsement was also compared between groups at matched observed PHQ-8 scores using logistic regression. Welch 95% confidence intervals were calculated for unstandardized mean differences. Standardized mean differences were expressed as Hedges g, calculated using the pooled standard deviation and a small-sample correction, with approximate normal-theory 95% confidence intervals. P values were rounded from unrounded output and are reported to 2 decimal places in the main text (3 when P<.01) and to 3 decimal places in Multimedia Appendix 1.

The study was not preregistered. After the initial analyses, a later, nonoverlapping cohort was added as a replication sample to assess whether the main findings held across cohorts.

Because expected total-score differences can depend on which items serve as anchors, the range across all 56 possible three-item anchor sets from items 1-8 was adopted after the initial analyses as the main summary (see Expected Total Scores Across Anchor Sets). No equivalence margin was prespecified, so these comparisons are descriptive and are not formal equivalence tests.

Magnitude was interpreted against two reference values chosen post hoc: the alpha-based SEM and 1 point, the smallest possible difference between two observed PHQ-9 totals. Subgroup analyses within the diagnosed group, partial invariance and subsampling checks, and additional item 9 and alternative-model analyses were exploratory (Multimedia Appendix 1, Table S8).

Additional analytic specifications and decision rules are detailed in Multimedia Appendix 1.

Reporting was informed by the Consensus-based Standards for the Selection of Health

Measurement Instruments (COSMIN) reporting guideline [32].

Reliability and Structure

Cronbach's alpha is reported descriptively by assessment group and cohort. Under COSMIN, internal consistency is rated indeterminate when evidence of unidimensionality is insufficient [33]. The SEM estimates the typical size of measurement error in an observed score.

The alpha-based SEM was calculated as the SD at the first eligible check-in multiplied by √(1alpha).

One-factor and correlated two-factor confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) models were fitted to the four-category items using polychoric correlations and weighted least squares meanand variance-adjusted (WLSMV) estimation with theta parameterization [34]. Following Tibubos et al [35], items 1, 2, 6, 7, and 9 loaded on a cognitive-affective factor, and items 3, 4, 5, and 8 loaded on a somatic factor. Item assignments vary across published models [36] [37] [38]. Wald 95% confidence intervals for the PHQ-9 latent factor correlations were based on the standardized CFA estimates and their standard errors. Models were evaluated for convergence, admissibility, loadings, factor correlations, and fit. Reported indices comprised the scaled chi-square statistic, scaled and robust versions of the comparative fit index (CFI), Tucker-Lewis index (TLI), and root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA), and one standardized root mean square residual (SRMR) per model and group. The published COSMIN criterion [33] and a separate RMSEA criterion of <0.06 were evaluated. The COSMIN criterion was met when CFI or TLI was >0.95, RMSEA was <0.06, or SRMR was <0.08. The separate criterion was applied regardless of the other fit indices [39, 40]. Table 1 reports selected indices and Multimedia Appendix 1, Table S3 gives the full results. A sensitivity model estimated residual covariances for sleep with fatigue and concentration with psychomotor change.

Omega total for the underlying continuous item responses (ωT) was estimated from the one-and two-factor CFA models. General-factor saturation describes how much of the variance in total scores reflects a depression dimension shared by all nine items. Higher values support using a total score. A Schmid-Leiman decomposition used minimum-residual extraction and oblimin rotation to estimate general-factor saturation without prespecified item assignments.

Three group factors were specified in both assessment groups in each cohort. Omega hierarchical (ωH), explained common variance (ECV), the percentage of uncontaminated correlations (PUC), and the proportion of reliable total-score variance attributable to the general factor (ωH/ωT) are reported descriptively. The ωH and ECV estimates were compared descriptively with reference values of 0.70 and 0.60, respectively, which apply when PUC is below 0.80 [41] [42] [43].

Unidimensional graded response models evaluated score precision. Conditional reliability and test information were examined across latent severity. Overall item response theory (IRT) model fit was assessed with the limited-information C2 statistic and its RMSEA.

Because residual associations between items can inflate information and reliability estimates [44], Yen's Q3 was computed from each group's graded response model, and meancentered Q3 (each Q3 minus the group's mean across all 36 item pairs) above 0.20 was flagged descriptively in both cohorts. Original-cohort parametric bootstrap reference values were obtained by simulating and refitting 500 datasets per group and are reported in Multimedia Appendix 1, Table S7 [45].

Pearson correlations quantified associations of PHQ-9 totals with GAD-7 totals and with the two routine-care ratings. Joint ordinal CFAs compared one distress factor with separate depression and anxiety factors. The heterotrait-monotrait ratio (HTMT) was calculated from polychoric correlations. Percentile 95% confidence intervals were based on 2,000 client-level bootstrap resamples attempted per group, using those that converged (Multimedia Appendix 1, Table S9). The point estimates and upper limits were compared descriptively with 0.85 and 0.90, respectively [46].

Invariance and Differential Item Functioning

Observed PHQ-9 totals ranged from 0 to 27. For the 56-anchor comparison, item 9 responses 2 and 3 were collapsed in both cohorts, yielding a model score range of 0-26. Cohortspecific analyses collapsed adjacent categories when a group-by-category cell contained fewer than 10 clients. Thus, the principal replication invariance model and the replication model using anchors selected by iterative purification retained all four item 9 categories. Invariance across assessment groups was evaluated in the correlated two-factor ordinal CFA using polychoric correlations and Wu-Estabrook identification by adding equality constraints sequentially for thresholds, loadings, latent-response intercepts, and residual variances [47]. Thresholds mark transitions between response categories. Loadings reflect how strongly an item relates to the underlying factor. Intercepts account for systematic group differences in the underlying item response at the same severity, with equal intercepts indicating scalar invariance in this sequence. Invariance testing used the scaled-shifted chi-square difference test (P<.05). Changes in CFI, RMSEA, and SRMR were reported descriptively against benchmarks developed for continuous indicators (a CFI decrease of ≥0.010, an RMSEA increase of ≥0.015, and an SRMR increase of ≥0.030 at the loading step or ≥0.010 at the intercept and residual steps) [48, 49].

Where scalar (intercept) invariance was rejected, intercept constraints were released in descending order of the score-test χ² until the difference test against the threshold-and-loading model no longer rejected equality. The practical impact of item noninvariance was evaluated in PHQ-9 points using expected-score differences [50] [51] [52]. Multimedia Appendix 1, Table S11 describes subgroup analyses and their cohort-specific testing procedures. Group differences in the somatic and cognitive-affective factor means were contrasted, using the partial scalar model in replication. Multimedia Appendix 1, Table S23 shows the domain-mean model with the diagnosed group as the reference.

To assess sensitivity to sample size, the scalar invariance test was repeated in 500 replication-cohort subsamples drawn at the original group sizes. The rejection proportion used an exact binomial 95% confidence interval.

In multigroup graded response models, the assessed comparison group's latent mean and variance were fixed at 0 and 1. The other group's latent mean and variance were estimated.

Anchor items had equal discrimination and thresholds across groups. The alternative sets selected by iterative purification comprised three items with the least evidence of DIF among those unflagged throughout purification and in the final screen [53, 54]. Likelihood-ratio DIF tests used Benjamini-Hochberg adjustment across the nine items within each contrast.

Expected Total Scores Across Anchor Sets

Signed and unsigned differential test functioning were computed from the model-implied difference in expected totals at 61 latent severity values from -6 to 6 [50] [51] [52]. The signed statistic averages those differences and the unsigned statistic averages their absolute values. Weights were the mean posterior probabilities across clients. To calculate the upper bound on the original 0-27 scale, one group's collapsed item 9 responses were assigned a score of 2 and the other group's a score of 3, then the assignment was reversed. The larger absolute difference at each severity was averaged with the same weights. The bound uses fitted probabilities without sampling uncertainty. Each anchor set used 2,000 parameter draws for its 95% percentile interval. For the timing sensitivity and subgroup models, one-sided 95% upper confidence limits were obtained as the 95th percentile of the draw-specific averages of the largest absolute expected-score differences on the 0-27 scale. The alpha-based SEM provided descriptive context for magnitude.

All 56 three-item sets from items 1-8 were fitted in each cohort to quantify sensitivity to anchor choice. The diagnosed group's latent mean was also estimated under each anchor set in multigroup graded response models fitted by expectation-maximization with 61 quadrature points from -6 to 6. Standard errors came from the observed information matrix computed by Richardson extrapolation. The 95% intervals were Wald intervals. These models collapsed item 9 responses 2 and 3 in the original cohort and retained all four categories in replication. Fits were required to converge with a positive definite information matrix.

Targeted checks refitted models after excluding one item from each of two locally dependent pairs. In the original cohort, separate models used items 4, 6, and 7 individually as the sole anchor to assess how the estimated group difference depended on anchor choice (Multimedia Appendix 1, Table S17). Discussion

Summary of Principal Findings

Across two cohorts, expected PHQ-9 totals at the same modeled depression severity differed by about half a point or less on average between clients diagnosed with autism and clients assessed without a diagnosis. Both groups received psychotherapy through the same autism-focused telehealth service. The magnitude of the average signed difference remained small across all 56 three-item anchor sets in each cohort, although its direction varied with anchor choice. The largest upper bound for the average absolute difference on the original 0-27 scale, accounting for the collapsed item 9 categories, was 0.524 points. Item-level differences and rejection of scalar invariance in the replication cohort coexisted with small differences in expected totals, a pattern also reported for the Beck Depression Inventory-II in autistic and nonautistic adults [16]. No equivalence margin was prespecified; the differences are described as small relative to the SEM (2.11 to 2.23 points) and to a 1-point difference in observed totals.

Reliability, Structure, and Precision

The cognitive-affective and somatic factors were highly correlated (0.83 to 0.85), and the general factor accounted for 76% to 84% of reliable total-score variance in the exploratory Schmid-Leiman models. This supports interpreting the PHQ-9 total as a group-level summary of depressive symptoms in this sample, consistent with findings in autistic adults in the community [14] and in a large psychotherapy sample [68]. The better fit of the two-factor model also supports distinguishing cognitive-affective and somatic symptom clusters when describing the structure of item responses [37].

General-factor estimates were weakest in the replication assessed comparison group, where omega hierarchical was 0.696 and explained common variance was 0.598, just below the respective descriptive benchmarks of 0.70 and 0.60 [42, 43]. In the original cohort, omega hierarchical was similar in diagnosed and assessed comparison clients (0.775 and 0.772). The two-factor models met the published COSMIN criterion on the standardized root mean square residual, while the root mean square error of approximation exceeded 0.06 [33]. Residual associations between sleep and fatigue and between concentration and psychomotor change suggest symptom clustering beyond the modeled factors and may inflate model-based estimates of precision. The confirmatory bifactor solution for the original diagnosed group was unstable across starting values, so the general-factor interpretation rests primarily on the exploratory Schmid-Leiman estimates.

The PHQ-9 and GAD-7 item responses were better described by two correlated dimensions than by a single dimension. This supports interpreting them as measures of related aspects of distress.

Comparability by Assessment Outcome

The threshold and loading tests provided little evidence of differences between assessment groups in either cohort. The scalar invariance test yielded P=.82 in the original cohort and rejected equality in the replication cohort (P=.003). After freeing the depressed mood and anhedonia intercepts in the replication cohort, the comparison with the threshold-and-loading model yielded P=.07. Strict invariance was rejected in both cohorts. When the replication cohort was subsampled at the original cohort's group sizes, 193 of 500 subsamples (38.6%) rejected scalar invariance, suggesting that sample size may partly explain the different test results (Multimedia Appendix 1, Extended Results).

The rejection of scalar invariance in the replication cohort was statistically detectable but practically negligible. No fit index reached a conventional cutoff for noninvariance at the intercept step. The items involved differed by 0.10 points or less each, and these differences partly canceled, leaving expected totals within about half a point. With 8,600 clients, the chisquare difference test could detect differences too small to affect interpretation of the total score.

The expected-score analyses showed how item-level differences translated to the total. In the replication cohort, lower expected scores for depressed mood and anhedonia were partly offset by higher expected scores for fatigue and psychomotor change (Multimedia Appendix 1, Table S13). Depressed mood was the only item identified by both the intercept-release analysis and the item response model's differential item functioning screen. The two analyses assessed different model parameters.

Observed mean PHQ-9 scores were 0.95 points higher among diagnosed clients in the original cohort and 0.80 points higher in the replication cohort. Both gaps exceeded the magnitude of the corresponding average signed expected-score differences under every threeitem anchor set examined. The estimated latent mean was higher in the diagnosed group under every set, although seven of 56 model-specific 95% intervals included zero in the replication cohort. This pattern is consistent with higher modeled depressive symptom severity among diagnosed clients and depends on the assumptions used to link the groups. Iterative purification can select items with undetected differential item functioning [54], and a response tendency shared across items could be absorbed into the latent mean difference.

At the modeled severity where the assessed comparison group's expected total was 10, the diagnosed group's expected total ranged from approximately 9.5 to 10.5 across anchor sets.

The range crossed 10 in both cohorts. These comparisons describe expected scores at a common modeled severity. Individual screening classifications require separate evaluation. Within diagnosed clients, several exploratory subgroup contrasts showed item noninvariance, and the direction of the signed total-score difference depended on anchor choice for men versus women and Black versus White clients.

The single-group graded response models showed poor absolute fit (C2 RMSEA 0.103-0.117) and residual dependence for the sleep-fatigue and concentration-psychomotor pairs. In the two-factor CFA, allowing residual covariances for both pairs led to the same interceptequality decisions in both cohorts. After dropping fatigue and psychomotor change, the estimated differences on the seven-item scale were -0.027 points (95% CI -0.248 to 0.163) in the original cohort and -0.135 points (95% CI -0.284 to 0.002) in the replication cohort. The expected-score estimates remain conditional on the fitted graded response models.

Item 9

At a PHQ-8 score of 5, fitted item 9 endorsement was lower among diagnosed clients in the replication cohort. The original-cohort estimate pointed in the opposite direction and was imprecise. The joint test of whether the fitted contrasts differed between cohorts yielded P=.06.

The post hoc comparison at a score of 5 yielded an unadjusted Wald P=.03 and addressed the narrower question of how the contrasts differed at that score. In the original cohort, 7 of 234 diagnosed clients (3.0%) and 2 of 32 assessed comparison clients (6.3%) endorsed item 9 at exactly that score. This raw contrast pointed in the opposite direction from the fitted originalcohort contrast, and the small number of endorsements limits its interpretation (Multimedia Appendix 1, Table S18).

Robeson et al [15] reported item 9 loading noninvariance in a university sample. The graded response model screens in this study did not flag item 9 in either cohort. In the separate ordinal loading analysis, the between-cohort contrast was clearer on the standardized scale, with the unstandardized interval including zero. The evidence for item 9 noninvariance by assessment outcome in this service remains limited.

Roughly half of first-check-in endorsers who contributed a second eligible questionnaire endorsed item 9 again. These observations describe the subset who completed a second questionnaire during active psychotherapy and may reflect persistence of the experiences captured by item 9 as well as changes in symptoms or reporting (Multimedia Appendix 1, Table S18, Panel G).

Suicidality measures developed in general populations have limited validation in autistic adults, and standard questions may be interpreted differently [69, 70]. The Suicidal Behaviours Questionnaire-Autism Spectrum Conditions was developed with autistic adults for research and may help structure conversations about suicidal thoughts and support needs [71]. The present analyses addressed item endorsement and comparability by assessment outcome. Suicide-risk prediction and screening accuracy were outside the study's scope.

Association With Recorded Depressive-Disorder Codes

PHQ-9 totals distinguished poorly between clients with and without a recorded depressive-disorder code (AUC 0.598-0.650). These values fell below the COSMIN benchmark of 0.70 for comparisons against a reference standard [33]. The treatment-plan codes included remission diagnoses and could have been recorded before or after the PHQ-9. Clinicians could also see PHQ-9 scores when assigning codes. The analysis therefore describes how well scores distinguished recorded code status. Criterion validity and the diagnostic performance of PHQ-9 cutoffs in this setting remain uncertain.

Clinical Implications

These findings extend prior research on the PHQ-9 in autistic adults [14] to an autismfocused telehealth psychotherapy service. Factor structure and reliability were examined separately among clients who received an autism diagnosis through the service and clients who completed the same assessment without receiving a diagnosis. Score functioning was compared between these groups and, in exploratory analyses, across subgroups of diagnosed clients. The findings support using the PHQ-9 total to summarize depressive symptoms at the group level in this setting. The better-fitting two-factor model met the COSMIN criterion for structural validity based on the standardized root mean square residual [33], while its RMSEA exceeded the present study's conservative cutoff of 0.06. When observed group differences are small, their interpretation should take into account the size of the modeled score differences and how their direction changed with the items used to link the groups.

The psychometric findings for item 9 were mixed. Robeson et al [15] reported a group difference in the item 9 factor loading in a university sample. In the present study, the omnibus loading tests provided little evidence of loading differences, while an exploratory item-specific analysis suggested that the standardized loading contrast varied between cohorts. Estimated differences in endorsement at matched PHQ-8 scores ranged from 1.2 to 2.2 percentage points in absolute magnitude and pointed in opposite directions across cohorts at scores of 5 and 10. This pattern provides little basis for interpreting an endorsement differently by autism assessment outcome. In either group, clinicians should review item 9 separately from the total and ask what the client meant by an endorsement, even when the overall score is low. Direct follow-up can clarify whether the client is reporting a wish to be dead, thoughts of suicide, or thoughts of selfharm.

For interpreting change in an individual's score, single-administration reliability estimates implied a 95% minimal detectable change of 5.8 to 6.2 points using coefficient alpha and 5.7 to 6.1 points using graded response model reliability, assuming independent errors with equal variance. These estimates were similar to the pooled value of 5.72 points reported by Wang et al [72] in samples that excluded people seeking mental health care. The estimates provide a descriptive reference for measurement error. Establishing a reliable-change threshold for this service would require repeat assessments when symptoms are expected to remain stable.

Responsiveness and the smallest change that matters to clients require separate evidence.

Strengths, Limitations, and Future Directions

The primary comparison was based on structured, in-service autism assessments conducted by licensed psychologists with specialized training in diagnosing autism in adults [29]. Both groups sought an autism assessment and received psychotherapy through the same service, reducing differences in referral context and care setting. The primary comparisons included 12,555 clients across two nonoverlapping cohorts, with the replication cohort more than twice the size of the original. Expressing modeled differences in PHQ-9 points made their magnitude interpretable alongside the statistical tests. Evaluating all 56 three-item anchor sets in each cohort showed how estimates changed with the choice of items used to link the groups.

Subsampling the replication cohort at the original group sizes suggested that sample size may partly explain why the scalar invariance tests yielded different results. Neurodivergent members of the author team and advisory groups that included autistic community members informed the research question and framing.

The study was not preregistered. The replication cohort and the main summary across anchor sets were added after the initial analyses, and the subgroup and follow-up item 9 analyses were exploratory. Future studies should prespecify the primary comparisons, the analysis of anchor choice, and the item 9 follow-up tests before examining the data.

Both cohorts came from one telehealth service and included adults who sought an autism assessment and received psychotherapy there. Clients assessed without a diagnosis may share autistic traits or other clinical characteristics with diagnosed clients [7]. Classification error could reduce the ability to detect item-level differences between assessment groups. In simulations, detection declined under some scenarios that introduced errors into the assessment labels (Multimedia Appendix 1, Table S21). The assessment groups differed in gender and race composition, and the analyses did not adjust for those differences. Some items also functioned differently by gender among diagnosed clients. Differences in group composition could therefore contribute to or obscure item-level differences by assessment outcome. In the original cohort, the estimated total-score difference remained close to zero when the analysis was limited to women (-0.054 points, 90% CI -0.347 to 0.204). A corresponding analysis was unavailable in the replication cohort. Three exploratory sensitivity analyses used 90% rather than 95% intervals, which understates their uncertainty relative to the other estimates. Replication across services and care models should document how diagnoses were determined, characterize both groups clinically, and examine whether results hold after accounting for demographic and clinical differences.

Questionnaire completion was optional. The 5,077 clients in the original analytic cohort Assessment outcomes were sometimes recorded after the first eligible check-in.

Restricting the original cohort to clients whose outcome was recorded by that check-in yielded a signed difference of -0.02 points (95% CI -0.47 to 0.37) and a one-sided 95% upper confidence limit of 0.96 points for the average absolute difference. That limit incorporates sampling uncertainty. The first eligible check-in was linked to therapy intake for 63.4% of original-cohort clients and 93.3% of replication-cohort clients, and the check-in schedule changed during the original observation window. Timing in care may therefore contribute to between-cohort differences, including those for item 9. The extract began at the first eligible check-in and contained only complete PHQ-9 and GAD-7 pairs. Future studies should retain the dates of therapy entry, autism assessment, and questionnaire administration, along with records of PHQ-9-only completion, so that timing and questionnaire availability can be examined directly.

Within diagnosed clients, subgroup analyses examined one characteristic at a time. Some subgroups were small, and two original-cohort intercept tests were uninterpretable because model nesting could not be confirmed. Future studies should recruit enough clients to support those comparisons, verify model nesting before interpreting difference tests, and use intersectional analyses to examine whether item functioning varies across combinations of characteristics [75]. Future studies could also examine the PHQ-9-AASPIRE, an adaptation of the PHQ-9 developed for autistic adults, with the same psychometric approach [76].

Conclusions

In a large evaluation study consisting of two cohorts within a national telehealth service, the PHQ-9 total score summarized a common depression dimension and measured depressive symptoms comparably in clients diagnosed with autism and clients assessed without a diagnosis. Scalar invariance was rejected in the larger replication cohort, but the item-level differences behind that result were 0.10 points or less each and had no practical effect on the total. Among diagnosed clients, scalar invariance was also rejected in most subgroup comparisons, but the effect on expected totals was again under 1 point; these exploratory results need replication.

Consistent with earlier research [14], these findings support using the PHQ-9 total as a brief summary of self-reported depressive symptoms and a starting point for discussing them in outpatient psychotherapy with autistic adults.

Group differences in item 9 endorsement were small and varied in direction across cohorts. Because item 9 asks about thoughts of death or self-harm, clinicians should review the response separately and follow up directly on any endorsement, even when the total is low.

Further research should examine how autistic adults interpret item 9, how well standard cutoffs perform, and how much change in a score is meaningful, and should include adults with intellectual disability, minimal or no speech, or higher support needs. Services can continue using the PHQ-9 while evaluating whether adaptations such as the PHQ-9-AASPIRE could complement or replace it in routine care. Autistic adults' experiences of depression should guide both the evaluation of these measures and decisions about their use.
