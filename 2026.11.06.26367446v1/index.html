How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

This study examined how people living with chronic spinal pain experience, interpret and navigate social representations of their condition and the interactions these generate across clinical, occupational, and personal settings.

Thirty semi-structured interviews were analysed using an approach informed by constructivist grounded theory, through initial, focused and theoretical coding.

Results: Five categories emerged, capturing how social representations and relational dynamics threatened the social self and prompted deliberate strategies for restoring agency.

Theoretical coding identified a central process (maintaining and rebuilding the social self), describing how participants navigated these threats across social contexts. Notably, approximately half the participants reproduced, without revision, representations of chronic pain typical of the general population, directing judgements of simulation or inadequate effort at others with the condition. These strategies primarily aimed to restore agency and maintain a viable social self, rather than to reduce pain.

Conclusions: These findings describe one process through which social environments shape the experience of chronic spinal pain by influencing how the social self is continually negotiated. Recognising these processes may help rehabilitation professionals attend to the social dimensions of chronic pain.

Introduction

Chronic spinal pain, including non-specific low back and neck pain, presents a persistent paradox. For many people, the severity of the pain experience exceeds what biological and radiological investigations can explain (Brodal, 2017; Maher et al., 2017). Moreover, even the variables that do predict the condition's evolution point less to symptom intensity than to psychosocial and cognitive factors (Mehling et al., 2015). Neuroimaging studies suggest that the transition to chronic pain involves a gradual shift in brain activity from nociceptive circuits towards regions involved in emotional processing (Hashmi et al., 2013), indicating that pain is not reducible to a passive response to sensory input. It is an experience shaped by the integration of sensory signals, cognitive and affective states, and the social and relational contexts in which it is interpreted and communicated (Brodal, 2017; Melzack, 2001). Sullivan et al. (2023) argue that the processes sustaining chronic pain are "transdermal", having causes both inside and outside the body, and that psychological and social factors contribute to pain itself rather than merely modifying a biologically produced experience.

Social factors, including healthcare relationships, occupational conditions, and the representations that structure how pain is evaluated and communicated, are increasingly recognised as shaping the experience and course of chronic pain (Sullivan et al., 2023; Karran et al., 2020).

However, how these influences are studied does not always capture the relationships and interactions through which they are experienced. In a previous scoping review, we identified 28 categories of social factors across six domains associated with the development of chronic low back pain (LBP) (Sonjon et al., 2025). Most of the included studies relied on quantitative designs in which social factors were operationalised through measurable individual characteristics or predefined categories, and were sometimes used as adjustment variables rather than examined as direct contributors to pain. These approaches provide limited access to the relationships and interactions through which social influences are experienced in everyday life. The literature thus tends to frame individuals through characteristics and categories rather than through the relationships and interactions in which their experience of chronic pain occurs. Recent critical work on the biopsychosocial model in LBP care has similarly shown that social dimensions are often only briefly addressed, with limited attention to interpersonal, cultural, and institutional power dynamics (Cormack et al., 2022; Mescouto et al., 2022). Our review highlighted an important gap, namely that patients' experiences within healthcare settings, including the relational dynamics of clinical encounters, were underrepresented in the literature.

One relational process that remains less often documented concerns how people with chronic pain are evaluated by others, including the credibility granted to their account of pain, the extent to which they are suspected of exaggeration, and whether their suffering is regarded as worthy of recognition (De Ruddere et al., 2013; De Ruddere and Craig, 2016). Thus, within their social environment, people living with chronic spinal pain encounter widely shared, often reductive, representations of pain and of those who experience it. Chronic pain is largely 3 . invisible to others, and when its severity cannot be accounted for by demonstrable tissue damage, its legitimacy may be called into question (De Ruddere and Craig, 2016). Against this background, in clinical, occupational, and family settings, people may encounter suspicions of exaggeration, malingering, lack of will, or secondary gain (De Ruddere and Craig, 2016; Perugino et al., 2022; Zajacova et al., 2021). Such stereotypes about people with chronic pain shape how behaviours are interpreted and may influence the credibility afforded to a person's account (García-Martínez et al., 2022). They are not necessarily expressed explicitly or mobilised deliberately, as evaluations may partly rely on automatic processes of social categorisation and implicit biases that can affect interactions and clinical decision-making (Amodio, 2014; Puddifoot, 2019). These representations may also be internalised by people living with chronic pain, shaping how they interpret their own situation (Perugino et al., 2022; Waugh et al., 2014).

These processes of social evaluation can be examined through the lens of social cognition, understood as the set of processes through which individuals perceive, interpret, and respond to social information in their interactions with others (Arioli et al., 2018; Lieberman, 2007). Social interactions also shape self-representations, including how individuals perceive their identity and social position in relation to others (Markus and Wurf, 1987). In chronic pain, Karos et al. (2018) propose that pain can threaten the social self by shifting control away from the person with pain, disrupting belonging through exclusion or invalidation, and giving rise to experiences of injustice. This last dimension may have consequences for pain itself.

Among people with nonspecific chronic LBP, Penn et al. (2020) found that perceived injustice helped explain the association between chronic pain stigma and movement-evoked pain severity. Taken together, this work suggests that the social environment actively shapes pain experience through the interactions and relationships in which pain is lived. Attending to the accounts of people living with chronic spinal pain provides insight into how these processes are experienced and interpreted across interactions ranging from close relationships to healthcare systems and institutional settings.

The clinical relevance of psychosocial dimensions is well established, through their associations with quality of life in chronic LBP and the benefits of care that takes psychosocial obstacles to recovery into account (Agnus Tom et al., 2022; Hill et al., 2011). What remains less well documented is how these social situations and interactions are experienced by people living with chronic pain. Qualitative research has begun to document what matters to people within chronic pain care pathways (Franklin et al., 2016). Building on this work, the present study focuses on how representations of chronic pain, whether held by others or by people living with the condition themselves, are encountered in everyday relationships and institutional settings. The objective of this study was to explore, through participants' own accounts, how people living with chronic spinal pain interpret the influence of these representations and interactions on their daily life, their pain experience and its evolution, and their healthcare pathway. 4.

Materials and methods

Design

This study used an inductive approach and a qualitative design informed by the principles of constructivist grounded theory, as developed by Charmaz (2017). Within this paradigm, categories and theory are not considered to pre-exist the data. They are constructed through the interaction between researchers and participants, from situated knowledge embedded in a social environment. This epistemological position concerns the way in which the analysis constructs its categories, not the nature of pain or cognition. This approach was chosen because it makes it possible to explore, from the data themselves, how social representations and interactions shape the social experience of chronic pain, and to develop a theoretical model grounded in the experience of those concerned, one that can then be brought into dialogue with concepts and findings from the scientific literature. Data were collected through individual semi-structured interviews, using a guide that was stabilised after approximately the first ten interviews. Although early memos accompanied data collection, formal coding began once all interviews were complete. The study was therefore informed by constructivist grounded theory, rather than designed as a full application of it: its constructivist epistemology and analytic procedures, described in the Data analysis section, were retained, whereas the fully iterative and concurrent logic of theoretical sampling (i.e., directing data collection according to emerging theoretical categories) was not applied. Consistent with contemporary constructivist grounded theory guidance (Charmaz and Thornberg, 2021), the study retained an emphasis on reflexivity, transparency, and an open, non-deductive engagement with the existing literature.

Researcher reflexivity

Individual interviews were conducted by the lead investigator, YS, and by AC, with YS conducting the majority of them. YS is a man, a physiotherapist and a doctoral researcher in cognitive neuroscience, with ten years of clinical experience in physiotherapy and prior academic experience in research on chronic pain and social cognition. During recruitment and at the beginning of each interview, he introduced himself to participants as a doctoral researcher in cognitive neuroscience, rather than in a clinical capacity, and explained the general topic of the study, which concerned representations and interactions around chronic pain. AC is a woman, a physiotherapist and a second-year Master's student in rehabilitation research, with prior clinical experience in physiotherapy. During recruitment and at the beginning of each interview, she introduced herself to participants as a second-year Master's student conducting research in the field of rehabilitation. No prior relationship existed between the investigators and the participants before the first contact made to arrange the interview. In line with Charmaz and Thornberg's requirement for "methodological self-consciousness" (Charmaz 5 . and Thornberg, 2021), which involves making one's assumptions explicit rather than setting them aside, the investigators' clinical background as physiotherapists was considered both a resource and a potential source of bias. This background gave them familiarity with people living with chronic pain and with how chronic pain is typically framed in rehabilitation settings, which likely facilitated rapport during interviews and helped them follow the healthcare contexts participants described. It also risked steering interpretation toward clinically familiar categories, such as biomedical explanations of pain or standard descriptions of fear-avoidance, rather than the framings participants used themselves. YS's prior academic work on chronic pain and social cognition added a further layer of vigilance, as this background may have sensitised the analysis to certain dimensions of the material, including representations, social judgments and interactions. Initial coding of a subsample of 12 participants was also conducted independently by DM, a woman, a general practitioner and a second-year Master's student in disability and cognition, before being compared with the initial coding produced by YS on the same subsample. This comparison provided an external perspective on whether the emerging codes reflected participants' accounts rather than the lead investigator's prior clinical or theoretical assumptions. Theoretical coding leading to the identification of the central process was then developed independently by YS, JBVH and EC, before their respective analyses were compared and discussed in order to reach a shared central process. EC is a man, an associate professor and a psychologist. JBVH is a man, a CNRS research director in social cognition.

The diversity of these clinical, methodological and theoretical backgrounds strengthened the grounding of the interpretation in participants' accounts by bringing the lead investigator's reading into discussion with independent perspectives.

Study participants

Participants were recruited from several Pain Evaluation and Treatment Centres (Centres d'Évaluation et de Traitement de la Douleur, CETD) in the Auvergne-Rhône-Alpes region, France. The centres are not named in order to preserve the anonymity of the recruitment context. These centres correspond to a tertiary level of care for chronic pain, generally reached after a prolonged care pathway. This setting was chosen to recruit participants with substantial first-hand experience of living with chronic spinal pain and of navigating the healthcare system in relation to it.

Eligible participants were adults aged 18 years or older with a diagnosis of chronic spinal pain, either chronic non-specific LBP or chronic neck pain, persisting for more than three months despite treatment (Haute Autorité de Santé, 2019), with or without peripheral radiation. Patients were not included if their spinal pain was attributable to a tumoural or infectious cause, or to vertebral fracture or dislocation. They were also not included when unexplored or newly occurring warning signs suggested an underlying pathology requiring specific or urgent care, such as unexplained weight loss, a history of cancer associated with fever, thoracic pain, fever, or deterioration in general health status. These criteria were based on a subset of the red flags described by the Haute Autorité de Santé (HAS) for the assessment of common LBP (Haute Autorité de Santé, 2019), transposed to chronic neck pain. Radiating pain, neuropathic components, or known neurological symptoms were not exclusion criteria when they had already been investigated, monitored, or managed by the pain physician. In all cases, participation depended on prior medical screening performed by the pain physician managing the patient, on the basis of the medical diagnosis established during a scheduled consultation.

Sampling

A purposive sampling strategy was used, combining an a priori target sample size with an internal diversification strategy intended to capture a diversity of experiences within a homogeneous population.

The target sample size of 30 participants was set before data collection began. Marshall et al. recommend that grounded theory studies generally include between 20 and 30 interviews, and report that, in their review, studies exceeding this range do not show evidence of greater scholarly impact (Marshall et al., 2013). This sample size was also considered consistent with the principle of information power, according to which sample adequacy depends on the aim of the study, the specificity of the participants, the use of existing theoretical frameworks, the quality of the dialogue, and the analytic strategy (Malterud et al., 2016). It also seemed ethically preferable not to continue recruitment beyond a number of participants likely to provide sufficient information in relation to the study aim. Because data collection and analysis were conducted sequentially (see Design), saturation was not assessed iteratively during recruitment. The sample size of 30 participants was therefore set in advance as a criterion of sample adequacy, rather than as the result of theoretical saturation assessed during the course of analysis.

The homogeneity criterion defining the study population was the presence of chronic spinal pain, including chronic non-specific LBP and chronic neck pain. Internal diversification aimed, as far as possible, to include a range of participants within this population, irrespective of their statistical frequency in the clinical population. The dimensions sought included pain location (low back or neck pain), pain duration, age, gender, socio-economic status, and area of residence. This strategy aimed to enrich the range of experiences collected and to support comparison of internal variations within the phenomenon under study, namely chronic spinal pain (Malterud et al., 2016). Chronic LBP and chronic neck pain were therefore treated as variations of the same phenomenon, examined through the same analytic framework, rather than as two populations requiring separate explanatory frameworks.

Recruitment

Recruitment and inclusion of participants took place between March 2024 and February 2025.

Potential participants were identified by physicians from the CETD during scheduled consultations, based on the medical diagnosis and the eligibility criteria described above. Eligible patients received oral information and a written information sheet from their physician. Patients who agreed to be contacted were then approached by the lead investigator (YS) or by the investigator (AC), by telephone or email, to arrange an interview. Recruitment was also supported by information posters displayed in the waiting rooms of the CETD. All patients who expressed interest in the study and were contacted by the investigators took part in the semi-structured interview planned as part of the study. Participants did not receive any compensation for their participation.

At the beginning of each interview, information about the study and the interview procedure was repeated to the participant. Participants were able to ask any questions about the study before the interview began. Before recording, they also signed a form authorising the recording and processing of their voice. Each participant was reminded that they could take a break during the interview, decline to answer a question, choose not to discuss a topic, stop the interview, or withdraw their consent to participate and to the use of their data at any time, including after the interview had ended. Participants could also request that specific passages from the interview be removed before data analysis.

Data collection

Data were collected through individual semi-structured interviews conducted by YS and AC.

Each participant took part in a single interview session, with an indicative duration of approximately one hour. Interviews took place either in a dedicated room at the CRNL (Lyon Neuroscience Research Centre, Bron) or on the premises of the participating CETD. A secure videoconferencing option (BigBlueButton) was available at the participant's request and in accordance with lockdown requirements in cases of suspected or confirmed COVID-19 infection, but no interviews were ultimately conducted by videoconference. No person other than the participant and the investigator was present during the interviews, and no repeat interviews were conducted.

At the beginning of each interview, the investigator explained the general aim of the study, stated that there were no right or wrong answers, and reminded the participant that they were free not to answer a question or to return to it later. Before recording began, the participant's agreement was confirmed and they were informed that the recording was starting. Interviews were recorded using a Philips VoiceTracer digital recorder. A pseudonymisation code, generated for each participant before the interview, was read aloud at the beginning of the audio recording.

The semi-structured interview guide was developed in collaboration with researchers in 8 sociology and anthropology (see Acknowledgements) and is provided as supplementary material. In keeping with the inductive nature of the study, the initial version of the guide included a dedicated theme on the history and subjective experience of pain, and explored the participant's healthcare pathway through two separate questions, one addressing diagnosis and consultations, the other addressing the experience of being accompanied by healthcare professionals and by personal or professional networks. Following a preliminary review of the material collected from approximately the first ten interviews, conducted independently of formal coding (see Design), the guide was revised. The theme addressing the history and subjective experience of pain was no longer retained as a dedicated theme, in order to refocus the interview on social representations and interactions around chronic pain rather than on the phenomenological experience of pain itself. The two questions on the healthcare pathway were merged into a single question. A new question exploring the participant's sense of proximity or belonging with other people living with chronic pain was also added. The remaining domains of the interview guide were unchanged. This revised version was used for the majority of interviews. The final version of the interview guide is provided as supplementary material 1 on Zenodo (https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.23963293) (in French, the language in which interviews were conducted).

The guide began with questions about the participant's biography and, for the majority of interviews, the place of pain in their daily life, in order to better understand their context and create a climate of trust before addressing the central topics of the study. Interviews then explored the participant's representations of chronic pain and of people living with it, the representations they attributed to healthcare professionals and to their personal and professional networks, and the relational and bodily consequences of these representations for their daily life, their pain, and their healthcare pathway. The guide also addressed the participant's relationship to other people living with chronic pain, including any sense of proximity, belonging, or mutual recognition. Demographic information, including age, gender, family situation, socio-economic status, education level, area of residence, mode of transport, lifestyle and leisure activities, frequency of healthcare consultations, pain duration, and current treatments, was collected at the end of the interview when it had not already been discussed. This choice aimed to avoid beginning the interview with a series of closed questions that could have elicited socially expected answers or conditioned the rest of the exchange.

Once the interview was completed, the participant was asked whether they wished to add anything they considered important for this research before the recording was stopped.

After the recording had been stopped, time was set aside for debriefing with the participant.

Interviews were audio-recorded and then transcribed verbatim for analysis. Transcripts were not returned to participants for review or correction. This choice was made for ethical reasons: most participants did not have access to a secure email account through which transcripts could have been transmitted safely, and several participants experienced mobility difficulties related to their chronic pain, with some relying on medically equipped transport services that 9.

could not be arranged for an additional visit to the research sites. Requiring participants to travel again for this purpose was considered to impose an unjustified burden.

Data analysis

Interview transcription was carried out by YS, AC and DM. Once transcription was completed, the audio recordings were destroyed, in accordance with what had been agreed with the Data Protection Officer (DPO) during the ethical assessment of the study, in order to protect participants' pseudonymity as far as possible and to minimise the existence of identifiable personal data. The transcribed verbatim accounts were managed and coded using NVivo software (QSR International), release 1.7.2 (1560). The final codebook, including category definitions, hierarchical structure, and the number of coded interviews and segments per code, is provided as supplementary material 2 on Zenodo (https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.23963293).

The analysis followed the principles of constructivist grounded theory as described by Charmaz (2017). Data were analysed through initial coding, begun independently by DM and YS on approximately twelve interviews, then continued by YS alone after comparison of the emerging codes. During this phase, segments of the transcripts were named using active, process-oriented language. Several phases of revision took place after the analysis of 3, 7, 18, 25 and then 30 interviews. Codes were merged when they catalogued similar phenomena, deleted and redistributed to other codes when they were not significant in the corpus, or refined in their wording and definition.

Focused coding began halfway through the initial coding of the interviews and was conducted by YS alone. During this stage, the most significant codes were systematically compared, refined and developed into analytic categories. This step made it possible to structure a hierarchy between categories, codes and subcodes. Memo-writing accompanied the whole process of initial and focused coding.

Once initial and focused coding of the 30 interviews had been completed, theoretical coding began, with the aim of identifying a central process accounting for the relationships between categories. This stage was conducted independently by YS, JBVH and EC, each developing their own central process. The three analyses were then compared during joint meetings, in order to discuss their similarities and differences, until a single central process was agreed by the three authors and presented in Results.

Although sensitising concepts from the literature on social cognition and chronic pain informed the interpretive lens brought to the data, the categories were developed from participants' accounts and remained grounded in them. In line with Charmaz and Thornberg's recommendation for an open and non-deductive engagement with the existing literature (Charmaz and Thornberg, 2021), this literature was used as a sensitising resource rather than as a predetermined framework to be confirmed.

Results

Sample characteristics

Thirty participants were included in the study. The mean age was 49.9 years (SD = 18.0, range 18-77), with 16 women (53.3%), 10 men (33.3%), and 4 non-binary individuals (13.3%). Pain was located in the lumbar region for 16 participants (53.3%), in the cervical region for 10 participants (33.3%), and in both regions for 4 participants (13.3%). The median pain duration was 8 years (range 0.5 to ≥20). For nine participants (30.0%), pain duration exceeded 20 years but could not be determined more precisely; for descriptive purposes, these cases were recorded as ≥20 years. Category 1: Social representations and relationship to stereotypes of chronic pain

This category brings together the ways participants described chronic pain and people living with it. Their accounts included bodily, behavioural and moral stereotypes, but also judgments about themselves, about other people with chronic pain, and about people without chronic pain. These representations mattered because they shaped how pain was evaluated in social situations and how interactions unfolded. Participants did not simply describe these representations; they also positioned themselves in relation to them, by contesting, transforming or internalising them in light of their own experience. One prominent pattern concerned how participants' own experience of chronic pain related to the representations they held of other people living with the condition. The corpus revealed a clear divergence rather than a single pattern. For approximately half of the participants, lived experience left prior representations of other pain sufferers unchanged, leading them to validate stereotypes shared with the general population: "There are people who stop paying attention to their appearance, who, because of the pain, only wear what they call comfortable clothes, even though you can still look presentable, even quite stylish, and be comfortable at the same time.

And then there is a kind of letting go, sometimes blaming the physiotherapist, saying, 'He's not capable because he can't relieve my pain, ' or the doctor, 'That one is useless.

Category 2: Relational and institutional dynamics of chronic pain

The social environment, from immediate interactions to organisational structures, shaped not only the recognition and legitimacy of chronic pain in social situations, but also participants' understanding of their own experience and of their place within these interactions. These mechanisms took several forms, including interactional stereotypes, relational asymmetries, normative or institutional constraints, and supportive resources. These dynamics mattered because they affected whether participants felt believed, socially included, and able to make decisions about their own lives. One of the most consistently documented mechanisms was the delegitimation of participants' accounts of their own pain, through psychologisation, suspicion of exaggeration, or explicit doubt. This differed from the more automatic effects of stereotyping, in which assumptions about what a person in pain should look like could shape an interaction without either party's awareness. Delegitimation involved a more explicit questioning of what participants described, including whether their pain was real, how severe it was, or how it should be understood. For some participants, this had consequences that extended over years: "...

Category 3: Threat to the social self and to agency

Where the social environment shaped the recognition and legitimacy of chronic pain from the outside, its consequences unfolded within the person. Chronic pain progressively weakened the social self through the internalisation of others' real or anticipated gaze, the incorporation of pain into personal identity, the perception of oneself as a social burden, and the contraction of the relational world. These processes were endured rather than chosen, cumulatively altering participants' perceived place, legitimacy, and sense of control over their own trajectory.

Threats to three fundamental social needs, namely autonomy, belonging, and recognition, were documented across almost all thirty interviews. The most consistent finding was an alteration of self-image experienced as a break with a prior self. For several participants, chronic pain had not simply added a constraint to an otherwise continuous identity; it had produced a different person. For many participants, this biographical rupture was accompanied by a parallel threat to autonomy, extending beyond functional limitation to affect the person's sense of ownership over their own inner experience. One participant described how his colleagues' repeated warnings about his pain began to shape his attention to his own bodily sensations: "So I pay more attention to it [the perception of pain] afterwards.

Maybe they're right, in the sense that I should be more aware of it in everyday life to avoid situations that cause pain. But because, for now, it doesn't come from me, I experience it as a kind of loss of ownership over my own subjectivity. " Non binary person, 20-29yrs, Low back pain, 2-5yrs

This account illustrates a threat to autonomy that extended beyond the capacity to act, affecting the participant's sense of authority over their own experience. The remaining dimensions of this category, including perception of oneself as a burden, contraction of the relational world, and threats to belonging and recognition, are detailed in the codebook in supplementary materials.

Category 4: Deliberate strategies for restoring agency

Where the preceding categories documented processes that were endured rather than directed, this category captured a qualitatively different register: the deliberate behavioural responses through which participants worked to restore their sense of agency and reconstitute a viable self. These responses operated across several domains. They ranged from sustained interoceptive monitoring and the careful calibration of physical effort to the strategic management of pain's social presentation through decisions about when, with whom, and in what terms to disclose or conceal its existence. Participants also described asserting their own perspective against social and medical assignations, selectively affiliating with peers as sources of practical learning and recognition, and reorganising roles and commitments to preserve participation despite constraints. What distinguished these processes from coping in the conventional sense was not their variety but their orientation: they targeted the restoration of perceived control over one's own experience and one's place in the social world, not the reduction of pain itself.

A recurrent strategy involved an active monitoring of bodily states, oriented towards functional control rather than pain relief. Participants described building, over time, a working expertise in reading their own signals: "You have to sort through the information that comes in, because when you live with chronic pain, you pay a lot of attention to your body.

When I walk, am I going to be in pain? It's like a sword of Damocles hanging over your head. The kind of pain I have gives no warning. It can start at any moment, in any situation. So you're always on alert, knowing that it could start. " Man, 60-69yrs, Low back pain, ≥ 20yrs For several participants, monitoring increasingly involved anticipation. One participant illustrated this shift by describing a new pattern in her own thinking: before any physical movement, she would first ask herself whether she could do it, something she had not done before the onset of chronic pain. Participants therefore appeared to evaluate their bodily capacities before acting, based on what they had learned from previous experiences of pain. Another dimension concerned the deliberate management of pain's social visibility. Participants made context-dependent decisions about what to reveal and what to conceal, to whom, and when. As one participant explained: "In general, I think people with chronic pain, you can't see it on our faces. Even if sometimes I have to make it more obvious for certain doctors, it isn't written on my face that I'm in pain everywhere. I'm not going to say every few seconds, Ah, I'm in pain, just to remind people. At some point, you learn to mask. People with pain mask, just like other people do. We try to be perceived well because we want to fit in. We don't want to be isolated. You're asking me about it now, so I'm telling you. But I'm not going to explain to my baker that I'm in pain everywhere. " Man, 30-39yrs, Neck pain, 10-15yrs

Concealment was only one aspect of this process. When disclosure was judged strategically useful, to negotiate workplace adjustments, to manage others' expectations, or to establish the terms of a relationship, participants actively chose to make their pain visible on their own terms. Rather than representing opposite behaviours, concealment and disclosure formed complementary strategies through which participants sought to maintain control over their social participation. Against such social assignations, several participants described active selfassertion that went beyond information management. One participant described this effort: "I feel like the role I play in how people see this is to show that it happens, that it is possible to be young, active, athletic, to do physical work, and still have back pain. In doing so, participants challenged prevailing stereotypes while reclaiming a social identity threatened by chronic pain. Role reorganisation emerged as another important regulatory process. Participants described making deliberate choices about which activities, relationships and engagements to prioritise and which to reduce or relinquish, not as passive withdrawal but as a means of preserving a coherent sense of participation. For some, this reorganisation extended to a deeper revision of values and priorities, reshaping what it meant to occupy one's roles as a worker, a parent, or a partner in ways that remained liveable. Finally, deliberate affiliation with peers who shared similar experiences differed from more general forms of social support. Participants described feeling understood without having to justify their pain, and sometimes used others' experiences as a source of vicarious learning to adjust how they managed their own situation: "There was one person in particular who also had a lot of pain and who told me, 'I've learned to manage it. ' I tried to draw inspiration from her. It brought me relief, first of all emotionally. I'm not saying that my chronic pain decreased, but it became more bearable. " Woman, 70-79yrs, Neck and Low back pain, < 2yrs

These interactions also reshaped participants' understanding of chronic pain and those living with it, sometimes challenging previously held stereotypes and prototypes. Emotional regulation cut across each of these processes and is detailed in the codebook in supplementary materials.

Category 5: Embodied, cognitive and perceptual experience of chronic pain

This category specifies how the social processes described above also shaped the embodied experience of pain itself. It documents how participants attended to bodily signals, made sense of fluctuations, and tried to make an invisible experience communicable to others. Across participants' accounts, chronic pain emerged as a deeply personal perceptual experience that resisted external quantification, saturated cognitive resources, and required considerable interpretative work to become communicable to others. Unlike the preceding categories, which traced how social interactions shaped participants' sense of self from the outside, this category documented what that shaping produced within participants' own perceptual and cognitive experience, and how that experience in turn altered their engagement with the social world.

A recurrent finding concerned the fundamental incommensurability of chronic pain as a subjective experience. Participants consistently described a gap between externally observable markers and the intensity of what they experienced, making pain difficult to compare across people and difficult for others to fully grasp. One participant put this directly: "Pain can be on a different scale for each person, depending on what they feel.

Sometimes that isn't taken into account enough. You can say, for example, 'Today my pain is seven out of ten, ' but my seven might be like twenty-eight for someone else. That's an exaggerated example, but I think this is something people don't always understand, including healthcare professionals, family, and people in everyday life. " Man, 20-29yrs, Low back pain, 5-10yrs

A second finding concerned the cognitive and affective weight of chronic pain. Participants described pain as persistently demanding attention and leaving less room for work, relationships and everyday activities. They also described a reciprocal relationship between pain and emotional states. Negative social interactions, including those described in Category 2, could increase stress and anxiety, which participants associated with greater pain. One participant described this directly: "Emotions really matter when it comes to managing pain. And when you keep taking hits all the time, of course it makes things worse. It increases stress, then anxiety, and that creates really unpleasant cycles where, in the end, it hurts even more. " Woman, 20-29yrs, Neck pain, 5-10yrs

Several participants also reported the converse: sustained engagement in an absorbing activity could temporarily attenuate perceived pain, only for it to return once the engagement ended.

Such bidirectional dynamics between attentional allocation and pain modulation appeared across multiple accounts, pointing to a coupling between cognitive and perceptual processes that the social environment could activate in either direction. Present in a smaller part of the corpus, a further aspect concerned the cognitive work participants undertook to make chronic pain communicable. When direct description fell short, participants constructed metaphors 20 to capture aspects of their experience that were otherwise difficult to put into words. One participant described the intensity of his pain on waking by telling his doctors that, every morning, he "wakes up and looks for the debris of the house that fell on him during the night, as if someone had picked him up and thrown him to the ground". Another described the intimacy of the experience: pain, he said, "is like a toothbrush, you don't lend it to anyone. It's something very intimate". These metaphors helped participants convey private perceptual experiences in terms that others could partially grasp. The limits of this communication connect with the asymmetries of understanding and delegitimation described in Category 2. The remaining dimensions of this category, including the progressive alteration of physical and cognitive capacity, the interplay between pain and sleep disturbance, and the existential weight of pain without prospect of resolution, are detailed in the codebook in supplementary materials.

Taken together, these categories describe more than a set of social experiences surrounding chronic pain. They show how chronic pain becomes socially interpreted, interactionally tested, embodied in perceptual and cognitive experience, and actively renegotiated through strategies aimed at preserving agency and social identity.

Central process: Maintaining and rebuilding the social self

The analysis revealed a central process through which living with chronic spinal pain becomes organised around a continuous negotiation of the social self. This process, which we name maintaining and rebuilding the social self, describes how the social environment shapes, over time, the cognitive and perceptual processes through which chronic pain is lived, interpreted and acted upon. Figure 2 provides an overview of the dynamic relationships between the components of this process.

The central process unfolds across three analytically distinct components. Social representations and interpersonal interactions (Categories 1 and 2) create the conditions under which the social self becomes threatened. These repercussions (Categories 3 and 5), in turn, call for adaptive responses through which participants attempt to restore agency (Category 4).

As participants' experiences evolve, changes occurring in one component also reshape earlier representations and future interactions. Social support operates across these components rather than as a distinct stage of the process. any direct interaction took place. Prior expectations about how others would respond were also reflected in how participants attended to and interpreted their own bodily experience, shaping the cognitive context in which pain signals were processed and given meaning (see figure 2, arrow from Category 1 to Category 5: shaping pain appraisal).

These representational frameworks became particularly visible through concrete interactions. Whether in clinical encounters, workplaces or close relationships, what was at stake was the recognition of the person's pain and, by extension, of the person themselves as a credible actor in their own experience. Whether deliberate or more automatic, delegitimation extended beyond questioning pain itself to challenging the standing of the person reporting it.

The passively undergone consequences were an alteration of self-image: a biographical rupture, a sense that one's own perceptual experience had become subject to others' authority, a diminished sense of social legitimacy and control. These experiences also shaped how participants approached subsequent interactions, often expecting misunderstanding, disbelief or the need to justify themselves before being recognised as legitimate. Social support ran across all stages of this process rather than constituting a distinct phase (see figure 2, social support as a transversal resource). When sustained and well-calibrated, it could buffer this erosion. When it receded over time or tipped into paternalism, it reproduced, in a more benevolent register, the same threat to autonomy that delegitimation posed more overtly.

Participants did not remain passive, however. They reengaged the negotiation from their own side. The deliberate strategies they deployed, interoceptive monitoring, the management of pain's social visibility, self-assertion against medical and social assignations, and selective affiliation with peers, were oriented not towards pain relief but towards recovering perceived control over their experience and reclaiming a social identity that chronic pain had placed in question. Peer contact carried a particular consequence: it could revise the very representational frameworks that had framed the process from the outset, closing a feedback loop between adaptive responses and cognitive expectations (see figure 2, arrow from Category 4 to Category 1:transforms and contests representations). The form and intensity of these strategies varied with contextual factors such as professional status, gender, pain duration and area of residence, which appeared to shape how these processes were experienced, particularly the social pressures participants faced and the resources available to them.

Running through all of this was an experiential dimension that the preceding account risks obscuring. Pain occupied attentional resources, its perceived intensity shaped by emotional states and social interactions alike. The cognitive and perceptual experience of chronic pain was shaped by social interactions and expectations, and in turn influenced how participants interpreted and responded to subsequent situations. What participants had come to expect shaped what they noticed in their bodies. When interactions threatened their standing, the affective weight of pain signals shifted (see figure 2, arrow from Category 5 to Category 3: negative affect and pain amplification). And as strategies took hold, how participants read both their own experience and the people around them gradually changed. The social self that par-23 ticipants worked to maintain and rebuild was at once biographical, relational and embodied.

Discussion

Thirty people living with chronic spinal pain described an experience that extended beyond pain intensity or bodily limitation, and was shaped by the social contexts in which pain had to be recognised and managed. Using an analysis informed by constructivist grounded theory, the analysis led to a model in which social representations, relational dynamics, and cognitive and perceptual processes were closely connected. Together, these dimensions formed a continuous process through which the social self was maintained and rebuilt. By social self we mean the representation of oneself as constructed and continuously negotiated through social interactions (Karos et al., 2018; Markus and Wurf, 1987), distinct from agency (Bandura, 2001), which refers to the perceived capacity to act upon one's situation. Social experiences reshaped cognitive interpretations, behavioural adjustments, and subsequent expectations over time.

One of the most unexpected findings concerned what happened within the group of people living with chronic pain. Approximately half the participants reproduced, without apparent revision, representations of chronic pain that one might expect to find in the general population. Judgements about simulation, lack of willpower, or inadequate effort were directed at other people with chronic pain. The literature on stigma in chronic pain has focused primarily on interactions between patients and healthcare professionals (Perugino et al., 2022; De Ruddere et al., 2013; De Ruddere and Craig, 2016), and this focus has left largely unexamined what happens within the group. People living with chronic pain can themselves reproduce representations that threaten recognition of chronic pain. Pryma (2017) has described something analogous in the context of contested illness: the implicit moral community that distinguishes legitimate sufferers from malingerers does not operate only from outside. Those inside the community apply it too, often against each other.

Participants also associated legitimacy with specific characteristics. These characteristics can be considered in relation to the dimensions of warmth and competence described in the Stereotype Content Model. Groups associated with disability are commonly positioned within the pity quadrant, characterised by relatively high warmth but low perceived competence (Fiske, 2018). The intragroup prototype in our data only partially matched this pattern.

The person with chronic pain was described as organised and capable, with an emphasis on continuing to function despite pain. Restraint in expressing pain was also part of this image and may reflect broader cultural stereotypes of stoicism, which coexist with stereotypes of exaggeration and weakness (Zajacova et al., 2021). Participants appeared to associate legitimacy with competence and restraint, using these criteria to distinguish between people they regarded as truly in pain and those they perceived as exaggerating. Froud et al. (2014) described a related tension: continuing to meet social and occupational expectations despite pain could demonstrate competence. At the same time, this could make the pain itself less credible to others.

A second contribution concerns the recurrent burden of legitimising pain across interpersonal, organisational and institutional settings. Participants described having to make their pain credible not only in healthcare encounters, but also in relationships with relatives, colleagues and supervisors, and within workplace, welfare and administrative systems. At times, the reality or severity of pain was questioned explicitly. At others, social representations and social categorisation processes shaped interactions without deliberate intent. In both cases, participants were repeatedly required to explain, demonstrate or justify limitations that were not readily visible. Chronic pain had to be managed while also being continually rendered socially credible. This recurrent demand for proof had consequences across multiple dimensions of participants' experience. It contributed to cognitive and emotional overload, complicated decisions about when and how to communicate pain, and intensified threats to the social self.

This pattern is consistent with the account of pain as a threat to social needs proposed by Karos et al. (2018), particularly recognition, belonging and autonomy. While some episodes involved deliberate questioning of pain's reality, others reflected automatic stereotype activation operating without conscious intent. This distinction has direct implications for how rehabilitation training addresses clinician bias (Amodio, 2014).

Social support appeared as a context-dependent and continually negotiated influence rather than a stable resource. Several participants described how those around them gradually stopped accounting for the persistence of their pain, not because it had resolved, but because its continuity had become, for others, unremarkable. Depending on its form and timing, it could buffer threats to the social self or facilitate the restoration of agency. When support took paternalistic forms, it could also reduce participants' perceived autonomy, although through a different relational process from explicit delegitimation.

One implication of the proposed model is that social representations appear to influence behaviour through the cognitive and perceptual processing of social situations. These interpretations shaped how participants evaluated their own possibilities for action and oriented their efforts to restore the social self. Over time, these efforts and their consequences reshaped future expectations and social representations. Peer contact, in particular, could revise the very representations that had structured the process from the outset, closing a feedback loop and supporting a recursive rather than sequential model. For rehabilitation practice, this suggests that interventions targeting peer learning and social affiliation may not simply provide emotional support, but may act on the representational frameworks through which people with chronic pain interpret both their own experience and that of others. More broadly, the social interactions documented here appear to constitute an important context in which the sources of self-efficacy (Bandura, 1997) are produced, sustained or depleted, an observation that may guide both assessment and intervention in chronic spinal pain rehabilitation.

The present findings also extend the motivational account of pain as a threat to the social self proposed by Karos et al. (2018), by showing empirically how these threats arise and accumulate across everyday social contexts. Rather than representing a simple consequence of chronic pain, maintaining and rebuilding the social self emerges as an ongoing cognitive and relational process through which people continually negotiate recognition, legitimacy and agency. The proposed model also has implications for how chronic pain is recognised and legitimised within clinical practice. Vollert et al. (2026) argue that no biological measure can prove or disprove another person's pain, and that self-report remains the only indispensable element of clinical assessment. Woo (2026) extends this position by noting that, for many people living with chronic pain, the central problem is not excessive reliance on measurement but persistent failures of recognition, as stereotypes, stigma, and unequal distributions of clinical authority shape whose suffering is acknowledged and whose is not. Coninx and Stilwell (2021) reach a compatible conclusion from a different starting point, arguing that because pain is irreducibly subjective, the social conditions under which it is communicated are not peripheral to clinical assessment but central to it. The present findings provide an experiential account of what this means in practice. Participants described how the absence of a visible biological explanation created a recurring space for doubt, not only in clinical encounters but across workplaces, administrative systems, and close relationships. In each context, uncertainty about pain could become uncertainty about the person reporting it, calling into question the legitimacy of their experience and, consequently, the need for recognition and accommodation. Several participants described anticipating disbelief before seeking care, and at times choosing not to express the full extent of their pain as a result. These accounts show how an epistemological problem, the irreducibility of subjective pain experience to any external criterion, becomes an everyday burden through which the social self is continually negotiated.

Limitations

Initial and focused coding was carried out primarily by a single researcher, with a second coder involved only for an initial subsample. Although this is consistent with constructivist grounded theory principles, which do not require coding consensus as a marker of objectivity (Charmaz and Thornberg, 2021), it limits the extent to which analytic decisions could be independently traced across the full corpus.

Theoretical saturation was not formally demonstrated through iterative sampling. Data collection and analysis were conducted sequentially, and the sample size was defined in advance (Malterud et al., 2016), but this should not be interpreted as evidence of theoretical saturation in the strict grounded theory sense.

Participants were recruited through tertiary pain assessment and treatment centres. Their accounts may therefore reflect the experiences of people with long, complex healthcare trajectories and repeated encounters with clinical and institutional systems. The model may be less directly transferable to people with chronic spinal pain who are managed in primary care, who do not seek care, or whose pain has not led to prolonged institutional contact.

The study was conducted in France, within a specific healthcare and social protection context. Similar social and relational processes may arise in other settings where the legitimacy of chronic pain is contested, but the forms they take are likely to depend on local institutional and cultural conditions.

Conclusion

Chronic spinal pain is experienced within social contexts in which it may need to be explained, justified, and defended. The model proposed here describes how this process shapes cognitive expectations, threatens the social self, and leads people to develop strategies for preserving agency and social identity. The contribution of this study is to show how social representations, interactions, and responses become linked over time in the ongoing negotiation of pain legitimacy.

Recognising these findings may help rehabilitation professionals to understand how people continually negotiate the credibility of their pain and, through this, maintain and rebuild the social self. Future longitudinal and mixed-methods studies should examine how these processes evolve over time and whether interventions targeting social interactions, peer learning, and pain legitimacy can improve rehabilitation outcomes.
