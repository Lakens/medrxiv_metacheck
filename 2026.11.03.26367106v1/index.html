Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Metacheck does not yet analyze automatically whether sentences which include non-significant p-values are correct, but we recommend manually checking the sentences below for possible misinterpreted non-significant p values.

Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). Statcheck might still miss one-sided tests that are described in another sentence, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

If you want to extend the package to be able to download files from additional data repositories reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Background: Malignant effusions are clinically accessible yet underutilized sources of metastatic tumor cells.

However, abundant background immune cells complicate tumor cell isolation and molecular characterization.

We integrate AI-powered single-cell imaging and morphology-guided label-free tumor cell enrichment with genomic profiling to characterize phenotypic and genomic heterogeneity in malignant effusions.

We applied a pre-trained deep learning model to brightfield images of single cells from malignant effusions of 10 patients with metastatic breast cancer to extract morphological features, quantify tumor cell fraction (TCF), and guide label-free microfluidic enrichment. We profiled copy-number alterations and somatic mutations in enriched tumor cell fractions and compared them with available matched effusion cell-free DNA and solid tumors.

Results: AI-powered single-cell morphological analysis identified putative tumor cells in all 10 patients (median TCF, 5.4%; range, 0.51-15.8%; n = 25 samples). AI estimates of major immune-cell populations varied across patients and samples and correlated with flow cytometry measurements (n = 21 paired samples, Pearson r = 0.69-0.75). Unsupervised analysis identified four tumor cell morphotypes, revealing intra-and inter-patient morphological heterogeneity and longitudinal variation in morphotype composition. Genomic profiling of enriched tumor cell fractions by low-pass whole-genome sequencing (n = 26 samples) and wholeexome sequencing (n = 24 samples) identified copy-number alterations and somatic mutations, with tumor fraction influencing genomic signal strength. Comparisons of genomic profiles from tumor cell-enriched fractions with matched effusion cell-free DNA and solid tumors revealed shared genomic alterations, providing orthogonal support for tumor origin and relatedness, while differences across analytes revealed genomic divergence and tumor heterogeneity.

Conclusion: AI-powered analysis leverages single-cell morphology for tumor cell identification and label-free enrichment, while integration with genomic profiling enables simultaneous interrogation of phenotypic and genomic heterogeneity in malignant effusions. This approach expands the potential of malignant effusions for liquid biopsy applications and biomarker development and discovery.

INTRODUCTION

Malignant effusions are severe complications of advanced malignancy, characterized by fluid accumulation in the pleural cavity (pleural effusion) or the peritoneal cavity (ascites) 1, 2 . Pleural effusions most commonly occur in lung and breast cancers [2] [3] [4] , whereas malignant ascites are frequently associated with ovarian, gastrointestinal, and breast cancers 1, 5 . Drainage of pleural or ascitic fluid by thoracentesis and paracentesis, respectively, is routinely performed for diagnostic evaluation and to relieve symptoms associated with fluid accumulation 6, 7 . Although the initial fluid collection is commonly evaluated by cytopathology to establish a diagnosis of malignancy, large volumes of fluid obtained during subsequent therapeutic procedures are often discarded 8 . These repeatedly accessible specimens, which contain both intact metastatic tumor cells and cell-free DNA 9 , represent an underutilized source of tumor material for liquid biopsy testing.

While tissue biopsy of metastatic lesions remains the clinical standard for biomarker testing 10 , repeated sampling is often infeasible because of procedural risk and limited access to metastatic sites. These challenges have accelerated interest in liquid biopsy approaches that enable longitudinal characterization of the evolving genomic landscape of metastatic disease 11, 12 . Because drainage of fluids is clinically indicated, malignant effusions can serve as a source of metastatic cells for molecular profiling without requiring an additional invasive procedure 13 . However, the tumor cell fraction (TCF) in malignant effusions can be highly variable, with tumor cells comprising only a small fraction of the cellular population amid abundant immune and other nonmalignant cells 14, 15 . The complex cellular admixture poses a major obstacle to isolating and molecularly characterizing tumor cells in these fluids. Existing approaches for tumor cell identification and enrichment from malignant effusions have relied on predefined molecular markers, including epithelial markers such as EpCAM and cytokeratins [16] [17] [18] [19] , or physical characteristics such as cell size 18 . Although imaging-based approaches can also incorporate cellular morphology to identify tumor cells, these methods generally require fluorescent labeling and predefined molecular phenotypes 16, 17, 19 . Such strategies may bias recovery toward tumor cells that conform to these predefined characteristics, potentially limiting representation of phenotypically and genotypically heterogeneous tumor populations in malignant effusions.

Advances in high-dimensional imaging and artificial intelligence (AI) offer an opportunity to use intrinsic cellular morphology as a label-free phenotype for cell identification and isolation [20] [21] [22] . More recently, AI-powered morphology-based classification and enrichment have been applied to malignant effusions, demonstrating the feasibility of isolating tumor cells without relying on predefined molecular markers 23.

Whether such approaches can resolve morphological heterogeneity among tumor cells in malignant effusions and, importantly, whether morphologically identified tumor cells recapitulate the genomic features of the underlying malignancy remain incompletely understood. Establishing this connection between cellular phenotype and genomic identity would enable malignant effusions to be interrogated simultaneously as phenotypic and genomic representations of metastatic disease.

Here, we addressed these questions by applying AI-powered single-cell imaging and morphology analysis for label-free identification and enrichment of tumor cells (AI-identified TCs or AI-TCs) from malignant effusions. We evaluated AI-derived cell composition against conventional flow cytometry, identified phenotypically distinct tumor cell morphotypes through unsupervised analysis of image-derived features, and performed low-pass whole-genome and whole-exome sequencing of AI-TC-enriched fractions. By integrating morphological, copy-number, and mutational data with available matched solid tumor and effusion cell-free DNA profiles, including longitudinal samples, we investigated whether AI-TCs capture phenotypic heterogeneity and whether downstream genomic profiling provides orthogonal evidence of tumor origin, genomic relatedness, and divergence.

METHODS

Patients and samples

Patients. The study involved 10 patients with metastatic breast cancer and cytology-confirmed malignant pleural or ascitic fluid treated at the UCSF Comprehensive Cancer Center. Eligible participants enrolled in this non-therapeutic research study were identified by their primary breast oncologist and by the procedure team (UCSF Interventional Radiology Clinic) that performed the thoracentesis and paracentesis.

Upon written informed consent, the patients were enrolled in the UCSF IRB-approved tissue collection protocol "Collection of Tumor Tissue, Blood, and Clinical Data from Patients with Breast Cancer" (CC10753).

Sample and data collection.

We collected malignant effusion fluid for research after the initial visit, following diagnostic thoracentesis and/or paracentesis. Because these procedures are part of standard clinical care, and the drained fluid is typically discarded, participation in this study posed minimal additional risk to the participants. Serial malignant effusion fluid samples were collected when the participants returned for repeat malignant effusion drainage. Patient data (molecular and clinical) and research materials were de-identified and labeled with a unique study identification number.

Processing of malignant effusion samples.

Pleural and ascitic fluid specimens were processed to generate single-cell suspensions for cryopreservation and molecular analyses (Fig. 1). The overall workflow consisted of (A) initial fluid processing and isolation of cellular material, (B) red blood cell (RBC) lysis when needed, (C) enzymatic digestion of cellular aggregates, (D) cryopreservation of isolated cells, and (E) processing and storage of cell-free supernatant.

Initial fluid processing.

Pleural and ascitic fluid specimens, ranging from approximately 100 mL to several liters, were aliquoted into 50-mL conical tubes and centrifuged at 300 × g for 10 min at room temperature. The supernatant was retained and processed separately as described below.

Cell pellets were resuspended in 15 mL DMEM/F12 supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and mechanically dissociated by repeated pipetting. Cell suspensions were passed through a 50-µm cell strainer.

Material retained on the strainer was recovered by inverting the strainer onto a sterile Petri dish and flushing with 5 mL of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). Recovered cellular aggregates were transferred to a separate 50-mL tube and subjected to enzymatic digestion as described below.

The filtrate was centrifuged at 300 × g for 10 min at room temperature. Samples with visible RBC contamination were subjected to RBC lysis before further processing (see next step). Cell pellets without substantial RBC contamination were resuspended in 3-5 mL DMEM/F12 supplemented with 10% FBS and combined into a single 50-mL tube. After enzymatic digestion of cellular aggregates, the resulting single-cell suspension was combined with the corresponding filtered cell fraction. The combined suspension was subsequently centrifuged at 300 × g for 10 min at room temperature.

Red blood cell lysis.

For specimens with visible RBC contamination, RBC lysis was performed using RBC Lysis Buffer equilibrated to room temperature and protected from light. A 1× working solution was prepared by diluting 10× RBC Lysis Buffer 1:10 in deionized water. Cell pellets were resuspended in 10 mL of 1× RBC Lysis Buffer and incubated for 15 min at room temperature, protected from light. Samples were then centrifuged at 300 × g for 5 min. RBC lysis was repeated as necessary, for up to three cycles, until visible RBC contamination was eliminated. Following the final wash, cell pellets were resuspended in 10 mL DMEM/F12. Total cell number and cell viability were determined before cryopreservation.

Enzymatic digestion of cellular aggregates.

Cellular aggregates retained during filtration were enzymatically dissociated using collagenase. Lyophilized collagenase (50 mg) was reconstituted in 50 mL PBS containing 0.5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) to generate a 1 mg/mL stock solution. Reconstituted collagenase was aliquoted and stored at -20°C until use.

For digestion, 1 mL of the 1 mg/mL collagenase solution was added to the recovered cellular aggregates, and the total volume was adjusted to 10 mL with PBS. Samples were incubated at 37°C with agitation at 300 rpm and examined microscopically at approximately 10-min intervals. Digestion was terminated when a predominantly single-cell suspension was obtained, with digestion times of up to 90 min depending on the size and extent of the cellular aggregates.

Following digestion, samples were centrifuged at 300 × g for 10 min at room temperature. The supernatant was removed, and the cell pellet was resuspended in approximately 5 mL DMEM/F12 supplemented with FBS, with the volume adjusted as necessary according to pellet size. The resulting suspension was passed through a 50-µm cell strainer and combined with the corresponding cell fraction obtained during initial fluid processing.

Cell cryopreservation.

Single-cell suspensions were centrifuged at 300 × g for 5 min at room temperature, and the supernatant was carefully removed. Cell pellets were resuspended in chilled (2-8°C) CryoStor CS10 cryopreservation medium at a target concentration of 1-2 × 10^6 cells/mL. Aliquots of 1 mL were transferred to cryovials and incubated at 2-8°C for 10 min. Cells were cryopreserved using controlled-rate cooling at approximately -1°C/min or an isopropanolbased freezing container. Cryovials were subsequently transferred to liquid nitrogen storage (≤-135°C) the following day and maintained under cryogenic conditions until further analysis.

Processing of cell-free effusion supernatant.

Following the initial centrifugation of pleural or ascitic fluid, an aliquot of the recovered supernatant was reserved for cell-free DNA extraction and sequencing. The remaining supernatant was transferred to 50-mL conical tubes and centrifuged at 16,000 × g for 10 min at 4°C to remove residual cellular material and debris. The clarified supernatant was aliquoted into 4-mL cryovials and stored at -80°C until subsequent analyses.

Cell assays

Flow cytometry. The distributions of different cell populations in malignant effusions were estimated using standard flow cytometry procedures. Fluorescently labeled antibodies to CD45, CD68, EpCAM, and Claudin-4 were added to fixed single-cell suspension samples. Flow cytometry was performed on a BD FACSMelody machine using the following gating strategy to identify cell types: (1) leukocytes were identified by CD45 expression; (2) macrophages, a leukocyte subset, were identified by the co-expression of CD45 and CD68; and (3) putative tumor cells were identified by EpCAM and/or Claudin-4 24-26 expression and the lack of CD45 expression. The gating strategy enabled estimation of the TCF and the proportions of other cell types in each sample.

Cell imaging, classification (embedding) analysis, and cell sorting using the Deepcell platform.

The Deepcell instrument consists of hardware (microfluidic chip, high-resolution imager, and real-time signal processor), a deep learning convolutional neural network (malignant effusion AI model), and proprietary software for image analysis.

Cell imaging.

Single cells in suspension were hydrodynamically and inertially focused within a microfluidic cartridge and passed through the imaging region of the instrument. High-speed, high-resolution brightfield imaging was performed on individual cells in flow. The captured brightfield images were cropped around the cell (299 × 299 pixels), and the resized images were then fed in real time into a convolutional neural network, called the "malignant effusion AI model," to extract computer-vision-derived multidimensional morphological and deep-learning features for cell-type classification.

Imaging analysis and cell classification.

The convolutional neural network (malignant effusion AI model) was developed using a modified InceptionV3 architecture with 48 layers and approximately 24 million parameters. The model was trained on images of highly pure populations of breast tumor cells and immune cells, including leukocytes and macrophages. The training and test datasets consisted of ~2.6 million images and ~2.0 million images, respectively 23.

The convolutional neural network converted each cell image into an embedding vector that provides a quantitative representation of cellular morphology. The representation incorporated 51 human-interpretable morphometric features (e.g., cell size, shape, and texture) and 64 deep-learning-derived features that capture additional morphological patterns not readily interpretable by human observers 27 . After generating the embedding vector, the final softmax layer of the convolutional neural network produced class probabilities for each cell type (classification): tumor cells, leukocytes (non-macrophage), macrophages, and "other" cells. Each cell was assigned to the class corresponding to the highest predicted probability using the argmax function.

The TCF in each sample was calculated by dividing the number of cells predicted as tumor cells by the total number of cells with predictions. In a previous study, the model's classifier demonstrated excellent discrimination between breast carcinoma and non-carcinoma cells, with an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) of 0.96 23.

Cell sorting.

Each cell was assigned a class in real time as it traveled through the microfluidic cartridge.

The classification then triggered a decision that routed the cell to the appropriate collection reservoir. This was performed by the signal processor, which converted the classification into precisely timed hardware commands, ensuring the correct cell was sorted. If the classification probability, as determined by the softmax activation function, exceeded 0.85, pneumatic sorting valves were activated to direct the cell to a specific collection reservoir. Cells below the probability threshold or belonging to other classes were routed to the waste reservoir.

The sorted target cells were then retrieved by pipetting them from the collection reservoir and transferred to an Eppendorf tube for cell lysis and whole-genome amplification.

Data visualization.

The proprietary image-analysis software (Axon) enabled visualization of single-cell images and analysis of cell populations in each sample 28 . Cells were projected into two-dimensional morphology space using Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) 29 . Unsupervised Leiden clustering 30 was performed on the high-dimensional morphological representations to identify groups of cells with similar morphological phenotypes (morphotypes). Leiden cluster assignments were overlaid on the UMAP for visualization. Cell-density plots were generated in the common UMAP space to visualize regions preferentially occupied by cells from individual samples or cell populations.

Feature importance analysis.

Random forest classifiers were used to identify morphological and deeplearning-derived features associated with each morphotype cluster. A one-cluster-versus-all approach was used, in which cells assigned to each morphotype cluster were classified against cells from all other clusters. Features were ranked according to their importance scores, with higher scores indicating greater contribution to distinguishing a given morphotype cluster from the remaining clusters.

Imaging analysis and subsequent next-generation sequencing of AI-TC-enriched fractions (see next section) were performed as described below (Table S1, Table S2).

Cell viability.

For viability assessment, cells were stained with trypan blue, and viable cells were counted under a microscope. Cell viability was assessed in freshly prepared single-cell suspensions, cells thawed after cryopreservation, and cells sorted using the Deepcell instrument.

Sequencing of AI-identified tumor cells and normal control cells

Whole-genome amplification, library preparation, and sequencing.

Whole-genome amplification was performed using ResolveDNA WGA (BioSkryb Genomics) on genomic DNA released after cell lysis, according to the manufacturer's protocol.

Libraries for copy-number analysis were constructed from 500 ng of WGA product using the Watchmaker DNA Library Prep Kit with Fragmentation (Watchmaker Genomics). For low-pass whole-genome sequencing, the libraries were sequenced to <1X coverage on an Illumina NextSeq 1000 (Illumina) instrument with NextSeq1000/2000 P2 Reagents (300 Cycles) v3 (Illumina).

Low-pass whole-genome sequencing for copy-number analysis.

Copy-number alterations were analyzed from low-pass whole-genome sequencing data using ichorCNA software (bioconda r-ichorcna version 0.3.2) 31 . Briefly, the raw FASTQ files were aligned to the human reference genome, GRCh38.p13, using the BWA-MEM algorithm to generate BAM files 32 . Read-count coverage was calculated from BAM files using the readCounter command in HMMcopy with a bin size of 500 kb. The read counts were used as inputs to the ichorCNA software for copy-number analysis. Genome-wide copy-number profiles and segmented copynumber calls were generated using the optimal ichorCNA solution. Tumor fraction (TF) was estimated using ichorCNA lower-bound estimates set to 3% for visualization and calculation. Tumor-fraction estimates <3% were considered below the previously established lower limit for reliable tumor detection by ichorCNA 31.

Whole-exome sequencing (WES) of AI-TC-enriched and normal control cells.

Sequencing libraries for AI-TC-enriched (tumor) and normal control cells were prepared from 500 ng of WGA product using Watchmaker library preparation reagents. Normal control libraries were generated from leukocytes, except for patients ME003, ME007, and ME010, for whom macrophages served as normal controls. Leukocytes (CD45+) and macrophages (CD45+/CD68+) were isolated from the same sample by fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) performed in duplicate.

Exome capture was performed using 187.5 ng of library per sample with the Twist Human Core Exome Panel v2.1. The libraries were sequenced to a median target coverage of ~260x. Paired-end Illumina sequencing reads were aligned to the GRCh38 human reference genome using BWA-MEM. Alignments with mapping quality scores <20 were removed using SAMtools, and PCR and optical duplicates were identified using Picard MarkDuplicates. Base quality score recalibration was performed using GATK ApplyBQSR with gnomAD hg38 known sites and restricted to the Twist Exome target regions. Somatic variants were called with GATK Mutect2 (v4.2.2.0), restricted to Twist Exome 2.1 target intervals on the GRCh38 no-alt reference. Variant calling was performed in multi-sample tumor-normal mode, jointly analyzing sequences from two replicate libraries of each AI-TC-enriched sample against two matched normal control libraries (see previous section). The same normal control sequences served as the reference for all timepoints from a given patient. The GATK 1000 Genomes panel of normals and the gnomAD allelefrequency-only germline resource were used to exclude recurrent technical artifacts and germline variants. Calls were filtered with FilterMutectCalls (v4.2.2.0) using the default optimal F-score threshold; contamination and read-orientation bias models were not applied. Single-nucleotide variants were extracted with bcftools (v1.14).

To complement the GATK Mutect2 variant calls, two additional somatic variant callers were applied to the recalibrated BAM files from the AI-TC-enriched and normal control cells. Strelka2 33 was run through the nf-core/Sarek pipeline (v3.6.0) 34 in whole-exome sequencing mode using the GATK GRCh38 reference genome. Strelka SNV and indel VCFs were concatenated per sample using bcftools 35.

VarScan (v2.4.6) 36 was run independently. Pileup files were generated using samtools mpileup (v1.21) 35 with a minimum mapping quality of 1, restricted to exome target regions. VarScan somatic calling was performed with a minimum coverage of 3 and a minimum variant frequency of 0.01.

Caller-specific quality filters were applied prior to consensus generation: 1) MuTect2: Retained variants passing GATK FilterMutectCalls (PASS filter). 2) Strelka2: Retained PASS-only variants. VAF was calculated from tier 1 allele counts. VarScan: Retained PASS-only somatic variants (somatic status SS = 2) with somatic confidence score ≥ 20, tumor VAF ≥ 0.05, total depth ≥ 10, alt-supporting reads ≥ 3, and normal alt-supporting reads ≤ 1 to remove germline leakage.

For all callers, somatic tumor variants with VAF <0.05 were excluded based on the established limit of detection for whole-exome sequencing 37 . For patients with multiple samples from a single timepoint, VCFs were merged using bcftools, and the mean allele frequency across tumor samples was computed for each variant.

A combined variant set was generated for each patient-timepoint by taking the union of somatic variants passing quality filters across all three callers. Variants were annotated using vcf2maf (v1.6.22) (mskcc/vcf2maf: vcf2maf v1.6.16, n.d.) with Ensembl VEP 38 , selecting a single effect per variant based on the Ensembl canonical transcript. The reference genome used was GRCh38.p13 with the GRCh38 VEP cache.

Combined heatmaps of somatic variants were generated using ComplexHeatmap 39 . Variant heatmaps displayed the top 20 most frequently mutated genes, restricted to coding variant classifications (missense, nonsense, frameshift, in-frame indels, splice site, and translation start site mutations). Clinical annotations included effusion source, timepoint, and patient identity. Cancer-associated gene oncoplots used oncogenic gene lists from the maftools package 40 , supplemented with ESR1 and ESR2. TF was estimated from the WES data using ichorCNA, with analysis restricted to genomic regions targeted by the Twist Human Core Exome Panel v2.1.

Sequencing of cell-free DNA and solid tumors

Cell-free DNA isolation. Fluids from effusion samples collected in Cell-Free DNA BCT tubes were subjected to sequential centrifugation at 1,900 × g for 10 min at room temperature and 16,000 × g for 10 min at 4°C to remove cellular debris, yielding approximately 5.2 mL of clarified supernatant. Samples were treated with proteinase K, incubated at 60°C for 20 min, cooled on ice for 5 min, and cell-free DNA was isolated using the MagMAX Cell-Free DNA Isolation Kit on a KingFisher Apex instrument. Purified DNA was assessed using an Agilent Fragment Analyzer, with cell-free DNA and high-molecular-weight DNA defined as fragments of 100-1,000 bp and 1,000-6,000 bp, respectively, and total DNA yield was quantified using the Qubit dsDNA

High Sensitivity Assay.

Primary tumors. Formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded tumor blocks were cut into 5-10 unstained 10-µm sections. Regions with tumor cellularity greater than 25% were identified by histologic review using an adjacent hematoxylin and eosin (H&E)-stained slide as a guide. Genomic DNA was isolated from macrodissected tissue samples using the QIAamp DNA FFPE Tissue Kit (Qiagen).

Targeted sequencing and copy-number analysis using the UCSF500. Sequencing of cell-free DNA and primary tumor DNA was performed using the research use only (RUO) pipeline of the UCSF500 Cancer Gene Panel test provided on a fee-for-service basis by the UCSF Clinical Cancer Genomics Laboratory. The UCSF500 is a hybrid-capture next-generation sequencing assay targeting the coding regions of cancer-associated genes, selected intronic regions of genes recurrently involved in structural rearrangements, and genomic regions distributed across the chromosomes to assess copy-number alterations. The current assay targets 529 cancer-associated genes and selected introns of 47 genes. The sequencing libraries were enriched by hybrid-capture using custom oligonucleotide probes and sequenced as paired-end reads on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000. The sequence reads were computationally deduplicated and aligned to the human reference genome (UCSC hg19/NCBI build 37).

For variant analysis, single-nucleotide variants (SNVs) and small insertions and deletions (indels) were identified using the UCSF500 bioinformatics pipeline. The UCSF500 pipeline uses FreeBayes, GATK variant callers, and Pindel for SNV and indel detection, with Delly and Pindel for detecting larger indels and structural variants. Variants were annotated using ANNOVAR, and candidate SNVs, indels, and structural variants were reviewed using the Integrative Genomics Viewer (IGV). Germline polymorphisms and recurrent sequencing artifacts were removed through database-and pipeline-based filtering.

Genome-wide copy-number and zygosity analysis was performed from UCSF500 sequencing data using CNVkit. The UCSF500 panel includes genomic targets distributed at intervals across the genome, enabling assessment of chromosome-level and focal copy-number alterations, as well as alterations affecting the targeted cancer genes. CNVkit uses sequencing coverage from targeted regions, together with additional sequencing information available across the capture assay, to estimate relative copy number.

Clinical sequencing of solid tumors.

Results from clinically performed next-generation sequencing of solid tumor specimens were obtained by retrospective review of the electronic medical records. Molecular profiling had been performed as part of routine clinical care and was not conducted specifically for this study.

Available clinical molecular pathology reports were reviewed, and reported somatic genomic alterations were abstracted for comparison with genomic profiles generated from malignant effusion tumor cells and effusion cell-free DNA. When available, the anatomic site and date of tumor sampling, sequencing assay, and date of molecular testing were recorded.

RESULTS

Patients, samples, and assays

The study included 10 patients with metastatic breast cancer who developed malignant effusions, including seven with hormone receptor-positive/HER2-negative disease and three with triple-negative breast cancer; none had HER2-positive disease (Table S1). Eight patients had pleural effusions, and two had ascites.

The cohort was predominantly female, with six patients identifying as White. Ages ranged from 47 to 80 years, with a mean age of 61 years. Serial effusion samples were available from six patients, with 2-7 samples collected per patient.

Pleural and ascitic fluids obtained by therapeutic thoracentesis or paracentesis were processed to generate single-cell suspensions and cell-free supernatants (Fig. 1A). Single-cell suspensions were cryopreserved and subsequently analyzed by AI-powered single-cell imaging (Deepcell) and flow cytometry.

Matched cell-free DNA (n = 17 samples) from the same fluids and archival tumor blocks (n = 4 patients) were collected for sequencing (Fig. 1A). Data from clinical sequencing of tumors (n = 6 patients) were extracted from patients' medical records. The assays performed for individual samples are summarized in Table S2.

AI-powered single-cell morphology enables label-free cell-type classification.

We applied the Deepcell platform to perform high-throughput, label-free morphological analysis of single cells from malignant effusions. As cells flowed through the microfluidic system, brightfield images were acquired and analyzed in real time using a previously developed convolutional neural network trained to distinguish tumor cells from other cell populations in malignant effusions 23 (Fig. 1B; Fig. S1A). The model generated morphological embeddings comprising 51 interpretable morphometric features, including cell size, shape, intensity, and texture, and 64 deep-learning-derived features learned directly from cell images (Fig. S1B). These quantitative representations enabled visualization of cells in a 2-dimensional space. Fig. 1C shows the results of unsupervised clustering of all imaged cells (n = 25 samples) using the Leiden algorithm, along with representative brightfield images from each cluster.

Cell viability is preserved following imaging and sorting

Because genomic profiling requires recovery of intact cells, we evaluated cell viability before and after processing with the Deepcell instrument. Viability was assessed in fresh samples, following cryopreservation and thawing, and after imaging and cell sorting. Across the five samples evaluated, cell viability remained comparable after processing (Fig. 1D). Together with previous reports demonstrating preservation of cell viability following imaging and sorting 20, 23 , these findings support recovery of intact cells suitable for downstream molecular analyses.

AI-powered single-cell imaging reveals heterogeneous cellular composition of malignant effusions.

To characterize the cellular composition of malignant effusions, we applied AI-powered cell classification across 25 samples from 10 patients. Visualization of morphological embeddings using UMAP revealed two major clusters, with leukocytes (non-macrophage) predominantly occupying one cluster and macrophages and AI-TCs occupying a second (Fig. 2A). Although AI-TCs and macrophages showed partial overlap in several individual morphometric features, including cell size-related measures, as previously observed 23 (Fig. S1C), their separation within the multidimensional morphological representation indicates that classification incorporates morphological information beyond cell size alone. Cellular composition varied among patients, as demonstrated by sample density distributions projected onto a common UMAP representation (Fig. 2B). Leukocytes were the predominant cell population overall (median, 54.9%; range, 3.4-85%), followed by macrophages (median, 36.6%; range, 13.5-92.3%), except in serial samples from patient ME008, where macrophages were the most abundant (Fig. S2B). Non-leukocytes represented a smaller fraction of the cell population (median, 1.8%; range, 0.03-18.1%; Fig. 2C). Consistent with the malignant diagnosis by cytopathology, AI-TCs were detected in all samples (median, 5.4%; range, 0.51-15.8%; see Fig. 2C inset). Serial samples demonstrated changes in cellular composition within individual patients over time (Fig. S2A), indicating both intra-and inter-patient heterogeneity.

AI-derived estimates of major immune-cell populations correlate with flow cytometry

AI-derived estimates of the major immune-cell populations correlated significantly with flow cytometry measurements (n = 21 paired samples, Fig. 2D). Leukocyte proportions measured by AI correlated with CD45+/CD68-leukocytes by flow cytometry (Pearson r = 0.75, P = 8.2 × 10⁻⁵; Fig. 2E). AI-derived macrophage proportions also correlated with CD45+/CD68+ macrophages (Pearson r = 0.69, P = 2.7 × 10⁻⁶; Fig. 2F), including the marked macrophage-predominant effusions in patient ME008 (Fig. S2C), which was also observed using AI-derived cell classification (Fig. S2B, previous section). In contrast, estimates of TCF did not significantly correlate between the two approaches (Pearson r = 0.12, P = 0.595, Fig. 2G), whereas nonleukocyte populations were negatively correlated (Pearson r = -0.34, P = 0.133; Fig. 2H). Although initial AIbased cell classification used the highest predicted probability, a minimum confidence threshold of 0.85 was subsequently adopted for cell sorting. In a post hoc exploratory analysis, increasing the confidence threshold reduced AI-derived tumor-cell fraction (TCF) estimates but improved their correlation with flow cytometry (Fig. S2D). Among 21 samples, Spearman correlation increased from ρ = 0.33 (P = 0.14) when all AI tumorcell calls were included, regardless of classifier confidence, to ρ = 0.47 (P = 0.034) when only calls with confidence ≥0.95 were included.

Unsupervised analysis identifies distinct tumor cell morphotypes in malignant effusions

To determine whether AI-TCs exhibited morphological heterogeneity, we performed unsupervised clustering of their morphological embeddings using the Leiden algorithm. This analysis identified four distinct clusters, hereafter referred to as "morphotypes" (Fig. 3A). The distribution of morphotypes varied substantially among patients (Fig. 3B, Fig. 3C), and serial samples from individual patients also showed changes in morphotype composition over time (Fig. S3A, Fig. S3B), demonstrating both intra-and inter-patient morphological heterogeneity.

To identify the morphological and deep-learning features that distinguish each morphotype, we performed one-versus-all random forest analyses (Table S3). Morphotypes 1 and 2 were distinguished predominantly by deep-learning-derived features, whereas Morphotypes 3 and 4 showed greater contributions from interpretable morphometric features (Fig. 3D). Morphotype 3 was characterized by larger cell-size and shape-related measurements, including perimeter, area, maximum radius, maximum Feret diameter, and longaxis length, whereas morphotype 4 was associated with smaller values for these features and showed a strong contribution from the texture-related small-black-blob integral feature (Fig. S3C). Table S4 lists definitions for the selected morphological features.

Analysis of deep-learning feature patterns further showed distinct, non-human-interpretable signatures among the four morphotypes (Fig. 3E). These morphotype-associated signatures were also observed across samples from different patients, with tumor cells assigned to the same morphotype showing similar deeplearning feature profiles regardless of patient origin (Fig. S3D). Together, our analysis demonstrates that AI-TCs in malignant effusions can constitute morphological subpopulations that vary both across patients and over time within individual patients.

Morphology-guided enrichment enables genomic copy-number profiling of tumor cells from malignant effusions

To determine whether morphology-guided sorting could enrich tumor cells for genomic characterization, we subjected AI-TC-enriched and matched unenriched cell samples (immune-cell-predominant) to low-pass whole-genome sequencing following whole-genome amplification (see Methods). TF was estimated from genome-wide copy-number profiles using ichorCNA. Among paired samples, AI-TC enrichment increased sequencing-derived TFs in 14 of 26 samples (mean, 3-fold; range, 1-15.5-fold), including at least one sample from 7 of 10 patients (Fig. 4A; Fig. S4A).

Copy-number profiles varied substantially among patients, demonstrating inter-patient genomic heterogeneity. Comparison with matched unenriched fractions generally showed more pronounced copynumber alteration signals following AI-TC enrichment (Fig. S4B). Serial unenriched samples from patient ME002 similarly showed weak or absent copy-number alteration signals at several timepoints, with low ichorCNA-estimated TF, consistent with immune-cell-dominant samples with non-aberrant genomes (Fig. S4C). Interestingly, we did not observe copy-number alterations in ME010 or in longitudinal samples from ME007.

Genome-wide copy-number profiling of AI-TC-enriched samples from 10 patients identified recurrent chromosomal gains and losses characteristic of breast cancer, including gains of 1q and 8q and loss of 8p (Fig. 4B). Focal alterations included amplifications encompassing cancer-associated genes such as MYC at 8q24.21 and CCND1 at 11q13.3, as well as losses involving regions containing DUSP4 at 8p12 and BRCA2 at 13q13.1 (Fig. 4B). Known oncogenes or tumor suppressor genes located on chromosome regions with gains or losses, respectively, are listed in Table S5.

To determine whether copy-number alterations detected in AI-TCs were also represented in an independent tumor-derived analyte, we compared AI-TC profiles with matched effusion cell-free DNA. In patient ME008, AI-TC-enriched fractions from two ascites samples collected five days apart showed similar genome-wide copy-number alteration patterns, many of which were also evident in matched serial cell-free DNA samples (Fig. 4C). Similarly, the AI-TC-enriched fraction from patient ME006 showed multiple copynumber alterations that were also observed in matched effusion cell-free DNA (Fig. 4D). Notably, although ME006 had clinically HER2-negative disease, focal amplification of the ERBB2 locus, which encodes HER2, was detected in the AI-TC-enriched fraction (Fig. 4E). Amplification of the corresponding ERBB2 region was also evident in matched effusion cell-free DNA (log2 copy ratio, 1.2; Fig. 4D). Clinical testing by fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) of tumor cells obtained from fine needle aspirate of pleural fluid also revealed ERBB2/HER2 amplification (average ratio of HER2: Centromere 17, 2.6). Together, these tests provide independent support for this alteration. These findings demonstrate that morphology-guided enrichment enables profiling of genome-wide copy-number alterations in AI-TC-enriched samples from malignant effusions and recovers tumor-associated alterations also represented in matched effusion cell-free DNA.

Whole-exome sequencing of AI-TC-enriched samples identifies cancer-associated mutations and genomic relatedness across tumor-derived analytes

The same whole-genome-amplified DNA from AI-TC-enriched samples used for low-pass wholegenome sequencing was subsequently analyzed by whole-exome sequencing (WES). Across the cohort, WES identified somatic variants in multiple cancer-associated genes, including PIK3CA, ESR1, KMT2D, and CREBBP, among others (Fig. 5A). Mutation profiles varied across patients and serial samples, demonstrating substantial inter-patient and intra-patient genomic heterogeneity (Fig. 5A; Fig. S5, Table S6). Detailed longitudinal mutation profiles from patients with serially collected malignant effusions are shown in Fig. S5.

To assess genomic relatedness across tumor-derived analytes, variants detected by WES of AI-TCenriched samples were compared with available genomic profiles from matched effusion cell-free DNA and solid tumors analyzed by UCSF500 or clinical next-generation sequencing (Fig. 5B). Multiple alterations detected in AI-TCs were also identified in independently profiled tumor-derived specimens from the same patients, demonstrating shared genomic features across analytes. Conversely, other alterations were detected in only a subset of matched specimens, revealing genomic divergence across tumor-derived analytes. For example, in patient ME003, the ESR1 p.Y537S mutation was detected in AI-TC-enriched cells, matched effusion cell-free DNA, and a liver metastasis biopsy collected within five days of the effusion, but was not detected in the primary tumor resected six years earlier (Fig. 5B).

Together, these findings demonstrate that morphology-guided enrichment provides sufficient tumorderived material for WES and enables mutation profiling and comparison with independently analyzed tumorderived specimens.

Longitudinal profiling reveals dynamic morphological and genomic heterogeneity in malignant effusions

To investigate temporal changes in tumor cell phenotype and genomic composition, we integrated longitudinal single-cell morphological and genomic profiling in patients with serial malignant effusion samples.

Patients ME002 and ME014 were selected for detailed analysis because multiple serial samples were available together with matched genomic data from archival primary tumors and effusion cell-free DNA (Fig. 6).

Longitudinal changes in AI-estimated TCF (Fig. S6A) and morphotype composition (Fig. S6B) were also observed in additional patients with serial malignant effusion samples.

Patient ME002 had HR-positive/HER2-negative metastatic breast cancer and underwent repeated thoracentesis, during which six malignant pleural effusion samples were collected over 104 days. AI-powered single-cell analysis demonstrated marked changes in morphotype composition over time, with a progressive shift from predominantly Morphotype 1 toward increased representation of Morphotype 4 at later timepoints (Fig. 6A). Low-pass whole-genome sequencing of AI-TC-enriched samples identified multiple copy-number alterations, including focal amplification of 11q13 encompassing CCND1 (Fig. 6B). Copy-number alteration signals were prominent at days 0, 21, and 39, markedly attenuated at days 47 and 75, and increased again at day 104, paralleling changes in sequencing-derived TF from 23.4%, 29.0%, and 46.5% at the first three timepoints to 5.0% and 3.0% at days 47 and 75, respectively, followed by an increase to 20.0% at day 104 (Fig. 6B).

Comparison with an archival primary tumor collected 11 years earlier revealed multiple shared copynumber alterations, including amplification of 11q13, supporting genomic relatedness between the primary tumor and effusion-derived tumor cells (Fig. 6B). However, additional alterations differed between specimens.

In particular, a chromosome 17q gain detected in the AI-TCs was not evident in the archival primary tumor but was also detected in matched effusion cell-free DNA collected at day 0, demonstrating genomic divergence from the earlier primary tumor while providing independent support for the alteration in the metastatic effusion (Fig. 6B). Longitudinal WES further identified PIK3CA p.H1047R across four of six serial AI-TC-enriched samples, together with additional variants detected at subsets of timepoints (Fig. 6C, Fig. S6C).

Patient ME014 had HR-positive/HER2-negative metastatic breast cancer and underwent repeated paracentesis, during which three malignant ascites samples were collected over 23 days. Morphotype composition changed over this interval, with expansion of Morphotype 2 from day 0 to days 15 and 23 (Fig. 6A). Copy-number alteration profiling demonstrated numerous genomic alterations shared among the archival primary tumor collected 7.3 years earlier, effusion cell-free DNA, and serial AI-TC-enriched samples, consistent with genomic relatedness across tumor-derived specimens (Fig. 6B). Nevertheless, several alterations differed across specimens, including a chromosome 7 amplification apparent in the archival primary tumor but not in the effusion-derived samples, whereas focal amplifications on chromosome 10 were observed in the effusion-derived AI-TCs and cell-free DNA but not in the primary tumor (Fig. 6B). Longitudinal WES demonstrated persistent detection of several variants across all three effusion samples, including mutations in ALK, PTEN, and TP53 (Fig. 6C, Fig. S6C).

Together, these longitudinal analyses demonstrate dynamic changes in tumor cell fraction and morphological composition while revealing both persistent and divergent genomic features across serial effusion samples and tumor-derived analytes collected years apart.

DISCUSSION

Malignant effusions represent a clinically accessible source of metastatic tumor cells. However, background immune cells pose significant challenges for isolating and characterizing these cells. This study builds on prior work that established the feasibility of AI-powered, morphology-based enrichment of tumor cells from malignant effusions for downstream molecular characterization 23 . Here, we extend this approach by integrating single-cell morphology information with morphotyping and genomic profiling to interrogate tumor identity and heterogeneity in these fluids.

Comparison of the AI-derived cell identities with standard flow cytometry showed moderate to strong correlations in the proportions of major immune cell populations, supporting the ability of morphology-based classification to distinguish major cell types within the complex cellular milieu of malignant effusions. In contrast, the proportions of AI-TCs were not concordant between the two methods. This discordance may be due in part to fundamental technical differences in sample preparation and in how tumor cells are defined for the two methodologies, as well as to potential misclassifications by either method. Whereas flow cytometry identified tumor cells using two epithelial cell-surface markers (EpCAM and CLDN4), the AI-based approach relied on cell morphology and did not require tumor cells to express a specific surface antigen. This distinction may matter in metastatic disease, where epithelial-mesenchymal plasticity can generate heterogeneous tumor cell states or reduce epithelial marker expression, potentially limiting marker-based detection of tumor cells 41- 43.

Previous studies have demonstrated the feasibility of using tumor cells isolated directly from malignant effusions as a liquid-biopsy analyte for genomic profiling 15,44 . Tiran et al. performed low-coverage wholegenome sequencing of cells isolated from malignant pleural effusions from patients with metastatic breast cancer, including phenotypically enriched tumor-cell subpopulations 15 . Chen et al. subsequently used hexokinase 2 and cytokeratin-based identification to isolate tumor cells from malignant pleural effusions across multiple cancer types and performed single-cell DNA sequencing 44 . However, both studies used fluorescently labeled markers to detect or enrich for tumor cells, including ALDH1 in Tiran et al. 15 , and hexokinase 2 and cytokeratins in Chen et al. 44 In contrast, our label-free approach reduces reliance on predefined molecular or physical characteristics, potentially enabling representation of a wider spectrum of phenotypically heterogeneous tumor cells for molecular profiling.

Our analysis identified four tumor cell morphotypes, a classification based solely on image-derived phenotypic clusters rather than on discrete molecular or functional cell states. We observed heterogeneity in morphotype composition across patients and in serial samples from the same patient. Longitudinal changes in morphotype composition within individual patients suggest that tumor cell morphology may be a dynamic biomarker that could be monitored over time. Nonetheless, whether these morphotypes reflect distinct tumor cell biology warrants further investigation.

Morphology-guided, label-free enrichment and cell sorting enabled genomic characterization of AI-TCenriched samples. Copy-number and mutation analyses identified genomic alterations that provided orthogonal genomic support for the tumor origin of AI-TC-enriched populations. Comparison with genomic data from matched analytes (including cell-free DNA from the same effusion fluids and solid tumors from the same patient) demonstrated both genomic relatedness and divergence. Importantly, longitudinal analysis showed that sequencing-derived TF influenced the strength of the genomic signal. Samples with higher TF generally exhibited more readily detectable copy-number alterations, whereas samples with low TF showed attenuated genomic signals and, in some cases, fell below the detection limits of sequencing-based approaches. These observations highlight a fundamental challenge in genomic profiling of malignant effusions: highly abundant nonmalignant cells can dilute tumor-derived DNA. Increasing sequencing-derived TF through morphologyguided enrichment may therefore improve the recovery of tumor-associated genomic information, particularly from specimens with substantial nonmalignant-cell admixture. These findings further support malignant effusions as a source of cellular and cell-free analytes for liquid biopsy of metastatic disease. Because malignant effusions can be repeatedly collected during therapeutic drainage, they provide an opportunity to characterize the contemporary genomic landscape of metastatic disease without requiring an additional invasive procedure. This potential is illustrated by patient ME003, in whom ESR1 p.Y537S was detected in AI-TC-enriched cells, matched effusion cell-free DNA, and a contemporaneous liver metastasis, but not in the primary tumor resected six years earlier. ESR1 ligand-binding-domain mutations are frequently selected during endocrine therapy and represent an established mechanism of acquired resistance to aromatase inhibitors 45 . Similarly, ERBB2 amplification was detected in both AI-TC-enriched cells and effusion cell-free DNA from patient ME006, whose primary tumor had been clinically classified as HER2negative. Together, these examples illustrate how genomic profiling of malignant effusions may capture clinically relevant features of contemporary metastatic disease that are not represented by historical tumor characterization and could potentially inform treatment selection. Whether longitudinal changes in TCF or morphotype composition reflect treatment response, clonal selection, or clinical outcome will require evaluation in prospectively collected cohorts with detailed treatment and outcome data.

Limitations

The study has several limitations. The cohort was small and heterogeneous, and included only patients with metastatic breast cancer who received different treatments. The availability of serial malignant effusion samples, matched cell-free DNA and solid tumors for genomic comparisons varied. Because sequencing was performed on whole-genome-amplified DNA, amplification-related coverage nonuniformity, allelic dropout, and sequencing artifacts may have contributed to apparent differences in copy number and variant detection across serial samples. To confirm alterations detected in AI-TC-enriched fractions, we used genomic data from cell-free DNA isolated from the same fluid and matched solid tumors, which were not subjected to wholegenome amplification. Nonetheless, longitudinal differences in individual genomic alterations should not necessarily be interpreted as evidence of clonal evolution without independent validation. The sensitivity of genomic profiling was limited in samples with low sequencing-derived TF, and the absence of detectable copynumber alterations cannot distinguish insufficient tumor content from copy-number-neutral tumor genomes or other technical limitations of low-pass sequencing. Independent validation of the morphology-based classifier in a larger cohort and against additional orthogonal measures of tumor cell identity will be important. Whether morphotypes represent discrete tumor biological states remains unknown. Future work includes single-cell RNA sequencing of AI-TC-enriched samples to link individual morphotypes with genomic and transcriptomic states.

Conclusions

Despite the potential value of malignant effusions as a source of metastatic tumor material, these fluids remain an underutilized biospecimen. Characterizing tumor cells in malignant effusions can provide longitudinal snapshots of metastatic disease and help identify novel biomarkers and therapeutic targets for advanced cancers. Our approach converts routinely obtained, often discarded malignant-effusion material into a multidimensional source of information on metastatic tumor heterogeneity. The AI-powered single-cell morphology analysis platform enabled label-free identification, phenotyping, and enrichment of putative tumor cells, and genomic analysis provided orthogonal evidence for tumor origin of enriched populations while revealing phenotypic and genomic heterogeneity over time.
