Reporting p values imprecisely (e.g., p < .05) reduces transparency, reproducibility, and re-use (e.g., in p value meta-analyses). Best practice is to report exact p-values with three decimal places (e.g., p = .032) unless p values are smaller than 0.001, in which case you can use p < .001.

What we found

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). Statcheck might still miss one-sided tests that are described in another sentence, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Note on this version. This version substantially revises the medRxiv preprint posted on 29 December 2025 under the same DOI [1]; its title, objective and conclusions differ from version 1. Version 1 presented TabGraphSyn as a graph-guided generator with improved fidelity, utility and privacy, and this version re-evaluates those claims. The present evidence does not support version 1's statements that the GNN module is essential to the reported improvements or that its privacy audit confirmed robust deidentification. Historical values that were not regenerated are identified in Appendix A, which also lists the reporting corrections.

1 Background and significance

Synthetic patient data are proposed as a way to share clinical information without exposing individuals [2] [3] [4]. Their value rests on two claims that are usually evaluated together: that models trained on synthetic records perform like models trained on real records, and that the synthetic records do not disclose who was in the training data. The first is commonly measured by training on synthetic data and testing on real data (TSTR) [5], the second by distance-to-closest-record statistics or membership-inference attacks [6] [7] [8].

TSTR generalisation and membership inference both depend on correctly identifying records that were excluded from generator training. If designated holdout records enter training, utility estimates can become optimistic and the nominal non-member group no longer provides a valid membership contrast.

Leakage is a recognised source of irreproducibility in machine-learning research [9, 10], and distancebased privacy metrics alone are insufficient to establish protection against membership inference [11, 12]. We examine how these concerns interact within one generator family and how controlled interventions can distinguish them. Tabular diffusion models learn to generate mixed numerical and categorical records, either directly in feature space [13, 14] or through a continuous latent representation [15]. TabGraphSyn adds a graphderived condition to latent diffusion [1]. Its motivation is that nearby records may carry useful local information about joint feature structure that is not explicitly represented by an unconditional generator. A k-nearest-neighbour graph connects similar records, and a graph isomorphism network (GIN) [16] converts the record and its neighbourhood into a conditioning vector. This is a similarity graph inferred from a single table, not a graph of independently observed relationships between patients. Relational generators designed around links between tables provide the architectural precedent [17, 18].

The proposed benefit creates a diagnostic tension. A condition rich enough to preserve local structure may also retain the content of its source record. In addition, a high-fidelity synthetic table can appear useful even when the evaluation records entered generator training. We therefore study TabGraphSyn as a case in which training membership, the conditioning information path and attack sensitivity can be inspected separately. The earlier method report [1] evaluated marginal and pairwise fidelity, schema validity, classification, survival-related covariate recovery, augmentation and privacy proxies. Here, those objectives motivate the diagnosis; their historical numerical results are not treated as independently validated findings. The generator, cohorts, interventions and outcome definitions needed to interpret the present experiments are specified below.

Per-record conditioning raises a further question even when the split is valid. An embedding derived from a real record may retain enough of its content to guide the generator towards that record. Recent work systematically benchmarks membership attacks against tabular synthesis [19] and examines memorisation in tabular diffusion models [20]. We test whether leakage is associated with the information encoded in the condition, using random per-record codes, record latents and cluster-level conditions as controls.

2 Objective

We investigate how evaluation contamination and record-specific conditioning interact in synthetic clinical data generation. Four questions organise the study: which records reach each training stage; whether the composition of released synthetic records can suppress a membership contrast; whether record information recoverable from a condition is associated with disclosure after the split is corrected; and whether the tested alternatives offer utility gains over a corrected local TabSyn comparator within declared attack thresholds. The contribution is a mechanism-based diagnosis and evaluation procedure, supported by implementation evidence, interventions and bounded comparisons. This is not a new clinical prediction model or a claim that one generator is uniformly best.

3 Materials and methods

Scope and pipeline definitions

We distinguish three implementation states. The released implementation is the code associated with the December 2025 preprint [1], inspected here for its feature mask and data-loading behaviour. The postmask contaminated pipeline is the subsequent development version, in which informative per-record conditioning was enabled but the designated holdout remained in training. The train-only pipeline restricts training, conditioning and model selection to training rows. The paired retraining experiments compare the latter two states; they do not reproduce the original preprint's masked implementation or its exact numerical results. The correspondence between each published result and the code used to produce it remains incompletely established. Appendix A separates implementation findings, claims requiring reevaluation, and directly checkable reporting corrections.

Cohorts and splits

We evaluated four single-table clinical cohorts spanning 683 to 156,468 records (Table 1). TCGA is the Pan-Cancer Atlas clinical cohort used for survival modelling [21]. ACTG 175 is the AIDS Clinical Trials Group Study 175 trial dataset [22]; stored experiment files abbreviate it as "AIDS". WBCD is the Wisconsin Breast Cancer (Original) dataset, 683 complete records with nine integer cytology features and a benign/malignant label [23]. MIMIC-IV is a cohort of 156,468 hospital admissions derived from MIMIC-IV [24], with 13 numerical features (age, length of stay, ten laboratory values and days to the follow-up event) and 9 categorical features (including gender, race, insurance, language, marital status, admission type and location, and the in-hospital death flag). Each MIMIC-IV row is one hospital admission, the last for each patient, so no patient contributes more than one row. The in-hospital death flag (1 = died) is the main target. Two further columns record a post-admission outcome, a follow-up death indicator and the number of days to that event; they are removed whenever in-hospital death is predicted from the other columns. Insurance, language and marital status are missing for 2,898, 522 and 8,500 admissions; these gaps are modelled as a separate category during training and written back as missing in the released tables, and the stored training split has no other missing values. MIMIC-IV is a credentialed-access resource, and we report only aggregate statistics.

The MIMIC-IV cohort was built from version 3.1 of the hosp module. Admissions were joined to patient records, and for each patient only the admission with the latest discharge time was kept. Ten routine laboratory values (glucose, creatinine, urea nitrogen, white blood cell count, haemoglobin, platelet count, sodium, potassium, bicarbonate and haematocrit) were averaged over all results linked to that admission. Admissions with a non-positive recorded length of stay or lacking at least one of the ten laboratory values were excluded; the laboratory criterion removed 66,860 of 223,328 last admissions, leaving 156,468 admissions from as many patients. Age is the MIMIC-IV anchor age. The follow-up death indicator marks death in hospital or within 365 days of discharge, and the days to that event are capped at 365, the value also given to censored patients.

Each cohort has a fixed 90/10 split into training and designated holdout rows, stored with the code. The split is defined by a stored index permutation: its first n_train entries are training rows and its last n_holdout entries the designated holdout. The repository's preprocessing script generates this permutation by shuffling with seed 1234 and incrementing the seed until every categorical level occurs among the training rows; we used the stored files without regenerating them. The term designated holdout refers to these evaluation indices; the records are genuinely unseen by the generator only in the train-only arms.

For the distance attack, the designated holdout is divided in stored order into contiguous subsets H1, H2 and H3 (D4, Appendix B). H2 was reserved as the reference set of a density-ratio attack that was discarded after failing its positive control (D4); the distance attack does not use it. Distances are Euclidean after one-hot encoding of categorical columns and standard scaling (see Membership attacks). Storage type determines which columns are treated as categorical, so integer-coded categories are treated as numeric. Generator design and information flow

TabGraphSyn consists of a tabular variational autoencoder (VAE), a similarity-graph encoder and a conditional latent diffusion model [1, 15, 16, 25]. A record x_i contains numerical and categorical attributes.

The VAE encodes it into a continuous vector z_i and decodes generated vectors back into the original table schema. Its reconstruction and latent-regularisation objectives allow diffusion to operate in a continuous representation while the decoder handles mixed data types. Numerical inputs use quantile normalisation. The graph encoder and diffusion model are separate learned stages; their inputs and training membership must therefore be checked separately (Figure 1).

Graph construction and encoding. Each record is a node, and nearest-neighbour edges represent feature similarity within the available record pool. With informative inputs enabled, the GIN aggregates the record's own features and neighbouring features. It is trained to predict that record's VAE latent z_i, and its hidden vector h_i is cached as the condition. Thus, neighbourhood information and the source record's own information can both reach h_i. The presence of graph edges does not by itself establish that any subsequent gain comes from relationships among records rather than from encoding the record itself.

Conditional diffusion and sampling. Gaussian noise is added to record latents during training, and the denoising network learns to recover latent structure conditional on h_i under the diffusion formulation [25]. Generation starts from noise, applies 100 denoising steps with a selected condition, and decodes the resulting latent to a synthetic row. The condition therefore enters the generative process in addition to the random noise. The output is a new draw from a learned conditional distribution; it is not guaranteed to be independent of the real record that supplied its condition.

Condition selection is part of the model. For a cached pool of N conditions, the sampling distribution can be written conceptually as p_syn(x) = (1/N) sum_i p_theta(x | h_i), with decoding included in p_theta.

The implementation selects cached conditions without replacement when the requested output size does not exceed the pool. A release with N rows consequently uses each condition once. In the contaminated post-mask pipeline, N includes designated holdout rows; in the train-only pipeline, it includes training rows only. The cluster-conditioned variants replace these empirical point conditions with draws from a fitted mixture. This distinction is central to the output-removal and conditioning-content experiments.

The TabGraphSyn backbone used k = 5 neighbours, GNN hidden dimension 128, a multilayer-perceptron denoiser, quantile normalisation, 100 denoising steps, and maximum training lengths of 4,000 VAE, 1,000 GNN and 10,000 diffusion epochs with the repository's early stopping. The initial preprint stated k = 3, whereas the inspected implementation uses k = 5. Baselines follow their own model specifications. We report this discrepancy for reproducibility; we did not conduct a general sensitivity analysis over these settings. Settings not listed here are the defaults of the inspected code version; experiment configurations are documented in the declarations in Supplementary Data S1.

Conditioning information and training boundaries

Feature masking. In the source multi-table generator, a table's own features are hidden while its embedding is computed [17]. In the inspected released single-table code, the only table remained in the masked list. Node features and the inputs to graph construction were therefore constant. Under the implemented fixed-degree aggregation, this removes patient-specific variation from the embeddings. The relevant released files are unchanged from the first commit on 29 August 2025. The mask was removed on the development branch on 16 February 2026 (commit 672f797). The quantitative experiments analysed here use post-mask outputs or train-only variants. The earlier masked state is assessed through code and working records rather than a new reproduction run.

Per-record conditioning. After masking is removed, the GNN receives the record's own content and learns to predict its VAE latent. Sampling from its cached embedding consequently provides information specific to a real record. We test the disclosure implications of this design rather than treating the presence of a unique condition alone as evidence of leakage.

Training-set contamination. The inspected diffusion loader read the all-row latent file (latents.npy) rather than training-row latents (train_z.npy). The graph, GNN and conditioning pool also included all rows, and VAE model selection used an internal validation split from the full table. The local TabSyn adaptation contained the same all-row diffusion-loading path. Inspection of upstream TabSyn (amazonscience/tabsyn, commit cb5ac0f) showed training-only VAE and diffusion inputs [26]; the local loading problem should not be attributed to the published TabSyn method. Saved-run evidence and the limits of inference from the initial preprint's output files are reported separately in Results.

The train-only correction restricts the latent inputs, graph, GNN and conditioning pool to training rows. VAE model selection uses an internal 10% split of those training rows. The correction comprises 20 hunks across four files, including run isolation and alignment checks. Stored latent means were compared with all-row and training-row means to check saved-run provenance. The paired experiments evaluate this pipeline-level correction; they do not isolate the contribution of each changed component.

Experimental arms

Experimental arms were trained in separate code copies to preserve the source implementation. Table 3 defines their training and sampling inputs.

Contaminated post-mask arm: informative GNN conditions with designated holdout rows included in training and sampling. TCGA and ACTG 175 were retrained with the same seeds and settings as the train-only GNN arm. MIMIC-IV comparisons use two existing contaminated outputs.

Per-record conditions in the train-only pipeline: the record's GNN embedding, a representation of its own VAE latent, or a random vector drawn once per record. The record-latent control uses standardised principal components of the VAE latent (PCA-64); the random-code control uses Gaussian codes. Both are scaled to match the GNN conditions. The latter distinguishes records without directly encoding their features; comparing these arms tests recoverable content as well as uniqueness.

Cluster-level conditions p(h): each record is assigned to a component c(i). During training, its condition is freshly sampled from that component's Gaussian distribution with mean mu_c and covariance Sigma_c, while the target latent remains z_i. During generation, a component is drawn according to its fitted mixture weight and a condition is sampled from that component, rather than selected from an individual record's cached vector. The rule-based arm partitions GNN embeddings with a Gaussian mixture model (GMM); the granularity experiments use K-means partitions with requested K = 128, 512 or 2,048 where specified in Table 3, alongside existing K = 1 and rule-based endpoints.

The rule-based GMM selects K_lik from {1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32} by likelihood on an internal 10% split of training conditions, caps K at floor(n_train/100), and merges components smaller than 100 records. It uses full covariance, regularisation 10^-4 and random state 42; the effective component count after merging is reported in Figure 6. The K-means ladder uses three initialisations, random state 42 and component covariance regularisation 10^-4 I; singleton components are merged into the nearest centroid. These protocols vary both granularity and partitioning procedure, so the ladder is not a fully isolated intervention on K alone.

Latent-based partition control. A separate GMM assigns records to groups using standardised VAE latents after PCA-64 rather than GNN embeddings. The Gaussian condition means and covariances are still calculated in GNN-embedding space. This control changes which records are grouped together; it does not remove the GNN from the conditioning representation. We call it the latent-partition control throughout.

Graph information used only during training: in each clean TabSyn training batch, a record's latent z_i is replaced with probability p by λz_i + (1 -λ)z_j, where j is drawn uniformly from the five nearest training-latent neighbours of i (Euclidean distance on normalised latents) and λ ~ Uniform(λ_min, 1). The two declared settings were p = 0.5 with λ_min = 0.5, and p = 1.0 with λ_min = 0 (D12).

Sampling is unconditional.

No conditioning: the same backbone without a conditioning vector.

Clean TabSyn: the local TabSyn adaptation with two declared fixes, restricting diffusion inputs to training-row latents and passing the parsed seed to training. This is a corrected local comparator, not a claim to have reproduced every setting of the upstream benchmark.

Additional baselines: TabDiff [14], TabDDPM [13], CTGAN [27] and CTAB-GAN+ [28], trained on MIMIC-IV training rows only (D13).

Train-only arms used seeds 42, 123 and 456 unless stated otherwise and generated the training-set number of records. The contaminated post-mask arm used the all-row conditioning pool and full-table sample size. Replicate counts and convergence exclusions are stated in Table 3 and Results.

Replication and shared representations. In the declared TCGA, ACTG 175 and WBCD train-only experiments, each cohort's VAE was trained once and shared across the conditioning arms; GNN and diffusion seeds provide the stated repetitions. The contaminated paired comparator similarly shares its seed-42 VAE across its later seeds. The granularity and identity-content experiments reuse the previously trained representations. MIMIC-IV repeats reuse the seed-42 graph representation where applicable, so additional diffusion seeds are not independent end-to-end model replications. Seed-based uncertainty therefore describes variation conditional on these representations and the fixed data split. Utility. A random forest with 100 trees and seed 42 was trained on synthetic data and scored by AUROC on the fixed designated holdout (TSTR) [5]. The same classifier trained on real training rows gives TRTR.

TRTR is a model-specific empirical reference, not a theoretical upper bound. Targets are vital status for TCGA, the censoring indicator for ACTG 175, tumour class for WBCD, and in-hospital death for MIMIC-IV. Because the last is saturated (TRTR 0.998), D11 added six non-saturated tasks: in-hospital death after removal of post-admission outcome columns, and gender, insurance, marital status, admission type and admission location, each predicted from the remaining columns. For binary targets AUROC uses the predicted probability of the positive class; for multiclass targets it is the one-vs-rest macro average over the classes present in the real training rows (scikit-learn 1.4.2), with probabilities from the synthetictrained classifier aligned to those classes. Records with a missing target are excluded from training and testing, and missing categorical predictors are encoded as all-zero indicators. These tasks assess information preservation rather than prospective clinical validity. For historical comparison, we also applied the earlier evaluation protocol, which re-splits the full table after generation. That protocol does not provide an independent test for an all-row-trained generator.

Fidelity. We report the mean two-sample Kolmogorov-Smirnov statistic over numerical columns and the root-mean-square error between real and synthetic pairwise Pearson correlations, both computed against the holdout rows, and the median distance from each synthetic record to its closest training record. Lower KS and correlation error indicate closer agreement with the reference distribution. These fidelity measures do not establish clinical plausibility or privacy. Schema validity means preserving variable types and allowed formats; it is distinct from preserving treatment effects, survival processes or valid combinations of clinical attributes.

Membership attacks. The primary score is the negative Euclidean distance from each real record to its nearest synthetic record. For each contrast, categorical columns are one-hot encoded and all columns are standardised with one scaler fitted on the union of the two candidate sets and the synthetic table; missing values become zero after encoding. The main AUC compares training rows with H1; the control AUC compares H3 with H1 (D4). In train-only arms, H1 and H3 are both non-members, providing an empirical negative control. In contaminated arms they were exposed to training, so the same calculations describe nominal split contrasts rather than a valid membership test. Attack excess E is the main AUC minus the control AUC. The declared resolution is r = 1.96 × √[v(n_train, n_H1) + v(n_H3, n_H1)], where v(a, b) = (a + b + 1)/(12ab) is the Hanley-McNeil variance of an AUC of 0.5 between groups of sizes a and b with continuous, untied scores [29]: 0.0501 for TCGA, 0.1170 for ACTG 175, 0.2071 for WBCD and 0.0137 for MIMIC-IV. The calculation treats the main and control AUCs as independent although both use H1, assumes no tied scores, and ignores the uncertainty of the no-conditioning excess subtracted below. The shared H1 would be expected to correlate the two AUCs positively, which makes r conservative for E alone, whereas the estimated reference adds variance; r is therefore a null scale for E, not a confidence interval for D or for differences between arms. Exact zero distances (WBCD) depart further from these assumptions. Supplementary Table S1 gives the main AUC, control AUC, E and D of every analysed run.

We additionally report D = (E_arm -mean E_no-conditioning) / r. Values within +/-1 meet the study's operational threshold for no resolved additional signal relative to the backbone; they do not establish absence of absolute leakage. This scale is an approximate calibration, not a formal privacy bound. WBCD is not sufficiently informative for this comparison: 30% of holdout rows duplicate training rows, and the copy control reaches only 0.72 units of D (raw E = 0.2817; backbone E = 0.1330).

Reading the attack scale. AUROC is the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve: 0.5 corresponds to chance ranking and values above 0.5 indicate better member ranking under the specified score. E removes the empirical control contrast, while D additionally subtracts the no-conditioning backbone and divides by the declared resolution r. For example, TCGA D = 4 means that the arm's excess is approximately 4 x0.0501 = 0.2004 AUC units above the backbone excess; it is not a fourfold probability of identifying a person. Likewise, D near zero means similar measured signal to that backbone, not necessarily an absence of membership information.

The second attack family, membership inference via error prediction for tabular data (MIA-EPT) [30], uses per-column attribute predictors trained on synthetic data and an attack classifier on their error profiles. We used the released feature construction with the recorded adaptations in D5: direct labelled attack training without shadow models, a gradient-boosting classifier, runtime fixes, and a 20,000-row cap on MIMIC-IV predictor training. High-cardinality text classification and MIMIC-IV missing-value handling were added after earlier cohorts had produced partial results, before results existed for the affected cohorts; earlier cohorts were not rerun. These are adaptations rather than an exact reproduction of the published attack. Ground-truth membership is available to the evaluator; practical attacker access is not established by this experiment. The holdout is split in stored order into halves A and B, and members M are sampled without replacement from the training rows to size |A| (seed 42). Per-column attribute predictors are fitted on the synthetic table only; the challenge records in M, A and B are used only to compute error profiles. The attack classifier (histogram gradient boosting with library defaults and seed 42; no hyperparameters were tuned) is evaluated by stratified five-fold cross-fitting: each challenge record is scored by a classifier trained on the other four folds, and the AUC is computed from the pooled out-of-fold scores. The main comparison is M versus B and the control is A versus B, so B supplies the negatives in both; the MIA-EPT resolution applies the formula above with both terms equal to v(|B|, |B|). The MIMIC-IV cap and subsequent full-data rerun are examined explicitly in Results.

Positive controls. We submitted the training rows as a verbatim-copy synthetic table for each cohort and attack. Failure to detect this control limits interpretation of a null result. Successful detection checks sensitivity to that control but does not ensure equal sensitivity to other release mechanisms or sampling schemes.

Condition decodability. Five-fold out-of-fold ridge regression predicts each training record's VAE latent from its training condition; we report the mean per-dimension R-squared (D11). The ridge penalty is chosen from {0.1, 1, 10, 100, 1000} by efficient leave-one-out validation within each training fold; conditions and latent dimensions are standardised once over all training records before the folds are formed (fold seed 0), and no PCA is applied.

For cluster-level conditions the predictor is the fitted mean of the record's component. The no-conditioning arm is assigned R-squared = 0 and D = 0 by definition.

These are diagnostics of already-fitted representations, not tests of generalisation to new patients. This measures one form of linear record-content recoverability; it is not a complete information measure and does not capture every property of the component distribution.

Calibration of the project's metrics. The project's evaluation suite reported a membership-inference AUC, a distance-to-closest-record statistic, a detection score, TSTR, and the NewRowSynthesis metric of the SDMetrics library, version 0.27.0 [31, 32]. We applied the first two to generators with known ground truth: an exact or near-duplicate copy of the training data, the copy with added Gaussian noise, an independent sample from the same distribution, and an over-dispersed sample.

Historical perturbation controls. The saved output-removal comparisons include unperturbed conditions, sampling-time perturbations and training-time perturbations. The latter two add noise to cached conditions along directions estimated by local principal-component analysis of neighbouring conditions.

Sampling-time perturbation changes conditions only during generation; training-time perturbation

redraws perturbed conditions during model fitting while retaining the source record's target latent. These settings probe sensitivity to condition variation. Their presence in the output collection does not make them validated privacy protections, and the present intervention does not separately estimate the effect of every perturbation setting.

Output removal intervention. When the contaminated pipeline generates one output per full-table row, every designated holdout row also seeds an output. We mapped synthetic records to their conditioning source and removed outputs seeded by designated holdout records, then repeated the distance contrast (D7). Unconditional outputs underwent equal-sized random removal. The trained models were unchanged and still contained holdout information. This intervention isolates an output-composition effect; it is not a train-only retraining experiment or a formal lower bound on leakage of a differently trained model.

Declarations and statistical analysis

For the 12 diagnostic experiments D1-D12, written declarations recorded arms, measurements, predictions and decision rules (Appendix B), and their SHA-256 hashes are listed in Supplementary Data S1. Ten were frozen before launch; two were saved 33 and 77 seconds after launch, before reported outcomes were available. The latter launch failed out of memory and was rerun hours later. D13 separately records the MIMIC-IV baseline runs.

These are dated internal declarations rather than an assertion of public prospective registration. Training runs whose best diffusion loss occurred before epoch 200, or whose early-stopping loss exceeded 0.9, were excluded as diverged. Four of ten initial train-only MIMIC-IV trainings diverged; matched cluster-level and no-conditioning arms were rerun with clipping and all six converged. Two of three clean TabSyn MIMIC-IV seeds also diverged.

Welch two-sided t-tests compare independent arms over seeds, and paired t-tests compare contaminated and train-only runs sharing seeds. Seed bootstrap intervals use 10,000 resamples. With usually three seeds, uncertainty estimates are coarse and power is limited; non-significant differences are not equivalence tests. Seed variation conditional on one data split does not quantify uncertainty over populations or alternative splits. Prespecified decision thresholds are distinguished from descriptive comparisons, and the reported comparison p-values should not be read as a universal ranking across all arms. Analyses used Python 3.10, PyTorch 2.0.1 and NVIDIA A100 GPUs.

Decision criteria and comparators. D1-D13 are identifiers for dated experiment declarations, not references to a previous article; Appendix B summarises their scope and outcomes. The initial clustercondition decision used MIMIC-IV additional attack excess no greater than its declared resolution, with KS and correlation error no more than 0.005 above matched no-conditioning controls. Its TCGA utility criterion required a positive TSTR difference from no conditioning with a one-sided Welch p < 0.05 and additional attack excess within resolution. The later granularity comparisons were exploratory and could not reverse that decision. Graph-only mixup used a separate gain threshold of 0.010 and p < 0.025.

Comparisons against clean TabSyn answer a different question and use the reported two-sided Welch tests. We retain these distinctions rather than applying a single post hoc success rule to all experiments.

4 Results

Checking whether conditioning contains record information

Inspection of the released implementation identified constant inputs to graph construction and aggregation. A historical WBCD log recorded one distinct input vector among 683 records before masking was removed and 449 afterwards (Table 2). This supports the conclusion that the inspected masked implementation could not use patient-specific similarity information. It does not independently establish which executable state produced every result in the initial preprint. Historical NewRowSynthesis values, the share of outputs matching no real record, were 1.00 for ACTG 175 and TCGA and 0.41 for WBCD before the repair. These values describe record matching, not privacy guarantees.

Calibrating novelty and membership measurements

A historical comparison after removal of the mask reported lower mean KS statistics: 0.023 to 0.011 for ACTG 175, 0.009 to 0.003 for TCGA, and 0.032 to 0.010 for WBCD. Novel-record shares fell from 1.00 to 0.28, 1.00 to 0.06, and 0.41 to 0.09, respectively. These working-record values were not recomputed. The same release changed latent scaling and VAE training, preventing attribution of the changes to masking alone. The combination of improved fidelity and reduced novelty motivated direct tests of record-specific conditioning and evaluation sensitivity.

Calibration revealed that several evaluation scores could not answer the intended privacy question (Table 4). The legacy membership metric randomly divided real records into nominal members and nonmembers without reference to generator training. On simulated data it scored a near-copy at 0.466 and an independent sample at 0.481; using actual membership labels gave 1.000 and 0.516. The legacy distance statistic scored a copy at 0.245 and an independent sample at 0.498 in the calibration, showing that its numerical scale required empirical interpretation. The detection score measures real-versus-synthetic distinguishability and can reward copying. These findings explain why favourable fidelity and detection scores cannot substitute for a calibrated membership audit.

Tracing nominal holdout exposure through the pipeline

Saved latent means matched all-row training in 94 of 95 ACTG 175 runs, 99 of 100 TCGA runs, 86 of 87 WBCD runs and all 44 MIMIC-IV runs from the inspected development repository. Remaining cases were indeterminate under the tolerance; none matched training-only means. Saved train-only runs matched the intended training subset (Table 5). Available local TabSyn runs on WBCD and MIMIC-IV also matched all-row training. By contrast, the initial preprint's supplied baseline outputs have full-table sizes of 11,648, 2,139 and 683 rows, but sample counts alone do not establish their training membership.

The conditioning-source probe (D6) found 156,468, 11,648, 2,139 and 683 distinct conditions in the MIMIC-IV, TCGA, ACTG 175 and WBCD arrays, matching the full-table row counts. In these outputs each designated holdout row also supplied a condition. With source-to-output correspondence available to the evaluator, the output seeded by a record was its nearest synthetic neighbour for 80% of training and 80% of holdout records on MIMIC-IV, and 74% and 68% on ACTG 175, compared with 1/n under random correspondence. This is a source-linked proximity result under an informed evaluation, not proof that every patient was reconstructed exactly or that an external attacker has the mapping.

Testing masking by removing holdout seeded outputs

Earlier attacks had used the designated train/holdout labels on contaminated outputs (D4-D5). The distance attack did not exceed its cohort-specific resolution for any of 15 configurations. MIA-EPT exceeded resolution for one ACTG 175 configuration without perturbation (0.130 versus 0.110) and none on the other cohorts. Copy controls were detected. Because the generator had seen both nominal membership groups, these near-null contrasts could not establish privacy of the contaminated outputs.

Removing holdout-seeded outputs changed that contrast without retraining (Figure 2). Across 13 converged MIMIC-IV configurations, distance-attack excess changed from -0.0025 to +0.0017 before removal to 0.047-0.225 afterwards. For the unperturbed configuration it increased from -0.0003 to 0.2248. The unconditional control remained near 0.003 after equal-sized random removal. On ACTG 175, excess increased in all 13 configurations, from at most 0.098 to as much as 0.332, while five unconditional outputs remained within resolution. Among MIMIC-IV configurations meeting the declared fidelity margin, the smallest post-removal excess was 0.101, or 7.4 resolution units. The intervention supports masking by the presence of source-linked outputs for both nominal groups. It does not remove prior training exposure of the holdout.

Before removal, 6 of 34 conditioned outputs exceeded the corresponding TRTR reference by more than one standard error: 4 of 14 on ACTG 175, 1 of 5 on TCGA and 1 of 15 on MIMIC-IV. None did after removal, and none of 11 unconditional outputs did before or after. TCGA lacked saved source indices, so outputs whose nearest real neighbour was a holdout row were removed instead. This proxy and the change in output composition limit a causal interpretation of the TCGA removal test. Exceeding TRTR is descriptive evidence here, not a sufficient criterion for contamination.

An earlier contaminated-pipeline comparison on TCGA gave a conditioning-associated TSTR difference of 0.032 (0.9816 versus 0.9494, nominal p < 0.001). Its five conditioned replicates varied sampling seeds from one trained model, so they do not provide five independent training replicates. The corresponding train-only comparison gave +0.0097 (p = 0.061) with additional attack signal D = 4.0. The comparisons illustrate sensitivity to membership boundaries and replication design; their difference should not be treated as a precisely isolated effect size.

Paired retraining before and after the bundled train-only correction

With three shared seeds, the bundled train-only correction reduced TSTR and increased the relative distance-attack signal in every paired run (Figure 3, Table 6). On TCGA, TSTR changed from 0.9856 to 0.9577 (paired difference -0.0279, p = 0.008), while D changed from -0.30 to 4.00 (difference +4.30, p = 0.003). On ACTG 175, TSTR changed from 0.9666 to 0.9371 (-0.0294, p = 0.080), and D from 0.19 to 2.92 (+2.73, p = 0.001). The ACTG 175 utility reduction is directionally consistent but not significant at 0.05. These results evaluate the bundled correction in the post-mask implementation. The release size also changes from the full-table to the training-set size, so the comparison does not isolate training exposure from sample-size and other pipeline effects.

Contaminated-arm TSTR exceeded its TRTR reference on every seed: means were 0.9856 versus 0.9771 on TCGA and 0.9666 versus 0.9316 on ACTG 175. The earlier full-table re-split protocol gave 0.9926 versus 0.9752 and 0.9793 versus 0.9477. The generator's access to nominal test records, established independently by the loader and run evidence, explains why these are not valid estimates of out-oftraining generalisation. The small nominal membership contrast is likewise uninformative because both groups were exposed.

Testing additional MIMIC IV prediction targets

Across six non-saturated MIMIC-IV targets (TRTR 0.754-0.934), both contaminated outputs exceeded the empirical TRTR reference (Figure 4). The default output differed by +0.026 for in-hospital death, +0.038 for gender, +0.089 for insurance, +0.099 for marital status, +0.031 for admission type and +0.092 for admission location. The perturbed output differed by +0.006 to +0.035. Among 16 train-only runs, the largest positive difference on any target was +0.0011. Additional baselines ranged from 0.009 to 0.163 below TRTR. This pattern is consistent with the documented exposure of designated holdout rows; it does not make TRTR a theoretical ceiling or establish prospective clinical utility.

Relating conditional record information to attack success

The train-only GNN-embedding arm showed additional distance-attack signal D = 4.00 on TCGA, 2.92 on ACTG 175 and 18.7 on MIMIC-IV, the cohorts with informative resolution. Across ten train-only TCGA arms including no conditioning, decodability and D were strongly associated (Spearman rho = 0.96, p < 0.001; Figure 5, Table 7). On ACTG 175 the association was weaker (rho = 0.53, p = 0.14, nine arms). GNN-condition R-squared was 0.86 on TCGA and 0.75 on ACTG 175, consistent with its training objective of predicting the record's own latent. The association concerns the tested arms and does not by itself establish a general causal law.

Two controls distinguish a unique index from an informative condition. A random per-record code had negligible out-of-fold decodability (R-squared = -0.006 on TCGA) and no resolved additional distanceattack signal (D = -0.09). Conditioning on the record's own latent gave the largest signal, D = 7.20 on TCGA and 4.03 on ACTG 175. Together, these controls support the role of condition content in this model family; they do not prove that arbitrary random codes can never be memorised under other training regimes.

Evaluating utility after restricting the conditioning channel

Among comparisons with adequate replication and informative attack calibration, no tested train-only variant with attack signals within the declared thresholds showed a significant TSTR gain over clean TabSyn (Figure 7, Table 9). The ACTG 175 rule-based cluster arm differed by +0.0058 (p = 0.45). WBCD's largest difference was +0.0080 (p = 0.11), but privacy could not be assessed there. On TCGA, the GNN arm differed by +0.0077 (p = 0.12; D = 4.0), and the record-latent arm by +0.0230 (p = 0.006; D = 7.2). Contaminated-arm differences were +0.0356 on TCGA and +0.0323 on ACTG 175 and are not valid train-only improvements. None of 18 fidelity comparisons reached p < 0.05; the smallest was 0.056 for the ACTG 175 GNN arm. These null comparisons leave smaller effects unresolved.

Neighbour mixup during training also failed to meet its declared utility criterion on TCGA. TSTR differences from clean TabSyn were +0.0003 at probability 0.5 (p = 0.95) and -0.0012 at probability 1.0 (p = 0.82). At probability 1.0, fidelity worsened (KS +0.0126, p = 0.001; correlation error +0.0109, p = 0.005). No additional attack-signal difference was resolved. A zero-mixup control matched clean TabSyn metric for metric. The prespecified gain of at least 0.010 at p < 0.025 was not achieved; this tests two settings in one cohort, not every use of graph information during training. Clean TabSyn's mean TSTR was already within 0.004 of TRTR on ACTG 175, WBCD and the saturated MIMIC-IV target, while the TCGA gap was 0.027. Its holdout KS was 0.004-0.015 above that of a verbatim-copy reference. These comparisons describe the benchmark's dynamic range for the chosen metrics. Neither TRTR nor a copy-control fidelity score defines a universal optimum.

ACTG 175 provides a useful comparator-dependent exception. The rule-based cluster arm and K = 512 gained +0.0115 and +0.0072 over the no-conditioning backbone, with seed-bootstrap intervals excluding zero and additional attack signals within threshold. Against clean TabSyn, the differences were +0.0058 (p = 0.45) and +0.0014 (p = 0.85). With 214 holdout rows and three seeds, these observations motivate cautious reporting rather than a claim that conditioning never helps.

5 Discussion

Principal findings

This case study identifies two interacting evaluation mechanisms and one design-specific disclosure channel. First, nominal holdout exposure compromises both generalisation testing and the definition of non-members. Second, source-linked outputs for both nominal groups can suppress the distance contrast even when outputs remain close to their source records. Third, after the training boundary is enforced, informative per-record conditions are associated with measurable membership-attack signals. Output removal, paired retraining, identity-content controls and a second attack family provide complementary evidence. The contribution is a diagnostic sequence for separating these mechanisms within the studied implementation.

Interpreting the diagnostic interventions

The interventions answer different questions. Output removal changes which synthetic counterparts are released while holding the trained model fixed; the resulting attack change supports an outputcomposition mechanism. Paired train-only retraining changes the training boundary and associated run controls; it shows that the contaminated evaluation overstated utility in these runs while obscuring the residual attack signal. The random-code and record-latent controls then test whether a unique per-record condition or its recoverable content better explains that signal. Treating these comparisons separately avoids attributing every historical change to a single repair.

The released feature mask is a related implementation finding. It prevented the inspected code from using patient-specific graph inputs, so conclusions about similarity conditioning require an executable state in which that information is present. The historical mask repair coincided with other training changes and was not re-run here. We therefore distinguish the documented defect from any claim about its isolated quantitative effect or the exact origin of each published result.

Why contamination affects utility and membership contrasts

A privacy audit needs a valid distinction between exposed and unexposed records. When both nominal groups enter training and supply conditions, comparable proximity to synthetic outputs can make the contrast small. A near-null AUC in that setting is not evidence of privacy. At the same time, training exposure of evaluation records can improve TSTR without demonstrating out-of-training generalisation. The observed effects need not occur with equal strength in every generator; here, provenance checks and controlled interventions show that they matter in the post-mask pipeline.

TSTR above TRTR is a reason to inspect an evaluation, not proof of contamination. TRTR measures a particular classifier trained on a finite sample. A synthesis procedure can alter smoothing, weighting or regularisation, so a TSTR gain does not necessarily require access to test records. In this study, the contamination diagnosis instead rests on actual loader behaviour, saved-run evidence and the response to interventions. The MIMIC-IV pattern across six tasks provides additional consistency evidence. Smallholdout seed variation further limits the heuristic: clean TabSyn on ACTG 175 ranged from -0.008 to +0.013 around TRTR.

Record content as a conditioning channel

The GNN condition carries information about a real record beyond the randomly sampled diffusion noise. Its training target is the record's latent, and its inputs include that record's features. High out-of-fold decodability and the record-latent control support a content-mediated disclosure mechanism. The randomcode control shows that uniqueness alone did not produce the same signal under the tested training regime. Cluster-level sampling reduced the additional signal below the operational threshold in several arms, but the present comparisons did not establish a reliable utility advantage over clean TabSyn. This supports a bounded trade-off result rather than the identity of all utility gains with leakage.

The findings apply to the tested single-table design, conditioning strategies and attacks. They do not show that graph structure is generally unhelpful, that all record-specific conditions are unsafe, or that clusterlevel conditions are private against every adversary. Relational generation with genuine links between tables and a separately sampled relational structure is a different setting [18].

Controls matched to the failure mechanism

Different checks are needed at different boundaries. Input-variation and conditioning-ablation checks establish whether the intended information path is active. Saved row indices and latent provenance establish which records each stage used. Copy and non-member controls test the behaviour of the attack, while sampling-overlap checks test whether those controls represent the evaluated release. The MIMIC-IV MIA-EPT result illustrates the last distinction: a positive control can be easy to detect because its selected members are overrepresented in the predictor-training sample. A successful control therefore needs to be interpreted alongside the sampling procedure and the threat model.

Implications for evaluation practice

The safeguards below turn these observations into reproducible checks. Their purpose is to validate the information boundary and the sensitivity of the measurements before interpreting a utility-privacy tradeoff. Detection scores remain useful for distinguishability, and record-matching measures can reveal nearcopying, but neither alone establishes membership privacy [11, 12]. When the original privacy evidence does not support its interpretation, that limitation should be stated explicitly and the affected claim reassessed.

Evaluation safeguards

1. Verify which rows every stage reads, including the encoder, graph, condition pool, model selection and generator. Preserve indices or provenance checks with each run.

2. Keep an independent evaluation set outside training and model selection. Do not infer independence merely from a stored split name.

3. Report TSTR beside a matched TRTR reference and its uncertainty. Investigate unexpected gains using provenance and controls rather than treating TRTR as an upper bound.

4. Calibrate each attack on a copy control and an appropriate non-member control. Report raw AUC, control AUC, excess, and any baseline-relative transformation separately.

5. Check whether control construction, subsampling and source-linked overlap give the control a different exposure pattern from the evaluated arm.

6. Measure what record information a condition encodes, using identity-content controls and reconstruction or decodability probes appropriate to the threat model.

7. Verify adapted baselines against their intended training protocol. Match splits and replication where feasible and report divergence rather than silently substituting runs.

8. Check that conditions vary across records and that ablation changes the intended information path. A code repair and its isolated empirical effect are separate questions. 9. Pair fidelity improvements with record-matching and attack checks. Neither high fidelity nor novelty alone establishes privacy.

10. Record decision rules and deviations with their timing. Keep negative results bounded by the tested methods, attacks, datasets and statistical power.

Limitations

Most arms have only three seeds on one fixed split and share fitted representations; seed-bootstrap intervals are coarse, non-significant tests do not establish equivalence, and excluded divergence limits comparison to converged runs. MIMIC-IV clean TabSyn has one converged seed, as do its GNN arm and full-data MIA-EPT comparison. WBCD does not provide adequate attack resolution. The MIMIC-IV cohort keeps one admission per patient and requires complete laboratory panels, so it does not represent all hospital admissions. Several utility targets are descriptive rather than prospective clinical prediction tasks. The distance-attack resolution is an approximate null calibration and baseline subtraction is not a confidence bound for absence of leakage. Attack adaptations, ordered holdout subsets and subsampling choices also limit generalisation. The latent-partition control retains GNN-derived conditions and does not isolate all effects of graph encoding. We studied one implementation family and two attack families, without a formal guarantee such as differential privacy. The paired pipeline correction bundles changes to training membership, run controls and release size; the mask repair was not isolated experimentally.

Historical working-record values and the initial preprint's exact numbers were not regenerated. Outputfile size alone does not determine training exposure, and the association between released code and every original published result remains incomplete. Output removal tests a contaminated trained model and cannot bound the privacy of a separately retrained one.

6 Conclusion

Within the tested TabGraphSyn pipeline, designated holdout exposure inflated utility estimates and weakened nominal membership contrasts. After restricting training to training rows, informative perrecord conditions retained measurable disclosure signals, supported by content controls and a second attack family. Several cluster-level alternatives reduced the additional signal within the declared thresholds, but a reliable utility advantage over clean TabSyn was not established. The practical contribution is a mechanism-based evaluation sequence: verify training membership, validate attack sensitivity under the actual sampling procedure, and test what record information the condition carries. These checks support narrower and more defensible claims about synthetic clinical data.
