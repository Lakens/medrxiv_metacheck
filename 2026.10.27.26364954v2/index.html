Reporting p values imprecisely (e.g., p < .05) reduces transparency, reproducibility, and re-use (e.g., in p value meta-analyses). Best practice is to report exact p-values with three decimal places (e.g., p = .032) unless p values are smaller than 0.001, in which case you can use p < .001.

What we found

We found 20 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Marginal Significance works

List all sentences that describe an effect as ‘marginally significant’.

The marginal module searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The list of terms is a subset of those listed in a blog post by Matthew Hankins. The module returns all sentences that match terms describing ‘marginally significant’ results.

Some of the terms identified might not be problematic in some contexts, and there are ways to describe ‘marginal significance’ that are not detected by the module.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. Abstract

Background: Sequential application of repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (rTMS) and transcranial alternating current stimulation (tACS) may induce neurophysiological changes within sensorimotor and pain-related networks. However, the resting-state EEG effects of this combined approach in fibromyalgia remain unknown.

In this exploratory randomized controlled pilot study, 10 adults with fibromyalgia were assigned to active stimulation (n = 5) or a no-stimulation control group (n = 5). Active participants received one or two sessions of 10-Hz rTMS over the left primary motor cortex (1,000 pulses/session), followed by 10 daily sessions of bilateral 10-Hz tACS (1 mA, 20 min). Six-minute eyes-closed resting-state EEG recordings were obtained before and after the intervention or an equivalent control interval. Power spectral density was quantified in the delta, theta, alpha, and beta bands and analyzed after natural-log transformation using mixed repeatedmeasures ANOVAs across frontal, central-centroparietal, and posterior regions of interest (ROIs), followed by separate regional Time × Group analyses.

Results: Theta power increased significantly in the active group but not in controls, yielding a significant Time × Group interaction (F(1,8) = 15.09, p = .005, ηp² = .654). Follow-up analyses showed increased theta power in frontal (p = .015), central-centroparietal (p = .006), and posterior (p = .012) regions. However, the Time × ROI × Group interaction was not significant (p = .059), providing no evidence of regional differences in group-dependent changes. A significant Time × Group interaction was also observed for delta power (p = .019), but not for alpha or beta power.

Conclusions: Sequential motor-cortex rTMS followed by repeated bilateral 10-Hz tACS was associated with increased resting-state theta activity across frontal, central-centroparietal, and posterior scalp regions in fibromyalgia. Given the small sample size, post hoc ROI selection, lack of sham stimulation, and absence of component-specific control conditions, these findings should be considered hypothesis-generating and require replication in adequately powered shamcontrolled studies.

Introduction

Fibromyalgia is a chronic pain condition characterized by widespread pain, fatigue, sleep disturbances, cognitive complaints, and affective symptoms (Clauw, 2014). Although its pathophysiology remains incompletely understood, converging evidence points to altered central processing of nociceptive and non-nociceptive information and mechanisms consistent with central sensitization (López-Solà et al., 2017; Sluka & Clauw, 2016; Woolf, 2011). Neuroimaging studies further indicate abnormalities within pain-related and sensorimotor networks, including disrupted connectivity within the pain network (Cifre et al., 2012) and reduced communication between sensorimotor, insular, and thalamocortical regions (Flodin et al., 2014). Together, these findings suggest that altered sensorimotor processing contributes to pain hypersensitivity in fibromyalgia.

Consistent with this view, resting-state EEG studies have identified abnormalities in oscillatory activity, functional connectivity, and network organization in fibromyalgia and other chronic pain conditions (González-Roldán et al., 2016; González-Villar et al., 2020; Diniz et al., 2024; Zebhauser et al., 2023). However, no resting-state EEG measure has demonstrated sufficient consistency or specificity to serve as a biomarker of chronic pain (Ta Dinh et al., 2019; Zebhauser et al., 2023; Zortea et al., 2021). Although a recent systematic review reported an overall tendency toward reduced delta, theta, and alpha activity and increased beta activity in fibromyalgia (Silva-Passadouro et al., 2024), findings remain heterogeneous, including reports of increased slow-frequency activity in fibromyalgia and other chronic pain conditions (González-Roldán et al., 2016; Lopes et al., 2024). This variability likely reflects differences in methodology, participant characteristics, and clinical status and suggests that neuromodulatory effects should be examined across multiple frequency bands and scalp regions rather than assuming a uniform EEG signature (Silva-Passadouro et al., 2024; Thut et al., 2017).

Repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (rTMS) of the primary motor cortex (M1) can modulate cortical excitability and distributed pain-related networks. In fibromyalgia, clinical improvement following M1-rTMS has been associated with changes in thalamic, insular, cingulate, frontal, and parietal connectivity, together with reduced pain and normalization of motor-cortical excitability measures (Argaman et al., 2022a (Argaman et al., , 2022b;; Molero-Chamizo et al., 2023). Transcranial alternating current stimulation (tACS), in contrast, applies weak sinusoidal currents that interact with ongoing neural oscillations in a frequency-dependent manner (Herrmann et al., 2013; Vogeti et al., 2022). Stimulation at 10 Hz is of particular interest because it overlaps with the sensorimotor mu rhythm, although evidence regarding repeated bilateral 10-Hz sensorimotor stimulation remains limited, particularly in fibromyalgia (Bernardi et al., 2021; Lafleur et al., 2021).

The present protocol combined repeated tACS with preceding M1-rTMS. Because both interventions interact with the physiological state of the targeted network, their sequential application may produce effects that differ from those of either modality alone (Neuling et al., 2013; Ridding & Ziemann, 2010; Hosseinian et al., 2021). However, evidence for such interactions derives mainly from concurrent or phase-synchronized paradigms in healthy individuals, and their relevance to sequential clinical interventions remains uncertain. Moreover, neuromodulation studies have reported post-stimulation changes outside the frequency range of stimulation, justifying an exploratory examination of multiple EEG frequencies and scalp regions.

Therefore, this exploratory randomized controlled pilot study investigated resting-state EEG changes associated with a sequential neuromodulation protocol consisting of high-frequency M1-rTMS followed by repeated bilateral 10-Hz sensorimotor tACS in individuals with fibromyalgia. Given the limited and heterogeneous literature, analyses were conducted across multiple frequency bands and scalp regions without assuming a priori that stimulation effects would be restricted to alpha activity or to electrodes directly overlying the stimulation sites.

Methods

Participants

Ten adults with fibromyalgia participated in the study (nine women and one man; mean age = 50.5 years, SD = 9.1). Participants were recruited through advertisements at a local hospital and in collaboration with the Associació Mallorquina de Fibromiàlgia, Síndrome de Fatiga Crònica/EM, Sensibilitat Química Múltiple i Electrohipersensibilitat (AMASC). All participants underwent screening for contraindications to non-invasive brain stimulation and EEG recording, and none met any exclusion criteria. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Regional Government of the Balearic Islands (reference GOIBE549363/2026), and all participants provided written informed consent before taking part.

As this was an exploratory pilot study, no formal a priori power calculation was conducted. The sample size was determined pragmatically based on the number of eligible participants who could be recruited and complete the protocol during the predefined study period. The study was designed to provide preliminary estimates of intervention-related neurophysiological effects and to inform the design and sample size planning of future adequately powered trials. Recruitment and data collection concluded at the end of the predefined study period. Participants' sociodemographic characteristics and questionnaire scores are presented in Table 1.

Materials and Instruments

Prior to the experimental session, participants completed a structured clinical interview and a battery of self-report questionnaires administered online using Google Forms (Google LLC, Mountain View, CA, USA). All instruments were administered in their validated Spanish versions. The clinical interview collected information on sociodemographic characteristics, medical history, and fibromyalgia-related clinical features. The impact of fibromyalgia on health status and daily functioning was assessed with the Fibromyalgia Impact Questionnaire (FIQ; Burckhardt et al., 1991; Rivera & González, 2004). The FIQ evaluates physical functioning, work-related difficulties, and core fibromyalgia symptoms, including pain, fatigue, stiffness, sleep disturbance, anxiety, depression, and overall well-being. Pain severity and pain-related interference with daily activities were assessed using the Brief Pain Inventory (BPI; Cleeland & Ryan, 1994; Badia et al., 2003). Health-related quality of life was measured with the 36-Item Short Form Health Survey (SF-36; Ware & Sherbourne, 1992; Alonso et al., 1995), which assesses eight domains of physical and mental health. Alexithymia was assessed using the 20-Item Toronto Alexithymia Scale (TAS-20; Bagby et al., 1994; Martínez-Sánchez, 1996), which evaluates difficulty identifying feelings, difficulty describing feelings, and externally oriented thinking. The multidimensional experience of chronic pain was assessed with the West Haven-Yale Multidimensional Pain Inventory (WHYMPI; Kerns et al., 1985; Ferrer-Pérez et al., 1993), which measures pain-related interference, perceived social responses to pain, and participation in everyday activities. Depressive symptom severity was assessed using the Beck Depression Inventory-II (BDI-II; Beck et al., 1996; Sanz et al., 2003), whereas anxiety was assessed using the State-Trait Anxiety Inventory (STAI; Spielberger et al., 1983; Spielberger et al., 1982), which evaluates both situational anxiety (state) and the relatively stable tendency to experience anxiety (trait). Questionnaire scores are summarized in Table 1.

Experimental Design and Procedure

This exploratory pilot study employed a randomized, parallel-group controlled design with two resting-state EEG assessments: baseline (t0) and post-intervention or follow-up (t1) (Figure 1). Participants were assigned by simple randomization to either an active-stimulation group (n = 5) or a non-stimulation control group (n = 5). Because the control condition did not include sham stimulation, neither participants nor the study assessor were blinded to group allocation.

At baseline, all participants underwent a 6-min eyes-closed resting-state EEG recording (t0). Participants assigned to the active-stimulation group subsequently received a sequential neuromodulation protocol consisting of rTMS followed by repeated tACS. Owing to feasibility constraints, rTMS exposure was not identical across participants: two received two rTMS sessions administered on consecutive days, whereas three received a single session. Following the final rTMS session, all participants completed ten bilateral tACS sessions delivered on ten consecutive days. A second 6-min eyes-closed resting-state EEG recording (t1) was obtained after completion of the final tACS session. Participants assigned to the control group received neither rTMS nor tACS during the study period. Instead, they returned approximately two weeks after the baseline assessment to undergo a second 6-min eyes-closed resting-state EEG recording (t1), providing a time-matched comparison for the active-stimulation group.

EEG Acquisition and Preprocessing

Resting-state EEG was recorded using an actiCHamp Plus amplifier and a 64-channel actiCAP slim system (Brain Products GmbH, Germany). Sixty-one scalp electrodes were positioned according to the international 10-10 system. Signals were acquired at 1000 Hz with CPz as the online reference and FPz as ground. Vertical electrooculographic (EOG) activity was recorded from electrodes placed above and below the left eye. Electrode impedances were maintained below 10 kΩ. Participants completed a 6-min eyes-closed resting-state recording while seated comfortably and instructed to remain awake. EEG data were processed offline in BrainVision Analyzer 2. Following González-Roldán et al. ( 2025), the first 60 s were discarded and the remaining 300 s segmented into consecutive 1-s epochs. Data were cleaned using a 50-Hz notch filter, filtered (0.05-35 Hz; Butterworth) and corrected using the Gratton-Coles ocular-artifact correction procedure (Gratton et al., 1983). Epochs containing amplitudes exceeding ±100 µV, voltage differences greater than 100 µV, or voltage steps exceeding 100 µV were rejected automatically. Participants with fewer than 75% artifact-free epochs were excluded.

Power spectral density (PSD) was estimated using a fast Fourier transform with a 10% Hanning window and zero-padding to 1024 points (frequency resolution = 0.98 Hz). PSD values were averaged across retained epochs and computed for delta (1-4 Hz), theta (4-8 Hz), alpha (8-12 Hz), and beta (12-30 Hz) frequency bands.

Regional analyses were conducted using three exploratory scalp regions of interest (ROIs) defined after visual inspection of the topographical distribution of intervention-related changes. The frontal ROI included Fp1, Fp2, AF3, AF4, AF7, AF8, AFz, F1, F2, F5, F6, F7, F8, and Fz (14 electrodes). The central-centroparietal ROI included FC1, FC2, FC3, FC4, FC5, FC6, FCz, C1, C2, C3, C4, C5, C6, Cz, CP1, CP2, CP3, CP4, CP5, and CP6 (20 electrodes). The posterior ROI included P1, P2, P3, P4, P5, P6, P7, P8, Pz, PO3, PO4, PO7, PO8, POz, O1, O2, and Oz (17 electrodes). The arithmetic mean PSD was calculated across electrodes within each ROI, and the natural logarithm of the resulting mean was used for statistical analysis. Because ROI selection was data-driven, regional analyses should be considered exploratory and interpreted cautiously (Kriegeskorte et al., 2009).

rTMS Stimulation Protocol

High-frequency rTMS was delivered over the left primary motor cortex (M1), a target previously investigated for pain modulation in fibromyalgia (Passard et al., 2007; Lee et al., 2012; Argaman et al., 2022a Argaman et al., , 2022b)). The approximate M1 location was identified using standard 10-20 EEGsystem landmarks, and the stimulation hotspot was defined as the scalp position where singlepulse stimulation elicited the most consistent visible muscle twitch in the contralateral right hand.

Resting motor threshold was determined by visual observation as the lowest stimulator intensity producing a visible hand-muscle response in at least 5 of 10 consecutive trials (Badran et al., 2019). Stimulation intensity was set at 100% of resting motor threshold. Each session consisted of 20 trains delivered at 10 Hz (50 pulses per train; 1,000 pulses/session). Trains lasted 5 s and were separated by 8-s intertrain intervals, resulting in a total stimulation time of approximately 4 min.

Stimulation was delivered using an Apollo TMS system and an air-cooled figure-of-eight coil (MAG & More GmbH, Munich, Germany). Owing to feasibility constraints, rTMS exposure varied slightly among participants: three received a single session and two received two sessions on consecutive days. Following completion of the rTMS phase, all participants proceeded to the tACS intervention.

tACS protocol

tACS was delivered using a battery-powered constant-current stimulator (DC-STIMULATOR PLUS, neuroConn GmbH, Ilmenau, Germany). Two circular electrodes (20 mm diameter; ~3 cm² contact area) were positioned over C3 and C4 according to the international 10-10 EEG system, approximately corresponding to the left and right sensorimotor cortices. Conductive paste was used to ensure stable contact, and electrode impedance was maintained below 5 kΩ throughout stimulation.

A 10-Hz sinusoidal current (1 mA peak-to-peak) was applied for 20 min, including 30-s ramp-up and ramp-down periods. Participants completed 10 sessions delivered on consecutive days following the rTMS phase. During stimulation, participants were seated comfortably and instructed to rest quietly. Stimulation parameters were based on previous studies employing bilateral sensorimotor tACS (Lafleur et al., 2021; Wach et al., 2013) and were consistent with established safety recommendations for low-intensity transcranial electrical stimulation (Antal et al., 2017; Woods et al., 2016).

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics 29 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Separate mixed-design repeated-measures ANOVAs were performed for log-transformed delta, theta, alpha, and beta power, with Time (t0, t1) and ROI (frontal, central-centroparietal, posterior) as within-subject factors and Group (active stimulation, control) as a between-subject factor. The models included all main effects, two-way interactions, and the Time × ROI × Group interaction. Additional exploratory mixed-design ANOVAs were conducted separately for each ROI, with Time as the within-subject factor and Group as the between-subject factor, to examine regional longitudinal changes. Bonferroni-adjusted pairwise comparisons were used to examine differences between ROIs, changes across time within each group, and group differences at each time point. Statistical significance was set at p < .05, and effect sizes were expressed as partial eta squared (ηp²).

Results

Delta (1-4 Hz).

The mixed ANOVA showed significant main effects of Time, F(1,8) = 6.14, p = .038, ηp² = .434, and ROI, F(2,16) = 59.66, p < .001, ηp² = .882, as well as a significant Time × Group interaction, F(1,8) = 8.58, p = .019, ηp² = .518. Bonferroni-corrected comparisons indicated higher delta power in the frontal ROI than in the central-centroparietal and posterior ROIs (both p < .001), with no significant difference between the latter two regions (p = 1.000). Delta power decreased significantly from baseline (t0) to follow-up (t1) in the control group (p = .005), whereas no significant change was observed in the active-stimulation group (p = .757). The Time × ROI × Group interaction was not significant, F(1.19, 9.54) = 2.07, p = .183, ηp² = .206 (Greenhouse-Geisser corrected). Separate exploratory ANOVAs showed significant Time × Group interactions in the frontal (p = .027), central-centroparietal (p = .038), and posterior (p = .035) ROIs. Follow-up comparisons indicated significant decreases in the control group across all three regions (p = .002, .016, and .017, respectively), with no significant changes in the active-stimulation group.

Theta (4-8 Hz). Theta-band power showed a significant Time × Group interaction, F(1,8) = 15.09, p = .005, ηp² = .654, whereas the Time × ROI × Group interaction was not significant, F(2,16) = 3.39, p = .059, ηp² = .298. A significant main effect of ROI was also observed, F(2,16) = 57.99, p < .001, ηp² = .879. Bonferroni-corrected comparisons indicated higher theta power in the frontal ROI than in the central-centroparietal (p < .001) and posterior (p = .001) ROIs, with higher power also observed in the posterior than in the central-centroparietal ROI (p = .032).

Follow-up analyses indicated a significant increase in theta power from baseline (t0) to postintervention (t1) in the active-stimulation group when averaged across the three ROIs (p = .005), whereas no significant change was observed in the control group (p = .129). Separate exploratory ANOVAs showed significant Time × Group interactions in the frontal, F(1,8) = 9.62, p = .015, ηp² = .546, central-centroparietal, F(1,8) = 13.90, p = .006, ηp² = .635, and posterior ROIs, F(1,8) = 11.43, p = .010, ηp² = .588. Bonferroni-adjusted comparisons indicated significant increases in theta power in the active-stimulation group across the frontal (p = .015), centralcentroparietal (p = .006), and posterior (p = .012) regions, whereas no significant changes were observed in any ROI in the control group. Bonferroni-corrected comparisons indicated higher alpha power in the posterior (p = .002) and frontal (p < .001) ROIs than in the central-centroparietal ROI, with no significant difference between the frontal and posterior regions (p = .936). Discussion

The main exploratory finding was a group-specific increase in resting-state theta power after sequential rTMS and tACS. The active group showed increased theta from baseline to postintervention in frontal, central-centroparietal, and posterior regions, while the control group showed no comparable change. Although the Time × Group interaction was significant in the combined model and in all three separate regional analyses, the Time × ROI × Group interaction was not significant, providing no evidence of a preferential regional response. A significant Time × Group interaction was also observed for delta power, with a reduction in the control group but no significant change in the active group. No significant Time × Group interactions emerged for alpha or beta. Resting-state EEG findings in fibromyalgia are heterogeneous, although a recent review suggested an overall tendency toward reduced low-frequency activity, including theta (Silva-Passadouro et al., 2024). The present findings identify theta as a potential neurophysiological correlate of the combined intervention, but they should not be interpreted as normalization or clinical benefit.

The functional meaning of the theta increase is unclear. Higher theta has been linked to greater tenderness, fatigue, and disease impact in some studies (Diniz et al., 2024; Fallon et al., 2018), although the relationship between resting-state theta activity and clinical characteristics has varied across studies (González-Roldán et al., 2016; Lim et al., 2016; Vanneste et al., 2017). This suggests that its significance depends on region, measure, and outcome rather than reflecting a uniformly adaptive or maladaptive process. Differences in methodology, such as relative versus absolute power, further limit comparison, and cross-sectional group differences do not necessarily inform the meaning of longitudinal intervention-related change. Because we did not examine correlations between EEG changes and clinical outcomes, the increase cannot be considered a biomarker of analgesia or symptom improvement.

The theta increase was observed across all three scalp ROIs, extending beyond the sensorimotor electrodes. Previous neuroimaging studies have identified altered functional connectivity involving pain-related and sensorimotor networks in fibromyalgia (Cifre et al., 2012; Flodin et al., 2014), while changes in pain-related functional connectivity following motor-cortex stimulation have also been reported with tDCS and rTMS (Cummiford et al., 2016; Argaman et al., 2022a). However, scalp topographies do not uniquely identify neural generators (Michel & Brunet, 2019), and the present results do not establish whether the observed theta changes reflect modulation of specific cortical regions or distributed networks. The effect also emerged in the theta band despite 10-Hz stimulation. Post-stimulation effects are not necessarily confined to the stimulation frequency and may reflect plasticity-related processes rather than simple entrainment (Helfrich et al., 2014; Vossen et al., 2015), and effects outside the applied frequency have been reported previously (Bernardi et al., 2021; Tzvi et al., 2022). The null alpha finding may reflect a mismatch between the fixed 10-Hz frequency and individual mu rhythms, which we did not measure. It is also in line with a previous report of no aftereffects of 10-Hz tACS on sensorimotor alpha oscillations (Lafleur et al., 2021).

Several mechanistic limitations apply. Responses to stimulation depend on prior activity and stimulation history (Ridding & Ziemann, 2010), but the present design lacked rTMS-only and tACS-only conditions and an intermediate EEG assessment. We therefore cannot determine whether rTMS influenced the subsequent tACS response, and the theta increase should be considered in relation to the complete sequential intervention rather than as evidence of metaplasticity, additivity, or synergy. Likewise, because significant effects in one band and nonsignificant effects in others do not demonstrate frequency specificity unless compared directly (Gelman & Stern, 2006; Nieuwenhuis et al., 2011), the findings identify a prominent theta-band change but do not establish selective theta modulation, particularly given the additional Time × Group interaction observed for delta power. The physiological significance of the delta reduction in the control group is also uncertain, and the absence of a significant reduction in the active group cannot be taken as evidence that stimulation prevented such a change.

This study has further limitations. The sample was very small (n = 5 per group), so the effect sizes observed, particularly those with p values close to the significance threshold, are likely to be inflated (Gelman & Carlin, 2014); pilot data are better suited to planning future trials than to estimating effects (Bell et al., 2018; Leon et al., 2011). The no-stimulation control did not include sham stimulation and was unblinded, so nonspecific influences such as expectation, daily contact with the research team, and repeated attendance cannot be separated from stimulation effects. The ROIs were defined post hoc, and alternative electrode groupings were examined before selecting the present three-ROI configuration, increasing the uncertainty associated with regional inferences. Furthermore, rTMS exposure differed between active participants, and the groups differed at baseline in several characteristics (e.g., age and SF-36 vitality; Table 1). Four frequency bands and additional regional ANOVAs were analyzed without correction across frequency bands or separate regional analyses, increasing the risk of false-positive findings (Bender & Lange, 2001). Clinical outcomes were not reassessed after the intervention. Finally, vigilance, individual alpha peak frequency, and the aperiodic spectral component were not examined, so drowsiness, alpha slowing, or broadband changes in low-frequency power cannot be excluded as contributors to the theta increase.

In conclusion, sequential rTMS+tACS was associated with increased resting-state theta activity in fibromyalgia, but its physiological and clinical significance remains uncertain. Replication in adequately powered, sham-controlled designs with single-modality arms, intermediate EEG assessments, and EEG-clinical correlations is needed.
