Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 128 papers with 246 instances of power statements, 203 were correctly detected (true positives), 22 were missed (false negatives) and 21 were incorrectly detected (false positives). Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Hand microvasculature is conventionally presented through diagrams, cadaveric atlases and specialized optical instruments, while conventional clinical radiography cannot resolve individual capillary loops (5-15 µm lumen) in vivo, which dominate cutaneous oxygen exchange.

We present Microcirculation Mirror, a zero-install browser workstation that registers layered hand anatomy to a live 21-landmark pose and couples it to a nailfold video capillaroscopy (NVC) simulator, a lumped microcirculatory model and an imaging-physics module. The single-file application runs offline on a laptop or, over local HTTPS, on a phone as an installable app.

Results Its 3D atlas contains 136 named structures per hand in six systems from open Z-Anatomy/BodyParts3D meshes, plus a literature-parameterized capillary layer. The NVC module renders a 200×, 1.2-mm field with six deterministic patterns and quantified density, giant capillaries, avascular width fraction, hemorrhages, tortuosity and ramification. A lumped model reports nine estimates (SIM); a physics module shows that a 10-µm capillary occupies 0.07 of a 150-µm detector pixel; with iodinated contrast it retains ≈0.016% effective contrast, about 60× below a 1% detection threshold; unenhanced blood is ≈270× lower. The 2D stage renders in 2.9 ms per frame; system merging reduces atlas draw calls from 136 to 6.

The camera-overlay layers are templates, the atlas macro-anatomy is open mesh data, and the capillary layer is sampled. No patient data are used. The instrument is a product prototype, not a medical device, and provides a reproducible framework for making multi-scale microvascular reasoning visible.

Introduction

The microcirculation of the hand is studied across rheumatology, physiology and anatomy. Rheumatologists examine nailfold capillary loops to identify the scleroderma pattern and distinguish its early, active and late stages [1] [2] [3]. Physiologists investigate oxygen transport from capillaries measuring 5 to 15 µm in diameter to the surrounding tissue [4] [5] [6], while anatomists characterize the palmar arches and digital arteries supplying the skin [7, 8]. Despite these complementary perspectives, key microvascular structures and features, including capillary loops, the papillary plexus and the distinction between normal and abnormal nailfold architecture, remain difficult to visualize. Individual capillaries cannot be resolved using conventional clinical radiography [9, 10], are generally inaccessible to surface microscopy outside the nailfold, and exhibit variability that cannot be adequately represented by a single static diagram.

These limitations reflect a broader challenge in integrating anatomical information across spatial scales and imaging modalities. Cadaveric dissection reveals macroscopic vascular anatomy but does not readily demonstrate individual capillary loops. Two-dimensional atlases typically present the palmar arterial system and microvascular images separately, with limited spatial correspondence between them. Clinical nailfold video capillaroscopy provides magnified views of the living nailfold, but its use depends on dedicated equipment, operator expertise and standardized acquisition procedures, including the fourfield protocol considered here. Consequently, conventional anatomical resources and clinical capillaroscopy offer limited support for continuous exploration from a digital artery to the capillary bed it supplies.

Computational anatomy provides a potential framework for connecting these scales. Open mesh collections, including BodyParts3D and Z-Anatomy, contain named three-dimensional models of the hand and forearm [11, 12]. Browser-based hand tracking can estimate a pose comprising 21 landmarks from a single RGB frame [13], while Canvas2D and WebGL support the real-time rendering of layered anatomical annotations. Integrating these capabilities into a coherent interactive interface also requires clear differentiation among measured structures, anatomical templates, sampled features and simulated phenomena. Recent preprints describing browser-based anatomy instruments provide related examples at comparable levels of evidence in anatomy and biomedical visualization [14, 15]. Microcirculation Mirror was developed to investigate whether a browser-based instrument could combine these capabilities within a single HTML file while preserving anatomical relationships and explicitly communicating the limitations of its visual representations. The application registers skin, muscle, tendon, bone, nerve, artery, capillary and vein layers to a tracked hand and provides a separate three-dimensional hand atlas derived from open meshes. It also renders a simulated nailfold video capillaroscopy field at 200-fold magnification with six selectable patterns, implements a lumped microcirculatory model, and uses attenuation and detector calculations to examine whether conventional clinical X-rays can resolve individual hand capillaries in vivo. The application is designed to operate offline on a laptop or mobile phone without installation or reliance on a server-side database.

Our work presents the architecture and technical characterization of Microcirculation Mirror as a research and demonstration prototype. Its contributions comprise a reproducible approach to poseregistered, multiscale microvascular anatomy in the browser, an open quantitative hand atlas that distinguishes mesh-derived anatomy from procedural microanatomy, and integrated simulation and imaging-physics modules that clarify both the basis and the limitations of the visualized information. Evaluation is based on technical characterization and worked examples, consistent with the scope of a methods and instrument description.

Results

A single-file instrument for multi-scale hand anatomy

The application is distributed as a single HTML file with embedded CSS and JavaScript and support for optional local assets, including a float16 hand-landmark model, six GLB layers per hand, and a compact Three.js build. It requires no runtime build step, package manager, or server-side component. The same file supports four operating modes, comprising a demonstration with an illustrative hand, camera-based tracking in which MediaPipe HandLandmarker estimates 21 landmarks [13], a full-screen three-dimensional atlas, and a microscopy and imaging-physics workspace (Fig. 1).

The interface maintains the hand as a shared spatial reference while allowing users to change anatomical layers and viewing scales. The main stage presents the hand, while a panel on the right provides controls for anatomical layers, visualization settings, physiology, and numerical readouts. Presets provide access to arterial, capillary, venous, musculoskeletal, and nailfold views. Additional viewing controls allow users to alternate among a whole-field view, a split view, a lens centered on the index fingertip, a depth section, and a nailfold lens. Selecting a visible vessel opens an inspector containing its anatomical name, vessel class, lumen diameter, depth, erythrocyte velocity, oxygen saturation (SO₂), wall shear stress, and segment length.

Rendering is organized into four coordinated canvases (Fig. 1c). The shading canvas renders the skin envelope or camera veil, and the anatomy canvas renders bones, muscles, tendons, nerves, and lymphatics. A WebGL or Canvas2D canvas renders arteries, capillaries, veins, and erythrocytes, while an annotation canvas presents labels, the viewing lens, scale bars, and the depth ruler. All four canvases use the same landmark-derived pose, allowing the rendered layers to move together as hand position changes. A lumped physiology model uses the shared application state to generate the nine numerical readouts presented in the control panel.

Pose-registered macro-anatomy.

The tracker estimates 21 landmarks in screen coordinates, from which the application constructs orthogonal coordinate frames for the palm, four bone groups per digit, and the forearm. The digital groups comprise 20 groups in total, with the thumb represented using its carpometacarpal-tometacarpophalangeal segment. These frames are scaled according to tracked joint distances and smoothed using a frame-rate-aware exponential filter. Temporary tracking losses are bridged for up to 18 frames before the overlay is cleared. The resulting anatomical representation is pose-registered rather than segmented from the camera image. Bones, muscles, tendons, and nerves are canonical templates whose geometry is derived from hand proportions (Fig. 2).

Anatomical layers can be viewed independently or in combination. The arterial layer extends from the radial and ulnar arteries through the superficial and deep palmar arches to the common and proper palmar digital arteries, arterioles, and capillary loops. The venous layer includes venules, dorsal digital veins, and the superficial and deep palmar venous networks. The musculoskeletal layer represents the thenar and hypothenar eminences, interossei, lumbricals, and flexor tendons, while the neural layer depicts schematic median and ulnar nerve trunks and their digital branches. An optional lymphatic layer provides a sparse, blind-ended network. The skin envelope can be rendered as either a translucent veil or an opaque silhouette. A depth-section view attenuates structures according to their modeled depth, while a circular lens centered on the index fingertip reveals the combined anatomical layers.

The vessel inspector provides quantitative information for individual vascular segments, including modeled lumen diameter, depth, erythrocyte velocity, oxygen saturation (SO₂), and wall shear stress.

Wall shear stress is calculated from the Poiseuille velocity profile as 𝜏 = 8𝜂𝑣/𝐷, with viscosity set to 𝜂 = 0.0035 Pa s. All values are model estimates, and the inspector allows users to examine differences across vascular scales, including the nearly two-order-of-magnitude difference in diameter between a 0.9-mm proper palmar digital artery and a 10-µm capillary, together with their corresponding modeled velocities.

The true-scale view relates anatomical dimensions to the magnification required to make small structures visible. In the default view, macrovascular diameters are shown at approximately 1-3 times their modeled size and capillary diameters at 30-60 times their modeled size, while the capillary bed is sampled well below its expected anatomical density. At a scale of 1:1, a 10-µm capillary occupies approximately 0.02-0.05 pixels on a typical screen and therefore becomes a subpixel structure. The resulting visualization illustrates a sampling limitation analogous to the detector-sampling constraints that, together with limited attenuation contrast, prevent conventional clinical radiography from resolving individual capillary loops in vivo.

An open-mesh 3D atlas with a procedural capillary layer

The 3D atlas is a separate full-screen viewer built from the Z-Anatomy collection, with geometry derived from BodyParts3D [16]. The hand subset comprises 136 named structures per hand across six anatomical systems, including 29 bones, 14 joint capsules and ligaments, 47 muscles, fasciae, and tendon sheaths, 13 arterial segments, 10 venous segments, and 23 nerve segments (Fig. 3). The skeletal structures include the radius, ulna, eight carpals, five metacarpals, and fourteen phalanges. Each structure is annotated with English and Chinese names and mesh statistics (Supplementary Fig. 6); 36 structures also have a Foundational Model of Anatomy (FMA) identifier, and 26 include a brief description. The left and right hands are extracted independently. The atlas contains seven independently controlled layers representing the skeleton, ligaments and joints, muscles and sheaths, arteries, veins, nerves, and procedural microvasculature. Visibility and opacity can be adjusted for each layer. A two-stage exploded-view control first separates anatomical systems and then individual structures, while a cutaway plane clips the model along the X, Y, or Z axis. Search supports English names, Chinese names, and FMA identifiers. Selecting a structure opens an inspector showing its name, anatomical system, FMA identifier, bounding-box dimensions, and mesh counts. Seven viewing presets, palmar, dorsal, radial, ulnar, proximal, distal, and oblique, and a 26second guided tour provide reproducible viewpoints. Switching between hands loads the corresponding set of six GLB files.

Neither source dataset includes capillary, arteriole, or venule meshes. The microvascular layer is therefore generated at runtime on the volar surfaces of the five distal phalanges, with approximately 12 loops per fingertip and 60 loops per hand. Loop geometry is informed by published morphometry, including a loop height of 278.5 ± 53.3 𝜇m and limb diameters of 5.6-13.4 µm from volar-digit measurements [17], together with an outer loop width of approximately 39 µm [18]. The layer supports magnifications of 1, 10, and 20 times and is labeled "sampled for legibility". At unit magnification, loop height and width follow the published dimensions and become subpixel at the viewing scale used, whereas tube diameter remains exaggerated at all magnifications to preserve visibility. The resulting geometry is an illustrative model.

Provenance is documented throughout the atlas. Macro-anatomical structures use reference mesh data, whereas microvascular structures are procedurally generated. Their respective origins are identified in the layer labels, structure inspector, and methods note, with supporting documentation in the repository (Supplementary Fig. 1 and Supplementary Table S1). The atlas combines reference anatomy and synthesized microvascular geometry within a shared environment while retaining visible information about the source and status of each layer.

Nailfold video capillaroscopy at 200×

The microscopy workspace renders a 200× nailfold videocapillaroscopy field with 1 mm = 500 px and a 1.2-mm field width (Fig. 4). Each loop is drawn as a hairpin tube with an arterial limb, a venous limb, an apical segment and a pale lumen; erythrocyte particles move at a nominal ×0.1 slow motion, decoupled from the reported velocity. A 1-mm ruler and a 200-µm sub-scale are always visible. The user can select a loop, read its number, width and erythrocyte velocity, and switch between hairpin, tortuous, crossed and branched morphologies. A Krogh field can be overlaid on the selected apex, with a radial PO₂ field and a PO 2 (𝑟) curve. A separate skin cross-section shows the epidermis, papillary dermis, subpapillary plexus, reticular dermis, deep dermal plexus and subcutaneous tissue over 0-2.5 mm, with capillary loops, an arteriole, a venule, a lymphatic and the Krogh field.

The module uses published reference values for normal capillary density of 7-10 loops/mm, loop length of 198-215 µm, outer loop width of approximately 39 µm, and limb diameters of 5.6-13.4 µm [17, 18]. Reference erythrocyte velocities are 0.84 ± 0.53 mm/sin the arterial limb and 0.47 ± 0.29 mm/sin the venous limb [19], and the giant-capillary threshold is set at 50 µm. The simulated field represents a single illustrative 1.2-mm window rather than the four-field clinical acquisition protocol. The overlaid Krogh field is a normalized approximation for visualization; the analytical Krogh solution is presented separately in the Methods and Fig. 4d for comparison.

Six deterministic microvascular patterns

The pattern panel and the 3D capillary layer share six simulated profiles representing healthy microvasculature, nonspecific Raynaud-associated changes, early, active, and late systemic sclerosis (SSc) patterns, and diabetes-associated changes (Fig. 5). Each profile defines capillary density, the fractions of giant capillaries and avascular regions, hemorrhage count, tortuosity, and the fraction of ramified or bushy loops. A severity slider interpolates between the healthy baseline and the selected profile, and selecting a disease-associated pattern sets the slider to its nominal severity. Pattern generation is deterministic, with the selected profile, severity, and fixed seed producing the same field on repeated rendering. Users can therefore reproduce individual fields and compare profiles without variation from random resampling.

The healthy profile uses a visualization density of 9.2 loops/mm to generate the simulated field, whereas the physiology panel uses a separate default lumped estimate of 8.0 loops/mm. The pattern panel reports seven metrics, including capillary density (loops/mm), giant-capillary count, avascular width fraction (% of field width), expected hemorrhage count, tortuosity (% of loops), ramified or bushy loop count, and the fraction of abnormal loops. A rule-based classifier inspired by the Fast Track algorithm and the CAPI-score assigns labels of "normal", "non-specific", "SSc pattern-early", "SSc patternactive", "SSc pattern-late", or "diabetes-associated microangiopathy", each accompanied by the qualifier "simulated" [20, 21]. The classification rules are simplified for illustration and do not constitute a validated implementation of either method. Automated nailfold videocapillaroscopy (NVC) analysis remains an active area of research [22].

The Raynaud and diabetes profiles include small giant-capillary fractions as visualization parameters. These parameters are not used to assign an SSc classification to either profile. Contrast and spatial sampling limits of conventional clinical X-ray imaging for hand capillaries

The physics module evaluates whether conventional clinical X-ray imaging can resolve individual capillaries in the living hand. Under the imaging conditions considered, capillary visibility is limited by insufficient attenuation contrast and inadequate spatial sampling (Fig. 7). For unenhanced blood, the primary limitation is the small attenuation difference between blood and surrounding soft tissue. At 60 keV, their mass attenuation coefficients are approximately 0.2053 and 0.2025 cm 2 /g , respectively, corresponding to a difference of approximately 1.4% [9]. With respective densities of 1.06 and 1.03 g/ cm 3 , the linear attenuation coefficients differ by approximately 0.009 cm -1 , or 4.4%. A 1-mm column of unenhanced blood therefore produces approximately 0.09% transmission contrast, whereas a 10-µm capillary produces approximately 9 × 10 -4 % before pixel averaging. Averaging within a 150-µm pixel reduces the estimated contrast to approximately 6 × 10 -5 %.

Iodinated contrast material at 350 mg I/mL increases the linear attenuation difference to approximately 2.44 cm -1 , more than two orders of magnitude above that of unenhanced blood. The corresponding transmission contrasts are approximately 21.7% for a 1-mm vessel, 4.8% for a 200-µm vessel, and 0.24% for a 10-µm vessel. After averaging within a 150-µm pixel, the estimated contrast of the 10-µm vessel falls to approximately 0.016%, about 60 times below the representative 1% DSA detection threshold used in the analysis. Contrast enhancement therefore does not overcome the combined effects of small vessel diameter and pixel averaging under these conditions. Spatial sampling imposes an additional constraint. Clinical digital radiography and computed radiography detectors typically have pixel pitches of 100-200 µm, corresponding to Nyquist frequencies of 2.5-5 line pairs/mm. Sampling a 10-µm vessel across two pixels would require a pixel pitch of no more than 5 µm, corresponding to approximately 100 line pairs/mm and a sampling frequency 20-40 times higher than that of these clinical detectors. DSA can demonstrate arteries approximately 200 µm in diameter [26], but does not resolve individual capillary loops measuring 5-15 µm. Clinical CT generally provides coarser spatial resolution of approximately 0.5-0.7 mm, although ultra-high-resolution photoncounting CT can approach 150 µm. In the modeled CT scenario, partial-volume dilution reduces the signal from a 39-µm loop to well below 1 HU, beneath typical CT noise levels. Even the less stringent criterion of separating adjacent loops remains unmet under the assumed clinical sampling conditions. An inter-capillary spacing of approximately 143 µm requires sampling at intervals of no more than 72 µm, corresponding to approximately 7 line pairs/mm and exceeding the sampling capabilities considered for clinical DR/CR, DSA, and CT.

The module compares radiography, DSA, phase-contrast/micro-CT, NVC, OCT/OCTA, SDF/IDF, laser speckle contrast, NIRS/diffuse optics, photoacoustic microscopy and ultrasound localization microscopy on a resolution-depth map (Fig. 7c). Optical methods (NVC, OCT/OCTA, SDF/IDF) and, in research settings, photoacoustic microscopy and ultrasound localization microscopy can reach the capillary scale in vivo [27]. Research-grade X-ray phase-contrast and micro-CT can reach micrometre voxels in small excised samples [28], but their true resolution is coarser than their voxel size, they are not routine clinical hand imaging, and, to our knowledge, no published study has demonstrated individual capillary loops in a living hand. Conventional radiography, DSA and clinical CT do not resolve 5-15-µm loops in routine hand imaging. The true-scale view illustrates this spatial constraint directly on the hand, where individual capillaries occupy less than one pixel and are labeled as subpixel structures. Together, the resolution-depth comparison and true-scale visualization provide a reproducible quantitative analysis of imaging limitations without relying on patient data or making claims of clinical validation.

Deployment, performance and the phone

The application is a single-file, client-side system. In camera mode, frames are processed in the browser and are never uploaded; there is no server-side database and no patient data path. For phone use, serve.py -lan -https binds all interfaces, generates a self-signed certificate containing the LAN IPs, prints a phone URL and serves a mobile access page with a QR code, a device preflight (HTTPS, camera, WebGL, touch, installability), a one-minute setup and troubleshooting (Fig. 8). LAN mode generates a one-time token by default; the QR code carries it, and the first visit exchanges it for an HttpOnly cookie. The site is installable as a PWA, with an app-shell cache for offline demonstration.

The phone layout is the same application, not a separate build. It detects a phone viewport and rearranges the masthead, stage, presets, tabs and controls; the stage can expand to full screen; touch gestures support vessel picking (22 px tolerance), nailfold pinch-zoom and pan, and atlas orbit/pinch; and the camera preview uses an object-fit: cover landmark mapping so the overlay stays registered when the stage is taller than the video frame (Supplementary Fig. 5). Performance controls include device-pixelratio caps, low-power blur reduction, off-screen and hidden-tab pauses, microscopy rendering gated to its visible tab, a 960×540 phone video stream, and a merged six-draw-call atlas tier.

On the 2D stage, a frame with about 930 vessel paths plus anatomy renders in 2.9 ms; the nailfold field renders in 1.0 ms; the frame budget at 60 fps is 16.7 ms (Fig. 1d). In the 3D atlas, merging the six anatomical systems into six GPU meshes reduces anatomy draw calls from 136 to 6 (35 total including capillary patches, rings, feeding vessels and helpers), while retaining per-structure selection through invisible pick proxies. Render-on-demand means the atlas does not redraw when the camera and layers are still. On a software rasteriser (SwiftShader), the atlas remains CPU-limited; the performance path reduces draw calls and idle work but does not make software rasterisation equivalent to a hardware GPU. The performance numbers are engineering benchmarks, not clinical performance claims (Supplementary Fig. 3).

Discussion

Microcirculation Mirror integrates multiscale anatomical exploration, representation of evidence provenance, and imaging-physics calculations within a browser-based instrument. These capabilities address a challenge in microvascular visualization, namely the difficulty of relating macroscopic vascular anatomy to microscopic structures while preserving spatial context and communicating the evidential basis of each representation. The principal contribution is therefore not any individual visualization, but an integrated environment in which users can examine anatomical relationships, explore simulated mechanisms, and inspect the limitations of the displayed information.

The instrument supports continuous exploration across anatomical scales. Users can begin with the palmar arches on a tracked hand, follow the digital arteries, visualize the capillary bed, and open a simulated nailfold field. Enabling the true-scale view illustrates how individual capillaries become smaller than a single pixel. A shared hand reference, coordinate framework, and interaction model connect structures spanning nearly three orders of magnitude in vessel diameter. Unlike static illustrations or conventional atlases, the integrated environment maintains spatial and interactive continuity as users move between scales. Pose registration therefore offers a means of linking anatomical levels that are typically presented separately, although its educational value remains to be established empirically.

An equally important feature is the clear identification of information sources and their evidential status. The interface differentiates tracked hand pose from template anatomy, mesh-derived structures from procedural capillaries, published reference values from model estimates, and simulated patterns from patient images. Information about provenance is integral to interpreting the visualizations, not merely supplementary annotation. Labeling bones as templates, the capillary bed as a sampled representation, and the Krogh field as a normalized visual approximation helps prevent visual realism from being mistaken for subject-specific accuracy. Simulation makes selected mechanisms and assumptions available for examination, while source labels clarify that the visualizations do not represent clinical measurements.

The integration of imaging physics extends the instrument beyond anatomical visualization. The limitations of conventional clinical radiography are examined through attenuation coefficients, detector sampling, and true-scale visualization. Under the assumptions used in the module, a 10-µm capillary occupies approximately 0.02-0.05 pixels at true scale, while its effective contrast after pixel averaging is approximately 0.016% when filled with iodinated contrast material; the corresponding contrast for unenhanced blood is approximately 270 times lower. These estimates illustrate how vessel dimensions, attenuation differences, and spatial sampling jointly constrain visibility. They also contextualize the use of nailfold video capillaroscopy, optical coherence tomography, and side stream dark-field imaging for microvascular observation, alongside emerging applications of photoacoustic imaging and ultrasound localization microscopy. The calculations apply to the specified conventional clinical imaging conditions and do not establish a universal limit on X-ray visualization of capillary-scale vessels. These contributions must be interpreted within the scope of a prototype that has neither incorporated patient data nor undergone clinical validation. The anatomical layers are pose-registered templates, the vascular network is schematic, and the capillary bed is sampled at a density substantially below that of the represented tissue. The nailfold visualization is limited to a single illustrative 1.2-mm field and does . not constitute a complete four-field clinical acquisition. The six simulated patterns are generated from literature-informed parameters, with no patient measurements used in their construction. Physiological outputs are derived from a lumped model without individual calibration, and the microvascular flow index and heterogeneity index are empirical approximations. The application is not a medical device and must not be used for diagnosis, screening, risk stratification, or treatment decisions. These limitations informed the interface design, particularly the separation of tracked, template-based, and simulated content.

The present evaluation is restricted to technical characterization, including structural coverage, frame time, draw calls, file size, imaging-physics calculations, and simulator determinism. It does not establish usability, educational benefit, or agreement with a clinical reference standard. Further evaluation could compare learning outcomes or task performance between users of the instrument and users of a conventional atlas. Think-aloud studies could examine how users interpret true-scale views and whether provenance labels support accurate understanding of reference anatomy and simulated content. Expert review of the simulated nailfold patterns could assess their morphological plausibility and consistency with the intended categories, but would not constitute clinical validation of the classification system. Landmark registration should also be evaluated against an independent marker-based reference. Deterministic pattern generation, structured JSON export, and single-file deployment provide a basis for reproducible testing, although these studies have not yet been conducted.

Greater anatomical fidelity represents another direction for development. The current instrument combines open reference meshes for macro-anatomy with procedural representations of microanatomy. Incorporating subject-specific optical coherence tomography or nailfold video capillaroscopy data, or replacing the procedural capillary bed with a micro-CT-derived scaffold, would move the corresponding layer toward an image-derived anatomical model. These extensions would require revised provenance labels and validation procedures appropriate to the acquisition and reconstruction methods. The modular capillary layer and system-level provenance metadata provide an architectural basis for incorporating acquired nailfold fields, segmented optical coherence tomography volumes, and patient-specific meshes while preserving information about the origin and processing of acquired and simulated content.

The deployment approach may also broaden access to computational anatomy. A single HTML file designed to run offline on a laptop or phone, without installation or a patient-data workflow, reduces some of the infrastructural requirements associated with conventional visualization platforms. The documented deployment pathway using a local network, HTTPS, token-based access, and progressive web application functionality is intended to support reproducible demonstrations. It allows the same instrument to be shared with research or clinical teams without requiring a vendor platform, license server, or cloud account. The MIT-licensed code and CC BY-SA atlas further support reuse within their respective licensing conditions. Taken together, these features position Microcirculation Mirror as a research and demonstration instrument and as an architectural reference for computational anatomy projects that seek to combine interactive visualization with communication of evidential limits.

Limitations

Microcirculation Mirror remains a research and demonstration prototype with limitations in anatomical sampling, model formulation, and technical implementation. The macroscopic view includes . approximately 12 capillary loops per fingertip and 60 per hand, a sparse representation that does not reproduce tissue-level capillary density or network topology. The rule-based categorization of the six simulated nailfold patterns is not a validated implementation of Fast Track or CAPI-score and is intended only for illustration. The visualized Krogh field is normalized and radially exaggerated, with the analytical solution provided separately for comparison; its spatial extent and gradients therefore do not constitute quantitative predictions of tissue oxygenation. The empirical microvascular flow and heterogeneity indices have likewise not been validated against measurement-based indices. Atlas rendering remains constrained by CPU rasterization, and the optimized rendering path does not eliminate the need for GPU acceleration for efficient performance. The application supports tracking of only one hand at a time, while camera-based operation on mobile phones requires WebGL and HTTPS. Single-file deployment simplifies distribution but does not guarantee equivalent functionality or performance across devices and browsers.

Software architecture

The application is a single HTML file assembled from modular JavaScript sources by tools/build.py. It uses no runtime framework. The 2D stage is rendered with Canvas2D across four stacked canvases (shade, anatomy, vessels, HUD); the 3D atlas uses Three.js r140 with OrbitControls and GLTFLoader. Hand tracking uses MediaPipe Tasks Vision 0.10.14 [29] with a local float16 hand_landmarker.task model and a CDN fallback. The 3D atlas uses Three.js [30]; MP4 export uses a local H.264 WASM encoder [31]. The project began as a migration of the single-file NeuroMirror viewer [32]. The code is MIT-licensed.

Hand tracking and coordinate frames

MediaPipe returns 21 landmarks per hand in normalized image coordinates. The application mirrors the x coordinate for the front camera, converts to display coordinates, applies an object-fit: cover mapping when the video aspect differs from the stage, and smooths the landmarks with an exponential filter whose time constant is frame-rate aware. From the smoothed landmarks it builds a palm frame (wrist, index MCP, little MCP), sixteen bone frames (four per finger) and a forearm frame. Each frame is an orthonormal basis scaled by the distance between its defining joints. Detections are considered lost after 18 consecutive frames without a hand; the overlay is then cleared.

Anatomical templates and atlas extraction

The camera overlay's anatomy is generated procedurally from canonical hand proportions and the tracked frames. The 3D atlas is extracted from the Z-Anatomy Startup.blend (Blender 3.6.5) with a righthand region 𝑥 ∈ [-0.38, -0.15], 𝑦 ∈ [-0.12,0.10], 𝑧 ∈ [0.69,1.00] m and a left-hand region mirrored in x. Mesh and curve objects in the Skeletal, Joints, Muscular, Cardiovascular and Nervous collections are evaluated, world transforms are baked, the proximal end is clipped at 𝑧 = 1.00 m, and one GLB is exported per system with one node per structure. Metadata are written to atlas-meta.json; the left-hand set is a separate extraction. The capillary layer is generated at run time from the literature parameters in Supplementary Fig. 2b.

Nailfold simulator

The NVC field is 1.2 mm wide with 1 mm = 500 px. Loop spacing is 1/𝜌, where 𝜌 is the pattern density in loops/mm; avascular gaps are one to four contiguous intervals whose total width is proportional to the avascular fraction; each loop receives a seeded morphology (hairpin, tortuous, bushy, ramified or giant); giant loops are forced for SSc patterns; haemorrhages are drawn as sub-papillary deposits; and loop width, height and perfusion are modulated by the physiological state. The random seed is deterministic per pattern and index. Metrics are computed from the generated field and reported with the pattern profile, reference ranges and a non-diagnostic disclaimer.
