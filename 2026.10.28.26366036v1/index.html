Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Abstract

Background. Medication errors caused by confusion between similar drug names (look-alike/soundalike, or LASA, errors) have been reported for decades, but the underlying case reports have never been assembled into a public, machine-readable dataset. Between 2002 and 2005 we compiled such a collection from the published literature and from the United States Pharmacopeia (USP) Medication Errors Reporting (MER) Program. It remained in five unreleased Microsoft Access databases.

Methods. In 2026 we exported and reconciled the five databases into case-level and pair-level files. We mapped drug names to RxNorm and to WHO Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical (ATC) classes, computed five orthographic similarity measures, and recorded membership on the FDA and ISMP lists of drug names with recommended Tall Man letters. A limited PubMed search added pairs reported from June 2005 to February 2026. We describe the collection and compare the two sources.

Results. The dataset contains 709 literature case records , 753 USP reports (1997)(1998)(1999)(2000)(2001)(2002)(2003), and 17 post-2005 records, covering 724 confused drug name pairs. Only 69 of the 708 original pairs appeared in both sources. Published cases were more severe than voluntary reports: among records with a coded outcome, 15.2% of literature records and 1.2% of USP reports were fatal. Similarity measures discriminated reported pairs from random re-pairings of the same names (area under the ROC curve 0.88-0.92), but 30% of pairs had relatively low measured similarity (BI-SIM below 0.40). Of the pairs with at least three records or at least one fatal-coded record, 122 appear on neither Tall Man list. Automated approximate matching of drug names to RxNorm, used in building the dataset, itself produced nameconfusion errors of the kind the dataset records; these were found by manual review and removed.

Conclusions. This reconstructed collection is a public historical record of drug name confusion through 2004. It shows how little published and voluntarily reported confusions overlap. It can serve as a benchmark for name-similarity methods and as a starting point for review of confusable names.

1. Introduction

Drug name confusion is a recognized source of wrong-drug errors. Names that are similar in spelling and sound are more likely to be confused [4]. Regulators and safety organizations respond with name review before approval and with interventions such as Tall Man (mixed-case) lettering [7]. Research on these problems needs examples of names that have actually been confused, with as much detail about each error as the sources allow.

Two kinds of source exist. Published case reports describe individual errors, often in detail, but favor serious and unusual events. Voluntary reporting programs, such as the USP MER Program, collect many more events, including near misses, but each report is brief. To our knowledge no public case-level collection combines the two.

Toh's thesis [8] assembled such a collection: case reports extracted systematically from the published literature, combined with USP MER Program reports obtained for research use. The thesis analyzed the data, but the databases themselves were never released. This paper describes the reconstruction of those databases into a public dataset, reports what the reconstructed data show about the two sources, and documents the dataset's construction and limitations for reuse.

2. Methods

>Methods

2.1 Original data collection (2002-2005)

Toh collected the data for a Master's thesis [8], supervised by Lambert and supported by AHRQ grant R01 HS011609.

Literature cases.

Case reports were identified by searching PubMed/MEDLINE, International Pharmaceutical Abstracts, Embase, and Google for 1965 to May 2005, using combinations of terms for drug name confusion, medication error, look-alike, and sound-alike. Each article was read in full and its cases coded into an Access database with a standardized coding manual. Coded variables included the two names, which drug was intended and which was given, setting, the roles of the people who made and discovered the error, patient demographics, the NCC MERP outcome category [9], and the reference. A case that involved more than two names was coded once for each confused pair, under a shared case number.

Two additional raters independently coded a random subsample. The variables central to this dataset (the two drug names and the direction of the error) met the preset thresholds of more than 80% agreement and Cohen's kappa above 0.70, as did the patient and case-report descriptors. Thirteen of the 32 coded variables fell below those thresholds, mostly the more judgment-dependent error and product fields (type of error, outcome category, person who made the error, setting, and origin of error) [8]. All discrepancies were resolved by consensus before the database was finalized, so the released values are reconciled codings, not independent ones.

USP MER Program reports.

The USP/ISMP MER Program is a voluntary system through which health professionals report actual and potential medication errors [1, 8]. In October 2002 Lambert asked USP in writing for research access to name-confusion reports. USP agreed [2] and delivered an extract in 2004. The reports describe error events and contain no patient identifiers.

USP supplied the extract already restricted to name-confusion reports; we applied no further eligibility filter. The file had 4,173 rows covering 753 reports, usually one row per drug, with extra rows where a report listed several strengths or dosage forms. We kept the first row for each combination of report and drug position (1,506 rows) and combined the two drug positions into one row per report, giving 753 reports and 424 unique pairs. Alternate strengths and dosage forms listed within a report are therefore not represented at the pair level (347 of 1,506 drug positions listed more than one product row).

The reports span ten USP error types: brand names look alike (385), generic names look alike (91), nomenclature (90), brand names sound alike (73), suffix problems (35), brand and generic names look alike (35), generic names sound alike (14), brand and generic names sound alike (13), name similar to a former name (10), and prefix problems (7). The nomenclature, suffix, prefix, and former-name types include errors in which a single name was ambiguous across products. These produce ten pairs with the same name in both positions (e.g., Prelone syrup released in a second strength without a distinguishing name).

2.2 Reconstruction and reconciliation (2026)

The five Access databases overlapped. We exported all tables to CSV and reconciled them as follows.

1. DrugNameConfusions, the primary database: 695 verified literature case records in 22 related tables. Foreign keys were resolved to readable values. 2. Literature-based Case Reports, an earlier thesis snapshot of 584 cases, all contained in DrugNameConfusions. Kept for provenance only. 3. Lambert working set: 95 cases compiled by Lambert from ISMP Medication Safety Alerts (2002) (2003) (2004), medication error publications by Cohen and Davis (1991-1994), Teplitsky (1994), and other sources. Matching on drug pair and case details found 80 already in DrugNameConfusions; of the 15 others, one was a duplicate on manual review, so 14 records were added (695 +14 = 709). 4. USP Case Reports: the 753 reports described above. 5. Canonical pair list: the thesis master list of 367 pairs, expanded to 708 by taking the union with all pairs in the literature (353) and USP (424) case files.

Pair keys were built from alphabetically sorted, lowercased, whitespace-trimmed names so that formatting differences would not create duplicates. Pairs are unordered.

Post-2005 search.

In February 2026 we searched PubMed for June 2005 to February 2026 with the query ("drug name" OR "medication name" OR "look-alike" OR "sound-alike" OR "LASA") AND ("confusion" OR "error" OR "mix-up" OR "wrong drug") AND ("case report" OR "case study" OR "incident"). Of 267 results, title and abstract screening rated 29 as likely and 78 as possible case reports. The likely reports yielded 16 new pairs (17 records), none already in the dataset. This search was a limited addition, not an update of the original review.

2.3 Enrichment

All enrichment used Python scripts, which are included in the deposit. API responses are cached so that reruns do not depend on the current state of the APIs. Enrichment was run on the 708 original pairs; the 16 post-2005 pairs were added afterward.

RxNorm. Each of the 988 distinct names in the original pairs was looked up with the NLM RxNorm API [3]: exact match first, then approximate match (accepted with a score of at least 5.0), then a match after stripping common salt suffixes. For brand names, the generic ingredient was resolved through RxNorm's ingredient relations. All accepted matches were then reviewed by hand (Section 2.5).

ATC classification.

ATC codes for RxNorm-mapped drugs were obtained from the NLM RxClass API [6] at Level 4. For each pair we recorded whether the two drugs share a class at Levels 1-4.

Orthographic similarity.

Five measures were computed for all pairs: BI-SIM: Kondrak's n-gram similarity [10], which aligns overlapping bigrams by dynamic programming with partial credit for character matches, normalized by the length of the longer string. Bigram-based measures have been used to study drug name confusion since the 1990s [4].

Bigram Dice coefficient: 2 × shared bigrams / (bigrams in name 1 + bigrams in name 2).

Normalized edit similarity:

1 -(Levenshtein distance / length of the longer name). Jaro-Winkler similarity [5], which gives extra weight to a shared prefix.

Trigram Dice coefficient: the Dice coefficient on character trigrams.

Tall Man lists.

Each pair was checked against the lists published together by ISMP [7]: the FDAapproved list of established names with Tall Man letters (24 pairs) and the combined list of FDA and ISMP-recommended pairs (99 pairs, which includes the FDA pairs).

2.4 Analysis

We report counts and percentages. Severity distributions are given separately for each source, excluding literature records whose outcome could not be assigned an NCC MERP category. Because a literature case involving several names is coded once per pair, case and death columns count records, not distinct incidents or patients. We also give the number of distinct case numbers.

To check whether the similarity measures separate confused pairs from arbitrary ones, we built 2,124 control pairs (three per original pair) by randomly re-pairing names from the 708 original pairs. Self-pairs and pairs that appear in the dataset were excluded. We report the area under the ROC curve (AUC) for each measure. This benchmark compares reported pairs with random pairs drawn from the same vocabulary. It does not estimate how well a measure would flag confusable names prospectively; random pairs are likely easier to separate than the plausible alternatives a screening method must handle.

2.5 Technical validation

Row counts were checked against the totals recorded in the thesis databases (695 literature cases, 4,173 USP rows, 367 pairs), and ten random rows were inspected after each transformation.

In July 2026 the dataset was audited twice: first by OpenAI Codex, then by a separate Claude session that checked each of Codex's claims against the data files. Six defects were confirmed and corrected, and the reported figures were re-derived from the released files (37 of 37 checks passed). In October 2026 a second independent review (OpenAI Codex) recomputed every number in the draft of this paper from the released files, and all five similarity scores for all 724 pairs; all reproduced. It found that pair names differing in capitalization from the drug dictionary had lost their RxNorm and ATC fields at the pair level (24 pairs). These were filled from the dictionary. It also identified the record-versus-incident distinction described in Section 2.4, a personal name in one literature reference (now withheld), and several inaccurate codebook descriptions, all corrected.

Drug name confusion in the matching step.

A manual review of every accepted RxNorm match then found 24 that resolved to a different product: 22 of the 52 approximate matches and 2 of the 566 exact matches. Several were look-alike errors of exactly the kind this dataset records. Nolvadex (tamoxifen) was matched to Nolvadent, Lidex (fluocinonide) to Bidex, Primacor (milrinone) to Primacur, and IL-II (interleukin-2) to angiotensin II. In some cases a name was matched to the very drug it had been confused with in the source report: Ultracef (cefadroxil) to Ultracet, E-Vista (hydroxyzine) to Evista, and camphorated tincture of opium to opium tincture. These errors had passed both audits, which confirmed that the figures reproduced but not that each match identified the right drug. The 24 names are now treated as unmatched, with the reason for each recorded in the deposited correction script, and all figures below reflect the correction. The released USP file was compared column by column with the internal file; it differs only by two withheld columns (practitioners' free-text narratives and USP accession numbers), and no remaining text field exceeds 100 characters.

3. Results

>Results

3.1 Composition

The dataset contains 1,479 case records: 709 from the literature, 753 USP reports, and 17 from the post-2005 search (Table 1). The 709 literature records come from 679 distinct case numbers. They cover 724 confused pairs and 1,036 distinct names (1,016 ignoring case). The two sources overlapped little. Of the 708 original pairs, 284 appeared only in the literature, 355 only in USP reports, and 69 in both. Most pairs were reported once; 56 had five or more records. Table 2 lists the most frequently reported pairs.

3.2 Severity

Outcomes differed sharply between the sources (Table 3). Of the 409 literature records with a coded outcome, 60.9% involved harm (categories E-I) and 15.2% were fatal. Of the 753 USP reports, 12.5% involved harm and 1.2% were fatal, while 32.0% were category A (circumstances with capacity to cause error). For 300 literature records no outcome category could be coded.

There were 72 fatal-coded records: 62 in the literature, 9 in USP reports, and 1 from the post-2005 search. The 62 literature records come from 54 distinct case numbers. Thirty-nine pairs have at least one fatalcoded record; the pairs with the most are chlorpromazine/chlorpropamide (6), Isordil/Plendil (5), amiodarone/amrinone (4), and Lasix/Losec (4).

3.3 Orthographic similarity

Mean BI-SIM was 0.500 (SD 0.199) for confused pairs (0.493 excluding the ten same-name pairs) and 0.192 for control pairs. All five measures discriminated the 708 original pairs from controls: AUC 0.920 for BI-SIM, 0.917 for trigram Dice, 0.912 for normalized edit similarity, 0.909 for Jaro-Winkler, and 0.878 for bigram Dice. Pairs found in both sources had higher mean BI-SIM (0.559) than pairs found only in the literature (0.532) or only in USP reports (0.470).

Similarity was far from uniform: 221 of the 724 pairs (30%) had BI-SIM below 0.40. These are reported confusions with relatively low measured orthographic similarity; the data do not show why they were confused.

3.4 Therapeutic class and ingredient

Both drugs had an ATC class in 188 pairs. Of these, 51.6% shared a Level 1 group (anatomical main group), 30.9% Level 2, 26.1% Level 3, and 21.8% Level 4. Twenty-six pairs resolved to the same ingredient in RxNorm, that is, different products of the same drug (e.g., Depo-Medrol and Solu-Medrol).

3.5 Tall Man lists

Nine pairs are on the FDA Tall Man list, and 33 on the combined FDA and ISMP list, including those nine. Pairs on the combined list had more records on average than other pairs (4.3 vs 1.9). Another 122 pairs had at least three records or at least one fatal-coded record but appear on neither list. They include 34 of the 39 pairs with a fatal-coded record, among them Isordil/Plendil, amiodarone/amrinone, and Lasix/Losec.

4. Discussion

This collection documents drug name confusions published in English-language literature through 2004 or reported to USP in 1997-2003. Reconstructing it shows three things.

First, the two sources capture largely different confusions. Only 69 of 708 pairs appear in both, and the published cases are far more severe: 15.2% of coded literature records were fatal, against 1.2% of USP reports. Published reports favor serious and unusual outcomes, while voluntary reports capture more near misses. Neither source alone describes the range of name confusion, and neither supports estimates of how often errors occur.

Second, orthographic similarity separates reported pairs from random ones, as earlier work found [4], but many reported confusions involve names with low measured similarity. A screening method based on spelling alone would miss these pairs. The causes of these errors, such as sound, context, packaging, or handwriting, cannot be determined from the records here.

Third, regulatory Tall Man lists cover a small part of the reported confusions. Thirty-four of the 39 pairs with a fatal-coded record are on neither the FDA nor the combined FDA and ISMP list. Some of these names were later withdrawn or changed; amrinone, for example, was renamed inamrinone because of its confusion with amiodarone [1]. List inclusion also reflects considerations other than report counts, so this is not by itself evidence that the lists are incomplete. It does identify pairs that merit review.

The construction of the dataset itself illustrates the problem. Approximate string matching, a reasonable way to tolerate misspellings, mapped Nolvadex to Nolvadent and Ultracef to Ultracet. A similarity score above a threshold is not evidence that two names refer to the same drug, for software any more than for a pharmacist, and checks that only confirm that results reproduce will not catch it.

Limitations.

The collection is a historical record. It reflects what was published through 2004 and reported to USP in 1997-2003, and it makes no claim to be current. The 16 post-2005 pairs come from a single PubMed search with title and abstract screening only; IPA, Embase, ISMP alerts, and FDA sources were not searched. Well-known later confusions are therefore missing; for example, Brilinta (ticagrelor) and Brintellix (vortioxetine), the subject of a 2015 FDA safety communication and a later Sentinel analysis [11], are not included. The literature is restricted to English-language publications. Case records are not independent. A case involving several names is coded once per pair, and some case reports describe several incidents. Record counts should not be read as counts of incidents or patients, and frequency comparisons across pairs should account for this dependence. Thirteen of the 32 literature coding variables did not reach the preset reliability thresholds (Section 2.1).

RxNorm coverage is 60.1% and ATC coverage 38.4%. Many unmatched names appear to be older brands, misspellings, or products not marketed in the United States. Each brand was resolved to a single ingredient, so combination products carry only one of their ingredients (Percocet, for example, is recorded as acetaminophen), and ATC classes include classes that RxClass assigns to an ingredient through combination products. The ingredient and class-sharing fields are therefore approximate. RxNorm describes current products, and a brand name reused for a reformulated product may map to the wrong ingredient for the era of a report; we found and removed one such case (Kaopectate) but cannot rule out others. The enrichment and ROC analysis cover the 708 original pairs; for the 16 post-2005 pairs, ATC sharing below Level 1 was not computed.

The counts differ from those in the thesis, which reports 1,675 screened articles, 777 case reports, and 370 pairs. This archive has 1,664 bibliographic records, 709 literature case records, and 367 pairs in the thesis master list. The differences come from the thesis-era restriction to verified cases and late deduplication; the original case-by-case exclusion decisions could not be recovered.

Reuse.

Possible uses include benchmarking name-similarity and name-screening methods against historically confused pairs, studying confused pairs with low orthographic similarity, comparing published and voluntarily reported errors, and identifying candidate pairs for Tall Man lettering or other interventions.

9. Use of generative AI

The 2026 archiving work was carried out with Claude Code (Anthropic; Claude Opus models), working under the direction of the first author. Claude Code wrote the Python code that exported the Access databases, reconciled them, queried the RxNorm and RxClass APIs, computed the similarity measures, and built the master files. It also drafted the codebook, the README, and this paper. OpenAI Codex and separate Claude sessions were used to audit the data and the manuscript (Section 2.5).

The RxNorm matching code written by Claude Code accepted approximate matches that confused one drug name with another (Section 2.5). A later Claude Code session found these errors on manual review, and the first author reviewed the corrections. We report this because it is an instance of the error the dataset documents, and because neither the code's author nor two audits caught it until each match was checked by hand.
