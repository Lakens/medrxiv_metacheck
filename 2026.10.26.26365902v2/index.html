Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 225 papers containing 405 instances of non-exact p-values, the module correctly detected 269 cases (true positives) and incorrectly identified 78 (false positives). It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. Abstract

Background and Objectives: Despite standard genetic testing, approximately two-thirds of patients with developmental and epileptic encephalopathy (DEE) remain undiagnosed. Pathogenic noncoding and structural variants (SVs), that are difficult to detect or resolve using short-read sequencing, may contribute to this diagnostic gap. To evaluate the diagnostic contribution of long-read genome sequencing (lrGS), we recruited a cohort of 124 families with sporadic DEE who remained unsolved by prior ES.

Methods: Trio lrGS (Oxford Nanopore or PacBio HiFi) was performed, complemented by short-read genome sequencing (srGS) and mRNA sequencing (blood or fibroblast) of index cases, where available. Comprehensive variant analysis included small nucleotide variants, indels, SVs, and short tandem repeats. To identify the diagnostic yield of each method, we compared lrGS, srGS and ES re-analysis, and evaluated the additional value of RNA sequencing.

Results: We established a diagnosis in 21 of 124 families (16.9%). Fourteen variants were retrospectively detectable in prior ES. Two cases with non-coding variants in SCN1A and RNU2-2 were detectable by both srGS and lrGS. Five SVs also required genome sequencing (5.6% of the total cohort), three of which could be detected, but not confidently resolved by srGS due to breakpoint complexity, repeat-mediated rearrangements, and large copy-neutral events: LrGS enabled accurate characterization of Alu-mediated CDKL5 and STXBP1 deletions and a MEF2C deletion with large inversion. A copy-neutral SCN1A inversion was well characterizable by both srGS and lrGS. One balanced translocation t(X;17) disrupting CDKL5 was resolved by lrGS (srGS unavailable). RNA sequencing confirmed pathogenicity of two SVs including t(X;17), the MEF2C deletion with inversion, and two CNVs. In addition, ZNF569 and ZNF570 emerged as candidate epilepsy genes from our cohort.

Discussion: In ES-negative DEE, the incremental diagnostic yield of genome sequencing was modest, while lrGS provided particular value for the structural resolution of complex rearrangements, especially those involving repetitive sequences. The finding that twothirds of diagnoses were retrospectively detectable in prior ES highlights the substantial value of systematic reanalysis.

[div-01]

Further gains from genome sequencing may increasingly depend on improved interpretation of non-coding variation and integration of long-readspecific information. Our findings indicate that complex SVs represent the class for which lrGS currently yields the most distinctive diagnostic information.

1. Introduction

Developmental and epileptic encephalopathies (DEEs) are a group of severe neurodevelopmental disorders characterized by often drug-resistant epilepsy, accompanied by varying degrees of developmental impairments. 1 They span clinically recognisable syndromes such as Dravet syndrome, Lennox-Gastaut syndrome, and infantile epileptic spasms syndrome. 2 While each syndrome is individually rare, their combined incidence is approximately 1 in 590 children. 3 Antiseizure medications reduce seizure frequency in only a subset of patients, leaving most children with severe seizures and profound developmental impairment, placing lasting burdens on families and caregivers. 4 Most DEEs have a genetic etiology. 5 Identifying this underlying cause can inform parents about prognosis, expected comorbidities, and recurrence risk in future pregnancies, and can increasingly guide selection of targeted therapies. 1 Genetic testing currently relies on a combination of exome sequencing (ES) and chromosomal microarray testing (CMA), yet these establish a diagnosis in fewer than half of patients, leaving the majority of children with DEE unsolved. 6 Several factors contribute to this diagnostic gap. First, pathogenic variants may reside in non-coding regions that are not captured by ES. 7 Secondly, complex structural variants (SVs) and balanced rearrangements contributing to disease aetiology go undetected by CMA. 8 Short-read genome sequencing (srGS) addresses some of these gaps, increasing yield by approximately 7%, 9 nevertheless remains limited for detection of large insertions, inversions, repeat-mediated and complex SVs. 10 Long-read genome sequencing (lrGS) addresses several of these limitations by generating reads that span entire structural variant loci and providing improved read alignment in complex regions, substantially enhancing SV discovery. 11, 12 One recent study increased the diagnostic yield by approximately 10% in a cohort of patients with rare disease using lrGS. 13 Since DEEs are genetically heterogeneous and may involve complex variants that are typically missed with srGS, they are well suited for lrGS approaches. 14 However, the clinical utility of lrGS in genetically unresolved DEE remains incompletely defined, particularly regarding which diagnostic findings genuinely require long-read sequencing versus systematic reanalysis of existing ES or short-read genome data.

Here, we applied trio lrGS to 124 families with DEE unsolved after ES, complemented by index srGS and, where available, index RNA sequencing (RNAseq). We evaluated the diagnostic contribution of lrGS across variant classes and specifically assessed whether identified variants could be detected or resolved using available ES and srGS data. Using two in-house developed variant detection pipelines, we demonstrate that trio-based lrGS can uncover clinically relevant variants across multiple variant classes, while providing particular added value for the detection and resolution of complex SVs that may remain difficult to characterize with conventional short-read approaches.

2. Methods

>Methods

Cohort description

For this collaborative European study, patients with DEE who remained without a molecular diagnosis after ES were selected for lrGS by clinical experts at participating centers. A prior ES was performed either through local diagnostic services or the Epi25 collaborative. 15 Onset of seizures occurred in the first year of life in 86 individuals, between 1 and 2 years in 32 individuals, and between 2 and 7 years in 6 individuals. The final cohort comprised 121 trios, 2 quartets, and 1 duo of DEE index patients and unaffected parents, recruited from February 2022 onwards.

Sequencing and data processing

Fresh EDTA blood samples were obtained from all index patients and parents, and genomic DNA was extracted for sequencing. Trio ONT lrGS was performed for 101 families (98 trios, 2 quartets, 1 duo) on the Oxford Nanopore PromethION 24 platform, achieving an average coverage of 32±18x (R10 flow cells 37±19x, R9 21±9x). The remaining 23 trios were contributed through the Solve-RD consortium and sequenced with PacBio HiFi (~10x coverage; eMethods 1). 16 Library kits and flow-cell chemistries for both platforms are provided in eMethods 2. SrGS was additionally available for 109 index cases (86 in the ONT cohort and all 23 PacBio trios;). Additionally, RNAseq was available for 97 index cases, comprising 80 samples from whole blood (PAXgene tubes) and 17 from cultured skin-derived fibroblasts, details provided in eMethods 3.

Reads were aligned to GRCh38/hg38 using minimap2 17 and processed with the standardized internal workflows megSAP or PhAnTOM-LR. small sequence variants were called with Clair3 or Clair3-Nova (depending on the processing site), 18 SVs with Sniffles2 (v2.4 or v2.6; pipeline-specific implementations in), 19 CNVs from long-read depth with ClinCNV (10 kb bins), 20 and repeat expansions with Straglr, focusing on known pathogenic tandem-repeat loci curated by STRchive; 21, 22 reads were phased with longphase or WhatsHap. 23, 24 Because SV-caller version affected sensitivity, every candidate SV was manually curated in IGV verifying breakpoint orientation, additional breakpoints, and mobile-element involvement, and excluding alignment artefacts before classification. An average read-length N50 of 14,963±3,256 bp was reached, with approximately 26,715±3,311 SV calls per sample. Processed datasets were shared and uploaded to the Solve-RD platform RD-connect for collaborative analyses. 25

Variant annotation and prioritization

Detected DNA variants were filtered and prioritized based on variant type, variant quality, minor allele frequency (MAF) in population databases, pathogenicity prediction scores, and phenotypic compatibility. MAF thresholds of <0.1% were applied for presumed autosomal dominant (AD) and <1% for autosomal recessive (AR) inheritance. Variants frequently observed in internal cohorts or 1kG-ONT datasets were excluded, whereby retained candidate variants were restricted to ≤3 for AD and ≤10 for AR inheritance models. X-linked variants were filtered according to proband sex and corresponding inheritance patterns. For genes associated with AD disorders, de novo variants were prioritized, and for genes associated with AR disorders, homozygous and compoundheterozygous variants were considered. Variants passing population and inheritance-based filtering were further prioritized based on their presence in or near (2kb up-/down-stream) genes from a curated list of established epilepsy and intellectual disability (Epi/ID) genes, and genes associated to the proband's HPO terms. SVs affecting coding regions and transcription start sites were prioritized as potentially high-impact. SNVs were prioritized if they had CADD scores >20, ClinVar pathogenic/likely pathogenic/uncertain significance annotations, or AlphaMissense scores >0.5. Intronic variants were evaluated using SpliceAI, and variants with a score >0.5 were prioritized. 26, 27 Prioritized variants matching the clinical phenotype were classified according to the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) and Association for Molecular Pathology (AMP) developed guidelines. 28, 29 Trio-lrGS datasets were analyzed first to evaluate whether diagnostically relevant variants across different variant classes could be reliably identified using lrGS data. Where available, singleton srGS datasets were analyzed in parallel or subsequently by independent researchers, to assess whether (i) variants identified by lrGS were also detectable and adequately characterizable using srGS, and (ii) independent srGS analysis would identify additional potentially pathogenic variants missed by lrGS. Prior ES data were retrospectively reviewed for all diagnostic variants where available. RNAseq was used to assess transcript-level consequences and support variant interpretation. SrGS and RNAseq were not available for all participants.

Minigene Assay

The splicing impact of the deep-intronic SCN1A variant (c.265-2028C>G) was assessed with a pET01 minigene splice assay in HEK293 cells; the complete protocol is provided in eMethods 4.

3. Results

We performed lrGS in 124 trios with DEE previously unsolved by ES. 109 of the index cases also received srGS not to miss SNVs and for determining the added value of lrGS, and 97 of the index cases received additional RNAseq of blood samples (n=80) or fibroblasts (n=17). In total, we solved 21 cases, of which 7 harbored complex SVs (n=5) or deep intronic variants (n=2) not detectable by ES in retrospect, while 14 were SNVs which were confirmed in ES re-analysis. All variants affected known epilepsy genes, and were identified in the initial lrGS analysis. Independent review of available srGS and/or RNA data did not reveal additional diagnoses. Identified (likely) pathogenic variants and their ACMG classification are listed in Table 1. Complex structural variants detected by lrGS and associated case reports

Balanced translocation leading to fusion transcript of CDKL5

A preschool-aged female (family 1) presented with focal tonic seizures that started in the first six months of life, which soon evolved to epileptic spasms. At the time of the last examination, she suffered from severe generalized muscular hypotonia, was non-verbal and non-ambulatory, had profound intellectual disability, and feeding difficulties. EEG showed diffuse slowing and multifocal epileptiform discharges. lrGS revealed a balanced de novo translocation t(X;17)(p22.13;p11.2). The patient had not previously undergone chromosomal analysis. Breakpoint evaluation in IGV revealed disruption of the CDKL5 gene, with the breakpoint located within intron 4 (chrX:18571502) and a second breakpoint at chr17:20929358 (Fig. 1A). Fibroblast extracted RNAseq revealed the formation of a fusion transcript combining the first three exons of CDKL5 fused with part of a lncRNA CCDC144NL-AS1 downstream of the breakpoint on chr17, supporting loss of function of CDKL5. Additionally, RNAseq demonstrated extreme skewing of the X-chromosome in favor of expression of the fusion transcript, which was in line with the severe phenotype observed in the patient. Trio-based phasing showed that the disrupted allele originated from the paternal X chromosome. SrGS was not available for this patient. The translocation was later confirmed in a diagnostic setting using optical genome mapping.

Copy-neutral inversion between SCN1A and SLC38A11

An adolescent female (family 2) diagnosed with Dravet syndrome presented with feverassociated seizures in the first six months of life. Subsequently, she developed both feverassociated and afebrile seizures of multiple types (focal aware, generalized tonic-clonic, focal myoclonic to tonic and bilateral myoclonic seizures). She showed mild developmental delay with autistic features, crouched gait and mild scoliosis. EEG performed betweeen five and ten years revealed multifocal epileptiform discharges. lrGS revealed a de novo copy-neutral inversion with breakpoints in intron 20 of SCN1A (chr2:166014984) and intron 7 of SLC38A11 (chr2:164929688). The rearrangement was accompanied by a 3 bp deletion at the SCN1A breakpoint and a 4 bp duplication at the SLC38A11 breakpoint (Fig. 1B). RNAseq was inconclusive, since SCN1A is not expressed in blood nor fibroblasts. So far, SLC38A11 has no established disease association, shows no intolerance to loss-of-function variation (pLI = 0, LOEUF = 1.17), indicating that loss of function is an unlikely disease-causing mechanism for this gene. The eight additional genes located within the inverted segment were not considered disrupted by the rearrangement. Upon subsequent srGS evaluation, the inversion and the breakpoints were well detectable and characterizable in singleton srGS as well, indicating that the diagnostic contribution in this case derived from genome rather than specifically long-read sequencing. De novo calling enabled rapid prioritization of this inversion and supported its interpretation.

Large chromosomal inversion with single exon deletion of MEF2C

A middle-aged woman (family 3) presented with severe global developmental delay starting in the first six months of life. She never acquired speech, and exhibited severe intellectual disability and autistic features. Motor development was mildly delayed. Epilepsy onset occurred between five and ten years of age with generalized tonic-clonic seizures, later evolving into pharmacoresistant epilepsy. EEG showed generalized epileptiform discharges and slow background activity. MRI showed mild cerebellar and parietal atrophy. Trio-lrGS identified a de novo 11,031 bp deletion affecting the first exon of MEF2C (chr5:88872465_88883496del) which was embedded within a complex rearrangement comprising two additional downstream deletions (chr5:88883496_88885137del and chr5:160616291_160652998del) and an approximately 71 Mb inverted insertion (chr5:88916302_160616291inv) between them (Fig. 1C, D). The single-exon MEF2C deletion was confirmed by qPCR, while the chromosomal analysis also confirmed the presence of a paracentric inversion of the long arm of chromosome 5 with breakpoints and rejoining points in bands q14 and q34, respectively. Expression analysis in blood-derived RNAseq showed reduced MEF2C expression (30%, p=0.001), consistent with partial loss of gene dosage. Subsequent srGS also identified the single-exon MEF2C deletion and the large inversion as separate events. Of note, srGS is associated with high false-positive rates for inversion calls and has particular difficulty resolving large inversions. 30 Consequently, genuine large inversions may be dismissed as artefacts and overlooked.

Small Deletion of STXBP1 with breakpoints in Alu-regions

An adolescent female (family 4) with severe global developmental delay, markedly limited speech (approximately 15 words), delayed independent walking with an ataxic gait, microcephaly, and a history of epileptic spasms with onset in the first six months of life, was found to carry a de novo heterozygous deletion at 9q33.3 (chr9:127656273_127659122del, GRCh38) encompassing exon 4 of STXBP1. This deletion was identified by trio-lrGS and was absent from gnomAD. This deletion was also called in srGS, however, due to the presence of flanking Alu elements, precise breakpoint resolution was not achievable using srGS, but with long-reads (Fig. 2A). Alignment analysis (Waterman-Eggert) of the flanking sequences ±100 bp of the reported breakpoints demonstrated >80% similarity of the immediate upstream reference sequence with the furthest downstream sequence of the deletion, and >88% similarity of the immediate downstream reference sequence with the furthest upstream sequence of the deletion. This is consistent with an established mechanism of deletion by recombination between homologous Alu elements. 31, 32 STXBP1 is not expressed in blood-derived, precluding transcript-level evaluation.

Alu retrotransposon-mediated exon deletion of CDKL5

Another female patient in her early teens (family 5) presented with tonic seizures starting in the first six months of life, which rapidly evolved into hypermotor-tonic-spasms sequence seizures, accompanied with hypsarrhythmia on EEG. Subsequently, she developed additional seizure types, including atypical absences and tonic seizures. At the time of the last follow-up, she had severe global developmental delay, lack of head control and profound intellectual disability, with hand stereotypies. Comorbidities included cortical visual impairment, sleeping problems and feeding difficulties. She was found to harbor a heterozygous deletion of exon 10 in CDKL5 (Fig. 2B-C). Inspection in IGV revealed soft-clipped bases upstream of this deletion mapping to an AluSx3 reference sequence. Using the Dfam database for transposable element sequence alignment, we traced the origin of these soft-clipped bases to a novel insertion of AluYb8, sharing approximately 87.6% sequence similarity with the original AluSx3 element. 33 The new Alu retrotransposon insertion resulted in exon-level deletion of CDKL5. Due to the mapping limitations of Alu elements in srGS, accurate breakpoint determination was only possible with lrGS. As CDKL5 is not expressed in blood, and only blood-derived RNAseq data was available for this individual, transcript-level consequences could not be assessed.

Causal variants missed in prior ES analyses or only detectable by GS

In addition to these complex SVs, causal CNVs were identified in four cases and causal SNVs in 12 cases; all likely pathogenic or pathogenic variants in epilepsy-related genes are listed in Table 1. In two cases, the identified SNVs were not detectable by ES, and required either sr-or lrGS: a deep-intronic SCN1A variant (c.265-2028C>G) identified in a patient (family 6) with Dravet syndrome (Fig. 3A), and bi-allelic non-coding variants in RNU2-2 (family 7, n.[ 104T>G];[171_179]). The predicted inclusion of a 151-bp poison exon (c.265-2029_265-2179) caused by the de novo deep-intronic SCN1A variant was confirmed to be deleterious using a minigene assay (Fig. 3B-D).

All CNVs and the other 10 SNVs were retrospectively detectable in prior ES but missed in the initial analysis. The CNV diagnoses (n=4) were previously missed due to absent CNV calling in local ES pipelines. Several pathogenic variants (n=4) were only detected after application of updated gene panels and HPO-based filtering, while some variants (n=3) were prioritized in this study by de novo calling and reverse phenotyping, since only singleton ES had been performed prior to the study for these families. These variants had previously been classified as variants of uncertain significance (VUS) and were not further investigated. Additional patients were diagnosed following improved mosaicism detection (n=2), or clinical re-phenotyping (n=1, Table 1). These findings demonstrate that a substantial proportion of diagnoses in apparently exome-negative DEE can be recovered through systematic reanalysis using updated analytical and interpretative approaches.

Variants of Uncertain Significance in known epilepsy genes

Seven individuals harbored rare compound-heterozygous (n=5), hemizygous (n=1) and de novo heterozygous (n=1) variants in established epilepsy genes (RNU2-2, IL1RAPL1, EPG5, ZNF335, TUBB3, LRP2, and SZT2). Due to limited functional evidence and/or lack of phenotypic concordance, these variants were considered of uncertain, but possibly disease-relevant findings.

In the recently established disease gene RNU2-2, two previously unreported variants (n.73C>T; n.119G>A) were identified in a compound-heterozygous state and were classified as VUS. 34 In a male patient with infantile epileptic spasm syndrome that later evolved to Lennox-Gastaut syndrome, a hemizygous intronic 1.2 kb insertion was identified in the X-Chromosome gene IL1RAPL1. The proband additionally showed severe intellectual disability with no speech development, independent walking for short distances and marked spasticity in the lower limbs. The inserted segment partially aligned with an L1M4 subfamily element on the negative DNA strand. The patient's mother was a heterozygous carrier, and the family history was suggestive of X-linked inheritance, including a maternal uncle who suffered from early childhood seizures and died in the first six months of life, possibly due to sudden unexpected death in epilepsy (SUDEP) (Fig. 2D-E). IL1RAPL1 is not expressed in blood and fibroblasts. As the patient had passed away between 35 and 40 years of age, no additional functional studies could be performed. Despite this suggestive family history, insufficient genetic evidence and the non-specific phenotype precluded definitive pathogenic classification. Compound-heterozygous variants in SZT2 (c.5626T>C, p.Ser1876Pro; c. *1365C>T, p.?) were considered less likely to be causative, as blood-derived RNAseq revealed no significant changes in gene expression or splicing. In patient 23 harboring compound-heterozygous variants in EPG5 (c.6724del, p.Met2242CysfsTer5; c.2718+945C>T, p.?) RNAseq showed only mildly reduced expression (17%, p=0.006). Similarly, a patient carrying compound-heterozygous variants in LRP2 (c.13273G>A, p.Val4425Ile; c.2626G>T, p.Ala876Ser) displayed no features consistent with Donnai-Barrow syndrome, such as characteristic craniofacial, ocular, renal, or auditory findings. Following reevaluation, these variants were deemed unlikely to explain the phenotype. A rare de novo variant in TUBB3 (c.643C>T, p.Leu215Phe) was identified in a patient with global developmental delay, complex brain malformation (partial corpus callosum dysgenesis, septum pellucidum agenesis, pontine atrophy, left hippocampus inversion and cerebellar malformation with partial vermis and hemisphere fusion) and pharmacoresistant structural epilepsy. Additional findings included bilateral optic nerve atrophy due to congenital hydrocephalus. Pathogenic TUBB3 variants (OMIM #614039) are associated with midline brain malformations including vermis atrophy, consistent with this patient's phenotype. 35 Abnormal gyration has also been reported as a characteristic TUBB3-phenotype feature but this could not be assessed due to a lack of updated neuroimaging, as the family did not wish further investigations. Therefore, the variant remained classified as a VUS. Furthermore, a pathogenic ZNF335 variant (c.3486_3487+24del) and a de novo missense variant (c.1519T>C, p.Trp507Arg) was detected in compound-heterozygous state in another individual, but insufficient clinical data -particularly regarding head circumference and neuroimaging -prevented definitive genotype-phenotype correlation.

ZNF569 and ZNF570 identified as new candidate genes

In a young boy with Lennox-Gastaut syndrome (family 22), we identified a homozygous deletion affecting the first two exons of both ZNF569 and ZNF570, two zinc-finger genes. This variant was not observed in the in-house databases, and observed only once in gnomAD v4 in a heterozygous state. GnomAD also contains no homozygous loss-offunction variants for either gene. Both parents were heterozygous carriers, and segregation analysis by PCR showed that the unaffected brother and sister were not homozygous for the deletion. Both ZNF569 and ZNF570 are brain-expressed genes belonging to the large family of KRAB-containing C2H2 zinc-finger transcription factors, which play essential roles in neurodevelopment. 36 Although population data suggests that both genes tolerate heterozygous loss, no homozygous loss-of-function variants were observed in gnomAD for either gene, suggesting that a complete loss may be deleterious. Notably, several related KRAB-ZNF genes are already known to be implicated in neurodevelopmental disorders and epilepsy, including ZNF142, in which biallelic variants cause a syndromic neurodevelopmental disorder with intellectual disability and early-onset seizures, 37 and ZNF711, where pathogenic variants have been reported in individuals with intellectual disability and epileptic seizures. 38 In the absence of additional affected individuals or functional evidence we considered this deletion a candidate locus requiring further validation.

Epilepsy genes exhibit higher expression in fibroblasts than leukocytes

RNAseq contributed to variant interpretation in four families: In family 1, fibroblastderived RNAseq confirmed a CDKL5 fusion transcript, in family 3, blood-derived RNA showed mild reduced expression of MEF2C (30%, p=0.001), in family 8, blood-derived RNAseq demonstrated reduced DEPDC5 expression and double exon skipping, consistent with a paternal deletion, and in family 9, blood-derived RNAseq showed 50% reduced expression of multiple genes consistent with the ~9.3Mb de novo deletion encompassing NR4A2. The deletions in families 8 and 9 were simultaneously also prioritized via Solve-RD RNAseq project. 39 Heterozygous deletions in CDKL5 were identified in three patients (families 5, 10, and 11), including an Alu-mediated deletion. Their effects on expression could not be assessed by analysis of the blood-derived RNA that was available from these patients, whereas fibroblast RNAseq in the patient with a balanced translocation involving CDKL5 (family 1) confirmed aberrant transcript expression. In family 4, fibroblast-derived RNAseq would also have provided transcript-level information on the identified STXBP1 deletion, which was not captured in the blood-derived RNAseq. Our internal RNAseq database of PAX-blood (n=455) and fibroblast samples (n=129) revealed that 1,510 of 1,948 (77.5%) epilepsy genes are expressed in blood versus 1,669 of 1,948 in fibroblasts (85.7%; using a cutoff of average TPM>1).

4. Discussion

In this cohort of 124 individuals with DEE who remained undiagnosed after prior ES, a lrGS-based diagnostic workflow established a new molecular diagnosis in 16.9% (21/124). The majority of these diagnoses (14/21; 67%) were retrospectively detectable in the original ES data, whereas 7 (one third of the pathogenic variants (33%, 7/21), or 5,6% of the total cohort) were exclusively detectable in srGS and/or lrGS. In five cases the variants were complex SVs affecting established DEE genes. Although detectable with srGS, lrGS enabled complete reconstruction of the complex rearrangement, allowing accurate interpretation, particularly when repetitive regions such as mobile elements were involved. Our findings identify complex SVs as the variant class for which long-reads currently provided the most distinctive diagnostic information.

The high proportion of diagnoses retrospectively detectable in ES emphasizes the continuing value of systematic reanalysis in individuals with apparently negative genetic testing. Variants had been missed because of absent CNV calling, outdated virtual gene panels, lack of parental sequencing and de novo prioritization, limited sensitivity for mosaic variants, or conservative variant interpretation at the time of initial testing. Consequently, reanalysis of existing ES should remain an integral component of the diagnostic pathway before or alongside escalation to genome sequencing. The successful detection of these variants using lrGS nevertheless underscores its value as a robust orthogonal approach capable of detecting and validating a broad spectrum of variant types, extending beyond just complex SV detection. lrGS performed particularly well in resolving SVs involving repetitive sequences. Two of the complex SVs identified in our study, the CDKL5 exon 10 deletion (family 5) and the STXBP1 deletion (family 4), were mediated by Alu elements (Fig. 2A, 2B). Furthermore, we detected a hemizygous LINE-1 insertion of uncertain significance in the X-linked IL1RAPL1 gene in a male patient with DEE. Mobile elements retrotransposons constitute roughly 45% of the human genome and are well-recognized drivers of genomic instability through retrotransposition and non-allelic homologous recombination. 31, 32, 40 Their repetitive nature complicates short-read alignment and breakpoint assignment. Long reads spanning the repetitive elements enabled precise breakpoint resolution and elucidation of the underlying rearrangement mechanism. Similarly, lrGS facilitated reconstruction of the MEF2C deletion and 71-Mb inversion as components of a single complex rearrangement. These cases illustrate that the value of lrGS extends beyond variant detection to establishing the architecture and mechanism of complex genomic events.

Our results also highlight one candidate locus warranting further investigation. The homozygous deletion affecting ZNF569 and ZNF570 (family 22) is of particular interest, as both genes encode zinc-finger transcription factors, a family increasingly implicated in neurodevelopmental regulation and transcriptional control. 36 Complete loss of two adjacent regulatory brain-expressed genes provides a biologically plausible mechanism for neurodevelopmental phenotypes, including DEE. However, in the absence of functional data, this locus remains a preliminary candidate. Additional cases and experimental validation assessing their roles in neuronal development will be required to establish a definitive disease association.

Establishing a molecular diagnosis in DEE has increasing therapeutic relevance. We identified SCN1A disrupting variants in two individuals (family 2, family 6) with Dravet syndrome, one of the most extensively studied DEEs for which genotype-informed treatment strategies are already applied in the clinic. 41 One of the SCN1A variants (index of family 6, deep-intronic de novo SCN1A variant c.265-2028C>G) results in a poison exon inclusion. Antisense oligonucleotides designed to suppress very similar poison exons are already being tested in phase 3 randomized controlled trials. 42 Complex SVs were also identified in well-known genes such as STXBP1 and CDKL5, for which targeted approaches are in development and supported by robust preclinical outcomes. [43] [44] [45] Comprehensive identification and mechanistic characterisation of such SVs using lrGS may become increasingly relevant to identify patients who may benefit from emerging gene-targeted interventions.

Our analyses also highlight current technical challenges in clinical implementation of lrGS. Automated SV calling did not consistently capture the complete architecture of complex rearrangements, and manual review in integrative genomics viewer (IGV) was essential to identify additional breakpoints, correct orientation assignments, recognize mobile element involvement, or exclude alignment artifacts. For example, the CDKL5 translocation (family 1) was initially reported as a head-to-head rearrangement; however, manual inspection demonstrated a head-to-tail orientation. Similarly, the de novo MEF2C deletion (family 3) was initially detected as a single-exon deletion. Careful visual inspection revealed it to be part of a complex rearrangement involving a large inversion and two additional deletions. Additionally, a substantial proportion of variants initially reported as de novo insertions in ONT data were, upon manual inspection, found to be alignment artifacts inherent to ONT technology rather than true insertions. To ensure consistency and reproducibility of variant detection across participating analysis centers, we performed cross-site validation using three positive controls drawn from the solved cohort described in this study (families 2, 3 and 5). This exercise revealed that even minor differences in bioinformatics pipelines, particularly software versions, could result in discrepant outcomes, either detecting or missing diagnostically relevant variants. Notably, one positive control (the CDKL5 de novo deletion) was missed when using Sniffles v2.6.1, but detected using an alternative workflow using Sniffles v2.4. These observations emphasize the need for standardized and validated analytical workflows, complementary SV callers, and careful visual review when lrGS is implemented diagnostically.

RNAseq provided functional evidence in four diagnosed individuals but did not independently identify additional diagnoses in this cohort. Its contribution was limited in part by tissue availability: most RNA samples were derived from blood, in which several relevant epilepsy genes are insufficiently expressed for meaningful transcript-level assessment. Our expression analysis indicates that fibroblasts provide broader coverage of epilepsy-associated genes than blood, and our previous work indeed showed that fibroblast-derived RNAseq significantly increases diagnostic yield in exome negative epilepsy patients. 46 Several limitations should be considered. First, this was a clinically selected cohort rather than a population-based diagnostic study, and the observed yields may therefore not generalize to all individuals with ES-negative DEE. Second, sequencing platforms, coverage, and bioinformatic pipelines differed across subsets of the cohort. Third, srGS and RNA sequencing were not available for all participants, precluding a uniform head-tohead comparison of lrGS with these approaches across the entire cohort. Fourth, methylation information inherent to the long-read sequencing data was not analyzed because sufficiently validated and standardized analytical workflows were not available at the time of the study. This represents a potential source of additional diagnostic information: rare de novo differentially methylated regions have been reported to be enriched in individuals with unexplained neurodevelopmental disorders and can either provide diagnostic clues themselves or help identify underlying disease-causing (noncoding) genomic variants. 47, 48 Moreover, both PacBio HiFi and ONT sequencing have been shown to enable detection of disease-associated methylation signatures. 49, 50 Consequently, our study did not assess the potential incremental diagnostic contribution of methylation analysis, and therefore does not capture the full range of information that may ultimately be obtainable from a comprehensive lrGS-based diagnostic workflow.

In conclusion, trio lrGS contributed to molecular diagnosis in individuals with previously unresolved DEE, with its most distinctive value lying in the detection and structural resolution of complex rearrangements, particularly those involving repetitive and mobileelement sequences. At the same time, the finding that two-thirds of diagnoses were retrospectively detectable in prior ES highlights the substantial diagnostic value of systematic reanalysis. While lrGS greatly expands the spectrum of genomic variation that can technically be detected, the incremental diagnostic yields reported to date remain more modest than initially anticipated. Further advances may therefore depend increasingly on our ability to interpret variation outside the protein-coding genome. Recent discoveries of non-coding disease genes, including spliceosomal small nuclear RNA genes that account for a substantial proportion of previously unexplained NDDs, 7, 34 illustrate the diagnostic potential of this largely unexplored genomic space. Integrating sequence variation with additional information inherent to long-read data such as haplotype-resolved methylation profiles, may help prioritize and interpret non-coding variation and represents an important next step towards realizing the full diagnostic potential of lrGS.
