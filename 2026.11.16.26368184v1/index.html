To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 3 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Background: Community pharmacies are often the first point of care in low-and middleincome countries, yet the knowledge, attitudes and practices (KAP) of their workers regarding antibiotics and antimicrobial resistance (AMR) are undocumented in the Democratic Republic of Congo (DRC).

Objectives: To assess KAP regarding antibiotic use and AMR, and their determinants, among community pharmacy workers in Bukavu, Eastern DRC.

Methods: A cross-sectional survey was conducted in April-June 2026 among 423 community pharmacy workers, one per pharmacy, selected by multistage sampling across the three health zones of Bukavu, using a structured, interviewer-administered questionnaire. Knowledge (8 items), attitudes (9 items) and dispensing practices (4 items) were scored using modified Bloom's cut-offs. Multivariable logistic regression identified determinants.

Results: Good knowledge was found in 19.4%, favourable attitudes in 17.5% and appropriate dispensing practices in 27.4%. Overall, 96.5% dispensed antibiotics without a prescription and 9.2% knew the WHO AWaRe classification. University education and pharmaceutical qualification were independently associated with good knowledge [adjusted OR (aOR) 4.38 and 2.41] and appropriate practices (aOR 5.74 and 2.01). Good knowledge was associated with favourable attitudes (aOR 4.86, 95% CI 2.68-8.82), which in turn predicted appropriate practices (aOR 6.62, 95% CI 3.50-12.53). In addition, 57.0% knew of "Machange", an informal mixture of medicines: 99.6% of those describing its composition listed at least one antibiotic, and 65.6% of those familiar with it sold it. Conclusions: Community pharmacy workers in Bukavu show substantial gaps in antibioticrelated KAP. Stewardship interventions should combine education with regulation, diagnostic capacity and AMR surveillance within a One Health framework.

Introduction

Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) is one of the defining public health challenges of the 21st century, threatening decades of progress in the prevention and treatment of infectious diseases and undermining the effectiveness of life-saving antimicrobial therapies. 1, 2 In 2021, bacterial AMR was directly responsible for an estimated 1.14 million deaths worldwide and contributed to nearly 4.7 million deaths overall, with the mortality burden concentrated in low-and middleincome countries (LMICs), where infectious diseases remain common. 1 In Africa, systematic reviews have demonstrated widespread resistance to commonly used antibiotics across both hospital and community settings. 3 Beyond its human cost, AMR places a growing burden on health systems by prolonging hospital stays, escalating healthcare expenditure, and narrowing therapeutic options, thereby jeopardizing progress toward universal health coverage and sustainable development. 2, 4 Antibiotic overuse, including inappropriate prescribing, self-medication, improper dispensing, and poor treatment adherence, remains one of the main preventable drivers of AMR. 1, 2, 5 Optimizing antibiotic use at every level of healthcare is therefore a cornerstone of global strategies to preserve the effectiveness of existing antibiotics and limit the future consequences of resistance. 6, 7 The One Health approach, which recognizes the interconnectedness of human, animal and environmental health, is increasingly promoted as an essential framework for combating AMR, as resistant organisms and resistance genes circulate across these domains. 2, 6, 7 However, awareness of the One Health concept remains limited among many medical professionals, and increasing its dissemination is crucial for effective AMR control and prevention. 8 As recently emphasized, the One Health approach is particularly important for addressing neglected zoonotic diseases, which are linked to antimicrobial resistance and require coordinated strategies across sectors. 9 Community pharmacies are key players in health systems worldwide and are often the first point of contact for people seeking treatment, particularly in LMICs where access to formal healthcare is limited. [10] [11] [12] [13] Positioned between health systems and communities, pharmacy workers are well placed to promote rational antibiotic use through dispensing, counselling, and referral, 11, 12 making them increasingly recognized as partners in antimicrobial stewardship (AMS). 11, 13 However, these roles depend on pharmacy workers having adequate knowledge, favourable attitudes, and sound dispensing practices regarding antibiotics and AMR. 7, 14 LMICs bear a disproportionate share of the AMR burden due to a high prevalence of infectious diseases combined with weak healthcare delivery and pharmaceutical regulation systems. 1, 2 Limited access to affordable medical consultations and shortages of qualified health professionals have made community and informal pharmacies one of the most accessible sources of antibiotics in these settings. 11, [15] [16] [17] High out-of-pocket healthcare costs often lead individuals to bypass formal consultations and obtain antibiotics directly from pharmacies without prior clinical evaluation, increasing the risk of inappropriate use. 1, 2, 13, 18 At the same time, AMS in LMICs remains constrained by limited laboratory capacity, weak AMR surveillance, poor regulatory enforcement, and insufficient financial and human resources. 2, 7 These challenges highlight the need for robust evidence on antibiotic-related knowledge, attitudes, and practices among community pharmacy workers, particularly in settings where pharmacies play a central role in healthcare access. 11, 13 The Democratic Republic of Congo (DRC) exemplifies many of these structural challenges.

Despite a heavy burden of infectious diseases, the country faces a persistent shortage of qualified health professionals, limited diagnostic capacity, weak health system governance, and recurrent economic crises, population displacement, and armed conflict, all of which strain an already fragile healthcare system. 2, 3, 19 Healthcare financing continues to rely heavily on out-ofpocket payments, making consultations unaffordable for much of the population and driving many to seek treatment directly from community pharmacies. [20] [21] [22] In Bukavu, community pharmacies have proliferated rapidly in response to this demand, yet regulatory oversight remains weak, and access to diagnostic and antimicrobial susceptibility testing is limited.

Recent microbiological data from Bukavu have documented high resistance rates among clinically important pathogens, confirming that irrational antibiotic use is a documented, not theoretical, problem. 23, 24 Consequently, pharmacies have become one of the most accessible entry points into the healthcare system, particularly for managing common infectious conditions, extending well beyond their traditional dispensing role. 1, 3, 11, 12 Yet the rapid growth of community pharmacies, combined with weak enforcement of pharmaceutical regulation and AMS, raises concerns about the quality of antibiotic dispensing and its contribution to inappropriate antibiotic use and AMR. 2, 13, 14 While numerous studies have assessed healthcare professionals' knowledge, attitudes, and practices regarding antibiotic use and AMR, evidence remains uneven across regions, with most research concentrated in Asia, the Middle East, and a limited number of African countries. 11, 13, 17, 25, 26 Data from Central Africa are particularly scarce, and few studies have specifically examined community pharmacy workers despite their growing role in antibiotic access and dispensing. 11, 15 In the DRC, existing research has largely focused on hospital, prescriber, or population-level settings, leaving community pharmacy workers' knowledge, attitudes, and practices (KAP), especially in the eastern part of the country, poorly documented. 19, 23, 24, 27 Previous studies in the DRC have reported gaps in healthcare providers' knowledge of antibiotic use and resistance in hospital settings in Kinshasa, 27 while populationlevel and prescriber-focused studies have documented widespread inappropriate antibiotic use.

However, no study to date has comprehensively assessed KAP among community pharmacy workers in Eastern DRC, and the specific pathways through which these workers influence antibiotic access-including dispensing without prescriptions, patient counselling, and referral practices-remain poorly understood. This evidence gap limits the ability to design contextspecific antimicrobial stewardship interventions and strengthen the role of community pharmacies in preventing AMR. 2, 12 Against this background, this study assessed KAP toward antibiotic use and AMR among community pharmacy workers in Bukavu, Eastern DRC, and explored the determinants of good knowledge, favourable attitudes, and appropriate practices. By generating context-specific evidence in a setting where community pharmacies are integral to primary healthcare access, this study aims to address a significant gap in the literature and inform AMS initiatives, strengthen pharmaceutical practice, and support evidence-based public health policy in the DRC and other resource-limited settings.

Eligible participants were those actively involved in dispensing medicines or providing pharmaceutical services to clients, regardless of professional qualification (pharmacists or non-pharmacist personnel).

Eligibility criteria

Community pharmacy workers were eligible to participate if they were employed in a community pharmacy located in Bukavu during the study period, were involved in dispensing medicines or providing pharmaceutical services to clients, and voluntarily agreed to participate after providing written informed consent. Workers who were temporarily absent during the survey period, declined to participate, or were not directly involved in pharmaceutical service provision were excluded from the study.

Sampling procedure

Because no comprehensive and up-to-date registry of community pharmacies existed in Bukavu, a multistage sampling strategy stratified by health zone and health area was used.

Stage 1 (health zones and health areas): all three health zones of Bukavu (Ibanda, Kadutu and Bagira) and all of their health areas (37 health areas in total) were included. Stage 2 (allocation): the target sample of 423 pharmacies was allocated to each health area in proportion to its population, using population figures from the 2025 South Kivu Provincial Health Report. An initial list of community pharmacies was obtained from the South Kivu Provincial Health Division; however, local health authorities acknowledged that this list was incomplete because of the rapid and largely uncontrolled expansion of community pharmacies across the city. It was therefore used for planning only and not as the sampling frame.

Stage 3 (selection of pharmacies): in each health area, field teams first listed all operating community pharmacies by systematically walking through the health area, thereby building an on-site sampling frame. The number of pharmacies allocated to that health area was then drawn from this list by simple random sampling without replacement, using a random number generator. A selected pharmacy that was closed on two visits or had no eligible worker present was replaced by the next pharmacy randomly drawn from the same list. Where fewer pharmacies were operating than had been allocated, all operating pharmacies in the health area were included and the shortfall was reallocated to the other health areas of the same health zone in proportion to their population. Stage 4 (selection of participants): one worker was interviewed per pharmacy; if more than one eligible worker was present, one was selected at random by lottery. Each of the 423 participants therefore came from a different pharmacy, and the number of pharmacies equals the number of participants. Two health areas (Ciguri and Mushekere) had no operating community pharmacy at the time of the survey and therefore contributed no participants; the remaining 35 health areas contributed between 3 and 40 pharmacies each. The final sample comprised 423 pharmacies (and 423 workers): Ibanda (n = 146; 34.5%), Kadutu (n = 182; 43.0%) and Bagira (n = 95; 22.5%). The planned and achieved sample sizes for each health area are given in Supplementary File S2, and the locations of the surveyed pharmacies are shown in Figure 1.

Study variables

The main outcome variables were participants' KAP concerning antibiotic use and AMR, assessed using eight, ten and seven items, respectively, of which eight, nine and four items were retained in the final scores after reliability analysis (see Assessment of internal consistency).

Explanatory variables were age, sex, highest educational level, professional qualification, years of professional experience and prior training on antibiotic use and antimicrobial resistance. For analysis, educational level was dichotomized as primary/secondary (n = 112) or university or above (n = 311), and professional qualification as pharmaceutical (pharmacists, pharmacy technicians (G3) and pharmacy assistants (A2); n = 110) or non-pharmaceutical (nurses, physicians, laboratory technicians, midwives and other personnel; n = 313).

Data collection instrument

Data were collected through a structured, interviewer-administered questionnaire developed from previously validated KAP questionnaires on antibiotic use and antimicrobial resistance and adapted to the Congolese context following a review of the relevant literature. The questionnaire included sections on socio-demographic and professional characteristics; KAP related to antibiotic use and AMR; and items on the dispensing and composition of locally known mixed antibiotic preparations ("Machange"). The questionnaire was administered in French. Prior to data collection, it was reviewed by public health experts and pre-tested among 10 community pharmacy workers who were not included in the study sample, for clarity, relevance and contextual appropriateness. The full questionnaire, with the wording of every item, its response options and the scoring key, is provided in Supplementary File S3.

Data collection procedures

Data were collected between April and June 2026 by trained research assistants through faceto-face interviews in selected community pharmacies using KoboCollect tools. Study objectives were explained individually to each eligible participant, and written informed consent was obtained before each interview. Interviews were conducted in the most private area of the pharmacy practicable, to ensure confidentiality and minimize interruptions. All eligible participants completed the questionnaire.

Assessment of KAP

Knowledge was assessed using eight items covering antibiotic use, indications, dispensing and AMR; attitudes using ten items on participants' perceptions of, and professional attitudes towards, antibiotic dispensing and AMS; and antibiotic-dispensing practices using seven selfreported items on routine dispensing behaviour. All items are listed in Table 2. Each item was scored as 1 for a correct (knowledge), favourable (attitude) or appropriate (practice) response and as 0 otherwise; "don't know" and "prefer not to answer" responses were scored 0. For expired antibiotics, only returning them to the health zone central office was considered appropriate, and for hand hygiene only the answer "yes" (as opposed to "not very often" or "no"). Domain scores were the sum of the retained items only (eight knowledge, nine attitude and four practice items; see below). Participants were classified as having good knowledge (≥80% of the maximum score, ie ≥7 of 8 items), favourable attitudes (≥60%, ie ≥6 of 9 items) or appropriate practices (≥75%, ie ≥3 of 4 items); all others were classified as having poor knowledge, unfavourable attitudes or inappropriate practices, respectively. A lower threshold was used for attitudes because the highest observed attitude score was 8 of 9 and only 10 participants (2.4%) reached the 80% level, which would not have allowed multivariable analysis. Non-prescription dispensing was explored with two distinct questions: whether the participant dispensed antibiotics without a prescription (and, if so, how often), and whether there were specific antibiotics that the participant never dispensed without a prescription (and, if so, which). These questions measure different things: a participant could restrict a few antibiotics to prescription-only dispensing while dispensing others without a prescription.

Assessment of internal consistency

The internal consistency of the knowledge, attitude and practice scales was assessed using Cronbach's alpha. Items with a corrected item-total correlation below 0.10 were excluded from the final scores. All eight knowledge items met this criterion and were retained (α = 0.707). For attitudes, one item (not stopping antibiotic treatment once symptoms improve; item-total correlation 0.05) was excluded, increasing alpha from 0.692 to 0.710 and leaving nine items.

For practices, three items (not dispensing antibiotics without a prescription, not substituting a prescribed antibiotic, and adapting dispensing to children and adults; item-total correlations 0.06-0.09) were excluded, increasing alpha from 0.521 to 0.652 and leaving four items: checking expiry dates, never dispensing at least one antibiotic without a prescription, returning expired antibiotics to the health zone central office, and practising hand hygiene during working hours. Final domain scores were calculated using the retained items only. Excluded items are still reported descriptively in Table 2, where they are marked with a dagger ( †); itemlevel statistics are given in Supplementary File S4.

Statistical analysis

Data were entered, cleaned and analysed using Stata/MP version 17.0 (Stata Corp LLC, College Station, TX, USA). Categorical variables were summarized using frequencies and percentages, and continuous variables were described using means and standard deviations or medians and interquartile ranges (IQRs). Normality of continuous variables was examined using the Shapiro-Wilk test and graphical methods. For categorical variables, comparisons between groups were performed using Pearson's chi-square test or Fisher's exact test; for nonnormally distributed continuous variables, the Wilcoxon rank-sum test was used. Bivariate associations between each candidate variable and each outcome are reported in Supplementary File S5. Multivariable logistic regression was used to identify independent determinants of good knowledge, favourable attitudes and appropriate practices. Covariates were specified a priori on the basis of the literature (age, sex, educational level, years of professional experience, professional qualification and prior training) and the same set was kept in all three models, irrespective of bivariate P values, to allow comparison across domains; knowledge level was added to the attitudes model, and knowledge and attitude levels to the practices model.

Individual KAP items were not entered as predictors, because they are the components from which the outcome scores were derived. Health zone was not included in the main models; a sensitivity analysis adding it is reported in Supplementary File S5. Analyses were completecase: age was missing for 6 participants (1.4%), so the multivariable models included 417 participants; no other variable had missing values.

The final models included the following covariates (Table 3): Multicollinearity was assessed using variance inflation factors (mean 1.38 for knowledge, 1.35 for attitudes and 1.35 for practices; all within acceptable limits). Model fit was assessed using the Hosmer-Lemeshow test: knowledge χ² = 4.28, P = 0.831; attitudes χ² = 5.37, P = 0.717; practices χ² = 9.87, P = 0.275; all indicating good fit. Continuous variables (age and professional experience) were retained as continuous variables. Reference categories were: female for sex, primary/secondary for education, no for training, non-pharmaceutical for profession, poor knowledge for knowledge level, and unfavourable attitude for attitude level.

Adjusted odds ratios (aORs) with 95% confidence intervals (95% CI) were reported, and statistical significance was set at P < 0.05. All regression models consistently reported aORs, 95% CIs and P values.

Sociodemographic and professional characteristics of community pharmacy workers

Table 1 summarizes the socio-demographic and professional characteristics of the 423 community pharmacy workers who participated in this study. The median age was 28 years (IQR 24-34; age missing for 6 participants), and the median duration of professional experience was 3 years (IQR 1-5). Most participants were women (65.0%). Additional findings on "Machange"

A total of 241 (57.0%) of the 423 participants reported being familiar with "Machange", a locally available mixture of several medicines sold as one preparation. Participants familiar with "Machange" were asked which medicines it usually contains; across all responses, 45 distinct pharmaceutical molecules from several therapeutic classes were reported (Figure 2).

Antibiotics were the most frequently reported class (16 molecules; 35.6%), followed by analgesics/non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs; 8; 17.8%), antiparasitic agents (5; 11.1%) and antifungal drugs (4; 8.9%). Of the 236 participants who gave an informative answer on its composition, 235 (99.6%) listed at least one antibiotic. In addition, 158 of the 241 participants familiar with "Machange" (65.6%; 37.4% of all participants) reported selling it.

These findings are based on reported formulations and not on laboratory or pharmaceutical verification of the actual composition of "Machange."

Discussion

Principal findings

Community pharmacy workers play a key role in promoting rational antibiotic use and preventing AMR in LMICs, where pharmacies are often the first point of contact with the health system and are recognized as AMS partners. 2, 12, 14, 29 As shown in Table 2 and Table 3, community pharmacy workers in Bukavu, Eastern DRC, showed marked deficiencies across all three KAP domains: fewer than one in five had good knowledge (19.4%) or favourable attitudes (17.5%), and only 27.4% reported appropriate dispensing practices, lower than levels reported among qualified pharmacists or in settings with more integrated national AMS programmes. 7, 10, 13, 25, 30, 31 Higher education and pharmaceutical qualification were independently associated with better knowledge (four-fold and two-fold increases, respectively), consistent with evidence that academic training predicts AMR-related knowledge, 13, [32] [33] [34] while favourable attitudes were the strongest predictor of appropriate practices. This sequential pathway supports the KAP conceptual framework, 13, 14 though educational interventions alone appear insufficient without behavioural-change strategies, regulation, supervision and health-system strengthening. 7, 26 A further contribution was the description of "Machange", an informal antibiotic-containing drug mixture well known among Bukavu pharmacy workers but not previously documented in the literature. This finding is reinforced by recent microbiological data from Bukavu showing high resistance among clinically important pathogens. 1, 3, 23, 24 These findings reinforce evidence that education alone cannot resolve stewardship gaps in LMICs; effective stewardship requires clinical training, regulation, supervision, diagnostic capacity and health-system strengthening within a One Health framework, consistent with WHO strategy. 2, 6, 7, 26, 35

Knowledge regarding antibiotic use and AMR

Overall knowledge of antibiotic use and AMR was low, highlighting gaps among frontline providers and concerns about community-level AMS. Since pharmacies are a principal antibiotic access point in LMICs, this may drive inappropriate dispensing, poor counselling and continued resistance. 2, 12 This is consistent with studies from Jordan, 33 Palestine, 36 Ethiopia 17 and Senegal, 37 and with a 28-country African survey and a Sri Lankan study reporting widespread deficits despite growing stewardship efforts. 10, 13 The very limited awareness of the WHO AWaRe classification, a central tool for rational antibiotic selection and consumption monitoring, 2, 38, 39 echoes reports of limited AWaRe implementation where stewardship and continuing education remain underdeveloped. 7, 13, 31, 40, 41 Limited knowledge of susceptibility testing likely reflects restricted laboratory access and financial barriers common across Africa. 2, 3 These results should be read against a local context of rising AMR amid underdeveloped surveillance and diagnostic capacity. 2, 3 Recent Bukavu microbiological data confirming high resistance among clinically important pathogens show that irrational antibiotic use is a documented, not theoretical, problem. 23, 24 Strengthening pharmacy education is therefore essential, but must be integrated with competency-based training, supervision, stewardship tools and stronger regulation for sustainable improvement. 7, 14, 26, 29

Attitudes toward antibiotic use and AMR

Only a small proportion of participants held favourable attitudes towards rational antibiotic use.

While many supported routine regulatory inspection and completing antibiotic courses despite symptom improvement, unfavourable attitudes were common regarding non-prescription dispensing and self-limiting conditions. This indicates that knowledge alone does not secure favourable attitudes, which are also shaped by behavioural, regulatory and contextual factors. 26, 42, 43 These results echo findings from Ethiopia, 11 Benin, 44 Jordan 32 and Bangladesh, 45 and align with the 28-country African survey, where higher knowledge did not consistently translate into favourable attitudes or practices. 13 This suggests the knowledge-attitude-practice gap is a widespread LMIC phenomenon attributable to patient pressure, financial constraints, competition and weak regulatory enforcement. 11, 13, 44, 46 In multivariable analysis, good knowledge was strongly associated with favourable attitudes (aOR 4.86; 95% CI 2.68-8.82), which were in turn the strongest predictor of good practices (aOR 6.62; 95% CI 3.50-12.53) across all models. Although causality cannot be inferred, this sequential pattern supports the KAP framework and mirrors findings among healthcare professionals elsewhere. 13, 14, 43, 47, 48 Prior antibiotic/AMR training was not independently associated with more favourable attitudes after adjustment, consistent with evidence that education alone rarely produces durable behavioural change without audit, feedback, supervision and multidisciplinary programmes, 7, 26 and with the European Delphi consensus on the need for supportive professional environments and clear guidance. 35 Building favourable attitudes therefore likely requires strengthening curricula, continuing education, behaviouralchange programmes, regulation and integration of pharmacies into national stewardship efforts, rather than knowledge transfer alone. 2, 7, 12

Practices regarding antibiotic use and AMR

Fewer than one-third of participants reported appropriate dispensing practices, with widespread non-prescription dispensing, inappropriate management of expired antibiotics and suboptimal hand hygiene despite reasonable awareness of misuse-related risks, confirming that knowledge does not automatically translate into practice. 2 Almost all participants dispensed antibiotics without a prescription, and fewer than half restricted even a single antibiotic to prescriptiononly dispensing. This mirrors evidence that non-prescription dispensing is common in LMIC pharmacies where physician access is limited, consultations are costly and regulation is weak, 25, 26 with comparable findings from Nigeria, South Africa, Sri Lanka, Palestine and Tanzania, 40, [49] [50] [51] [52] simulated-patient studies in Jordan, 53 and the 28-country African survey. 13 The low rate of appropriate expired-antibiotic disposal, including resale of expired antibiotics by nine participants, is concerning, as improper disposal promotes environmental contamination and selection of resistant organisms, a recognized AMR pathway within the One Health framework. 2, 5, 6, 37, 54, 55 Poor hand-hygiene adherence similarly reflects the link between stewardship and infection prevention and control. 2, 37 Unexpectedly, prior training was independently associated with lower odds of appropriate dispensing after adjustment; given the cross-sectional design and lack of data on training content or recency, this may reflect selective training of workers in higher-need settings rather than a causal effect. 7, 14, 26 Favourable attitudes remained the strongest independent predictor of good practice (aOR 6.62), mirroring findings among healthcare professionals in Saudi Arabia 48 and reinforcing that effective stewardship requires continuing education, behavioural change, supervision, regulation, providerpharmacist collaboration, better diagnostics and stronger health-system governance. 2, 7, 14, 26, 35

Determinants of KAP

Consistent with the KAP model, higher educational attainment and pharmaceutical qualification were independently associated with better knowledge and practices, mirroring the 28-country African survey 13 and reflecting greater access to scientific literature and clinical decision-making skills. However, persistent inappropriate practices even among qualified staff indicate that pre-service education alone cannot guarantee rational dispensing, reinforcing the need for continuing education and stewardship-oriented practice environments. 7, 14, 35 Age, but not years of experience, was independently associated with attitudes, each additional year raising the odds of favourable attitudes by about 7% (aOR 1.07; 95% CI 1.02-1.11; P = 0.002), consistent with KAP studies suggesting clinical maturity, rather than employment duration, shapes stewardship attitudes. [56] [57] [58] The strongest associations overall remained the sequential knowledge-attitude-practice pathway (aOR 4.86 and 6.62, respectively), supporting the KAP model despite the cross-sectional design. 48 The unexpected inverse association between prior training and good practice likely reflects variation in training quality or timing, residual confounding, or preferential training of higher-risk workers. 7, 14, 16, 26 These findings support integrated interventions, combining education, continuing development, behavioural change and stronger pharmaceutical governance, within national AMR action plans. 2, 7, 12, 14, 35

"Machange": an emerging public health concern

A novel finding was the identification of "Machange", an informal mixture of pharmaceutical products, predominantly antibiotics alongside analgesics/NSAIDs, antiparasitics and antifungals (Figure 2), known to more than half of participants (57.0%) and sold by about twothirds of those who knew it. To our knowledge, this is the first report of this practice among community pharmacy workers in the DRC and one of the first in the peer-reviewed literature.

Combining multiple antimicrobial and non-antimicrobial products in a single preparation may expose patients to inappropriate antibiotic selection, incorrect dosing, adverse reactions and interactions, and could promote resistance through subtherapeutic exposure and selective pressure, recognized drivers of AMR. 1, 2, 5 As this study did not assess its composition, indications or outcomes, no causal relationship with AMR can be established.

This practice likely reflects broader pressures common to resource-limited settings, including poverty, unaffordable healthcare, shortages of prescribers and diagnostics, weak regulation and high out-of-pocket costs, driving informal responses to patients' immediate needs, 2, 7, 25 consistent with adaptive practices documented elsewhere under similar constraints. 14, 26 Given the high proportion of antibiotics reportedly involved, and recent evidence of substantial AMR among clinically important pathogens in Bukavu, 1, 2, 23, 24 "Machange" warrants urgent attention from researchers, clinicians and public health authorities. Our findings should be regarded as preliminary rather than proof of harm, given the absence of pharmaceutical, microbiological or toxicological data. Future qualitative, mixed-methods and laboratory studies should characterize its composition, motivations for use, economic determinants and contribution to AMR, to inform context-specific stewardship interventions and evidence-based policy in the DRC, and more broadly, informal pharmaceutical practices in resource-limited settings worldwide.

One Health approach and neglected zoonotic diseases

The One Health approach is particularly relevant to the findings of this study, as AMR transcends human health and involves animal and environmental reservoirs. Neglected zoonotic diseases, such as Q fever, brucellosis, and leptospirosis, are linked to antimicrobial resistance through shared pathways of antibiotic use in human and veterinary medicine, environmental contamination, and inadequate surveillance systems. In resource-limited settings like the DRC, where diagnostic capacity for zoonotic diseases is limited and medical professionals' knowledge of these conditions is often insufficient, 8 the One Health approach offers a comprehensive framework for coordinated action. Strategies used in this context include: (i) integrated surveillance systems that monitor AMR across human, animal, and environmental sectors; (ii) responsible antibiotic use in both human and veterinary medicine; (iii) improved infection prevention and control measures, including water, sanitation and hygiene (WASH) interventions; (iv) community education and awareness campaigns targeting both healthcare professionals and the public; and (v) multidisciplinary collaboration between physicians, veterinarians, pharmacists, microbiologists, and public health authorities. 9 These strategies are essential for addressing the interconnected challenges of AMR and neglected zoonotic diseases, and for preserving the effectiveness of antimicrobial agents for future generations.

Public health implications

These findings carry important implications for AMS in the DRC and other LMICs. As community pharmacies are among the most accessible entry points into healthcare, suboptimal knowledge, attitudes and dispensing practices may substantially influence antibiotic consumption and the spread of AMR. 2, 12, 35 Beyond individual competence, our findings highlight structural barriers, including limited diagnostic and surveillance capacity, workforce shortages, inconsistent stewardship implementation and weak regulation, [1] [2] [3] compounded by high out-of-pocket costs pushing patients toward self-medication through community pharmacies. [20] [21] [22] 25, 59 Improving stewardship in Bukavu, as elsewhere in sub-Saharan Africa, will require more than educational interventions: stronger regulation, continuing professional development, improved diagnostic and surveillance capacity, and multidisciplinary collaboration between pharmacists, physicians and public health authorities are all needed, 2, 7, 14, 26 an urgency reinforced by recent evidence of substantial AMR among clinically important pathogens in the DRC. 23, 24 A comprehensive, multisectoral response aligned with the One Health approach, strengthening regulation, education, diagnostic capacity, infection prevention and control, WASH interventions and access to affordable, quality-assured antibiotics, could substantially reduce the future AMR-related mortality burden. 1, 2 Integrating community pharmacies into national AMR action plans, and strengthening the professional, regulatory and health-system environments in which pharmacy workers operate, is therefore a public health priority for the DRC and similar resource-limited settings, in line with the WHO Global Action Plan on AMR. 2, 7, 12, 14, 35

Strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths. It is among the first to comprehensively assess KAP regarding antibiotic use and AMR among community pharmacy workers in Eastern DRC, addressing an evidence gap in Central Africa, a region underrepresented in the antimicrobial stewardship literature. 3, 14 The sample of 423 workers, recruited across all three health zones of Bukavu using multistage sampling with random selection of pharmacies and workers within each health area, together with a structured questionnaire adapted from previously validated instruments and pretested locally, and standardized data collection procedures, supports the reliability of the findings. Multivariable logistic regression, controlling for confounders, further strengthened the internal validity of the identified determinants, moving beyond simple description of KAP levels to inform future interventions and policy. As community pharmacies are increasingly recognized as key AMS partners and are often the first point of access to antibiotics in LMICs, 2, 12, 29 these context-specific findings can usefully inform pharmaceutical policy, professional education and stewardship interventions in the DRC. An additional strength was the incidental identification of "Machange" during data collection: although not a pre-specified objective, its repeated mention by participants provided novel evidence of a previously undocumented practice and a starting point for future research from a One Health perspective.

Several limitations should be noted. The cross-sectional design precludes causal inference between the identified determinants and KAP outcomes. Antibiotic dispensing was selfreported and may be affected by recall and social desirability bias, potentially overestimating adherence to appropriate practices. The absence of an up-to-date pharmacy registry in Bukavu prevented a fully probabilistic sampling frame, so residual selection bias cannot be excluded; because one worker was interviewed per pharmacy, workers in larger pharmacies may also be under-represented, and the exclusively urban setting may limit generalizability to rural areas or other DRC provinces. The heterogeneity of the study population, which included both pharmaceutical and non-pharmaceutical personnel, may influence the observed KAP scores and associations; however, this reflects the reality of community pharmacy staffing in the DRC and was accounted for in the analysis through adjustment for professional qualification. Prior antibiotic/AMR training was also self-reported, without verification of its quality, duration or content, which may explain its unexpected association with dispensing practices. The relatively modest Cronbach's alpha for the practice scale (0.652) after removal of three items should also be acknowledged when interpreting the practice score; because item reduction for the attitude and practice scales was data-driven and the final practice score relies on four items, domain classifications may be sensitive to the choice of items and cut-offs. The cut-off for favourable attitudes (≥60%) was lower than for the other domains because very few participants reached 80%, and the analysis was complete-case (age missing for six participants). Finally, as "Machange" was not a primary study objective, no pharmaceutical, microbiological or toxicological analyses were performed to confirm its composition or effects. Despite these limitations, this study provides an important baseline evidence base on antibiotic-related KAP among community pharmacy workers in Eastern DRC and lays the groundwork for future intervention, operational and One Health research to promote rational antibiotic use and curb AMR in resource-limited settings.

Recommendations

Findings indicate widespread knowledge gaps (including near-absent awareness of the WHO AWaRe classification), unfavourable attitudes, and inappropriate dispensing (notably nonprescription antibiotic sales, poor management of expired antibiotics, and the emergence of "Machange"), driven largely by weak regulation, an incomplete pharmacy registry, and limited diagnostic capacity. The following recommendations translate these findings into action.

Health authorities should strengthen enforcement of prescription-only dispensing regulations through regular inspections and supportive supervision, establish and maintain an official registry of licensed community pharmacies to close the regulatory gap identified in this study, and formally integrate community pharmacies into the National AMR Action Plan as partners in stewardship, surveillance and public awareness campaigns. 2, 12, 29 For stewardship programmes, the low knowledge and AWaRe-awareness observed calls for regular competency-based continuing education on rational antibiotic use, the AWaRe classification, infection prevention and stewardship principles, supported by decision-support tools such as national guidelines and AWaRe-based dispensing algorithms, 2 and multidisciplinary stewardship networks linking pharmacists, physicians, microbiologists and public health authorities within a One Health approach. 7, 14 At health-system level, addressing the diagnostic and surveillance gaps underlying poor knowledge and inappropriate practice requires investment in microbiological laboratory capacity and antimicrobial susceptibility testing, integration of national AMR surveillance with the WHO Global Antimicrobial Resistance and Use Surveillance System, 2 reduced financial barriers to formal healthcare to curb self-medication, 25 and strengthened infection prevention and control and WASH programmes. 1, 2 Professional pharmacy organizations should reinforce mandatory continuing education, certification and competency assessment for dispensing personnel, 12, 29 and strengthen interprofessional collaboration between pharmacists, physicians, nurses and microbiologists. 14, 60 Given the novel and unexplained nature of "Machange", priority research should characterize its composition, motivations and dispensing patterns through qualitative and mixed-methods studies, and its pharmacological, toxicological and microbiological effects through laboratory-based investigations, alongside implementation research evaluating the cost-effectiveness of community pharmacy stewardship interventions 7, 14, 26 and operational and surveillance studies on antibiotic self-medication, financial barriers and AMR trends to inform national policy in the DRC. These research priorities should be implemented within a One Health framework that recognizes the interconnectedness of human, animal and environmental health, and that addresses the structural and feasibility constraints facing resource-limited settings.

Conclusions

This study provides one of the earliest comprehensive assessments of antibiotic-related KAP among community pharmacy workers in Eastern DRC, revealing substantial deficiencies across all three domains. Better knowledge and dispensing practices were linked to higher education and professional qualification, while favourable attitudes were the strongest predictor of appropriate dispensing, suggesting that effective stewardship may depend on behavioural, regulatory and health-system factors, not knowledge alone. The study also revealed "Machange", a widely known and widely sold but previously undocumented informal antibiotic mixture requiring urgent further investigation. The near-absent awareness of the WHO AWaRe classification (9.2%) underscores the need for targeted training on this essential stewardship tool. By filling an important evidence gap from a region largely absent from the stewardship literature, these findings can inform pharmaceutical policy, stewardship programmes and future research in the DRC, and reaffirm community pharmacy workers as essential partners in preserving antibiotic effectiveness and combating AMR within a One Health framework.
