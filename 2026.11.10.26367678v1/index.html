No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). Statcheck might still miss one-sided tests that are described in another sentence, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Introduction. Diet quality scores are well-established predictors of cardiometabolic health but compress an individual's food intake into a single dimension, potentially masking dietary variation relevant to disease risk. Whether this hidden variation carries prognostic information has not been tested at scale.

We applied non-negative matrix factorization (NMF) to the reported intake of 148 individual foods and beverages from 87,162 UK Biobank participants. We tested the association of NMF-derived intake factors with incident coronary artery disease (CAD), type 2 diabetes (T2D) and chronic kidney disease (CKD) over a median 9.4 years of follow-up using Cox proportional hazards models. We evaluated the prognostic value of the factors beyond diet quality scores using stepwise Cox models and net reclassification improvement (NRI), and characterized their metabolic profiles through metabolome-wide association analyses (MWAS) of 249 circulating metabolites.

Results. NMF identified eight reproducible intake factors that were stable across geographically distinct samples. Four factors were significantly associated with incident cardiometabolic disease: a high sugar and processed foods factor was adversely associated with T2D (HR 1.19; 95% CI 1.11-1.27) and CKD (HR 1.15; 1.07-1.23), while a plant-based and fish factor was protective for CKD (HR 0.90; 0.84-0.96) and a cheese and whole grains factor was protective for CAD (HR 0.94; 0.90-0.98). The factors improved disease discrimination beyond both diet quality scores (C up to +0.0049) and correctly reclassified 8-16% more individuals, whereas adding either score to models containing the factors produced negligible further improvement. The high sugar and processed foods factor was associated with depletion of omega-3 fatty acids and the plant-based and fish factor showed enrichment in polyunsaturated fatty acids.

Conclusions. Food-level dietary intake profiling captures clinically relevant variation that 48 established diet quality scores mask, offering a path toward more personalized dietary 49 assessment for cardiometabolic disease prevention. 50.

[div-01]

Cardiometabolic diseases, including coronary artery disease (CAD), type 2 diabetes (T2D) and chronic kidney disease (CKD), are the leading causes of morbidity and mortality worldwide, accounting for a combined 12.5 million deaths in 2023 1 . Suboptimal diet is a leading modifiable risk factor for these diseases, [2] [3] [4] yet adherence to population-level dietary guidelines remains low [5] [6] [7] . One reason may be that while current guidelines offer some advice based on life stage and specific health conditions, they do not account for an individual's behavior, biology or preferences 8 . Personalized dietary guidance may improve long-term adherence, but it requires methods that describe what an individual actually eats, including which foods are consumed together, rather than only how healthy their diet is overall 9, 10.

A central challenge in nutritional epidemiology is how best to assess and represent dietary intake. A priori diet quality scores like the Alternative Healthy Eating Index-2010 11 (AHEI) and alternate Mediterranean Diet score 12 (aMED) quantify and evaluate the healthfulness of a person's diet by in a single number [11] [12] [13] . Because two people can reach the same score through different combinations of foods, a single score may conceal disease-relevant differences between them; whether this hidden variation carries prognostic information has not been tested at scale 14.

Data-driven methods, including principal component analysis (PCA), factor analysis and clustering 14, 15 , have been developed to derive empirical dietary patterns from food intake data 14.

However, these methods yield patterns with both positive and negative food weights that are hard to interpret, typically assign each individual to one dominant pattern and usually require foods to be collapsed into broad groups prior to analysis [14] [15] [16].

Non-negative matrix factorization (NMF) is a representation learning method that identifies groups of foods that tend to be eaten together, directly from each person's reported intake. As food intake is naturally non-negative (people eat zero servings or more of each food) and often sparse (many foods are eaten only occasionally), NMF suits the structure of dietary data 17, 18.

Importantly, NMF represents each person's dietary intake as a blend of multiple intake factors rather than assigning them to a single dietary pattern, and it allows the contribution of individual foods to be traced through each factor, properties that may facilitate more personalized dietary guidance 9, 18 . Previous applications of NMF in nutritional epidemiology identified dietary patterns and described their characteristics but did not test whether these patterns predicted health outcomes over time, reduced the food intake data into food groups before analysis and assigned individuals to a single pattern [22] [23] [24] 46.

To test whether food-level dietary patterns capture clinically relevant variation that diet quality scores miss, we applied NMF to 148 foods and beverages reported by 87,162 UK Biobank (UKB) participants. We derived eight intake factors in a discovery sample and confirmed their stability in held-out replication and geographically distinct validation samples. We related these factors to incident CAD, T2D and CKD over a median follow-up of 9.4 years, tested whether they added prognostic information beyond the AHEI and aMED, estimated their potential population impact and characterized their metabolic profiles. Finally, we compared individuals with nearidentical diet quality scores but different factor profiles and traced differences in their predicted risk to specific foods.

Results

This study included 87,162 UKB participants with at least two valid 24-hour dietary recalls (Fig. 1a). Participants had a mean age (SD) of 59.2 (7.8) years, mean body mass index (BMI) of 26.3 (4.3) kg/m 2 and 57.0% were female (Extended Data Table 1). Over a median follow-up of 9.4 years, we observed 3,459 (4.0%) incident CAD events, 1,452 (1.7%) incident T2D events and 1,723 (2.0%) incident CKD events.

Eight intake factors replicate across independent samples.

We applied NMF to the reported intake of 148 individual foods and beverages in a discovery dataset of 55,839 UKB participants recruited in England (70% random sample; Fig. 1b). The method identified groups of foods that are commonly consumed together, which we refer to as intake factors, without requiring foods to be aggregated into pre-defined groups 19 . A structured selection process determined that eight factors best balanced the ability to represent individual variation in dietary intake with the stability and interpretability of the factors (Methods; Supplementary Figs. 1 2 3 4). The remaining participants recruited in England formed a replication dataset (n = 23,932) and participants recruited in Scotland and Wales formed a geographically independent validation dataset (n = 7,391; Fig. 1a).

The eight factors spanned a wide range of dietary intake behaviors (Fig. 2a), with a mean of 11.3 active foods per factor and a median of 43% of foods that were non-zero but below the active threshold (Methods; Supplementary Table 9). Factor 1 (F1; prudent base) was the broadest, defined by tea, high-fiber fruits, whole grains and low-fat dairy. Factor 4 (F4; high sugar and processed) concentrated on refined grains, added sugar and sugar-sweetened beverages (SSBs). Factor 6 (F6; fruit and LF dairy) was defined by tropical fruits (grapes, berries, melons, pineapple) and low-fat yogurt. Factor 7 (F7; plant-based and fish) was a nutrient-dense profile defined by plant-based fats (nuts, seeds, avocado), spinach and oily fish. Notably, foods from single food groups (e.g., vegetables) were active across multiple factors, reflecting the diversity of culinary contexts in which individual foods are consumed. Interfactor correlations were low (maximum |𝑟| = 0.35), suggesting that the factors captured distinct dimensions of dietary intake (Fig. 2b).

To confirm that the factors were not specific to the discovery sample, we tested their reproducibility in two ways (Methods). First, we independently derived factors in the replication and validation datasets. All eight factors reproduced in the replication dataset, with high agreement in food composition (top-10 food overlap per factor of 67-100%; Extended Data Fig. 1). In the geographically independent validation dataset, six of eight factors reproduced; the prudent base (F1) and plant-based and fish (F7) factors did not, likely reflecting regional differences in food culture between England and Scotland/Wales. Second, we applied the discovery-derived factor structure to new participants and found that it explained dietary variation equally well in the replication dataset and with only a modest decrease in the validation dataset (Methods; Supplementary Table 10). Together, these results indicate that the eight factors capture reproducible patterns of dietary intake.

Most participants' diets combine several intake factors.

NMF represents an individuals diet as a blend of all eight factors. To descriptively characterize this variation, we normalized factor loadings to proportional contributions and assigned individuals to archetypes based on their dominant factor loading (Methods). Most participants (52%) had a Mixed archetype, meaning no single factor accounted for a large share of their dietary intake (Fig. 2c; Supplementary Table 11). Among participants with a dominant factor, those in the plant-based and fish (F7) archetype were predominantly female (78.4%), had the highest diet quality scores and lowest BMI (24.4 kg/m 2 ), whereas the high sugar and processed foods (F4) archetype was predominanly male (70.8%) had the lowest diet quality scores and highest BMI (28.1 kg/m 2 ; Extended Data Table 1). Factor-diet quality score correlations were modest (maximum |𝑟| = 0.37) and two factors, F3 (meat and starchy vegetables) and F8 (raw salad), showed little correlation with either score, suggesting they capture variation in dietary intake not well represented by the aggregate AHEI and aMED scores (Supplementary Fig. 5). The combination of an individual's primary and secondary factor loading was also associated with their AHEI and aMED scores (Supplementary Fig. 6).

Intake factors are associated with incident cardiometabolic diseases.

We tested each factor in relation to incident CAD, T2D and CKD using Cox proportional hazards models adjusted for demographic, socioeconomic and lifestyle covariates in the combined sample of participants recruited in England (n = 79,771; Methods; Fig. 1c). Hazard ratios (HRs; pooled across imputed datasets) were expressed per 1-SD higher factor loading, with all eight factors entered simultaneously. Three factors had FDR-significant associations (Fig. 3a). The high sugar and processed foods factor (F4) had the strongest adverse associations, with HRs (95% CIs) of 1.19 (1.11-1.27; P = 9.2 x 10 -6 ) for T2D and 1.15 (1.07-1.23; P = 5.2 x 10 -5 ) for CKD. Individuals in the highest tertile of F4 loading consumed approximately 1.0 more serving/day of white bread, 0.6 more of added sugar, 0.3 more of crisps and 0.2 more of fried potatoes than individuals with zero loading on F4 (Supplementary File 6). The plant-based and fish factor (F7) showed the strongest protective association, with a HR of 0.90 (0.84-0.96; P = 8.9 x 10 -4 ) for CKD. The highest-tertile F7 consumers ate approximately 0.25 more servings/day of unsalted nuts and 0.11 more of seeds than individuals with zero loading on F7 (Supplementary File 6). The cheese and whole grains factor (F2) was protective for CAD (HR 0.94, 0.90-0.98; P = 0.003). Several additional associations were nominally significant, including F5 (aromatic vegetables and rice) for T2D (HR 1.07, P = 0.015), F8 (raw salad) for T2D (HR 0.94, P = 0.041) and F2 for CKD (HR 0.94, P = 0.045).

Results were robust in pre-specified sensitivity analyses, including extended washout periods, restriction to participants with three or more dietary recalls, and compositional regression models that account for the substitution structure of dietary intake (Supplementary Files 11-12). E-values for FDR-significant associations ranged from 1.33 to 1.66, indicating that unmeasured confounding would need to be at least moderate to explain these findings (Supplementary Table 12). Pre-specified subgroup analyses tested interactions with sex, age, BMI, smoking status and ethnicity; no interaction survived correction for multiple testing (9 of 120 tests nominally significant at P < 0.05, none at Bonferroni-corrected P < 4.2 x 10 -4 ; Supplementary Files 9-10).

Random survival forests (RSF) ranked the high sugar and processed foods factor (F4) as the most important dietary factor for T2D (7th of 23 predictors) and CKD (4th), and the meat and starchy vegetables factor (F3) as the most important dietary factor for CAD (10th; Methods; Supplementary File 14).

Intake factors add prognostic value beyond established diet quality scores.

To test whether the factor-disease associations were independent of overall diet quality, we additionally adjusted for AHEI and aMED in the primary Cox models. The core associations (F4 adverse for T2D and CKD; F7 protective for CKD; F2 protective for CAD) remained FDRsignificant after adjustment for either score (Fig. 3b).

We next asked whether the factors and diet quality scores carried overlapping or distinct prognostic information using stepwise Cox models fitted in the discovery dataset and validated in the held-out replication dataset (Methods). Adding the eight NMF factors to the covariate-adjusted model improved discrimination for all three outcomes (C = + 0.0018 for CAD, +0.0046 for T2D, +0.0049 for CKD; Table 1). The improvements were confirmed by bootstrapped confidence intervals and time-dependent area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) at an 8-year horizon (Table 1; Supplementary File 15). By comparison, adding AHEI or aMED individually produced smaller improvements (C  +0.0025). The relationship was asymmetric: adding AHEI or aMED to a model containing the NMF factors produced negligible further improvement (C =  +0.0005), whereas adding the factors to a model containing either score improved discrimination by +0.0017 to +0.0045 (Table 1). This indicates that the factors capture most of the diet-related prognostic signal contained in AHEI and aMED scores, while also carrying additional information that the scores do not.

To assess whether this improved discrimination translated into better risk stratification, we computed the category-free net reclassification improvement (NRI) at an 8-year horizon.

Replacing AHEI or aMED with the NMF factors improved risk classification for all three outcomes (NRI 0.08-0.16). The improvement was the largest for T2D (NRI = 0.162 [0.088, 0.236] vs. AHEI; 0.164 [0.091, 0.238] vs. aMED) and replicated in the held-out dataset (Table 1; Extended Data Table 4).

Finally, to estimate the potential population health impact, assuming causal associations, we computed population attributable fractions (PAF) for each FDR-significant factor-outcome pair (Methods). An estimated 20.9% [14.9, 27.9] of the incident CKD cases and 14.9% [7.2, 22.2] of T2D cases were attributable to the highest (vs. lowest) tertile of intake of the high sugar and processed foods factor (F4), while lowest (vs. highest) tertile of intake of the plant-based and fish (F7) and cheese and whole grains (F2) factors accounted for 17.6% [10.6, 24.9] of CKD and 8.2% [3.3, 12.7] of CAD cases, respectively (Supplementary File 17). After mutual adjustment for all eight factors, PAFs remained significant for CKD and CAD but were attenuated for T2D (Supplementary File 17).

Intake factors have distinct metabolite profiles.

To characterize their metabolic profiles, we tested the association of each factor with 249 circulating NMR metabolites (1,992 factor-metabolite pairs) in the discovery dataset and validated results in the held-out replication dataset (Methods; Fig. 1c). Across the eight factors, 1,226 pairs (61.5%) reached FDR significance (Methods), predominantly involving lipoprotein subclass remodeling and fatty acid composition. Of those, 413 (33.7%) replicated in the held-out dataset (Bonferroni-corrected P <0.05), consistent with prior diet-metabolite studies 20, 21 and with concordant effect sizes between datasets (Fig. 4a). Replication rates varied by factor: F4 (high sugar and processed foods) and F7 (plant-based and fish) had the highest rates (64%), whereas F3 (meat and starchy vegetables) and F5 (aromatic vegetables and rice) had few replicated associations. The metabolic profiles for F4 and F7 were consistent with their contrasting dietary compositions and cardiometabolic risk associations. F4 was associated with depletion of circulating docosahexaenoic acid (DHA;  = -0.20 per SD) and omega-3 fatty acids, whereas F7 showed strong positive associations with polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs;  = +0.13) and DHA ( = +0.11). Associations with high-density lipoprotein (HDL) particle remodeling were particularly prominent for F2 (cheese and whole grains; Fig. 4b). Full MWAS results are shown in Supplementary File 19.

Participants with the same diet quality scores but different intake profiles differ in cardiometabolic risk markers.

To test whether the additional information captured by NMF manifests in clinical biomarker profiles, we matched participants in the replication dataset (n = 23,932) on their diet quality score (energy-adjusted AHEI or aMED) and total energy intake, using a caliper of 0.25 SD on each variable (Methods; Fig. 1c). Matching on energy intake ensured that differences between paired participants reflected dietary composition rather than how much they ate overall, yielding 11,958 AHEI-matched and 11,959 aMED-matched pairs. We then computed an NMF-derived composite cardiometabolic risk score for each participant (Methods) and designated the higher-scoring member of each pair as the "adverse" member and the other as the "favorable" member. Among the top 5% of AHEI-matched pairs with the most divergent NMF profiles, termed discordant pairs (n = 598), adverse members had significantly higher BMI (Cohen's d = 0.22, FDR q = 0.006), body fat percentage (d = 0.19, q = 0.006), white blood cell count (d = 0.26, q = 0.001) and lower vitamin D (d = -0.20, q = 0.008; Fig. 5a, Supplementary Table 13). Among aMED-discordant pairs, differences extended to lipids (HDL-C: d = -0.25; triglycerides: d = 0.14; ApoA: d = -0.19; all q < 0.05) and inflammatory markers (CRP: d = 0.19, q = 0.012; Fig. 5b, Supplementary Table 14).

Thus, people whom diet quality scores rate as equally healthy can differ meaningfully in what they eat and in clinical risk markers.

To illustrate risk attribution at the individual level, we examined one of the most discordant pairs, matched not only on AHEI score (~41) but also on sex, age, BMI, smoking status and alcohol intake (Methods; Extended Data Fig. 2a). Individual A's diet was dominated by F1 (prudent base; 64%), with smaller contributions from F4 (high sugar and processed foods; 17%) and F2 (cheese and whole grains; 14%). Individual B's diet was more evenly spread, led by F5 (aromatic vegetables and rice; 41%), followed by F8 (raw salad; 15%), F2 (14%) and F7 (plant-based and fish; 13%; Extended Data Fig 2b). Despite similar AHEI scores, Individual A's profile predicted higher CKD risk (relative HR = 1.21, computed from that individual's factor scores relative to the cohort mean) and Individual B's higher T2D risk (relative HR = 1.24), each driven by different foods (Extended Data Fig. 2c). For T2D, Individual A's risk was driven mainly by crisps, white bread, added sugar, sweets and fried food, whereas Individual B's came from refined grains and red meat. Dietary advice for Individual A might therefore focus on reducing sugary and processed snacks, and for Individual B on replacing refined grains and red meat with whole grains and poultry, seafood or plant-based protein.

Discussion

Applying NMF to food-level intake data from 87,162 UKB participants, we identified eight reproducible intake factors with four significant factor-disease associations: the high sugar and processed foods factor (F4) with higher risk of T2D and CKD, and the cheese and whole grains (F2) and plant-based and fish (F7) factors with lower risk of CAD and CKD, respectively. The factors had distinct metabolic profiles, with F4 showing a pro-atherogenic profile and F7 a strong shift toward PUFAs. The factors added prognostic value beyond both the AHEI and aMED, whereas neither score added meaningfully beyond the factors, and among with matched scores, those with the most divergent factor profiles differed in adiposity, inflammatory and lipid biomarkers. Food-level intake factors therefore capture clinically relevant dietary variation that diet quality scores mask.

The factor-disease associations identified here refine findings from prior dietary pattern studies.

The adverse F4 factor recapitulates the "Western" pattern linked to T2D and cardiovascular risk in PCA-based studies [22] [23] [24] [25] . The association with CKD may partly reflect the high sodium content of several top-weighted F4 foods and ultra-processed foods in general, consistent with evidence linking sodium intake to reduced kidney function 26, 27 . The protective F7 factor aligns with foods emphasized by AHEI and aMED [28] [29] [30] [31] and the "prudent" pattern that has been linked to healthier outcomes 23, 32, 33 . A notable finding was that the coffee, cheese and whole grains factor (F2) was the only factor protective for CAD. This is consistent with evidence supporting coffee [34] [35] [36] and fullfat and fermented dairy [37] [38] [39] as protective for cardiometabolic disease. The separation of cheese into a distinct factor from other dairy products would likely be diluted if dairy intake were aggregated into a single group, illustrating the value of food-level resolution. Although not significant after multiple testing correction, the nominally adverse association between the aromatic vegetables and rice factor (F5) and T2D also highlights this advantage. While vegetable intake is generally considered protective, the vegetables in this factor are co-consumed with highglycemic white rice and white pasta, which may offset their benefits for blood sugar control. Our approach differs from prior NMF applications 17, 18, 40 in three key aspects: we retained 148 individual foods rather than aggregating to food groups, we preserved continuous mixture representations rather than clustering participants into discrete groups and we tested whether NMF-derived factors carried prognostic information beyond established diet quality scores.

A central finding of this work is that NMF intake factors add prognostic value beyond established diet quality scores, and that the reverse is not true. When both were tested in stepwise Cox regression models, the factors subsumed nearly all diet-related prognostic signal captured by AHEI and aMED, while retaining significant discrimination and reclassification gains. This asymmetry likely reflects a fundamental difference in how dietary intake is represented. Whereas AHEI and aMED compress food intake into a single score 11, 12 , NMF preserves the multidimensional structure of dietary intake behavior. The discrimination advantage replicated for T2D and CKD in the held-out dataset; the attenuation for CAD likely reflects limited statistical power (~970 events), though the direction of improvement was consistent. The PAF estimates further suggest that the identified factors could represent meaningful targets for cardiometabolic disease prevention, with the largest attributable burdens observed for CKD. After mutual adjustment for all eight factors, the independent PAFs remained significant for CKD and CAD, whereas the T2D PAF for F4 was attenuated, suggesting that part of this factor's population-level T2D burden is shared with correlated intake factors. These findings have practical implications at three levels. For research, food-level factors complement rather than replace diet quality scores. The scores remain simple, guideline-based summaries that are easy to communicate, whereas the factors capture variation the scores miss, including two factors (F3 and F8) that were largely uncorrelated with either score, and they identify which combinations of foods carry risk. For clinical care, an individual's factor profile could direct counseling to the specific foods driving their predicted risk, as the matched-pair example illustrates, rather than to an overall score that two people can reach through very different diets.

For population health, if the associations are causal, the PAF estimates suggest that shifting intake away from the high sugar and processed foods factor could prevent a meaningful share of incident T2D and CKD. Because the gains in discrimination were modest in absolute terms, the factors are better viewed as a way to target dietary advice than as a stand alone risk prediction tool.

The metabolomics results provide insights into the biological characteristics associated with each factor and their potential relevance to cardiometabolic disease. The depletion of circulating DHA, omega-3 fatty acids and elevated very-low-density lipoprotein triglycerides characterizing the high sugar and processed foods factor closely mirrors lipidomic biomarkers of ultra-processed diets and low seafood intake 41 . This likely reflects displacement of nutrient-rich foods by energy-dense foods 27 . Conversely, the enrichment in PUFAs for the plant-based and fish factor aligns with controlled feeding studies that identify circulating PUFAs as biomarkers of nut and plant-based oil consumption 42 . The shift toward large, cholesterol-rich HDL particles associated with the cheese and whole grains factor is consistent with metabolomic studies linking dairy and whole grain intake to favorable lipoprotein profiles 43 . These divergent profiles suggest that distinct eating behaviors influence cardiometabolic disease through heterogeneous biological mechanisms that a single summary score cannot resolve.

The study should be interpreted in the light of some limitations. The factor structure replicated with high fidelity within individuals recruited in England but showed attenuation in those recruited in Scotland and Wales, suggesting that dietary factors are shaped by population-level food cultures and would need to be re-derived in cohorts with substantially different dietary norms.

Additionally, although NMF is data-driven, analytical decisions including food preprocessing and the choice of eight factors involve subjective judgement. Finally, NMF introduces eight continuous exposures compared with a single diet quality score, raising the question of overfitting. However, likelihood ratio tests penalize for additional degrees of freedom, the adjusted R² metric accounts for model complexity and the factors added discrimination beyond models already containing AHEI or aMED while the reverse did not hold.

Despite these limitations, the UKB's scale, linked EHR records and the discovery-replicationvalidation design 53 allowed us to derive and replicate intake factors, test their associations with cardiometabolic disease, characterize their metabolic signatures and identify individuals whose cardiometabolic risk is masked by diet quality scores. The food-level resolution of individual risk profiles could enable more targeted dietary counseling by identifying which foods drive a given patient's cardiometabolic risk. Integrating NMF-based profiling into clinical workflows would require validation in diverse populations and development of interpretable reporting tools.

These results suggest several directions for future work, including validation in diverse cohorts with different dietary cultures and assessment instruments, randomized trials testing whether targeting an individual's adverse factors improves cardiometabolic risk beyond standard guidance, and extensions that capture meal timing and interactions between foods, which additive factors cannot represent 57, 59 . Ultimately, our findings demonstrate that food-level dietary intake profiling captures clinically meaningful variation that established diet quality scores mask, offering a path toward more personalized dietary assessment to support cardiometabolic health.

Methods

Ethical Approval

UK Biobank (UKB) data access was approved by the UKB through project application number 1251. The UKB study received ethical approval from the NHS National Research Ethics Service (Ref 11/NW/0382), and all participants provided informed consent. Researchers do not need separate approval.

Study population

The UKB is a multi-center prospective cohort study of more than 500,000 participants aged 40-70 years at enrollment 44 . Participants were recruited from 2006 to 2010 at 22 assessment centers across England, Scotland and Wales, with baseline information, health and lifestyle questionnaires, physical measures and biological samples collected according to standardized procedures [45] [46] [47] . Between 2009 and 2012 a subset of participants completed up to five online 24hour dietary recall assessments using the Oxford WebQ 48 . We restricted the analysis to participants who completed at least two valid dietary recalls to better reflect usual dietary intake 28 , resulting in an initial sample of 102,316 participants. The Oxford WebQ has demonstrated acceptable validity and reproducibility when using 2 dietary recalls 49 . A dietary recall was considered invalid if it reported atypical intake, duration of less than 5 minutes, had no completion time or reported implausible daily energy intake, defined as <800 or >4,200 kcal/d for male and <500 or >3,500 kcal/d for female 28, 50 participants. We excluded participants who were pregnant, or had prevalent CAD, T2D, CKD, other diabetes mellitus or cancer. Cancer was defined using the union of 11 cancer sites with non-melanoma skin cancer (ICD-10 C44) excluded 51.

Participants with glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) ≥6.0% (42 mmol/mol) were excluded to remove individuals with undiagnosed or borderline diabetes 52 . We defined baseline as the date of each participant's last valid dietary recall. The final analytic cohort included 87,162 participants.

For analyses requiring independent cohorts, we used a three-way geographic split 51 . The discovery (n=55,839) and replication (n=23,932) datasets included participants recruited from assessment centers in England using a 70/30 random split stratified by age, sex and number of valid dietary recalls. The validation dataset (n=7,391) included all participants recruited from Scotland and Wales, providing an independent test of generalizability across regional dietary cultures. The discovery and replication datasets were recombined (n=79,771) for all primary Cox association analyses after structure replication of dietary factors was confirmed. The discovery dataset was retained separately for metabolomics association analysis, added prognostic value model training and discordance analysis; the replication dataset served as the held-out validation cohort for each of these analyses. The Scotland and Wales validation dataset was held out exclusively for NMF factor generalization assessment 51.

Dietary assessment

Dietary intake was assessed using the validated Oxford WebQ, a web-based 24-hour recall dietary assessment tool 48 . Out of the 102,316 participants with at least two valid dietary recalls, 42,898 completed two, 28,884 completed three, 13,511 completed four and 1,869 completed five.

Participants reported their consumption of up to 206 food and beverage items during the previous day on each occasion. For this study, we derived intake estimates in servings per day, averaging intake across all valid recalls for each participant to approximate usual dietary exposure. 196 individual foods and beverages remained after recoding and quality control (Supplementary Information).

Food matrix construction.

We removed alcoholic beverages and plain drinking water from the food matrix prior to factor derivation. Alcohol was excluded from the NMF input matrix because alcohol intake (continuous grams (g) per day) was included separately as a covariate in the outcome models, avoiding overlap between the derived dietary factors and the adjustment variables 51 . Plain drinking water was excluded because it contributed no nutrients and loaded heavily across all NMF-derived factors in preliminary analyses, diluting factor-specific signal.

Rarely consumed foods and beverages (<5% of population) were removed. The remaining 148 food intake values were preprocessed in three sequential steps: winsorization at the 99 th percentile, log transformation (log(1 + x)) and division by the standard deviation (SD) of each food. Preprocessing parameters including the rarely consumed foods that were dropped, 99 th percentile thresholds and standard deviations, were computed on the discovery dataset and applied to the replication and validation datasets.

Diet quality scores.

Estimated nutrients and energy intake from the Oxford WebQ were used to compute two a priori diet quality scores, the Alternative Healthy Eating Index-2010 (AHEI) and the alternate Mediterranean Diet Score (aMED). For each participant, the mean intakes across all valid 24-hour dietary recalls were analyzed. The AHEI score was developed based on the consumption of 11 foods and nutrients that have been shown to predict chronic disease risk, including fruits, vegetables, nuts and legumes, alcohol, red and processed meat, whole grains, sodium, trans fats and long-chain omega()-3 fatty acids 53 . For this study, we used the AHEI-2010 version, which has been adapted to the UKB 11 . We could not include the energy from transfat due to a lack of information in the UKB, and we used seafood intake for the -3 component because the UKB only provides information about total -3 intake 31 . Each component was scored continuously from 0 (least healthy) to 10 (most healthy), producing a total score ranging from 0 to 100 (Supplementary Table 1). The aMED score calculates adherence to 9 eating behaviors that characterize the Mediterranean diet 12 . Intakes of vegetables, legumes, fruits, nuts, whole grains, fish, and monounsaturated (MUFA) to saturated (SFA) fatty acid ratio above the median received 1 point, while intakes below the median received 0 points. Intakes of red and processed meats above the median received 0 points, and intakes below the median received 1 point (Supplementary Table 2). Alcohol intake between 5-25g received 1 point, with all other intake levels receiving 0 points. The final score ranged from 0 to 9. For both AHEI and aMED, a higher score reflects overall better diet quality 11, 12 . Both scores were adjusted for total energy intake using the Willett residual method, in which the raw score is regressed on total energy intake (kcal/day) and the residual is added to the score predicted at the sample mean energy intake 54.

Total energy intake included energy from alcohol and energy-adjusted scores were used in all downstream analyses.

Outcome ascertainment

Incident events for CAD, T2D and CKD were identified using the UKB first occurrence of health outcomes data (Category 1712), which maps Read codes from primary care, ICD-9 and ICD-10 codes from hospital inpatient data, and ICD-10 codes from death registrations to three-character ICD-10 codes with the first occurrence date as the date the code was first recorded across any source 55 . Incident events were defined using registry-grade evidence only (hospital inpatient records, death registrations or primary care) with participants flagged as incident by self-report alone treated as censored 51 . First occurrences data were accessed on 22 September 2022, with censoring dates of 30 September 2021, 31 July 2021, and 31 October 2021 for participants recruited in England, Scotland and Wales, respectively.

Baseline prevalence for all diseases and clinical risk factors was assessed using a combination of first occurrence data, cancer registry data, self-reported illness, medication use, surgical history and/or biochemical markers. Baseline prevalent CAD, T2D, CKD, other diabetes mellitus and cancer cases were excluded from the cohort. Incident cases of other or unspecified diabetes mellitus (n=473) were additionally excluded to control for reverse causation 56 . Participants were followed from the date of their last valid dietary recall until the first occurrence of CAD, T2D or CKD, death, loss to follow-up or the end of outcome ascertainment based on the nation-specific censor dates, whichever came first. Data sources, ICD codes and evidence rules for all prevalent and incident disease definitions are provided in Supplementary Table 5.

Assessment of covariates

Multivariable models were adjusted for a range of demographic, socioeconomic, lifestyle and clinical covariates that were selected at baseline based on the existing literature. Covariates were organized into three sequential tiers of adjustment. Model 1 (M1, minimally-adjusted) included total energy intake (quintiles). Model 2 (M2, primary) additionally included ethnicity (White or non-White), highest educational qualification (7, 10, 13, 15, 19 or 20 years), annual household income (<18k, 18-31k, 31-52k, 52-100k or >100k), smoking status (never, former or current), alcohol intake (g/day), leisure-time physical activity (low, moderate or high), body mass index (BMI, kg/m 2 ), dietary supplement use (yes/no), family history of diabetes (yes/no) and family history of heart disease (yes/no). Nation was not included as a covariate because datasets were split by nation. The Cox analyses were sex-stratified, rather than including sex as a covariate, and age was the timescale (see Cox regression). Additional details on derived variable calculation and quality control can be found in Supplementary Information.

Missing data imputation

In contrast to data with genuine missingness, a "Prefer not to answer" (PNA) response can be considered an informative non-response 57 . PNA responses were therefore retained as an explicit factor level for annual household income, smoking status, ethnicity, highest educational qualification and supplement use, rather than recoding as missing. The average percentage of missing data across all final variables was ~1.0% (range: 0-4%; Supplementary Table 6). We performed missing data imputation separately for each dataset (discovery, replication and validation) using multiple imputation by chained equations (MICE) with the R package mice 58 . For each, five multiply imputed datasets were generated with a maximum of ten iterations for each imputation. The variables used as predictors in the imputation model included all baseline covariates, NMF intake factor shares, energy-adjusted diet quality scores (AHEI, aMED), and the Nelson-Aalen estimate of cumulative hazard and event indicator for each primary outcome. All statistical analyses were run independently in each of the five imputed datasets, with results pooled using Rubin's rule 59, 60.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed in R v4.6.1 and Python v3.11.

Non-negative matrix factorization

We applied NMF to the preprocessed food intake matrix V (participants  food) composed of the intake of 148 foods and beverages (servings/d) across 55,839 individuals in the discovery dataset.

NMF decomposes the input matrix into two lower-rank non-negative matrices: a participant-byfactor loading matrix W and a factor-by-food weight matrix H, such that V  W x H 19 . NMF summarizes the information available in food intake data into an optimal number of k latent intake factors and for each factor, each food has a weight that represents the contribution of the food to the construction of that factor 17 . The non-negativity constraint of NMF ensures that every factor is interpretable as an additive combination of foods consumed 19 . NMF was implemented using scikit-learn v1.6.1 NMF class with coordinate descent solver, Frobenius loss and _H = 0.005 and l1_ratio = 0.80 as regularization parameters.

Rank selection.

The number of latent factors (rank k) was determined through a multi-phase selection process involving stability analyses on the discovery dataset and evaluation of factor reproducibility in the replication dataset. In the first phase, explained variance, residual sum of squares and sparsity indices were computed using deterministic NNDSVD initialization 61 across ranks k = 2-40 without regularization to identify a candidate range. In the second phase, the sensitivity of factor structure to L1 regularization was evaluated across a grid of _H (0.0-0.5) and l1_ratio (0.8, 1.0) values. Factor structure was largely insensitive to penalty strength, reflecting the intrinsic sparsity of the food intake matrix (79.7% zeros). In the third phase, factor stability was assessed at candidate ranks k = 8, 10, 12, 14 using 30 random initializations per rank, with stability evaluated by Hungarian-matched H matrix cosine similarity across all pairwise run comparisons and 10 split-half resamples. In the fourth phase, the representative factorization was selected from 30 random initializations as the run with the lowest reconstruction error, following a multi-start approach analogous to that used in latent factor decomposition of genetic data 62 . We selected k = 8 as the final rank based on de novo replication performance (see below); all eight factors replicated with cosine similarity >0.98 between independently fitted discovery and replication models, whereas = 10 had two factors with cosine similarity <0.65 that failed to replicate. At k = 8, the factorization explained 29.2% of the squared Frobenius norm in the food intake matrix. Full rank selection diagnostics are provided in Supplementary Fig. 1 2 3 4.

Pattern interpretation.

Factors were interpreted based on H-matrix food loadings. For each factor, the relative contribution (%) of each food was calculated as the ratio of that food's weight to the sum of all food weights within the factor 18 . Foods with a relative contribution  2.5% were considered active in each factor 18 . An exclusivity index was calculated as the ratio of each food's loading on a given factor to its total loading across all factors, distinguishing foods unique to a given factor from widely consumed staples. Labels were assigned descriptively from the active foods in each factor.

Archetype assignment.

To descriptively characterize individual-level factor dominance, each participant's factor loadings were normalized to proportional contributions by dividing by their row sum. The archetype threshold was determined by starting at 50% and lowering in 10% increments until each archetype contained at least 200 participants, yielding a final threshold of 40%.

Participants whose highest proportional loading exceeded this threshold were assigned to that factor's archetype; all others were classified as Mixed. The raw salad (F8) archetype contained too few participants (n=18) for meaningful characterization and was merged into the Mixed group.

De novo replication.

To confirm that the factors reflected stable dietary structure rather than sample-specific noise, we fitted NMF independently in the discovery, replication and validation datasets using identical hyperparameters (k = 8, _H = 0.005, l1_ratio = 0.80, 50 random initializations, 500 maximum iterations). The representative factorization was selected for each dataset as the run with the lowest reconstruction error. Factors were aligned across dataset pairs using the Hungarian algorithm applied to the pairwise cosine similarity matric of H-matrix rows.

Per-factor cosine similarity, Spearman ρ on food loadings and Jaccard overlap of the top 10 foods were computed for each matched factor pair.

Generalization.

To assess the generalizability of the factors, the H-matrix was frozen from the discovery dataset and held-out participants were projected onto it using non-negative least squares (NNLS) via scikit-learn's NMF.transform(). Given held-out data 𝑉 𝑡𝑒𝑠𝑡 and fixed H, 𝑊 𝑡𝑒𝑠𝑡 = min‖𝑉 𝑡𝑒𝑠𝑡 -𝑊 𝑡𝑒𝑠𝑡 𝐻‖ 2 , subject to 𝑊 𝑡𝑒𝑠𝑡 ≥ 0. Generalization was evaluated in the replication (within-population) and validation (geographically independent) datasets by comparing reconstruction performance with that of the discovery dataset and examining differences in perfactor loading distributions using Kolmogorov-Smirnov tests.

Cox proportional hazards regression

Cox proportional hazards models were used to estimate hazard ratios (HR) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for the association between each NMF factor and incident cardiometabolic disease.

Analyses were conducted in the combined English sample (discovery + replication) 51 . Given the strong confounding effect of age on both dietary intake and disease risk, we used age as the timescale. Participants entered the risk set at their age at baseline (date of last valid dietary recall) and exited at their age at event or censoring 63 . Events occurring within the first year of followwere excluded to reduce the potential influence of reverse causation. The baseline hazard was stratified via sex with ties handled using the Efron approximation.

Factor scores (W-matrix loadings) were z-score standardized using the combined-sample mean and SD for each factor and all eight factors were entered into each model simultaneously, such that the HR for a given factor represents the change in hazard per 1-SD increase, holding all other factors constant. Three tiers of covariate adjustment (M1, M2, M3) were applied as described above, with M2 as the primary model. Results were pooled across m = 5 imputed datasets using Rubin's rule for coefficient estimates and the D2 statistic for likelihood ratio tests 59, 60 . Doseresponse gradients were examined by categorizing participants into those with zero loading on a given factor and tertiles of positive factor loading (T1 -T3) with the zero-loading group as the reference. E values were computed for significant HRs to assess the potential impact of unmeasured confounding 64, 65 . Pre-specified subgroup analyses tested for effect modification by sex, age (above/below median), BMI (<25 vs. 25 kg/m 2 ), physical activity, smoking status and ethnicity (White vs. non-White). Multiple comparisons were corrected using Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) within each outcome (eight tests per outcome); FDR-corrected P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. The proportional hazards assumption was assessed using Schoenfeld residuals and visual inspection of scaled residual plots, which revealed no clinically meaningful time-varying trends (Supplementary File 8).

Sensitivity analyses.

Three pre-specified sensitivity analyses assessed the robustness of the primary Cox associations. First, the washout period was extended to 2 years to further address reverse causation. Second, analyses were restricted to participants with three or more valid dietary recalls to assess robustness when dietary assessment was more complete (n = 44,264). Third, BMI was removed from the M2 covariate set to quantify the proportion of the association potentially mediated through adiposity. As a sensitivity analysis for the factorization itself, we repeated NMF using Sparse NMF (SNMF/R 66 ) with the Python package nimfa 67 with an explicit sparsity penalty on the H-matrix. Full sensitivity analysis results are provided in Supplementary Table 8).

Compositional data analysis.

Dietary intake is inherently compositional; increasing consumption of one food necessarily displaces another when total energy intake is held constant 68 . To address this constraint, we applied log-ratio transformations that enable valid inference about the substitution structure of dietary intake 69 . The primary compositional analysis used the additive log-ratio (ALR) transformation 70 . For each participant, k -1 = 7 log-ratios ln ( 𝑠ℎ𝑎𝑟𝑒 𝑖 𝑠ℎ𝑎𝑟𝑒 𝑟𝑒𝑓 ) were computed, with the factor that was the most prevalent dominant loading in the population (F1) as the reference 70 . The HR for a given ALR term represents the risk change associated with substituting dietary share from the reference factor toward the alternative factor.

Factors with loading weights of zero have undefined log-ratios; zeros were handled using multiplicative replacement.

Added prognostic value.

To quantify the incremental prognostic value of NMF factors beyond established diet quality scores, we compared six stepwise Cox proportional hazards models, following the approach of Argentieri et al 51 : M2 (primary covariates only), M2 + NMF (all eight zscored NMF factors), M2 + AHEI (energy-adjusted AHEI), M2 + aMED (energy-adjusted aMED), M2 + NMF + AHEI and M2 + NMF + aMED. AHEI and aMED were always entered in separate models. Models were trained on the discovery dataset (n = 55,839) and validated on the held-out replication dataset (n = 23,932). For this analysis we used time-on-study as the timescale with age at baseline as covariate. Explained variation was quantified using 𝑅 2 from the CoxR2 package 71 . Discrimination was quantified using Harrell's C-index with bootstrapped 95% confidence intervals on C versus M2 (500 resamples). Both 𝑅 2 and C-index were pooled across the five MICE imputations via Fisher's 𝓏 transformation: 𝑅 2 values were transformed by taking the square root, converted to z-scale, averaged, inverse-transformed, and then squared 51, 72 . C-index values were mapped to Somers' D (D = 2C -1) before Fisher's 𝓏 transformation and mapped back after averaging. Likelihood ratio tests comparing each expanded model to M2 were pooled via the 𝐷 2 statistic. Time-dependent area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (tdAUC) was computed at 8-year horizons using the timeROC package 73 and pooled across imputations via Fisher's 𝓏 transformation and Somers' D, as above. Category-free net reclassification improvement (NRI) was computed at an 8-year time horizon using the nricens package 74, 75 . Linear predictors were converted to absolute predicted risks at the NRI horizon via the stratum-specific baseline cumulative hazard before computing reclassification. NRI was computed for each of the five MICE-imputed datasets and pooled via Rubin's rules. Confidence intervals were obtained from 1,000 bootstrap iterations within nricens.

Population attributable fraction.

For each FDR-significant factor-outcome association from the primary Cox regression, we estimated the population attributable fraction (PAF) using tertile contrasts. For adverse associations, the PAF was defined as the fraction of cases among individuals in the highest exposure tertile (T3) that would be prevented if their risk were reduced to that of the lowest tertile (T1); for protective associations, the direction was reversed. The unadjusted PAF used Levin's formula with the crude population prevalence of exposure 76 . The mutually adjusted PAF, which held the other seven NMF factor loadings constant, used Miettinen's formula with exposure prevalence among cases 77 , as applying multivariable-adjusted HR in Levin's formula with the crude population prevalence produces biased estimates 76, 78 . Bootstrap 95% confidence intervals were obtained from 500 resamples.

Random survival forest.

To rank the relative importance of NMF factors for each outcome and to explore potential nonlinear associations, we fitted random survival forests (RSF) separately for each primary outcome using the R package randomForestSRC 79 on the discovery dataset. As randomForestSRC implements missingness handling at each node split, RSF models used the pre-imputation dataset rather than multiply imputed datasets. Missing data rates were low (maximum 4.0% for leisure-time physical activity with all other covariates <1.6%). The exposure variables were the eight NMF-derived factor loadings entered as continuous, unstandardized W-matrix scores.

Covariates matched the M2 Cox model specification except for total energy intake, which was entered as a continuous variable rather than quintile dummies 80 . Default hyperparameter values were used (ntree = 1,000, nodesize = 15, importance = "permute"). Model performance was assessed using out-of-bag (OOB) prediction error, computed as 1 minus the OOB concordance index and convergence was confirmed by visual inspection of OOB error versus number of trees.

Two complementary variable ranking methods were computed for each outcome: permutation variable importance (VIMP) 81 and minimal depth 82.

Diet quality score discordance analysis

To investigate whether NMF factors capture dietary variation not fully represented by conventional diet quality scores, we identified individuals with closely matched AHEI or aMED scores but maximally divergent NMF factor loadings. All discordance analyses were conducted in the replication dataset (n = 23,932) using Cox regression coefficients estimated on the discovery dataset only.

For each diet quality score (AHEI and aMED, analyzed separately), 1:1 greedy nearest-neighbor matching without replacement was performed on energy-adjusted diet score and mean total energy intake using a caliper of 0.25 SD on each dimension 83 . Discordance between scorematched pairs was quantified using cosine distance on L1-normalized W vectors. Extremediscordant pairs were defined as those in the top 5% of cosine distance. Within each extremediscordant pair, members were classified as "adverse" or "favorable" using a composite risk score: where 𝑧(𝑊 {𝑖,𝑘} ) is the z-scored factor loading and 𝛽 𝑘 is the mean log(HR) for factor k across CAD, T2D and CKD from the discovery-only M2 Cox model.

The pair member with the higher composite risk score was labeled "adverse." Pair members were compared across 33 cardiometabolic risk factors using Cohen's d and Wilcoxon signed-rank tests. Benjamini-Hochberg FDR correction was applied across all 33 biomarker tests. To quantify the percentage of individuals classified as having high diet quality have elevated cardiometabolic risk when assessed by NMF factors, we computed composite risk scores among individuals in the top decile of AHEI and aMED and reported the fraction with above-median risk relative to the full dataset.

NMF metabolomics association analysis

To characterize the metabolic profiles associated with the NMF-derived intake factors, we conducted a metabolome-wide association study (MWAS) using nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy metabolomics from the UKB Nightingale Health platform 84 . A total of 249 metabolites were measured in plasma samples collected at baseline (instance 0), including 168 directly measured and 81 derived ratios. Metabolites with more than 50% missing values were excluded. Not all participants in the analytic cohort had NMR metabolomics data available. The sample sizes were n = 30,063 -30,508 in the discovery dataset and n = 23,932 in the replication dataset, depending on metabolite-level missingness. Identical analytical procedures were applied independently within each cohort.

Metabolite concentrations were inverse-normal transformed and NMF factor loadings were zscore standardized, applied within each dataset separately. For each NMF factor independently, we fitted ordinary least squares (OLS) regression models for each metabolite: metabolite ~ factor + covariates. Covariates included the Cox regression M2 covariate set (age at baseline, sex, BMI, total energy intake, alcohol intake, ethnicity, smoking status, educational attainment, household income, leisure-time physical activity and dietary supplement use) plus UKB assessment center (22 collection sites as a categorical variable) and fasting time (hours since last meal). Multiple testing was corrected using Benjamini-Hochberg FDR applied per factor (249 tests per factor). FDR-corrected P <0.05 was considered statistically significant. Significant associations in the discovery dataset were tested in the replication dataset. An association was considered replicated if P <0.05 with the same direction of effect. Beta concordance between discovery and replication datasets was assessed using Pearson correlation of effect estimates.
