To fix Exact P-Values

What we found

We found 1 imprecise p value out of 31 detected p values.

Show details

Reporting p values imprecisely (e.g., p < .05) reduces transparency, reproducibility, and re-use (e.g., in p value meta-analyses). Best practice is to report exact p-values with three decimal places (e.g., p = .032) unless p values are smaller than 0.001, in which case you can use p < .001.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (e.g., “decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

The APA manual states: Report exact p values (e.g., p = .031) to two or three decimal places. However, report p values less than .001 as p < .001. However, 2 decimals is too imprecise for many use-cases (e.g., a p value meta-analysis), so report p values with three digits.

To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 16 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Metacheck does not yet analyze automatically whether sentences which include non-significant p-values are correct, but we recommend manually checking the sentences below for possible misinterpreted non-significant p values.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Full text

Abstract

We report a pre-registered [ 11 C]UCB-J PET sub-study of a recently published randomized trial in which a single psilocybin dose produced rapid and sustained antidepressant effects in major depressive disorder (MDD). Synaptic loss has been implicated in MDD, and psychedelics induce synaptic remodeling in preclinical models within 24 hours, raising the possibility that psilocybin's antidepressant effects involve increased synaptic density. We used the PET radioligand [ 11 C]UCB-J targeting synaptic vesicle glycoprotein 2A (SV2A) to test this in thirty-five participants with moderate-to-severe recurrent MDD randomized 1:1 to a single dose of 25 mg psilocybin or 100 mg niacin placebo, both with psychotherapeutic support. [ 11 C]UCB-J PET was performed 2-7 days before and 15 (±7) days after dosing.

Despite significant clinical improvement in the psilocybin group, we found no evidence of a group-level increase in SV2A binding (VT or BPND) relative to niacin in the hippocampus or frontal cortex. In secondary analyses, higher baseline VT was associated with greater subsequent clinical improvement in the psilocybin group; this survived FDR correction at Day 8 and Day 15 (two of three pre-registered timepoints). Change in VT was associated with greater subsequent clinical improvement in unadjusted analyses, but these associations were attenuated after adjustment for baseline VT and baseline MADRS and did not survive FDR correction. These findings indicate that substantial antidepressant improvement after psilocybin can occur without a detectable group-level increase in SV2A binding, while raising the possibility that baseline SV2A binding could help identify individuals more likely to respond.

Introduc on

Synaptic plasticity contributes to resilience and adaptation throughout life (1-5), whereas synaptic dysfunction has been implicated in a range of neurological and psychiatric conditions (6) (7) (8), including major depressive disorder (MDD).

The hypothesis that depression involves stress-related synaptopathy in corticolimbic regions is supported by rodent models and postmortem findings of individuals with MDD, which have shown synaptic loss and reduced expression of synapse-related genes (9, 10). Such alterations may disrupt neural signaling and network function in circuits implicated in depression (11, 12).

The serotonergic psychedelic psilocybin has demonstrated rapid antidepressant effects in randomized clinical trials, that seem to persist well beyond drug elimination (13) (14) (15).

Whereas acute psychedelic effects are thought to involve serotonin2A (5-HT2A) receptormediated changes in cortical and network function (16, 17), persistent antidepressant effects suggest engagement of downstream processes, including transcriptional regulation and protein synthesis. Psilocin, the active metabolite of psilocybin, has also been reported to bind directly to the brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) receptor TrkB, suggesting a TrkB/BDNF-dependent pathway for psychedelic-induced plasticity (18). In preclinical models, psychedelics have been shown to promote structural plasticity, including dendritic spine density within 24 hours after administration of psychedelics (19). Psilocybin-induced increases in spine density appear most pronounced during the first days, although a subset of newly formed spines has been reported to persist for at least one month (20). This indicates that a brief pharmacological trigger can result in both rapid and sustained structural synaptic adaptations, mirroring the clinical antidepressant effects observed after a single-dose psilocybin in humans (13, 21).

There is ongoing debate regarding how synaptic alterations in depression are to be interpreted. One simplified "loss-restoration" framework proposes that depression is associated with synaptic loss, whereas antidepressants can promote their restoration. In contrast, perspectives such as iPlasticity suggest that treatment may induce a permissive state of heightened plasticity, in which environmental factors shape synaptic remodelling and clinical outcomes (22, 23). Taken together, this debate leaves unresolved whether treatmentrelated plasticity manifests as an increase in synaptic density, and when such changes emerge in relation to treatment and clinical improvement. Synaptic density has historically been quantified using postmortem and in vitro approaches.

The PET radioligand [ 11 C]UCB-J, targeting the synaptic vesicle glycoprotein 2A (SV2A), a presynaptic protein widely distributed throughout the brain, has shown close correspondence with the established synaptic marker synaptophysin, enabling estimation of synaptic density in the living human brain (24) (25) (26). A landmark study reported lower SV2A binding in affective regions in more severely depressed individuals compared to healthy controls, and that lower binding was associated with increased severity (27). Psilocybin has been shown to increase SV2A binding in the pig brain (28), but whether similar changes occur in humans with MDD, and with a similar time course, remains unknown.

Treatment response in MDD is highly heterogeneous, and the ability to determine in advance which individual is likely to benefit from a given treatment remains limited (29).

Pretreatment biomarkers could inform treatment selection and reduce delays associated with ineffective treatment trials. In our previous work, we found that SV2A binding was associated with illness chronicity in MDD, suggesting that SV2A may be linked to illness course and prognosis (30). Moreover, in a study of the rapid-acting antidepressant ketamine, lower baseline SV2A binding was linked to greater subsequent increases in SV2A in prefrontal regions, which in turn related to clinical improvement in exploratory analyses (31). This raises the question of whether SV2A-binding can predict treatment response to psilocybin.

Against this background, we examined [ 11 C]UCB-J PET data from a recently published randomized clinical trial in which psilocybin produced rapid and sustained antidepressant effects in patients with MDD (32). We preregistered the primary hypothesis that psilocybin would increase SV2A binding in the hippocampus and frontal cortex relative to placebo, reflecting a net increase in synaptic density by the time of follow-up PET. In preregistered secondary analyses, we examined whether treatment-related changes in SV2A binding were associated with antidepressant response, and whether baseline SV2A binding was associated with subsequent clinical improvement.

Study Design

Participants were randomized 1:1 to a single dose of 25mg psilocybin or 100mg niacin placebo, both treatment arms received five sessions of psychotherapeutic support from day -1 to day 15. Clinical follow-up extended to 365 days after dosing.

Magne c Resonance Imaging

Structural MRI was acquired 7-35 days before dosing at the Solna MRI Centre, Department of Clinical Neuroscience, Karolinska Institutet. The first 26 participants were scanned on a 3-Tesla General Electric Discovery MR750 scanner, and the remaining 9 on a 3-Tesla GE SIGNA Premier scanner. Scanner allocation was balanced across arms.

Positron Emission Tomography

Participants underwent [ 11 C]UCB-J PET (High-Resolution Research Tomograph; Siemens

Molecular Imaging) at two timepoints: baseline, 2-7 days before dosing, and follow-up, targeted to 15 days after dosing with an allowable window of ±7 days. PET variables are summarized in Table 1. During scanning, participants were positioned supine, with the head fixated.

[ 11 C]UCB-J was synthesized at the Karolinska PET centre in accordance with Good Manufacturing Practice (25). A bolus injection containing approximately 400 MBq (±10%, for a participant of 70 kg) was injected via antecubital venous cannula, followed by a saline flush.

A 6-min transmission scan using a rotating ¹³⁷Cs source was acquired for attenuation correction, followed by a 90-min emission scan acquired in list mode. Dynamic images were reconstructed into 37 frames (8 × 15 s, 8 × 30 s, 4 × 60 s, 5 × 120 s, 10 × 300 s, 2 × 600 s) using OP-3D-OSEM with point spread function modelling, yielding a resolution of approximately 2 mm at the center of the field of view.

Arterial blood was sampled via a radial artery cannula for derivation of the arterial input function (AIF) and radiometabolite correction. Continuous sampling was performed during the first 10 minutes using an Automatic Blood Sampling System (ABSS, Alogg technologies, Mariefred, Sweden) followed by discrete manual samples. The parent fraction in plasma and its radiometabolite profile were determined using protein precipitation followed by reversed-phase radio-HPLC.

Head motion was estimated using realignment of all frames to a reference formed from the first frames (first 4 min) in SPM. Four participants had translational displacement > 4 mm in at least one scan, for which motion correction was performed by frame-wise coregistration to the corresponding transmission image and retained if it resulted in changes > 5% in regional outcome estimates and applied to the corresponding paired scan as well.

Image Processing and Pharmacokine c Modelling

T1-weighted MR images were segmented into regions of interest (ROIs) using FreeSurfer 6.0 (33) according to the Desikan-Killiany atlas (34). MR images were coregistered to a summed PET image, with ROI definitions transferred to PET using the resulting coregistration matrix.

Image preprocessing was done using SPM12 (35), implemented in MATLAB R2014b (The MathWorks Inc., Natick, MA, USA). Time activity curves were extracted for all ROIs.

Kinetic modelling was performed using the open-source R package kinfitr (v0.8.0) (36).

Binding was quantified in two ways: (i) total distribution volume (VT) using the 1-Tissue-Compartment Model with a metabolite corrected arterial input function, and (ii) nondisplaceable binding potential (BPND) using a simplified reference tissue model (SRTM2) with centrum semiovale as reference region and k2' estimated from SRTM1 (37) (38) (39).

All image processing and kinetic modelling were conducted blinded to treatment allocation, using randomized study identifiers.

Clinical and psychological data

Depressive symptoms were assessed using the MADRS, by blinded raters. Assessments were performed at baseline (PET1), follow-up PET (PET2), and on days 8, 42, and 365 post-dose.

Acute subjective drug effects were assessed using the Mystical Experience Questionnaire (MEQ-30) and the Scale for Psychedelic Intensity Rating (SPIR). SPIR ratings were obtained approximately 90 minutes after dosing, while MEQ-30 was administered at the end of the dosing session.

Challenging Experience Questionnaire (CEQ) and Emotional Breakthrough Inventory (EBI) were used to assess challenging psychological reactions to psychedelics. They were administered at the end of the dosing session. The Lasting Effects Questionnaire (LEQ) is a fusion of questions from CEQ, MEQ and EBI, measuring lasting effects of psychedelic experience, administered at day 8, 15, 42 and 365.

Psychological flexibility was assessed using the Acceptance and Action Questionnaire-II (AAQ-II). AAQ-II was administered during treatment sessions and at follow-up assessments at days 42 and 365. The Satisfaction with Life Scale (SWLS) and Meaning in Life Questionnaire (MLQ) assessed subjective well-being and meaning in life, respectively, and were administered during treatment sessions and at Days 42 and 365.

Sta s cal Analysis

Primary analysis

The statistical analysis was preregistered on aspredicted.org (#152,777) on 28 November 2023, after completion of PET and MRI data collection (spring 2023) but before any statistical analysis of the imaging data; deviations from the registered plan are listed in Supplementary Table 16.

The dependent variable was [ 11 C]UCB-J binding, and the primary regions of interest (ROIs) were the bilateral hippocampus (HIP) and frontal cortex (FC).

For each outcome measure (i.e.

VT and BPND) and ROI we fitted an ANCOVA model with PET2 as the dependent variable, treatment group as predictor and the corresponding PET1 value as covariate. Both VT and BPND were preregistered outcome measures. VT was emphasized because it is estimated using an arterial input function and does not rely on a reference region, thereby avoiding assumptions regarding stability of the centrum semiovale across treatment conditions; BPND was reported as a complementary reference-tissue measure. Analyses were conducted in three steps. In Model 1 we adjusted for PET1 only; in Model 2 we additionally included time from dosing to PET2 (days); and in Model 3 we further adjusted for age and clinical covariates (duration of current episode, baseline depression severity, and number of previous episodes).

The significance level was set to 0.05. Given the high interregional correlations for the binding outcomes, no correction for multiple testing was applied.

Outlier detection, quality control and all analyses were conducted blinded to treatment allocation, using randomized study identifiers.

Secondary and exploratory analyses

Secondary analyses examined whether change in [ 11 C]UCB-J binding from baseline to follow-up examination was associated with change in depressive symptom severity on the MADRS. For the present PET sub study, the MADRS assessment obtained at the same visit as PET2 (Day 15 ±7) was prespecified as the primary clinical endpoint for analyses relating change in PET binding to clinical change. Other follow-up assessments were days 8, 42 and 365. For analyses involving MADRS outcomes at Day 42 and Day 365, observations obtained after post-randomization psychedelic use or reinitiation of antidepressant treatment were censored.

To account for multiple testing in the secondary analyses, false discovery rate (FDR) correction using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure was applied within each analysis family.

For the preregistered correlation analyses, correction was performed separately within each treatment arm and clinical follow-up timepoint across the four regional binding measures (frontal cortex and hippocampus; VT and BPND). For treatment-interaction analyses, FDR correction was applied across the eight interaction tests comprising two regions and four follow-up timepoints. Additional post hoc analyses examining the ΔVT-ΔMADRS association after adjustment for baseline VT were treated as a separate analysis family, with FDR correction across the eight tests comprising two regions and four follow-up timepoints within each treatment arm. Nominal and FDR-adjusted p values are reported throughout. Pearson's correlation coefficients (r) were calculated between regional ΔVT (PET2 -PET1) and ΔMADRS (follow-up -baseline, negative values indicate improvement). Parallel analyses for ΔBPND are reported in Supplementary Tables 2 3. Analyses were conducted separately within each treatment arm. To assess whether the association between ΔVT and ΔMADRS differed between treatment groups, we additionally fitted linear models with ΔMADRS as the dependent variable and ΔVT, treatment, and ΔVT × treatment as independent variables. The interaction term was interpreted as the formal test of between-group difference in associations. Because baseline VT was itself associated with clinical outcome and is mathematically incorporated in Δ VT, we performed additional post hoc analyses to assess whether the ΔVT-ΔMADRS association was independent of baseline binding. Partial Pearson correlations between ΔVT and ΔMADRS were calculated controlling for baseline VT. We additionally fitted linear models including baseline MADRS, baseline VT, and ΔVT as predictors of ΔMADRS. Variance inflation factors were examined to assess collinearity between baseline VT and ΔVT. These analyses were considered exploratory robustness analyses. Fully interacted models, including treatment interactions with baseline MADRS, baseline VT, and ΔVT, were additionally fitted to assess whether the adjusted ΔVT-ΔMADRS association differed between treatment groups.

We also examined whether baseline [ 11 C]UCB-J binding was associated with antidepressant response on the same timepoints. Pearson's correlations were calculated between baseline regional binding and ΔMADRS in both treatment arms. In addition, linear models including baseline binding, treatment, and their interaction were fitted to test whether these associations differed between groups. For baseline VT × treatment interaction analyses, FDR correction was applied across the eight tests comprising two regions and four follow-up timepoints.

Post hoc ROC analyses were performed in the psilocybin group using the response criterion specified in the statistical analysis plan (≥50% reduction in MADRS or >10-point reduction from baseline). AUC estimates and 95% confidence intervals were obtained using stratified bootstrap resampling (10,000 iterations). As a post hoc robustness analysis, associations between baseline VT and ΔMADRS were additionally adjusted for baseline MADRS to assess whether they were independent of initial depression severity.

Exploratory Spearman rank correlations were conducted to examine associations between regional ΔVT and ΔBPND and psychological outcome measures, including MEQ total score and subscales, SPIR, CEQ, EBI, as well as change in AAQ-II, LEQ, MLQ and SWLS at the available follow-up timepoints.

Finally, we repeated the same ANCOVA model used in the primary analysis across an extended set of anatomically defined ROIs that have previously been implicated in the neurobiology of MDD, including the anterior cingulate and medial prefrontal cortices, insula, amygdala, thalamus, and striatal regions, as well as global grey matter. These analyses were hypothesis-generating in nature.

Results

All participants in the psilocybin arm completed both PET examinations, while one participant in the niacin arm did not complete the follow-up scan. VT could not be quantified for two participants in the psilocybin arm, due to incomplete blood sampling.

Primary analysis

In the PET sample, MADRS decreased from 25.9 to 13.2 in the psilocybin group and from 26.2 to 24.5 in the niacin group between PET1 and PET2 (Table 1). Primary analyses revealed no evidence of a group-level change in SV2A binding two weeks after treatment, as detailed below. We found no evidence for treatment effect on VT in frontal cortex (psilocybin vs niacin: β = -0.49, 95% CI -2.42 to 1.44, p = 0.61) or hippocampus (β = 0.05, 95% CI -1.39 to 1.50, p = 0.94). Likewise, BPND did not differ between groups at follow-up, either in frontal cortex (β = 0.010, 95% CI -0.22 to 0.24, p = 0.93) or hippocampus (β = 0.013, 95% CI -0.14 to 0.16, p = 0.86) (Figure 1). Results were consistent in sensitivity analyses additionally adjusting for time from dosing to PET2 (Model 2) and age and clinical covariates (Model 3) (Supplementary Table 1).

Secondary Analyses

Change in [¹¹C]UCB-J binding and an depressant response

In the unadjusted analyses, lower ΔVT was associated with greater antidepressant improvement in the psilocybin group, with positive ΔVT-ΔMADRS correlations across follow-up. The strongest associations were observed at Day 42 (FC: r = 0.71, p = 0.003, pFDR = 0.012; HIP: r = 0.67, p = 0.007, pFDR = 0.013), whereas associations in the niacin group were weak or generally in the opposite direction (Figure 2, Supplementary Tables 2 3). Formal treatment interaction tests did not survive FDR correction (Supplementary Table 4). Because baseline VT was itself associated with clinical improvement and is mathematically contained in ΔVT, we examined the dependence of ΔVT on baseline VT and repeated the analyses adjusting for baseline values. Baseline VT was negatively correlated with ΔVT in the psilocybin group (FC: r = -0.52; HIP: r = -0.39), but this relationship was stronger in the niacin group (FC: r = -0.81; HIP: r = -0.78), with no evidence that the baseline VT-ΔVT relationship differed between treatment groups (FC interaction p = 0.823; HIP p = 0.480) (Supplementary Table 7). Variance inflation factors were low in the psilocybin group (approximately 1.3-1.5), indicating no substantial multicollinearity between baseline VT and ΔVT.

Partial correlations adjusting for baseline VT showed attenuated ΔVT-ΔMADRS associations (Supplementary Table 8). In regression models additionally adjusting for baseline MADRS, positive associations at Day 42 remained nominally significant in the psilocybin group (FC: β = 1.11, 95% CI 0.09-2.12, p = 0.035; HIP: β = 1.47, 95% CI 0.09-2.86, p = 0.039), but neither survived FDR correction (pFDR = 0.156). No adjusted ΔVT association survived FDR correction at any time point (Supplementary Table 9). Adjusted interaction analyses provided no robust evidence that the ΔVT-ΔMADRS association differed between treatment groups (Supplementary Table 10). No corresponding pattern was observed for ΔBPND. However, none of these interactions survived FDR correction (all pFDR ≥ 0.060) (Supplementary Table 6). No corresponding pattern was observed for baseline BPND. In a post hoc robustness analysis adjusting for baseline MADRS, the associations remained nominally significant at Day 8 and PET2 in both frontal cortex and hippocampus (all p < 0.05), although they did not survive FDR correction across the eight robustness tests (pFDR = 0.082) (Supplementary Table 5). Sensitivity analyses excluding the participant with the highest baseline VT are presented in Supplementary Table 17. Exploratory analyses

Exploratory analyses of psychological questionnaire measures yielded largely insignificant results. However, greater reductions in VT were associated with greater change in LEQ scores at Day 365 in the psilocybin group in both frontal cortex and hippocampus after FDR correction (Supplementary Table 11). Given the large number of exploratory comparisons, these isolated findings should be interpreted cautiously. In the exploratory ROI analyses, no significant treatment effects were observed in any region for either VT or BPND. Effect estimates were small and confidence intervals included zero (Supplementary Tables 12 and 13). Descriptive PET binding values for the exploratory regions are provided in Supplementary Table 15.

Discussion

In this placebo-controlled experiment in patients with MDD, we found no evidence that a single 25 mg dose of psilocybin administered with psychotherapeutic support increased synaptic density, indexed by [ 11 C]UCB-J binding to SV2A, two weeks after administration, despite rapid and sustained antidepressant effects in the psilocybin group. However, confidence intervals were wide and small-to-moderate treatment effects cannot be excluded.

This finding contrasts with a report of increased SV2A binding following psilocybin administration in the pig brain (28), but is consistent with a recent study in healthy volunteers that found no significant overall increase in SV2A binding one week after psilocybin, underscoring that translation to humans remains uncertain (40). Our finding extends this observation to patients with MDD, providing no support for a simple synaptic lossrestoration model of psilocybin's antidepressant effects. While SV2A PET may not capture all treatment-related synaptic plasticity, the findings are also consistent with the possibility that the antidepressant effects are mediated through other pathways, including expectancy and unblinding effects.

In the secondary analyses, higher baseline SV2A binding was associated with greater subsequent clinical improvement in the psilocybin group, with associations surviving correction for multiple comparisons at Day 8 and PET2 in both regions examined.

Associations remained in the same direction at Day 42 and Day 365 but did not survive FDR correction. One possible interpretation is that higher SV2A binding reflects a greater synaptic reserve that may facilitate treatment response, even if the antidepressant effect itself is not accompanied by an increase in SV2A binding. This finding raises the possibility that SV2A binding is associated with treatment response, but because the treatment interactions did not survive FDR correction it cannot be determined whether this reflects a psilocybin-specific predictor or a general prognostic marker. The ability to identify patients likely to respond could improve the risk-benefit balance of psilocybin treatment as it moves toward clinical use. In a post hoc analysis, we therefore explored its discriminative performance using receiver operating characteristic analyses. At PET2, baseline VT showed good discrimination between responders and non-responders, with area under the curve values of 0.89 in frontal cortex and 0.91 in hippocampus. These estimates should be interpreted cautiously given the small sample and the small number of non-responders and require prospective validation.

In contrast, the apparent association between greater reductions in VT and greater symptom improvement raised the possibility that psilocybin responders might show an early posttreatment reduction in SV2A binding. This was contrary to our hypothesis, but given that SV2A expression seems to generally increase during brain maturation, this could hypothetically reflect transient synaptic remodeling during a period of heightened, juvenilelike plasticity (22, 41, 42). However, this association was substantially attenuated after adjustment for baseline VT and baseline MADRS and did not survive correction for multiple comparisons. Fully adjusted interaction models provided no robust evidence that the association differed between treatment groups.

The study has several limitations, including the modest sample size. Missing arterial data prevented estimation of VT for two participants in the psilocybin group. The VT-MADRS analyses at Day 365 were based on a reduced sample due to censoring. Plasma free fraction (fP) was not measured, limiting interpretation of VT. As a presynaptic marker, SV2A binding may not capture postsynaptic or dendritic plasticity, such as spine remodeling, that could be relevant to treatment response. In addition, the single follow-up PET scan at approximately 15 days provides only a limited temporal snapshot of post-treatment SV2A binding and could miss out on earlier transient or later changes arising downstream of the clinical response.

In conclusion, our findings suggest that psilocybin can exert antidepressant effects in MDD without detectable increases in SV2A binding compared to control, two weeks after treatment. This does not rule out synaptic plasticity following psilocybin treatment but highlights the limitations of SV2A binding as a proxy marker. Secondary findings raise the possibility that SV2A binding may have a predictive value for treatment response. Put together, this suggests a more complex pattern than can be explained by a synaptic lossrestoration narrative. Further clarification of the role of synaptic plasticity in psilocybin's antidepressant effects would benefit from larger samples, earlier and repeated PET assessments, and multimodal approaches.
