Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). Statcheck might still miss one-sided tests that are described in another sentence, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Biliary Atresia (BA) is the leading cause of pediatric liver transplantation. Early mechanisms governing outcomes remain unknown. A multiscale atlas of outcome-stratified BA, resolving single cell, spatial and 3D-tissue states of livers sampled at Kasai portoenterostomy (KPE), established lymphatic dysplasia in infants on a trajectory toward liver failure and transplantation ('severe BA'). An architecturally fragmented, immature and CCL21 high lymphatic vasculature marked severe BA early on. In the epithelium, hepatocyte-to-biliary plasticity emerged across clinical outcomes, while hepatocyte dedifferentiation, acute-phase-reactions and MMP7/SOX9 high cholangiocyte expansion delineated severe disease. Ex vivo, lymphatic endothelial cells, isolated from severe BA livers, induced SOX9 high epithelial progenitors when cultured as assembloids with cholangiocytes. MMP7, the known BA mediator, was governed by ectopic CCL21-signaling on epithelium after inflammatory priming, as evidenced in CCL21 neutralization experiments. In sum, lymphatic endothelial dysplasia is an early phenomenon in BA that perturbs intrahepatic epithelial states and offers CCL21 as a target for epithelial stability.

INTRODUCTION

Biliary atresia (BA) is a progressive neonatal cholestatic disease and the most common indication for pediatric liver transplantation.( 1-3) After diagnosis, BA requires prompt surgical intervention. Yet outcomes following palliative Kasai portoenterostomy (KPE) remain variable. (1, 2) Despite progress in screening and diagnosis,(4, 5) the early pathophysiological determinants of BA native liver survival or failure remain elusive. This has deep implications for anticipatory care and target identification. Understanding the drivers of intrahepatic outcome early on is vital because KPE removes the obliterated extrahepatic bile duct remnant but leaves the intrahepatic disease process largely untouched. Despite important efforts in single-cell resolution studies of BA, (6, 7) there has been no comprehensive spatiotemporal BA atlas that links early molecular phenomena to concrete clinical outcome stratifications.

In BA, periportal hepatocytes and cholangiocytes undergo a continuum of reactive epithelial states. (8) As part of the so-called 'ductular reaction', CK19+ ductules expand into the parenchyma, largely without obvious bile-draining capacity. Matrix metalloproteinase (MMP)-7, induced by Wnt/β-catenin signaling, drives cholangiocyte injury and fibrosis in BA and also serves as a validated diagnostic biomarker. (4, 9, 10) While liver regeneration in many injury settings relies primarily on hepatocyte self-duplication without a classical stem cell, (11) Wnt signaling-linked LGR5+ progenitors, as found in the intestine, (12) can -in principle -also emerge in the injured liver. (13) Transdifferentiation between hepatocyte and cholangiocyte without stem or progenitor cell intermediates is wellestablished in the liver and has been demonstrated in different systems, including a NOTCHdeficient mouse model of Alagille syndrome (14) and even in human Metabolic Dysfunction-Associated Steatotic Liver Disease. (15) In BA, there have been reports of epithelial signatures consistent with both transdifferentiation but also LGR5+ cell subsets. (8, 16) However, the exact role, distribution and functional relevance of these epithelial states to the disease severity and outcomes, remains unclear.

Among the many cell types in the BA 'ecosystem', the role of the intrahepatic lymphatic vasculature remains poorly understood. The common referral of the 'portal triad' instead of tetrad underscores the negligence to acknowledge their relevance, despite the liver being among the largest producers of lymph generating 25-50% of lymph that passes through the thoracic duct back into the body's circulation. (17) Outside the liver, the lymphatic vasculature is well established as a regulator of fluid homeostasis, wound healing, and immune surveillance, with CCL21 recognized as a key mediator of lymphatic immune cell trafficking. (17) (18) (19) A reactive lymphangiogenesis has been proposed in chronic liver diseases. (20) Beyond merely responding to injury, here, we assess an active role of lymphatic endothelial cells (LEC) at the time of KPE to modulate the future ability of intrahepatic bile ducts to drain bile and evade transplantation.

Here, we provide a single-cell, spatial, and 3D-imaging-based atlas of early BA livers retrospectively stratified for outcome after KPE. Unbiased analysis identifies hepatic lymphatic dysplasia as an early determinant of rapid disease progression towards transplantation in BA.

Functional validation in tissue-and iPSC-derived organoids and assembloids reveals that excessive lymphatic CCL21 signals on inflammation-primed liver epithelium and shapes its fate in BA.

RESULTS

Multiscale profiling of BA identifies early determinants of divergent outcomes

To identify molecular, tissue and functional determinants of intrahepatic BA outcome, we sampled liver tissue at three German university hospitals at the timepoint of KPE and stratified for clinical outcome thereafter. We compared BA and non-BA cholestatic control patients that include Choledochal Cyst, Alagille Syndrome, and Progressive Familial Intrahepatic Cholestasis using a variety of experimental modalities to dissect BA-specific phenomena from general cholestatic effects. To link cell-specific molecular programs to multicellular tissue circuits, we combined single-nucleus RNA-sequencing (snRNA-seq) analysis of cryopreserved samples with multiplexed (mpIF) and 3D-immunofluorescence, spatial Stereo-sequencing (Stereo-seq) and a variety of ex vivo profiling and mechanistic organoid studies (Fig. 1A-B).

To delineate biological processes governing epithelial outcome after KPE, we retrospectively stratified for long native liver survival vs. infants with rapid progression toward liver transplantation (LTX) in the first year due to biliary epithelial dysfunction, independent of viral infections or other possibly confounding factors. We classified patients achieving jaundice clearance, defined as normalization of serum bilirubin levels by 100 days post-KPE, and sustained native liver survival as a 'mild BA' phenotype (n=14). Infants with persistent hyperbilirubinemia at 100 days post-KPE and LTX within their first year of life were classified as 'severe BA' patients (n=12). We deemed biosampling at KPE as 'pre-outcome' and used those samples for snRNA-seq, Stereo-seq, mpIF and tissue-cleared 3D-light sheet microscopy (Fig. 1B). For functional validation, we performed 'post-outcome' biosampling from BA and non-BA liver explant tissue received during LTX. From these tissues, we generated intrahepatic cholangiocyte organoids (ICO), isolated lymphatic endothelial cells (LECs) and conditioned media from minced tissue for proteomic analysis of soluble disease tissue mediators. For functional validations requiring multiple cell types -i.e. cholangiocytes and hepatocyte -we also used iPSC-derived fetal liver-like organoids (FLO) (21) and hepatocyte-like cells (iHeps) (22). Notably, patient age at KPE, histopathological fibrosis levels (as measured by the ISHAK score) and direct bilirubin levels did not significantly differ between mild and severe BA patients (Fig. 1C), enabling the identification of jaundice clearing mechanisms specifically.

Mean age at LTX was 6.6 (±2.2 SD) months in the severe BA group. Mean duration of native liver survival in the mild BA group is 6.8 (±2.7 SD) years, with only one patient being transplanted during the ongoing study. In sum, this study design offers an outcome-stratified BA cohort, as the basis for the different analyses that follow.

Maladaptive lymphatic dysplasia is an early determinant of poor outcome after KPE

To dissect the heterogenous cell types and states determining clinical outcomes, we leveraged snRNA-seq from a multi-center and retrospectively outcome-stratified patient cohort. Using mechanical homogenization, avoiding enzymatic dissociation, which typically biases against matrix-embedded and fragile stromal, endothelial and epithelial cell types in fibrotic tissue, we identified that most cell types were conserved across the three groups, including rarer cell types, e.g. lymphatic endothelial cells, T and B lymphocytes (Fig. 2A, Fig. S1A-F

for annotations).

While the overall inferred cell-cell interactions escalated with disease severity (Fig. S2A-C), LECs lost most of their physiological interactions in severe BA when compared to mild BA (Fig. 2B). A detailed assessment of expression modules for LEC identity, function and structure revealed a loss of PROX1, SOX18, ANGPT2, EPHB4 and EFNB2 in severe BA (Fig. 2C).

Surprisingly, severe BA LECs mount a selective surge in CCL21 and PDPN. Mild BA LECs displayed an opposite pattern, indicating critical differences of lymphatic endothelial identity, . vasculogenesis, remodeling, and integrity across BA outcomes. (23, 24) Interestingly, VEGF-C, the main stimulus of lymphangiogenesis was significantly decreased in endothelial cells (EC) in severe BA, while VEGF-C-receptor expression (FLT4) was significantly increased in LECs -potentially reflecting a failed compensatory response (Fig. 2D). When comparing most enriched biological processes between mild and severe BA LECs that were derived from the cell-cell interaction members, the mild BA LEC top terms 'blood vessel morphogenesis' and 'vasculogenesis' were missing in severe BA LECs, which suggests insufficient lymphangiogenesis in line with the aberrant gene signature (Fig. 2E). The top gene ontology (GO) term of severe BA LECs -'laminin interactions' -may further suggest their contribution to ECM remodeling.

In accordance with the unstable PDPN high , CCL21 high maladaptive LEC state in severe BA, we validated high PDPN+ and continuous CCL21-staining patterns in mpIF stainings (Fig. S3A-D). Further, mpIF of 28 FFPE liver sections confirmed insufficient formation of lymphatic endothelial vasculature. While mild BA livers show a consistent pattern of expanded lamellarlike lymphatic endothelial structures intercalated with the CK19+ epithelium as a sign of portal lymphangiogenesis, severe BA livers were characterized by an increased number of malformed, clumped PDPN+ aggregates in the periportal area and reduced numbers of elongated, endothelial structures (Fig. 2F). Whole mount IF and light sheet microscopy confirmed a sparse and fragmented lymphatic network most pronounced in severe BA, consistent with a dysplasia phenotype (Fig. 2G). In comparison, livers of cholestatic-fibrotic controls showed a dense, organized and highly branched lymphatic network.

A dysplastic, CCL21 high lymphatic vasculature suggested altered immune cell trafficking in severe BA. MpIF indicated consistent accumulation of CD3+ lymphocytes in BA livers and specifically at close distance to LECs. However, differences were not statistically significant compared to mild BA patients (Fig. S3D). After observing lymphatic dysplasia in severe BA, mpIF further confirmed that severe BA patients mount ductular reactions early on. Consistently, 7 out of 9 severe BA-patients but only 1 out of 10 mild BA patients classified as CK19 high tissue indicating a significant surge in CK19+ ductular cells in severe BA, when compared to mild BA patients with better clinical outcomes (Fig. S3C+D). The observations of largely colocalized lymphatic and biliary dysplasia in the expanding portal areas of severe BA patients suggested that interactions between CCL21 high lymphatic vasculature and cholangiocytes may be possible.

Further differential pathway inference showed thrombospondin (THBS) and transforming growth factor beta (TGFβ) enriched in severe BA (Fig. 2H). Surprisingly, both THBS and TGFβ, previously described as important regulators of liver injury and fibrosis, (25, 26) were amongst the few upregulated genes in LECs from severe BA indicating a fibrogenic LEC state in those patients proceeding towards poor clinical outcome (Fig. 2H-J). While the inferred number of cell populations involving hepatic stellate cells (HSCs) and portal fibroblasts were larger, this data, together with LEC pathway enrichment (Fig. 2E) discussed earlier, shows that LECs contribute to local fibrogenic circuits, which is relevant given their portal location in proximity to cholangiocytes.

We identified hepatocyte-to-biliary spectrum cell states (Hep trans), as a broad adaptive response in all cholestatic injuries (Fig. 3A). However, in severe BA livers the Hep trans state pivoted the strongest towards biliary transdifferentiation states (i.e.

SOX9/HNF1B high , HNF4A low ) and involved

LGR5. This finding demonstrated that transdifferentiation is found across cholestatic injuries but that severe cholestatic injury exacerbated the liver's plasticity response and engages the Wnt/β-catenin-responsive progenitor activity (Fig. 3D-F). Trajectory analysis inferred that periportal and interzonal hepatocytes may participate in transdifferentiation programs (Fig. 3B). Notably, we also found another distinct BA-specific hepatocyte subset enriched in severe BA. This 'Hep BA' cell state was characterized by the instability of hepatocyte identity (HNF4A low ) and transcriptional programs high in acute-phase, metal-/redox-stress and ECM-crosslinking signatures, e.g. CRP, SAA1, LOXL1 and TGM2 (Fig. 3C-E; Fig. S1D). These Hep BA cells were also enriched for GO terms like 'wound healing', 'viral response', 'innate immune response' (Fig. S4A). We then sought to check whether all other hepatocytes could glean the Hep BA phenotype by calculating a score for the top Hep BA genes (Fig. S4B). Interestingly, we found a stepwise increase of cells taking on this phenotype with increasing disease severity.

Importantly, regarding the cholangiocyte response across clinical BA strata, we identified a unique state enriched in severe BA patients characterised by a broad Interferon-responsive and chemotactic program suggestive of a lymphoid immune response and possibly post viral state (Fig. 3G, Fig. S4D). Condition-specific GO terms confirmed further differences with 'wound healing', 'cell-substrate adhesion', and 'response to TGF-β', being shared by mild and severe cholangiocytes, while 'response to virus', 'chemotaxis', and 'tissue remodeling' were specifically enriched in severe BA. Interestingly, these severe BA cholangiocytes were again highly expressing SOX9 as well as the BA-specific epithelial gene product and diagnostic biomarker MMP7 (4) (Fig. 3G, Fig. S4D). Of note, MMP7 expression levels of cholangiocytes derived from mild BA livers were equal to those from cholestatic controls. Because MMP7 is, thus far, not well established as severity marker after KPE, our results add this new dimension to it. While cholangiocytes from severe BA exhibited a highly activated state, they showed a significant loss of KLF5, a transcription factor required for biliary epithelial remodelling and integrity in cholestatic liver injury. (27) Aside from the epithelial cells, we also sought to characterize the disease severity differences in key mesenchymal and myeloid cell types of the BA liver (Fig. S4D). HSCs exemplified a shared 'wound healing', 'collagen organization' and 'viral response' phenotype in BA regardless of severity. Meanwhile in severe BA, we find 'interferon response', 'TOR signaling', and 'smooth muscle cell migration' that may indicate mesenchymal cell activation. For Kupffer-like cells, the BA condition showed strong enrichment of terms involved in the immune response, viral process, and cell adhesion. In severe BA, we find 'leukocyte proliferation' and 'response to dsRNA' terms to be uniquely enriched (Fig. S4D). Across all cell types, IFN signaling indicative of a lymphoid immune response was enriched in severe BA compared to mild and control (Fig. S4E).

Fetal liver-like organoids recapitulate SOX9 high structural atresia and HNF4A loss

BA has a presumed fetal origin, yet no fetal model has captured how hepatocytes and cholangiocytes jointly respond to a BA-specific microenvironment. We established a BAspecific multi-compartment fetal liver-like organoid (FLO) system, combined with patientderived liver secretome, to test what injury is sufficient to drive the epithelial plasticity and biliary lumen collapse ('atresia') seen in patients (Fig. 3H). After detecting an IFN-high signature in severe BA, we used the synthetic dsRNA analogue polyinosinic:polycytidylic acid (polyI:C) to model the convergent, virus-independent innate response, also previously shown to induce the BA phenotype. (28) We combined this injury with the addition of conditioned medium (CM) derived from minced BA and non-BA liver tissue, sampled at LTX (postoutcome biosampling) (Fig. 3H). Intriguingly, different from the literature above, polyI:C treatment alone, had no significant effect on lumen-forming biliary phenotypes or epithelial plasticity in FLOs (Fig. 3H). Yet, additional treatment of differentiated multilineage FLOs with CM from severe BA patients (but not non-BA controls, Fig. S5A+B), resulted in a complete biliary lumen-collapse ('atresia') (Fig. 3I). Both BA and non-BA CM treatment led to the transdifferentiation of HNF4A+ grape-like hepatocyte clusters to SOX9+ cells (Fig. 3J; Fig. S5A+C). At the same time, only the addition of BA CM largely suppressed HNF4A in hepatic organoid assemblies, when assessed at protein level using mpIF on FLOs (Fig. 3J; Fig. S5C). Thus, a BA-like model that combines a developmental, multicellular organoid with patient tissue secretome provides evidence that BA-specific niche signaling establishes cellular cues that promote disease and not innate epithelial programs alone drive BA-associated cell states.

Spatial Stereo-sequencing offers insights into adaptive and maladaptive lymphatic cell neighborhoods

Because snRNA-seq dissociates tissue architecture, we applied Stereo-seq to intact liver sections from cholestatic control and BA patients to spatially resolve the portal lymphatic niche at single-cell resolution. Stereo-seq captured the cellular complexity of the liver in both cholestatic control and BA patients (Fig. 4A+B, Fig. S6A-D) by detecting 434,563 cells and 15,679 genes and resolving hepatocyte-dominated hepatic regions and portal fibroblast enriched portal regions (Fig. S6E). Cholangiocytes formed duct-like structures surrounded by stromal, endothelial, and immune cells, while portal regions became expanded and interconnected in BA, all consistent with general understanding of BA, validating our spatial data. Cellular community analysis identified seven communities across tissues, two of which were dominated by cholangiocytes (Fig. 4C+D, Fig. S7A). Community 5 comprised mainly cholangiocytes and Hep trans cells, suggesting that transitional cells may acquire biliary identity. GO terms were enriched for 'cell polarity', 'movement', 'cell-cell junctions', 'morphogenesis', and 'development'. Community 3 comprised portal area associated populations, including portal fibroblasts, endothelial and lymphatic endothelial cells, and immune cells, and was enriched for 'ECM assembly', 'vessel morphogenesis', 'TGF-β production', 'chemotaxis', and 'development' (Fig. S7B).

Cellular co-occurrence analysis revealed greater co-occurrence of cholangiocytes with other cholangiocytes with increasing disease severity, consistent with our previous findings of expansion during ductular reaction of severe BA (Fig. 4E+F, Fig. S7C). Co-occurrence with lymphatic endothelium also appeared increased in severe BA but was not a consistent phenomenon across all samples. These patterns may partly reflect changes in cell abundance. Spatial profiling confirmed that inflammatory mediators and ductular reaction, including SOX9 and MMP7, were most strongly expressed in severe BA cholangiocytes (Fig. 4G). HSCs exhibited the highest expression of fibrogenic genes in severe BA (Fig. 4H), collectively corroborating the snRNA-seq findings at a spatial level.

Although data sparsity precluded identification of the Hep BA subcluster in the Stereo-seq dataset, projection of its top differentially expressed genes revealed a progressive increase in the Hep BA score with disease severity, validating the trend observed by snRNA-seq (Fig. 4I, Fig. S7D). An interferon-response gene score similarly increased in severe BA, spatially validating the severity-associated interferon signature (Fig. S7E).

Spatial analysis along the hepatic-portal axis showed that established hepatocyte zonation markers, including CYP3A4, GLUL, and HAL, followed their expected expression patterns. The cellular communities localized predominantly to hepatic or portal regions, or at their interface (Fig. 4J, Fig. S7F-H). To resolve regional differences in cell states, we delineated the hepatic and portal regions and calculated signed distances from their interface, using 100 μm bins radiating along the hepatic-portal axis (Fig. 4J, Fig. S7F). Cells were then classified within their respective regional niches as interface, midpoint, or core (Fig. 4K+L). This spatial stratification largely recapitulated the cholangiocyte signatures shown in Fig. 4G, while revealing additional regional heterogeneity. In severe BA, interface cholangiocytes expressed high levels of SPP1 and CD24, whereas midpoint-region cholangiocytes showed increased ductular reaction and inflammatory mediators, including CXCL2, CXCL6, and CXCL8 (Fig. 4K), consistent with our snRNA-seq data (Fig. 3G). Core-region cholangiocytes showed the strongest expression of ductular reaction markers, including KRT7, KRT19, and MMP7. Hep trans cells exhibited a similar spatial pattern, consistent with acquisition of a stronger biliary identity (Fig. S7I). Finally, HSCs showed greater profibrogenic activity at the hepatic-portal interface (Fig. 4L), suggesting that the local microenvironment contributes to HSC activation and architectural remodeling in BA liver tissue.

Lymphatic endothelium controls SOX9-and MMP7-states of immune-primed cholangiocytes

Post-outcome biosampling obtained tissue and cell isolates from BA and non-BA liver explants for functional validation using ICOs and ICO-LEC-assembled organoids. For in-depth profiling of the soluble BA microenvironment, CM was obtained as mentioned above and profiled with Proteomics, LegendPlex TM and ELISA.

Proteomics detected a total of 1823 proteins of which 134 were unique to BA (Fig. S8A-C).

Overall pathway analysis enriched 'epithelial cell development', 'immune system', 'ECM proteoglycans' and other pathways in BA compared to non-BA. Lipid metabolic processes and lysosome activity were also enriched, reflecting a high bile acid metabolism. When further comparing CM from mild (late LTX) and severe (early LTX) BA patients, the latter enriched pathways concerning innate immune system, ECM receptor interaction, fibrillar collagen trimer and cadherin binding as well as lysosome. ICAM-1 was more than 10-fold higher in severe BA CM than in mild, and CD44 was uniquely detected in severe BA, possibly resembling inflammatory endothelial activation and immune cell enrichment. Interleukin (IL)-18 was detected exclusively in CM from severe BA, but not in CM from mild BA, echoing reports linking IL-18 to post-Kasai outcomes.( 29) LegendPlex TM detected no significant differences between BA and non-BA secretome for TNF-alpha, IL-1B, IL-17A, and IL-8 surprisingly (Fig. S8D). Interferon-Gamma (IFN-γ), Interferon-alpha2 (IFN-α2), IL-12-p70, IL-33, IL-6 and MCP-1 were significantly higher in BA.

Given the previous observation of extensive biliary and lymphatic dysplasia in the portal areas of severe BA patients and the epithelial plasticity observed in BA-secretome-treated FLOs, we hypothesized that LECs from severe BA may directly affect the biliary epithelium and induce epithelial state changes via CCL21. To model lymphatic-to-epithelial effects, we isolated LECs from BA liver explants, leveraging magnetic bead-based fractionation, assembled them with non-BA ICOs in a 3D assembloid and observed SOX9 high LGR5 high biliary progenitor state, as in the severe BA tissue response highlighted earlier (Fig. 5A, Fig. 3D-G). Direct treatment of ICOs with recombinant CCL21 alone partially recapitulated the SOX9 high epithelial state and confirmed that cholangiocytes can respond to CCL21 (Fig. S9A+B). As not all patient-derived ICO lines were responsive to CCL21, we found that the permissive epithelial state for CCL21 was determined by the condition from which the ICOs were derived. Specifically, CCL21sensing ICOs were derived from livers with histopathological evidence of immune cell infiltration and ductular reaction (Fig. S9B).

Following up on the hepatocyte states identified in severe BA -transdifferentiating (Hep trans) and identity-destabilized (Hep BA) hepatocytes (Fig. 3A-D) -we treated iPSC-derived hepatocytes with CCL21 and interestingly observed alterations of their transcriptional profile, indicating CCL21-sensing in hepatocytes. Suppression of SOX9, LGR5 and HNF4A indicated broad collapse of both biliary and hepatocyte identity programs rather than a directed lineage transition (Fig. 5B). While bile acids have been shown to regulate cholangiocyte plasticity and promote biliary differentiation of liver progenitor cells, their potential to alter hepatocyte identity in a cholestatic milieu remains unclear. (30, 31) As bile acids largely determine the hepatocyte microenvironment in BA, we combined the CCL21 treatment with Chenodeoxycholic acid (CDCA) at a non-cytotoxic concentration and observed exacerbated identity loss relative to CCL21 alone. Intrahepatic CCL21 was elevated in BA liver secretome (Fig. 5C; Fig. S9C), consistent with the CCL21 high LEC transcriptome and mpIF staining observed at the earlier pre-outcome timepoint in severe BA patients, indicating sustained CCL21 elevation along disease progression. To model the effects of disrupted lymphatic signaling on the liver epithelium within the complex cholestatic and inflammatory BA environment ex vivo, we treated hepatocytes with CCL21 high BA CM in combination with indirect co-culture with polyI:C-primed PBMCs (Fig. 5D+E; Fig. S9D; pIC-PBMC CM). This BA-like environment induced upregulation of C-C chemokine receptor type 7 (CCR7) in some but not all patient epithelia. Further, it led to a surge of the BAspecific MMP7 (Fig. 5E). Assessing the translational value of CCL21-modulating strategies to improve epithelial outcomes, CCL21-neutralizing antibodies normalized the BA-associated MMP7 -high -state in ICOs (Fig. 5F; Fig. S9E), suggesting that CCL21 is not only a marker of the dysplastic lymphatic niche but also a tractable effector of BA epithelial states (Fig. 5G).

DISCUSSION

BA follows a multifactorial pathophysiology and is the leading cause of pediatric liver transplantation worldwide. (1, 32) To dissect the molecular and structural tissue features that bias patient outcomes of BA early on, we profiled infant liver tissue obtained at KPE that was retrospectively outcome stratified. Thereby, we distinguished distinct intrahepatic BA phenotypes and identified early intrahepatic determinants of rapid progression towards liver transplantation in BA.

Here, we establish across a series of experimental modalities (spanning transcriptional, mpIF, liver tissue secretome-based ELISA, functional experiments in organoids) that early CCL21 high lymphatic endothelial dysplasia is a hallmark of BA patients who proceed with poor outcome.

Structurally, severe BA exhibited a maladaptive aggregation of lymphatic cells and a loss of lymphatic endothelial morphology continuity, whereas mild BA, although also affected relative to cholestatic controls, displayed an adaptive lymphangiogenic response.

Interestingly, we found that lymphatic endothelium signals on liver epithelium via CCL21 and induces a SOX9 high LGR5 high biliary progenitor response and most interestingly an MMP7 high epithelial state in cholangiocytes. This is relevant since a landmark study and subsequent validations established MMP7 elevation not only as a BA-specific phenomenon and biomarker, both at serum and hepatic transcript level, but also as mechanistically relevant in BA mouse models. (2, 4, 9, 10, 33) Yet here we show an upstream lymphatic-to-epithelial circuit, based on findings that soluble mediators as CCL21 can shape biliary epithelial responses, contributing to maladaptive remodeling or tissue repair. (34) We demonstrate that hepatic lymphatic tissue can exert these ectopic epithelial effects after prior BA-specific inflammatory licensing. Ex vivo, we suggest that a CCL21 receptor, CCR7, can potentially be induced on epithelial cells exposed to the tissue supernatant of severe BA livers. Yet, CCR7 expression levels vary and may be controlled by epigenetically determined permissive states. Beyond CCL21's role in homing immune and trafficking metastatic cells toward the lymphatic system,(35) our data lends well into reports of ectopic CCL21 sensing in liver fibrosis (on myofibroblasts). (36) CCL21-signaling beyond the lymphatic endothelium is dependent on glycosaminoglycan binding in the ECM, (19) thus CCL21-accessability should be considered in subsequent studies. Interestingly, cholestasis-associated increase in proteoglycans on cholangiocytes and HSCs may alter the local retention and spatial presentation of LEC-derived CCL21, thereby modulating epithelial exposure to this chemokine.( 37) Consistent with this finding, 'ECM proteoglycans' pathway was enriched in BA liver's secretome relative to non-BA controls.

Our findings of maladaptive lymphatic dysplasia in severe BA may offer a shared mechanism for disease progression in severe inflammatory cholestasis, given a preprint report on lymphatic changes in PSC, although our focus on CCL21-effects on immune licensed-epithelium has a different angle. (38) Different from the report on lymphatic endothelium after bile duct ligation in mice, our mpIF analysis did not reveal alterations of immune cell trafficking or accumulation around dysplastic lymphatic vasculature. Of note, we were comparing BA patients to cholestatic control patients and not to healthy livers and differences in immune cell accumulation around LECs were not significant. The report further linked the increase of TGFβ in LECs to reduced LEC density and drainage capacity. (38) Interestingly, in our outcome-stratified BA cohort, enrichment of TGFβ, a driver of epithelial plasticity, was not significant in severe BA LECs. However, we observed partial fibrogenic activity in LECs of severe BA patients with enrichment of SPARC (Osteonectin) and COL5A2. Thus, LECs may contribute to liver fibrosis in BA. (14, 39) In HSCs, TGFβ was also not differentially expressed between HSCs from mild and severe BA, supporting our foundational observation in Fig. 1, that fibrosis is not an obvious driver of outcome. Overall, our study suggests that the lymphatic compartment in BA is not only a bystander injury but actively engages with the epithelium with partial contribution to fibrogenicity.

Consistent with the central role of the liver epithelium in BA pathophysiology, we observed highly plastic epithelial cell states with distinct phenotypes characterizing severe BA. SnRNAseq detected hepatic-to-biliary transdifferentiation unspecifically in BA and cholestatic controls, yet the epithelial spectrum is more nuanced in BA with increased SOX9/HNF1b expression, which was most prominent in transdifferentiating hepatocytes in severe BA.( 14) Interestingly, severe BA also engaged LGR5+, distinct from the direct transdifferentiation reported in adults.( 15) Functionally, we provide evidence for LECs stimulating epithelial progenitor reprogramming, engaging LGR5, using a novel assembloid system. This offers a new possibility of a lymphatic rheostat of the liver's LGR5+ progenitor subset, previously reported in cholestatic liver injury.( 13) Our FLO in vitro experiments recapitulated epithelial plasticity towards SOX9 high states under severe BA-'secretomes' and revealed that this state is not driven by intrinsic epithelial programs or viral mimetic-polyI:C alone. In addition, FLOs, exhibited biliary lumen-collapse despite increased SOX9 expression, similar to the ductular reaction in BA, and Lastly, we detected a unique cholangiocyte signature in severe BA that was characterized by proinflammatory and profibrotic programs with extensive expression of chemokines and cytokines, highlighting their active role in an inflammatory hub in BA pathophysiology. Additionally, MMP7 was significantly increased in severe compared to mild BA cholangiocytes at the timepoint of KPE.

One limitation of our findings is that the exact inflammatory priming of CCL21-sensing may involve factors beyond CCL21, which requires further analyses, but could involve additional cytokines, chemokines or extracellular vesicles. Regarding functional experimentation, the standard rhesus rotavirus (RRV)-induced mouse model of BA is restricted to a single mouse strain, and species-specific differences in the CCL21 system, including a third mouse Ccl21 gene (Ccl21c) with no human ortholog and predominantly non-lymphoid expression, may limit its translational relevance for validating CCL21-dependent mechanisms in BA. Moreover, biliary injury is induced postnatally in the RRV mouse model, whereas mounting data suggests fetal onset in humans, (5, 42, 43) further limiting its translational relevance. Therefore, we pursued a human tissue-centered approach, combining patient-derived tissue analyses with human liver-and iPSC-derived organoid models for ex vivo functional validation, thereby enabling the investigation of human BA pathophysiology in the most relevant context.

In summary, our multiscale atlas indicates that outcome after KPE is shaped by intrahepatic cell states already present at diagnosis raising the possibility that distinct pathogenic mechanisms may underlie the clinical diagnosis of BA. Our findings specifically underscore the previously underappreciated role of the lymphatic niche within the 'portal tetrad' in BA progression, offering potential opportunities for prognostic stratification and therapeutic interventions which should be addressed in future prospective studies.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Experimental Design

This study investigated intrahepatic determinants of clinical outcome in patients with BA After collection of liver tissue during LTX or KPE, samples were immediately snap frozen, stored in basal medium or in 10% Formalin at 4°C, respectively. Liver samples were further processed for respective experiments immediately or within a maximum of 24 hours after biosampling. Biospecimen collection and processing were reported in accordance with the applicable items of the Biospecimen Reporting for Improved Study Quality (BRISQ) guidelines.( 45)

Single-cell transcriptomics

For snRNA-seq, we used fresh frozen liver tissues from patients with BA and cholestatic controls (control n = 3, mild n = 4, severe n = 4). Nuclei were isolated from tissue pieces with less than 0.3 cm diameter and prepared for snRNA-seq capture as previously described. (46) In brief, tissues were homogenized in citric acid buffer (250 mM sucrose, 25 mM citric acid, 1 μg/ml Hoechst 33342) with glass tissue douncers. The homogenate was filtered through 35 μm cell strainers to remove debris, then centrifuged at 500g for 5 min at 4°C. Nuclei were washed in citric acid buffer and spun down again for a final wash before being resuspended in cold resuspension buffer (25 mM KCl, 3 mM MgCl2, 50 mM Tris-buffer, 0.4 U/μl RNaseIn (2313A, Takara), 1 mM dithiothreitol, 0.4 U/μl SuperaseIn (AM2694, Thermo Fisher Scientific) and 1 μg/ml Hoechst 33342). After removing the supernatant, nuclei were counted and immediately loaded into the 10x Chromium Controller using the 10x Genomics Single Cell 3′ Library Kit

Generation of FLOs and conditioned media induced injury-modeling

FLOs were generated from induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) as previously described. (21) On day 19 of differentiation, FLOs were treated with 40 µg/mL polyI:C (Miltenyi) or vehicle control (NaCl) for 22 hours. On day 20, medium was replaced with 600 µL of basal FLO medium, followed by addition of 600 µL of liver CM of BA or non-BA explants. FLOs were cultured under the respective medium condition for 5 or 7 days, with the medium replaced every two days, before fixation (30 min in 10% formalin at RT) and processing for mpIF.

Isolation and culture of liver lymphatic endothelial cells

Liver tissue with or without capsule was digested into single cells. Cells were plated at a density of 1 × 10⁶ cells/cm² on collagen-coated plates (5 µg/cm²; rat tail collagen, Corning) and cultured in EGM-2 medium (Lonza) supplemented with Y-27632 (10µM) and 25 ng/mL VEGF-C. The following day, plates were washed three times with DPBS containing 1% P/S, and fresh medium was added. Cells were cultured until approximately 80% confluency was reached, typically after 7-10 days, before MACS sorting was performed. PDPN-positive cells were isolated by indirect magnetic cell sorting using mouse IgG anti-human PDPN antibody at a dilution of 1:400 for 30 min, followed by anti-mouse IgG magnetic beads according to the manufacturer's instructions.

Statistics

Differential gene expression testing was calculated with the Seurat FindMarkers() function using the Wilcoxon Rank Sum test, with significance set at p-value <.05 and log-fold change of 0.25. For multiple testing, p-values were adjusted using the Benjamini-Hochberg method and considered significant at adjusted p-value <.05. CellChat identified enriched pathways to specific conditions by performing a paired Wilcoxon test within the rankNet() function.

ClusterProfiler applies a hypergeometric test for enrichment analysis, with significance thresholds set at Benjamini-Hochberg adjusted p-value <.05. Gene set score comparisons were calculated with a Wilcoxon Rank Sum test and adjusted with Benjamini-Hochberg method, significance set at adjusted p-value <.05. Significance was only reported if the calculated Cohen's effect size r was classified as small, moderate, or large. For all non-sequencing assays one-way ANOVA or unpaired t-test were performed and statistical results were reported when considered significant at adjusted p-value <.05. Data for mpIF, clinical characteristics and qPCR are presented as individual data points, with the mean±SD indicated, unless otherwise stated.
