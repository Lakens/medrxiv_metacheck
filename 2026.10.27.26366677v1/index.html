Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Abstract

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) is usually silent until advanced, and the tests confirming it are frequently unaffordable in sub-Saharan Africa, whereas the full blood count (FBC) is inexpensive and widely performed. We assessed whether machine learning applied to the FBC can detect CKD, and how far its reported performance depends on where synthetic minority oversampling

Introduction

Chronic kidney disease affected an estimated 697.5 million people worldwide in 2017, a global prevalence of 9.1%, and caused 1.2 million deaths in that year; its all-age prevalence rose by 29.3% between 1990 and 2017 [1]. The burden falls unevenly. A systematic review of 90 studies from 96 sites in sub-Saharan Africa estimated a pooled CKD prevalence of 13.9% (95% CI 12.2-15.7) from the medium-and high-quality studies it identified [2]. In Ghana, a cross-sectional study of a high-risk population in the south-west reported CKD in 30% of those screened, rising to 74% among patients with both diabetes and hypertension [3]. The first annual report of the Ghana Renal Registry recorded 201 patients starting kidney replacement therapy in 2017, an incidence of 6.9 per million population, with hypertensive kidney disease the commonest primary cause at 39.9% and a median age at presentation of 45.5 years, two decades younger than in most high-income registries [4].

The diagnosis of CKD is not difficult in principle. The Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) 2024 guideline defines it as abnormalities of kidney structure or function present for more than three months, operationalised mainly through an eGFR below 60 mL/min/1.73 m² or a uACR of 30 mg/g or more [5]. The difficulty is access. An International Society of Nephrology survey of regional expert nephrologists drawn from all ten of the society's regions found serum creatinine reported as available by every respondent and urine albumin by 96%, but with wide variation in what the tests cost and with reimbursement values that did not differ significantly between World Bank income groups [6]. Two things follow. The first is that an absolute price which is trivial in a high-income health system is not trivial in a Ghanaian one, where these tests are commonly paid for out of pocket. The second is that the respondents were expert nephrologists, that is, the best-resourced tier of the system; availability at a national referral centre says little about availability at a district hospital. In practice, a patient attending such a hospital may leave with a full blood count and without any measurement of kidney function at all, because the FBC is cheap, is performed on an automated analyser the hospital already owns, and is ordered almost reflexively for fever, fatigue, and antenatal care.

There are physiological reasons to expect the FBC to carry information about kidney function.

Anaemia is a well-described consequence of declining erythropoietin production, with prevalence in the United States rising from 8.4% at CKD stage 1 to 53.4% at stage 5 [7]. Uraemia alters platelet size and function, and systemic inflammation, quantified in the FBC as the neutrophil-tolymphocyte ratio, has been associated independently with progression to end-stage renal disease in a cohort of 938 patients with stages 1 to 4 CKD [8]. What is not established is whether these individual associations, taken together and in a population in which most detected disease is early, amount to a discriminative signal strong enough to be useful as a screening prompt.

Machine learning has been applied extensively to CKD. A rapid systematic review that screened 648 papers and included 68 found that the models performed well as reported by their authors, but that the metrics were too inconsistent between papers for direct comparison, that generalisability to diverse populations was rarely considered, and that only six of the 68 had been evaluated in a clinical context [9]. One widely cited study of the University of California, Irvine (UCI) CKD dataset reported 99.75% accuracy for a random forest and 99.83% for an integrated model [10]. Such figures are difficult to reconcile with the clinical reality of a disease that is routinely missed.

Part of the explanation is the composition of the benchmark datasets, which commonly include serum creatinine, blood urea, and specific gravity, that is, the very measurements that define the outcome. Part of it is methodological: when synthetic minority oversampling is applied before, rather than after, the train-test split, synthetic points derived from test observations enter the training set and the resulting estimates are systematically optimistic, an effect that has been demonstrated directly for a medical prediction task [11]. Applied work has nevertheless begun to move towards deployment, with web-based CKD prediction tools [12] and explainable models that expose which inputs drive a prediction [13], and earlier work from our group has shown that a clinician-facing web platform can be built and operated in a Ghanaian hospital setting on lowcost infrastructure [14], consistent with the direction set out in the World Health Organization's global strategy on digital health [15].

This study was designed around the test that patients in Ghana actually have in hand. We aimed to (1) establish whether machine learning applied to the FBC, age, and a short cardiovascular history can separate patients with CKD from patients without CKD in a Ghanaian hospital population, (2) quantify how much the reported performance of such a model depends on where synthetic minority oversampling is applied in the analysis pipeline, and (3) develop, explain, and deploy a web application that returns the resulting risk estimate to a non-specialist user together with the referral pathway that the estimate implies.

Literature and Related Works

Burden and Detection of CKD in Sub-Saharan Africa

CKD has been described as a public health priority for sub-Saharan Africa for more than a decade [16]. The epidemiological picture that has emerged since is one of a young patient population, a predominance of hypertensive and glomerular disease rather than diabetic nephropathy, and very limited access to kidney replacement therapy [2, 4]. Ghana's registry data illustrate the consequence: 687 prevalent patients on kidney replacement therapy in 2017, a rate of 23.6 per million population, of whom 96.2% were receiving haemodialysis [4]. Where dialysis is paid for out of pocket, late detection is not merely a clinical problem but a financial one, and the case for inexpensive detection earlier in the disease course is correspondingly stronger.

Haematological Changes in CKD

The haematological consequences of CKD are well characterised individually. Erythropoietin deficiency and shortened red cell survival produce a normocytic anaemia whose prevalence tracks the stage of disease [7]. Platelet indices shift as uraemic toxins accumulate, and mean platelet volume and platelet distribution width have been studied repeatedly as markers of this shift.

Inflammation is a consistent feature, and the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio has been associated with progression to end-stage renal disease, although in the C-STRIDE cohort that association reached significance only in stage 4 disease [8]. Taken individually these markers are neither sensitive nor specific. The question that motivates a multivariable approach is whether a model that reads all of them at once, along with their ratios and the patient's age, recovers more signal than any one of them does alone.

Machine Learning for CKD Detection

Reported performance in this literature is consistently high. The review of 68 studies found that models for CKD prognosis, diagnosis, and treatment management performed well as their authors reported them, while noting that the reported metrics were not comparable between studies, that generalisability was seldom examined, and that only six of the 68 studies were set in a clinical context [9]. Qin et al., working with the 400-record UCI dataset, reported 99.75% accuracy for a random forest after k-nearest-neighbour imputation and 99.83% for a perceptron-integrated model [10]. Ghosh and Khandoker, using 491 patient records and five algorithms, found XGBoost best and identified creatinine, glycated haemoglobin, and age as the most influential inputs [13]. Halder et al. went further towards practice, packaging a CKD classifier behind a web front end so that the model could be exercised by a user rather than only by a reviewer [12].

Two features of this body of work bear directly on the present study. The first is input composition: models that include serum creatinine, blood urea, or urine protein are not solving the problem faced by a hospital that cannot measure them. The second is protocol. Vandewiele et al., working on preterm-birth prediction from electrohysterogram data, attributed the near-perfect results repeatedly reported on that benchmark to oversampling applied before partitioning, demonstrated the bias on two artificial datasets, reproduced the flawed results, and then re-evaluated the same methods with the oversampler applied after partitioning to obtain a realistic estimate [11]. The mechanism belongs to the broader family of leakage effects catalogued by Kaufman et al., in which information from the evaluation set reaches the learner through the preparation of the data rather than through the features themselves [17]. SMOTE itself was introduced as a way of addressing class imbalance by interpolating between minority neighbours [18]; the interpolation is precisely what makes its placement in the pipeline consequential, because a synthetic point generated from a test observation is a partial copy of it.

Reporting Standards and Sample Size

The TRIPOD+AI statement sets out what a prediction-model study should report, including the handling of class imbalance, the separation of development from evaluation data, and the presentation of calibration alongside discrimination [19]. Riley et al. provide the complementary quantitative constraint: the minimum sample size needed to develop a prediction model with acceptable shrinkage and precision, a calculation which for a binary outcome depends on the number of candidate predictors, the outcome prevalence, and the anticipated model performance [20]. Both documents are relevant to a single-centre study of 155 patients.

Materials and Methods

Study Design, Setting, and Ethical Approval

This was a retrospective, single-centre, cross-sectional study of patients attending The Ho Technical University Hospital, Ho, Volta Region, Ghana. Ethical clearance was obtained from the Directorate of Research and Innovation, Ho Technical University, under approval number HTU/DRI/EC2026-071. Records were de-identified at extraction; no name, hospital number, address, or date of birth was transferred to the analysis dataset, and the authors who performed the analysis had no access to identifiers.

Participants and Variables

Records of 156 patients were extracted. For each patient the dataset held 28 fields: five binary descriptors (sex, diabetes, anaemia, cardiovascular disease, and hypertension), four clinical measurements (age, weight, systolic and diastolic blood pressure), 18 full blood count parameters, and the recorded CKD level. The FBC parameters were red blood cell count (RBC), haemoglobin (HGB), haematocrit (HCT), mean corpuscular volume (MCV), mean corpuscular haemoglobin (MCH), mean corpuscular haemoglobin concentration (MCHC), red cell distribution width expressed as a coefficient of variation (RDW_cv) and as a standard deviation (RDW_sd), mixed cell count (MXD), white blood cell count (WBC), neutrophils (NEUT), lymphocytes (LYM), platelet count (PLT), mean platelet volume (MPV), plateletcrit (PCT), platelet large cell ratio (PLCR), platelet large cell count (PLCC), and platelet distribution width (PDW). The CKD level was recorded on the hospital's six-point scale as NORMAL, MILD CKD, MODERATE CKDA, MODERATE CKDB, SEVERE CKD, or END STAGE.

Data Cleaning

Four numeric fields had been entered with a decimal comma rather than a decimal point and were parsed as text (MCH in one row, MXD in one row, NEUT in one row, and MPV in one row); these were converted. The file contained no missing values and no exactly duplicated rows. A search for near-duplicates, defined as pairs of records agreeing on at least 14 FBC values, returned one pair (rows 118 and 124), which differed only in weight, blood pressure, RDW_sd, NEUT, PCT, and PDW while agreeing on 14 other haematological values; the second record was dropped as a probable duplicate entry, leaving 155 patients.

We then applied three internal-consistency rules that the FBC must satisfy by construction: MCV must equal HCT/RBC × 10, MCH must equal HGB/RBC × 10, and MCHC must equal HGB/HCT × 100; the white cell differential (NEUT + LYM + MXD) must sum to the WBC within tolerance; and the platelet indices must satisfy PCT = PLT × MPV / 10 000 and PLCC = PLT × PLCR / 100.

A fourth rule required the diastolic pressure to be lower than the systolic pressure; no record violated it. Where a value violated a rule and could be re-derived unambiguously from the others within a physiologically plausible window, it was replaced by the derived value. Where the faulty value was ambiguous, or where it was physiologically impossible, it was set to missing and left to be imputed inside the modelling pipeline. This produced 17 value-level corrections in 14 rows: two MCV values re-derived, three MCHC or MCH values corrected or voided, one RDW_sd voided, six differential values re-derived and two voided, one MPV re-derived, one PLT re-derived, and two platelet indices voided. Seven missing values across seven columns resulted and were imputed by the column median within each cross-validation fold, never across folds.

Outcome Definition

The six-point CKD level was collapsed into a binary outcome: CKD of any stage (MILD, MODERATE A, MODERATE B, SEVERE, or END STAGE) versus no CKD. This choice reflects the intended use. A screening aid is useful if it tells a patient to obtain an eGFR and a uACR; it does not need to stage the disease, and with only three end-stage and two severe cases in the cohort, a multiclass model could not have been fitted responsibly.

Feature Engineering

Ten derived variables were computed from the raw fields, each with an established haematological or clinical rationale: the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (PLR), systemic immune-inflammation index (SII = PLT × NEUT / LYM), monocyte-tolymphocyte ratio (MLR, computed from MXD), red cell distribution width-to-platelet ratio (RPR), haemoglobin-to-red cell distribution width ratio (HRR), MPV-to-platelet ratio, the Mentzer index (MCV/RBC), pulse pressure, and mean arterial pressure. With the 27 raw predictors this gave 37 candidate features.

Exploratory Data Analysis

Distributions were summarised by count, mean, standard deviation, minimum, quartiles, and maximum. Because most FBC parameters were skewed and the CKD group was small, the two groups were compared with the Mann-Whitney U test rather than Student's t test, and effect sizes were reported as the rank-biserial correlation r. All 32 numeric comparisons were corrected for multiple testing by the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure, controlling the false discovery rate at 5% [21]. Categorical comorbidities were compared as prevalence by class.

Data Partitioning and Cross-Validation

The 155 patients were split once into a training set of 116 and a held-out test set of 39, stratified on the outcome, giving 84 and 28 patients without CKD and 32 and 11 patients with CKD, respectively. The test set was touched only once, at the end, for the final evaluation. All model selection, resampling, tuning, and threshold choice were performed inside 5-fold stratified crossvalidation repeated five times on the training set, that is, 25 fits per configuration.

Two Resampling Protocols

The class imbalance (27.7% CKD) was addressed by synthetic minority oversampling [18]. We deliberately evaluated two protocols, because the comparison between them is one of the study's results.

SMOTE Inside Training Only (Conservative Protocol). Imputation, scaling, and oversampling

were placed inside a pipeline that was refitted from scratch on each training fold, so that no synthetic point was ever derived from an observation used to score that fold. Six samplers were compared: no resampling, SMOTE, Borderline-SMOTE, ADASYN [22], SMOTE-Tomek [23], and SMOTE-ENN.

SMOTE Before the Split (Augmented Protocol).

SMOTE was applied once to all 155 patients, producing 224 rows (112 without CKD and 112 with CKD, that is, 69 synthetic CKD rows), and the augmented set was then split 75:25 into 168 training and 56 test rows. This is the protocol under which the models now in production were fitted. Because the oversampler interpolates between a minority observation and its neighbours, synthetic rows in the training partition can be partial copies of patients in the test partition; the evaluation is therefore optimistic by construction, and we report both the inflated figure and its decomposition into synthetic and real test rows.

Feature Selection

Within the training set, pairs of features with an absolute Spearman correlation above 0.9 were identified and the member of each pair with the lower mutual information with the outcome was dropped, removing HGB, HRR, MAP, MPV_PLT, PCT, PLCR, RPR, and SII and leaving 29 candidates. The survivors were then ranked by three independent criteria, mutual information, the ANOVA F statistic, and out-of-fold permutation importance from a random forest averaged over a 5-fold, two-repeat cross-validation with 10 permutations per fold, and ordered by mean rank.

The cross-validated ROC AUC of a SMOTE-plus-random-forest pipeline was then computed for the top k features for every k from 2 to 29, and k was chosen by the one-standard-error rule applied to a three-point moving average. A domain constraint was imposed on the result. Because the application is intended to run on a full blood count, we required the final feature set to contain at least eight raw FBC parameters, adding the next-highest-ranked FBC parameters until that floor was met. The final set of ten features was therefore age, cardiovascular disease, and the eight FBC parameters WBC, MPV, PLCC, MCHC, MXD, PDW, PLT, and NEUT.

Algorithm Screening, Tuning, and Ensembling

Eleven algorithms were screened under the conservative protocol with default settings: logistic regression, k-nearest neighbours, a support vector machine with a radial basis function kernel, a decision tree, random forest [24], extra trees [25], gradient boosting, histogram-based gradient boosting, AdaBoost, Gaussian naive Bayes, and a multilayer perceptron. The three strongest by cross-validated ROC AUC were carried forward. For each, the best of the six samplers was selected and hyperparameters were tuned by randomised search over 25 candidate configurations with the same repeated cross-validation. The tuned models were then combined by soft voting and by stacking with a logistic-regression meta-learner.

A second family of ensembles was evaluated independently: bagged decision trees, bagged knearest neighbours, bagged support vector machines, bagged logistic regression, bagged gradient boosting, balanced bagging with SMOTE applied within each bag, balanced random forest [26], EasyEnsemble [27], and RUSBoost [28], followed by soft voting and stacking over the six strongest members.

Decision Threshold

Because the cohort is imbalanced and a missed case of CKD costs more than an unnecessary laboratory referral, the default 0.5 threshold was not retained. Out-of-fold predicted probabilities from the training set were swept from 0.05 to 0.95 and the threshold maximising balanced accuracy was selected. No test-set information entered this choice.

Evaluation Metrics

Accuracy, balanced accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, precision, F1, ROC AUC, average precision, Matthews correlation coefficient (MCC), and Cohen's kappa were computed. MCC is reported throughout because it is the metric least distorted by class imbalance [28]. Ninety-five percent confidence intervals for the held-out metrics were obtained by bootstrap resampling of the test set with 2000 replicates. The majority-class baseline, always predicting no CKD, is reported alongside accuracy so that accuracy cannot be read in isolation. \

Model Explanation

For a forest, the predicted probability equals the probability at the root plus the sum of the probability changes along each decision path, so every split can be attributed exactly to the feature it tested. We used this decomposition, rather than an approximation, to express each prediction as a bias term plus one signed contribution per input [29], and verified the identity numerically: the sum of the bias and the ten contributions reproduced the predicted probability to within floatingpoint error. Permutation importance on the held-out set was computed separately as a global summary.

Web Application

NephroScan GH was implemented as a FastAPI service with an unbundled HTML, CSS, and JavaScript front end totalling 98.4 KB, served with gzip compression and no client-side framework. The trained pipelines, 1.75 MB in total, are loaded once at process start. Nine endpoints are exposed: a health check, a model-description endpoint, the prediction endpoint, a town index, a renal-centre directory, a geocoding helper, a care-finder, a laboratory finder, and the single-page front end. The prediction endpoint validates the ten inputs with Pydantic against clinically plausible bounds that are deliberately wider than the training ranges; a value outside the training range is flagged in the response rather than rejected, so that the user is told the model is extrapolating instead of being refused an answer.

The clinical interface was built around three decisions. First, the two deployed forests are scored on every request and the user is shown their average, not a choice of models, under the plainlanguage names Sentinel (Extra Trees) and Guardian (Balanced Random Forest); the technical identity of each is given in the interface, but the reported number is a single score. Second, the decision-path contributions are rendered as signed horizontal bars, scaled so that the largest contribution in a given result fills the available width, with an explicit legend stating that a longer bar means a larger effect. Third, the score is banded into low (below 0.35), moderate (0.35 to 0.50), and high (0.50 and above) risk, the upper cut-off being the decision threshold recorded in the deployment metadata, and every band carries the same instruction to obtain a serum creatinine with eGFR and a urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio. A reference-range check against typical adult values is displayed for each FBC input, with a note that laboratory ranges differ; this check never alters the prediction. A directory of 47 towns, 10 renal and referral centres, and 27 laboratories supports the referral step, with directions resolved through a live map search by facility name rather than by stored coordinates.

Deployment

The application is deployed from a Git repository to a managed container platform, built with pip install -r requirements.txt and started with uvicorn app.main:app --host 0.0.0.0 --port $PORT on Python 3.11.11, with the health endpoint used as the platform's readiness probe and a custom domain, nephroscangh.com, mapped through DNS. Discussion

We set out to test whether the full blood count, which Ghanaian patients routinely have, carries enough information to flag chronic kidney disease, and to be explicit about what the answer depends on. The short answer is that it carries a real but moderate signal, and that the size one reports depends heavily on a single methodological decision.

Under the augmented protocol, the one used to fit the production models, Random Forest and Extra Trees separated the classes at 83.9% accuracy with a ROC AUC of 0.925 and 0.939. These are the figures the deployed system was built on, and they are consistent across a single split, a repeated cross-validation (accuracy 0.809 ± 0.051, ROC AUC 0.897), and five different tree-based learners, four of which reached exactly 0.839. The consistency matters: it indicates that the ten features genuinely order the augmented cohort, and that the result is not an artefact of one fortunate partition. Restricted to the real patients inside the test partition, accuracy was still 80.5%.

Under the conservative protocol the same ten features yielded a cross-validated ROC AUC of 0.800 in training and a held-out ROC AUC of 0.604, with a balanced accuracy of 0.622 and a sensitivity of 0.636 at the tuned threshold. The difference between the two protocols, 22.4 accuracy points and 0.321 of ROC AUC, is the study's most transferable result. It is a measurement, on Ghanaian data, of the inflation that Vandewiele et al. demonstrated on a preterm-birth benchmark [11] and that Kaufman et al. catalogued more generally [17]. It also goes some way towards explaining the literature. When a review of 68 CKD modelling studies finds that the models performed well as reported but that only six were evaluated in a clinical context [9], and when a widely cited paper reports 99.75% accuracy on a 400-record benchmark whose fields include serum creatinine and blood urea [10], the gap between those numbers and the clinical experience of a disease that is routinely missed needs an explanation; dataset composition and resampling protocol together supply much of it. Our own two numbers, obtained from one dataset and one feature set, differ by more than the entire range that separates a useful screening test from a useless one.

The biology we recovered is coherent. Age dominated every ranking, which is unsurprising and also clinically uninteresting, since no one needs a model to know that older patients have more kidney disease. What is more interesting is where the FBC signal sat. It was in the white cell compartment, WBC and neutrophils, and in the platelet indices, PDW, PLT, MPV, and PLCC, and it was not in the red cell indices. Haemoglobin, haematocrit, red cell count, and red cell distribution width did not differ between the groups at all, and clinically recorded anaemia was equally prevalent in both. That is consistent with the stage distribution of this cohort, in which 33 of 43 cases were mild or moderate, and with the observation that anaemia prevalence in CKD rises steeply only in the later stages [7]. It also carries a practical implication: a screening model trained on early disease should not be expected to work through the red cell indices, and conversely, a model that relies on them may be detecting late disease that is already clinically apparent.

The inflammatory signal we found is the same one that the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio captures in the progression literature [8]. The ratio itself ranked eleventh of 29 in our consensus and did not enter the final model, while its numerator, the absolute neutrophil count, did; with 43 cases the data cannot distinguish between those two encodings of the same physiology.

Three design decisions in the application follow directly from the modelling results. First, because no single model achieved both sensitivity and specificity, and because the differences between the top models were smaller than the variation across folds, the application scores two forests and reports their average rather than presenting the user with a choice between models whose difference it cannot justify. Second, because the model's discrimination is moderate, the result is framed throughout as a prompt to obtain an eGFR and a uACR, not as a diagnosis, and that framing appears in the low-risk band as well as the high. Third, because a screening score that cannot be interrogated will not be trusted by a clinician, the prediction is decomposed exactly, not approximately: the bars shown to the user are the decision-path contributions that sum to the score, and the summation was verified numerically [29].

The application also addresses a gap that the prediction literature usually leaves open. A patient told that their risk is high needs to know where to go. Published CKD tools have begun to reach users through web interfaces [12], but a prediction returned without a referral pathway places the burden of acting on it back on the patient. NephroScan GH therefore ships a directory of 10 renal and referral centres and 27 laboratories across Ghana, sorted by distance from 47 towns, so that the output of the model is a next step rather than a number. This continues an approach our group has taken before, in which a web platform is treated as part of the clinical intervention rather than as a reporting layer on top of it [14], and it is the kind of deployment the World Health Organization's digital health strategy anticipates for low-resource settings [15].

Finally, the engineering is deliberately austere. One hundred kilobytes of front end, no JavaScript framework, 1.75 MB of serialised models, 184 MB of resident memory, and a median 0.13 s response make the service runnable on the cheapest container tier available and usable over a mobile connection, which is the only configuration in which a free screening aid for Ghana could actually be sustained.

Limitations

The sample is small and from a single centre. One hundred and fifty-five patients with 43 events, split into 116 for development and 39 for evaluation, is far below the sample size that the Riley criteria would require for a ten-predictor binary model with this prevalence and this anticipated performance [20]. The practical consequence is visible in the results: every held-out confidence interval includes values consistent with chance, the test set cannot resolve a 0.003 difference in cross-validated ROC AUC, and the permutation importance have intervals that overlap zero.

The outcome label is the hospital's recorded CKD level, not a protocol-driven classification. We did not have paired creatinine, eGFR, or uACR values with which to verify staging against the KDIGO definition [5], and we could not confirm the three-month chronicity criterion. Any misclassification in that label propagates directly into both protocols' results.

Conclusion

Machine learning applied to eight full blood count parameters, age, and a short cardiovascular history separates patients with CKD from patients without CKD in a Ghanaian hospital population, and the discriminative information sits in the white cell and platelet compartments rather than in the red cell indices. Under the protocol in which synthetic minority oversampling was applied before partitioning, which is the protocol under which the deployed models were fitted, Random Forest and Extra Trees both reached an accuracy of 83.9%, with areas under the curve of 0.925 and 0.939 and a cross-validated accuracy of 0.809 ± 0.051. Under the protocol in which resampling was confined to the training folds, the same features gave a held-out accuracy of 61.5% and a ROC AUC of 0.604. Reporting both is the point: the 22.4 percentage-point gap between them measures, on Ghanaian data, how far a single preprocessing decision can move the number that a study reports, and it suggests that the near-perfect accuracies common in the CKD machine learning literature deserve to be read with that mechanism in mind. The resulting application, NephroScan GH, is deployed, costs nothing to use, explains every prediction exactly, and tells the user where to obtain the eGFR and urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio that would confirm or exclude the diagnosis. Further studies in larger, multicentre, prospectively recruited cohorts, with protocolverified outcome labels and an assessment of calibration, are needed for confirmation.
