Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. Some differences come from the ground truth: it lacks p-values with a correction name (“p Bonf = .061”), and counts partial eta squared written with its subscript after it (“η 2 p = .05”), “p = .05”, and some table notes and figure legends (“† p < .10, *p < .05”), which this module leaves out, as non-significant p-values. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). Abstract

Objective The aim was to develop and evaluate the Passive Visual Field Measurement (PVFM), a novel visual field (VF) measurement tool that predicts the VF from a user's natural, intuitive interactions in a virtual reality (VR) environment. Due to the flexible nature of its input, the tool could be integrated with various interactive VR tasks and even run passively in the background of tasks users are already motivated to carry out, such as preventive exercises or rehabilitation training.

Methods The PVFM was developed and compared to a conventional VF measurement method, kinetic perimetry using the Octopus 900 perimeter, in a pilot study with n=4 retinitis pigmentosa patients. Both measurements were carried out for each eye, for a total of eight sets. The measured VFs were compared using radial-weighted Intersection over Union (IoU), showing the degree to which PVFM results align with conventional VF testing.

Results For patients with largely concentric visual fields (n=6 eyes), agreement between PVFM and kinetic perimetry was high, with radial-weighted IoU of 75.4% to 91.0% and mean absolute angular distances between VF boundaries of 0.31° to 2.68°. PVFM takes significantly longer than kinetic perimetry, however patient feedback indicates that PVFM is more enjoyable and engaging.

The preliminary evidence of this pilot study suggests the feasibility of PVFM as an intuitive and engaging method for detecting VF boundaries in patients with concentric peripheral visual field loss, with agreement with kinetic perimetry within the range of test-retest variability reported for conventional kinetic perimetry. However, PVFM showed drastically lower agreement in peripheral, non-concentric visual field areas, a current limitation requiring further investigation and refinement.

Introduction

Visual field (VF) testing is an essential tool for the diagnosis and continuous monitoring of various retinal and neuro-ophthalmological conditions such as glaucoma, retinitis pigmentosa, and age-related macular degeneration, all of which are characterized by the loss of parts of the visual field, so-called visual field defects. In glaucoma and retinitis pigmentosa in particular, this loss typically starts in the periphery and progresses inward, eventually leaving only a constricted central visual field, and can ultimately end in complete blindness [1, 2]. Crucially, this process is often difficult for affected individuals to notice on their own. Rather than perceiving a clear dark patch or a black tunnel where vision has been lost, as is oftentimes shown in visual representations or simulations of such conditions [3, 4], the visual system tends to perceptually complete these missing regions, and a considerable proportion of patients remain unaware of their visual field loss even when directly asked about it [5]. As a consequence, these conditions are often not diagnosed until severe, irreversible damage has occurred, and even after diagnosis, regular visual field testing remains essential to detect and respond to further progression.

A conventional method for VF testing, especially in patients with extensive peripheral vision loss, is kinetic perimetry [6] [7] [8] [9]. Patients are tasked with fixating a central marker on a screen in front of them while a target moves from a non-seeing area of the visual field towards a seeing area (e.g., from the non-seeing periphery towards the seeing center). As soon as the target is perceived, the patient presses a button, and the target's position at that moment -oftentimes corrected by reaction time -marks a point on the boundary of the visual field. By repeating this process from different directions and with varying start and end points, the boundary of the patient's visual field can be reconstructed by connecting the individual points. The result is known as as an isopter field, where an isopter line describes the connecting line between two adjacent boundary points [7].

While kinetic perimetry continues to be the convention for clinical visual field testing, it comes with notable limitations. It is typically performed by a trained medical professional using stationary, high-cost perimeters, which makes testing only feasible within clinical settings and makes it costly and logistically demanding to repeat frequently [10, 11]. Since routine clinical visits are oftentimes scheduled multiple months or even years apart, sudden changes in a patient's condition can go undetected for extended periods, which reduces the likelihood of identifying disease progression early enough to intervene effectively. These constraints have led to a growing interest in home-based, accessible, and frequent VF monitoring, particularly for patients with chronic and progressive conditions such as retinitis pigmentosa and glaucoma [12, 13]. Advances in virtual reality (VR) technology have made this increasingly feasible. Modern head-mounted displays are self-contained and portable and offer a considerably larger field of view than conventional monitor-based setups with common visual field sizes of 90-110° diameter [14]. They also provide head-tracking, which either enables the user to freely look around a virtual scene or, when head-centric fixation is required, to display visual stimuli at head-fixed positions, unaffected by accidental head movements. Building on these properties, a growing number of VR-based visual field testing devices have been developed in both academic and commercial contexts [10], such as the Advance Vision Analyzer (AVA) (Elisar VisionTechnology) [15, 16], VisuALL (Olleyes) [17], and the Toronto Portable Perimeter [18], several of which show good agreement with the current clinical standard for visual field testing [18] [19] [20] [21]. However, a two-year home-monitoring study, using the Toronto Portable Perimeter, found that patient compliance declined significantly over the course of the study, indicating that simply making a device available at home does not by itself guarantee sustained, motivated use [18]. This points to a second limitation of conventional perimetry: it can be mentally and visually demanding. Continuously fixating a single marker for several minutes -while also staying alert to press the button once the peripheral stimulus becomes visible -is cognitively taxing and can lead to fatigue [22], which in turn can lower patient adherence and reduce the reliability of the resulting measurements. This becomes especially problematic for patients who find it difficult to sustain attention on a single task for an extended period, such as young children or individuals with cognitive impairments [23, 24].

One method developed to address this issue is Saccadic Vector Optokinetic Perimetry (SVOP) [25]. Rather than requiring patients to maintain fixation and press a button, SVOP uses eye tracking to detect natural, involuntary saccadic eye movements towards stimuli, removing the need for sustained fixation or a manual response and making testing considerably more comfortable for children and other patients who struggle with conventional perimetry [25]. More recent VR-based methods use a similar approach, integrating eye-movement-based VF testing in head-mounted displays and reporting that such VR-based versions were consistently preferred by patients over both conventional perimetry and screen-based eye-tracking equivalents [26]. A limitation of these approaches, however, is that they depend on VR headsets with integrated eye-tracking hardware, which remains considerably more expensive than standard consumer VR headsets and is, for the most part, still confined to devices aimed at research, enterprise, or industry use rather than the casual consumer market.

Beyond diagnostic assessment, VR technology has also shown promise in other areas of visual healthcare, notably visual rehabilitation. Several home-based, VR-delivered training programs have been developed for patients with visual field defects, with reported improvements in visual perception and quality of life [27, 28]. Since patients in such rehabilitation tasks already interact naturally with stimuli distributed across the device's field of view, this raises an opportunity: the way a patient searches for and responds to these stimuli may itself carry information about the size and shape of their visual field, without the need to administer a separate, dedicated VF test. This work is motivated by this possibility. We propose a novel Passive Visual Field Measurement (PVFM) method for VR that estimates a user's visual field purely from the position and timing of their natural search-and-click behavior towards targets distributed throughout the virtual scene, without requiring the patient to fixate a specific marker and without a need for eye-tracking integration. Because the method has no fixed requirements regarding the visual scene beyond the presence of variably positioned targets to interact with and an incentive to find them quickly, it can, in principle, run passively in the background of a variety of gamified VR tasks, including visual rehabilitation exercises. This would allow to combine visual field monitoring and visual rehabilitation within a single, interactive and accessible VR experience, enabling patients to track changes in their visual field over time as an unobtrusive byproduct of tasks they are already motivated to perform.

The comparison is not intended as a test of medical validity or to replace standard kinetic perimetry for clinical diagnostics, but rather as a feasibility and design study with the goal of exploring whether VR based, eye-tracking-free visual field testing can provide patients with a simple and accessible way to monitor their own visual field.

Development of the passive visual field measurement

The PVFM tool was developed using the Unity 3D engine (Version 6000.2.6f2) and was integrated with a VR based search task developed in the same engine. The visual task and the PVFM tool are closely connected, but each focuses on a different aspect: The visual task is designed to encourage interactions with the virtual scene, whereas the PVFM tool uses the input data of these interactions -time and relative positions -to determine visual field boundaries.

Visual task design

The visual task features a single target, positioned randomly within a specified spawn area in front of the patient (Fig 1). Users are instructed to search the area for the target and -once they spot it -select it through point-and-click action with the VR controller. As soon as the target is selected, it disappears and a new target immediately spawns at a different position, repeating the process. The spawn area -the area in which the targets may appear -is a circular, spherically curved field at four meters distance from the user's viewpoint in the virtual environment. Its size R spawn , i.e. the maximum eccentricity from the patient's forward-facing direction at which targets could appear, could be set manually in advance of the session. During the experiments, this feature was used to prevent patients with small visual fields from having to search a large area, which would increase the average search time drastically. Passive Visual Field Measurement Tool

The aim for the PVFM tool is to predict the visual field of a patient based on only their interactions within the VR scene, without forcing patients into unnatural or non-intuitive behavior such as having to fixate the gaze on a central marker. Thus, the calculation of the visual field must be based on parameters that can be extracted from such natural interaction alone. Specifically, for the PVFM, two interaction parameters are used: Relative position between two consecutive interactions, and time between those two interactions. The relative position is further divided into two separate parameters -the direction and the magnitude of the vector between the two targets.

Given the spherical nature of visual field measurements, where distances are usually measured in visual degree rather than any linear unit of length, we will refer to the two positional parameters as radial angle for the direction and angular distance for the magnitude of the visual angle between the two. Lastly, the time between two interactions will be referred to as response time. Figure 2(a) shows a visual explanation of the radial angle and angular distance parameters. The core concept of the PVFM algorithm to predict the VF builds on the following assumption: If, after an interaction with one target, the next target is already within the patient's VF, the response time will likely be short, since the patient can directly focus and select the second target without having to search for it. Providing that, the VF area will be distinguished by samples with significantly shorter response times than the samples outside the VF. Thus, the response time of samples at different positions in the tested field of view may provide sufficient information to predict the VF.

As stated in the first Chapter Introduction, the results of a kinetic perimetry measurement are commonly represented in the form of an isopter field, an approximate outline of the VF area [7]. This representation makes the VF easy to visually interpret and is therefore one of the standard representations in clinical visual field diagnostics.

Thus, the aim for the PVFM is to provide such an isopter field. This poses a challenge, because unlike kinetic perimetry, PVFM does not provide inherent boundary points of the visual field. However, given the previous assumption regarding response times being significantly shorter within the VF than without, it can be assumed that the boundaries of the visual field mark a steep transition from short to long response times. Thus, by finding where these transitions happen, the VF boundaries can be predicted. In the following, we will explore the method applied.

In a first step, all samples of one measurement set (representing all samples for one eye in one patient) are divided into sections -a process called "binning" -based on their radial angle. Specifically, each sample is assigned to the closest 30° meridian, e.g. samples from -15° to 15° are assigned to the 0° meridian, samples from 15° to 45° are assigned to the 30° meridian, and so forth. The choice of binning samples into twelve 30° segments was made as it is in line with the 8-16 meridians commonly tested in standard kinetic perimetry [8, 29]. A higher number of segments would increase the number of required samples whereas a smaller number would reduce the accuracy of the visual representation of results.

Next, samples in each bin are sorted based on their angular distance. By going through all samples from smallest to largest angular distance and assessing the response times for each one, it can be assumed that there will be a noticeable change in response time at the angular distance where the seeing field transitions into a non-seeing fieldi.e. where the boundary of the visual field is. Similarly, peripheral windows of remaining vision will be characterized by a decrease in response time at the eccentricity of the inner (more central) edge of the window, followed by yet another increase at the outer edge. Specifically, samples of each meridian are analyzed in order of eccentricity, starting at the most central sample. If a sample's response time lies above the threshold, it is considered a potential candidate for a visual field boundary. To confirm, a second condition must be met: The median of the next five samples in order must also be above the threshold, ensuring that the current sample is not just an outlier. If both conditions are met, a boundary line from seeing to non-seeing area is marked at the average angular distance between the last sample considered within the VF and the first sample considered without the VF (see 'Predicted Visual Field Boundary' in Figure 3). The window size of five samples were selected empirically during initial testing, as a compromise between robustness to noise and sample size limitations. The median window must be large enough that isolated outliers do not trigger a false boundary. A larger window, however, October 27, 2026 7/24 would become impractical given the limited number of samples per meridian. An odd value was chosen so that the median is always a single, unambiguous sample value.

It is possible -in case of non-concentric visual fields or windows of visual field restthat there are multiple visual field boundaries along a meridian. Thus, the analysis of samples' response times along their order of eccentricity is continued even after finding an initial boundary. When at least three consecutive samples fall below the threshold, the average eccentricity between the last sample above the threshold and the first of the samples below the threshold is marked as a boundary point between non-seeing and seeing area, and the initial conditions for finding a boundary from seeing to non-seeing area apply once again. Again, this condition of requiring three consecutive below-threshold samples was selected empirically during initial testing. It needs to avoid false boundary detection from accidental fast responses (which can happen quite regularly if the patient happens to move their gaze in the right direction by chance) while still allowing narrow islands of preserved vision to be detected. Once all predicted boundary points have been calculated for each of the twelve meridians, the isopter field -representing the predicted visual field of the patient -can be created by connecting boundary points of adjacent meridians. This provides the desired visual representation of the visual field analogous to the results of kinetic perimetry.

An additional adjustment factor was added, increasing the threshold with increasing eccentricity. This addition was motivated by two known effects on response time: First, reaction time naturally increases with the eccentricity of the stimulus -approximately at a rate of 1.8ms/deg eccentricity , as determined in preliminary studies such as by Schiefer et. al. [33]. Second, response time is also affected by the time needed to move and orient the VR controller, which likewise increases with angular distance. The combined effect of eccentricity on response time was approximated for each individual patient. The factor by which the response time threshold increases is calculated as simple linear regression between the angular distance as independent variable and response time as dependent variable, based on the first 10 samples of each meridian in both eyes. Outlier samples not within the MAD-based capping-threshold described in Equation 1 were not included for regression calculations. This eccentricity-based threshold adjustment was applied to increase the accuracy of the detected visual field boundaries, in particular for peripheral visual field rests where the effect of an eccentricity-adjusted threshold is strongest. This factor was calculated only for the final boundary detection, as it relies on sufficient reference samples for the estimate.

The study aimed to compare the VF predicted by the PVFM to the VF measured using conventional kinetic perimetry as well as to gather patient feedback on the usability of and experience with the PVFM tool.

For a more quantitative comparison, however, the Intersection over Union (IoU) was calculated. IoU describes the ratio between the intersection and the union of two areas and is a common metric for evaluating the similarity between two shapes. Two different IoU variants were applied to the results.

A pixel-wise IoU compares the image output of the two VF areas pixel by pixel, dividing the number of pixels n intersect shared by both VF areas by the number of pixels n union contained in at least one of them, so IoU pixel = nintersect nunion . While this represents the most common, cartesian-coordinate based IoU approach, it can be argued that it does not adequately reflect the nature of VF data -since VFs are inherently radial in structure, pixel-wise IoU disproportionately weights peripheral regions, which cover a much larger pixel area than central regions of equal angular extent. For this reason, VF October 27, 2026 11/24 areas are typically reported as square degrees [38, 39], using a radial rather than cartesian coordinate system.

To accommodate this, a second, radial-weighted (or square-degree-wise) IoU was calculated. Here, pixels are weighted by a factor ω weight = 1 reccentricity , ensuring that each square degree (defined as 1° radialAngle • 1° angularDistance ) has the same influence on the IoU. An alternative way to conceptualize this radial-weighted IoU is as a measure of the average difference in radius length between the two VFs along any given meridian.

Both IoU methods have merit for comparing the two VF results, depending on the context in which the results are interpreted. Pixel-wise IoU allows comparison of the raw area in which any vision exists, independent of its position within the field of view, while radial-weighted IoU more accurately reflects the comparison of actual perception, as well as changes or differences in VF size.

Discussion

Visual field tests

The comparison of VF boundaries (Figure 4) shows that PVFM testing yields promising results for concentric, central visual fields, which are common in late-stage retinitis pigmentosa or glaucoma. However, PVFM performs worse at detecting peripheral areas of remaining vision, showing noticeably smaller VFs than kinetic perimetry.

To put these VF results in context with existing literature, Grobbel et al. [41] report that for kinetic perimetry on the Octopus 900, test-retest variability averaged 1°-3° for the III4e stimulus setting at a speed of 5°/s -the same settings used in this study.

Similarly, a study from Mönter et al. [8] found a mean test-retest variability of 1.3° using the Octopus 900 perimeter in a group of moderately advanced glaucoma patients, and Niederhauser et al. [6] report an intra-patient test-retest standard deviation of around 1° for Goldmann kinetic perimetry.

While the exact methodology of these studies differs from ours, limiting the comparability, the agreement between PVFM and kinetic perimetry for mostly concentric VFs -which ranged from 0.31° to 2.68° -falls within the range of test-retest variability reported in the literature above. This suggests that PVFM may be capable of providing VF predictions for concentric central VFs at the level of clinically conventional methods.

However, as noted earlier, one patient with a non-concentric VF showed substantial differences between methods, with mean absolute angular distances of 17.5°-22.3°, far exceeding the test-retest variability reported in the literature. Chapter Limitations and technical considerations will highlight potential reasons -and possible solutions -for this strong discrepancy in results. It should further be noted that, according to the kinetic perimetry results, only a single patient in the study population had remaining peripheral vision. Results for this VF type are therefore based on a sample size of one, October 27, 2026 15/24 October 27, 2026 16/24 and further testing is needed to better understand PVFM's capabilities in this context and to explore possible optimizations.

It should also be noted that for any VF test, the ground truth of a patient's visual field is unknown. New methods can only be compared against other methods such as kinetic perimetry. While these methods are well-established in the clinical context, it cannot be said with certainty how closely the measurement reflects the true visual field. Accordingly, not every deviation of the PVFM measurement from the kinetic perimetry measurement necessarily reflects a lack of accuracy in the PVFM method.

Learnings from the acquired data

The initial configuration parameters for the PVFM measurement -including the number of samples required per meridian, the threshold-based boundary detection method, and its individual parameter values -were partly based on preliminary internal testing using simulated visual field defects of varying sizes. These simulations followed the approach of Neugebauer et al. [4], who showed that the performance of visually healthy individuals with a simulated peripheral visual field defect approximately matches that of real patients in VR-based visual tasks, including visual search. The sample data from actual patients acquired in this pilot study now allows for a more accurate and informed selection of PVFM configuration settings for future studies. The following sections discuss what was learned from this sample data regarding these configuration choices.

A major factor in the prediction of VF boundaries with the PVFM method is the boundary threshold calculation method. While post-experimental modification of evaluation methods is generally discouraged, since it risks "cherry-picking" the method best suited to the exact samples acquired and thereby falsely suggesting higher accuracy than should be expected for a different sample set, the data acquired in this pilot study nonetheless offers an opportunity to learn about potentially better-suited sample sizes and VF boundary detection methods.

A common method for threshold-based detection is the z-score method, in which a sample is considered within the threshold if its distance from the reference mean falls below a certain factor of the reference standard deviation, with typical factors being 2, 2.5, or 3 [32]. This method failed to provide good results during preliminary internal testing, and the same trend holds true for the actual experimental sample data, even when using outlier-robust methods analogous to those described in Chapter Passive Visual Field Measurement Tool. It is found that the threshold varies substantially between patients -even relative to their range of response times. This occurs because some patients show very consistent response times for samples with small angular distance (used as reference samples), while others show much higher variance in response times for those same samples. Since these values are not proportional to the overall spread in response time, they lead to over-or under-estimation of the boundary threshold. These findings underpin the choice of a variance-independent threshold calculation method.

Having thus further solidified the selection of the pre-experimentally defined threshold calculation of t boundary = 1.5 • robust mean (Equation 2), a question remains how a higher or lower factor than 1.5 -resulting in a more or less sensitive VF area detection -would influence results. Again, the factor of 1.5 was chosen based on pre-experimental, simulated test samples, whereas the optimal method might differ for real patient sample data. Thus, several configuration variants of the PVFM algorithm were tested against the sample data to address this. These include varying the threshold factor to 1.33, 2.0, and 2.5. While the factor did influence the predicted VF boundaries, none of these values showed a clear improvement over the originally selected factor of 1.5. Smaller factors -i.e., lower thresholds -led to earlier detection of VF boundaries October 27, 2026 17/24 and thus smaller VFs, while larger factors -i.e., higher thresholds -led to larger VF sizes and detection of peripheral VF windows inconsistent with both kinetic perimetry and subjective interpretations of the visualized sample data, thus likely representing false-positive detections. As such, no evidence was found that adjusting the threshold factor by a fixed value would improve the quality of the PVFM method.

The tested calculation methods are not all-encompassing. Other approaches, such as different forms of cluster-based segmentation [42], also hold promise for this evaluation. However, implementing, testing, and comparing each of these methods is beyond the scope of this work, though it lends itself as a focus of future studies.

Beyond the boundary detection method, the sample data also provides insight into the sufficiency of the number of samples captured per set. As shown by the accuracy trend relative to sample count in Figure 5, a fraction of at least 0.8 -corresponding to roughly 300 samples per measurement, or about 25 samples per meridian -is recommended to achieve any meaningful result with the tested PVFM method, as further reducing the sample count may lead to a drastic reduction in accuracy. In other words, 25 samples per meridian may represent the best compromise between time and accuracy for the PVFM method, if time is a consideration. The average IoU across all measurement sets suggests a saturation curve between 50% and 100% of the samples, indicating that while increases in sample size beyond 30 samples per meridian may further improve alignment with conventional VF tests, the effect would likely be minor.

Limitations and technical considerations

A major limitation of PVFM identified in this study is its low alignment with conventional VF testing in peripheral VF areas, based on the results of the one patient displaying such areas. Specifically, large portions of the peripheral VF detected by kinetic perimetry were not detected by PVFM. This discrepancy can be explained by two potential factors: First, the target in kinetic perimetry is moving, whereas targets in PVFM are static. Since the human eye has specialized mechanisms for detecting motion [43], moving targets can be assumed to be easier to detect than static ones. This suggests that introducing movement into the visual representation of PVFM targets -such as jitter or small back-and-forth motion that largely preserves the relative position between targets while still triggering motion-sensitive perception -could improve detection in future implementations.

Second, the limited screen brightness of the applied VR headset -the Meta Quest Pro -at 100 cd/m² did not allow for the target brightness to fully match that used in kinetic perimetry, which for the III4e condition is 318 cd/m² [8]. Degenerative visual field loss, as caused by conditions such as retinitis pigmentosa or glaucoma, does not always result in complete loss of vision in affected areas. Instead, it can gradually reduce light sensitivity, such that bright stimuli remain perceivable while dimmer stimuli are no longer detected. The brightness difference between the two target stimuli may therefore have caused difference in peripheral VF area detection between the two methods. This technical limitation of VR based VF testing has been reported in other studies [44] [45] [46]. As this is an inherent hardware limitation of the VR devices used, no straightforward solution exists for PVFM within the current setup. Sauer et al. [14] tested the maximum brightness of several VR headsets and found only one, the HTC Vive, scoring notably higher than 100 cd/m² at 180 cd/m². However, restricting PVFM to a single VR device model would substantially reduce its flexibility and accessibility, particularly for at-home use. As such, limited display brightness remains an open challenge for the PVFM method.

Opportunities and limitations of practical implementation

One goal of PVFM is flexibility for integration with a range of VR-based interactive tasks, ideally in a way that allows the PVFM to run as a background process without affecting the overlaying task design and without putting any restrictions on user behavior.

The main factor supporting this goal is the PVFM's minimal input requirements.

All data the method needs to predict VF boundaries -relative target position and response time -can be captured from natural scene interactions, without artificial or unintuitive constraints on the patient. Detecting and interacting with elements in a scene is an inherently natural process. PVFM does require patients to be encouraged to interact with targets as soon as they detect them, so that response time approximately reflects search duration. However, this kind of time-based challenge is already a standard feature of gamified tasks and training paradigms and is an intuitive driverjust informing a person that a task will be timed is usually enough for them to be inherently encouraged to complete the task quickly.

That said, while the user-facing demands of PVFM are minimal, successfully implementing it does impose several constraints on task design, which are listed in the following paragraphs.

The first and strongest is the requirement of randomized target positioning -or in other words, a visual-search component. With positionally fixed elements, such as a graphical user interface with static layout, response time would be prone to be influenced by users memorizing the locations of individual elements, undermining the method's core assumption of response time reflecting visual search durations.

Second, to keep patients interacting with targets in rapid succession -which is what makes response time a valid proxy for detection speed -target interactions must remain cognitively simple, free of tasks like reading or strategic planning. This makes PVFM best suited to fast-paced, action-oriented gamified tasks, and less compatible with puzzle-based or slow, deliberative ones.

Third, to resolve visual field boundaries for each eye independently, targets must be presented to one VR display at a time, ensuring that any given target is perceived by only one eye. This is a fairly specific design requirement, since most tasks are not built with monocular presentation in mind. It does not preclude integration with most game tasks -task mechanics are typically independent of whether content is shown to one or both eyes -but it does require a non-trivial modification to the visual pipeline of most candidate tasks.

Beyond these hard requirements, several additional design choices can improve PVFM's effectiveness without being strictly necessary. First, the number of simultaneous targets should be kept low. Although PVFM places no absolute restriction on target count, a higher target count raises the likelihood that a target will be detected and selected quickly even when it falls outside a patient's true VF range from a prior interaction. This narrows the response-time gap between within-VF and outside-VF samples, making boundary estimation less reliable. Second, although PVFM can theoretically accommodate target movement, the distance a target travels between initial detection and interaction -i.e., over the course of the reaction time -needs to be accounted for in the boundary detection process. Third, the visual presentation of the scene, including both target appearance and background, should remain consistent across measurements. Low target-background contrast or visually cluttered backgrounds can substantially distort boundary predictions.

PVFM was designed specifically to detect VF boundaries in peripheral visual field loss, as seen in conditions such as retinitis pigmentosa or glaucoma. Its applicability to other VF defect patterns -such as hemianopia (one-sided visual field loss) or age-related macular degeneration (central visual field loss) -has not been evaluated in October 27, 2026 19/24 this work. While the underlying response-time-based threshold detection may generalize independently of VF shape, adapting it to these patterns would likely require modifications to the threshold-calculation method, and would also affect which visual tasks are appropriate for PVFM integration.

A last consideration for practical implementation of PVFM is the time factor. The findings of this pilot study suggest that the PVFM method takes a considerable amount of time, 10 or even 20 minutes per eye. As shown in Figure 5 and discussed in Chapter , a reduction in sample size is not advisable, as it would likely decrease the accuracy of the measurement. However, unlike kinetic perimetry and other "active" VF measurement methods, PVFM can potentially be embedded in tasks that patients are already motivated to perform, such as rehabilitation or preventive training exercises. In that case, the samples needed for VF prediction could be collected in the background, at no additional time cost to the user. The duration of PVFM therefore remains a design challenge for future work, but it becomes a minor concern if the method is integrated into an existing task environment.

Conclusion

In this pilot study, we investigated the feasibility of predicting the visual field of patients with peripheral visual field defect through natural interactions in a virtual reality based visual task, without restricting the patients' gaze behavior or forcing other non-intuitive behavior. The developed Passive Visual Field Measurement method was capable of predicting visual field boundaries of mostly concentric visual fields with an accuracy of 0.32° to 2.68° mean angular distance compared to conventional automated kinetic perimetry testing, approximately matching the test-retest variance of those clinical visual field tests. However, for peripheral visual field areas of non-concentric visual fields, the developed method shows weak alignment with the kinetic perimetry reference. Overall, while the small study population (n=4) mandates further testing to statistically validate our findings, these preliminary results suggest that Passive Visual Field Measurement is a promising, accessible, intuitive, and engaging virtual reality based tool for monitoring peripheral visual field defects. However, improving detection in peripheral visual field areas remains a key challenge for future work.
