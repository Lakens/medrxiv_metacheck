Reporting p values imprecisely (e.g., p < .05) reduces transparency, reproducibility, and re-use (e.g., in p value meta-analyses). Best practice is to report exact p-values with three decimal places (e.g., p = .032) unless p values are smaller than 0.001, in which case you can use p < .001.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (e.g., “decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

The APA manual states: Report exact p values (e.g., p = .031) to two or three decimal places. However, report p values less than .001 as p < .001. However, 2 decimals is too imprecise for many use-cases (e.g., a p value meta-analysis), so report p values with three digits.

To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 3 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Metacheck does not yet analyze automatically whether sentences which include non-significant p-values are correct, but we recommend manually checking the sentences below for possible misinterpreted non-significant p values.

Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Background: Variability in low-density lipoprotein (LDL-C) response to popular diets is well documented, but the factors contributing to this variability remain unclear. We examined whether genetic predisposition to high LDL-C impacts the effects of healthy low-carbohydrate (HLC) and healthy low-fat (HLF) diets on LDL-C. Methods: The Diet Intervention Examining the Factors Interacting with Treatment Success (DIETFITS) trial randomized 609 adults (ages 18-50 years; body mass index 28-40 kg/m 2 ; not on lipid-lowering therapy) to a HLC or HLF diet for 12 months. This secondary analysis included 431 genotyped participants (HLC, n=223; HLF, n=208) with 24-hour recall and LDL-C data at baseline and 6 months. The primary exposure was a genome-wide polygenic score for LDL-C (PGSLDL).

The outcomes were 6-month change in LDL-C, LDL-C increase versus decrease, and new-onset hypercholesterolemia (LDL-C ≥130 mg/dL). Associations were evaluated using multivariable logistic and linear regression adjusted for baseline LDL-C, weight change, and other factors.

Results: Mean baseline LDL-C was 116 mg/dL (SD, 26.1) in the HLC group and 111 mg/dL (SD, 30.6) in the HLF group. On average, LDL-C increased on the HLC diet (+4.7% [SD,19]) and decreased on the HLF diet (-2.3% [SD, 22]), with substantial variability for both. In the HLC group, higher PGSLDL was associated with risk for LDL-C increase (OR per 1-SD, 1.43; 95% CI, 1.02-2.05) and new hypercholesterolemia (OR per 1-SD, 2.27; 95% CI, 1.18-4.81). Similar effects on LDL-C lowering were not seen for HLF. In both, change in saturated fat intake was associated with change in LDL-C, independent of PGSLDL, but this association was modified by PGSLDL only in the HLC arm (P for interaction <0.0001).

. Conclusions: Risk for elevated LDL-C on a HLC diet is greater for individuals with genetic propensity to high LDL-C. This effect may be partly attributed to heightened sensitivity to saturated fat within a low-carbohydrate diet.

Trial Registration: clinicaltrials.gov ID: NCT01826591.

Introduction

The cardiometabolic effects of dietary fat and carbohydrate have long been debated, with common diets centered on modifying these macronutrients (1). Low-carbohydrate diets, which replace carbohydrates predominantly with fat, have gained popularity for weight loss and glycemic control (2, 3). On average, low-carbohydrate diets tend to raise low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), an effect attributed in part to the accompanying increases in saturated fat intake (4).

However, this effect varies substantially from person to person, fueling debate over the risks and benefits of this dietary pattern (4). Identifying the factors that drive this heterogeneity is therefore central to understanding the cardiovascular implications of low-carbohydrate diets. Genetic variation may help explain why individuals differ in their LDL-C response to dietary change. Several dietary trials have evaluated whether individual genetic variants modify this response. Variants in genes involved in lipoprotein metabolism have been associated with differential lipid responses to changes in dietary fat or carbohydrate intake (5) (6) (7) (8) (9) (10) (11) (12). However, findings have been inconsistent. For example, some studies have reported greater LDL-C reductions among APOE ε4 carriers following low-fat (5-7), or low-saturated fat diets (8), while others have found no such modification (13, 14). Collectively, these studies suggest that genetic variation contributes to lipid responses to diet. However, because LDL-C is a highly polygenic trait, individual variants capture only a small part of the genetic contribution to dietary response, and their modest effects may contribute to the conflicting findings. Whether overall genetic predisposition influences the LDL-C response to dietary interventions remains unclear.

Polygenic scores aggregate the effects of many variants across the genome into a single measure, providing a framework for evaluating whether overall genetic predisposition shapes the lipid response to dietary interventions. We recently showed in the Women's Health Initiative that the association between saturated fat intake and LDL-C was stronger among individuals with a higher polygenic score for LDL-C (15). In agreement, a smaller observational study suggested that a polygenic score for LDL-C may modify the effects of low-carbohydrate, high-fat diets on LDL-C (16). Importantly, however, this hypothesis has not been tested in a randomized dietary intervention setting. Establishing whether overall genetic predisposition to high LDL-C identifies individuals who experience greater LDL-C increases on a low-carbohydrate diet could help inform the potential cardiovascular implications of this widely adopted dietary pattern.

Here, we use data from the Diet Intervention Examining the Factors Interacting with Treatment Success (DIETFITS) randomized clinical trial (17) and a validated, genome-wide, multi-ancestry polygenic score for LDL-C (PGSLDL) (18) to test whether polygenic background influenced the LDL-C response to the healthy low-carbohydrate (HLC) and healthy low-fat (HLF) diet interventions.

Methods

Study design

The DIETFITS trial was a single-center, parallel-group randomized controlled trial that enrolled 609 adults aged 18 to 50 years with overweight or obesity (body mass index [BMI], 28-40 kg/m 2 ). The trial design, protocol and interventions have been described previously (17, 19). Participants attended 22 diet-specific behavioral intervention sessions led by registered dietitians. Sessions were held weekly for 8 weeks, biweekly for the subsequent 2 months, every 3 weeks through month 6, and monthly thereafter until the end of the trial. During the initial 8 weeks, participants were instructed to reduce the net carbohydrate or fat intake to 20 grams per day or less, according to their assigned diet, maintaining the lowest level possible for at least one week, followed by gradual increases until each participant found an intake level that they felt they could sustain long term. Both interventions emphasized diet quality, including increased consumption of vegetables and whole foods and reduced intake of processed products, trans fats, added sugars, and refined grains. Neither intervention prescribed explicit energy restriction. Study assessments were conducted at baseline, 3, 6 and 12 months.

The present secondary analysis focused on changes from baseline to 6 months, selected a priori because dietary adherence and participant retention were greater at 6 months than at 12 months (17). The participant flow-chart is shown in Figure 1. Of the 609 trial participants, 585 had genotyping data. We excluded related individuals at the second degree or closer (n = 11), those whose genetically inferred ancestry groups were too small to support reliable ancestry-specific PGSLDL normalization (n = 4). We further excluded those with missing baseline or 6-month LDL-C measurements (n = 126) or dietary data (n = 13). The final analytic sample comprised 431 individuals, including 223 randomized to the HLC intervention and 208 randomized to the HLF intervention.

Dietary intake and clinical measures

Dietary intake was assessed using three unannounced 24-hour recalls, including two weekdays and one weekend day, using a standardized multiple-pass method (20). Recalls were processed using the Nutrient Data System for Research software (Nutrition Coordinating Center, University of Minnesota, Minneapolis, MN) to obtain nutrient-level data (21). Diet quality was assessed using the Healthy Eating Index-2010 (HEI-2010), calculated for each recall following standard scoring procedures (22, 23). All dietary variables, including macronutrient intake and diet quality scores, were averaged across the three recalls at each time point.

Clinical assessments, including body weight measures and blood collection, were performed by a nurse or phlebotomist at the Stanford Clinical and Translational Research Unit (Stanford University School of Medicine, California) at each study visit. Blood samples were collected after a 10-12 hour fast at each time point. Lipid measurements were performed by the Krauss Laboratory at the Children's Hospital Oakland Research Institute. Plasma triglycerides, total cholesterol and high-density lipoprotein cholesterol were measured by enzymatic point analysis on a clinical chemistry analyzer (Liasys 330). LDL-C concentrations were calculated using the Friedewald equation (24). Data collectors were blinded to the participants' assigned diet intervention.

Genotyping data and polygenic score calculation

Genotyping was performed using the Affymetrix UK Biobank Axiom microarray. Genetic relatedness was estimated using KING (25), and individuals with a kinship coefficient > 0.0884, indicative of a second-degree or closer relatedness, were excluded (Figure 1). Imputation was performed using the Trans-Omics for Precision Medicine (TOPMed) imputation server, with TOPMed version r3 as the reference panel (26). Genetic ancestry was inferred using the pgsc_calc pipeline (27), which projects principal components and assigns individuals to ancestry groups based on genetic similarity to the 1000 Genomes Project Phase 3 superpopulations (28). We used a previously validated multi-ancestry genome-wide PGSLDL (PGS000889) (18), calculated using pgsc_calc (27). Of the 9,009 autosomal variants included in the original score, 8,488 (94.2%) were available in the study cohort and used to calculate the score. The PGSLDL was adjusted for genetic ancestry using the 1000 Genomes reference panel and the eMERGE consortium approach (29), which accounts for differences in score means and variances across ancestry groups. Coefficients are expressed per 1-SD change in the ancestry-normalized PGSLDL.

Statistical analyses

All analyses were stratified by intervention arm. Continuous variables are presented as means and standard deviations, and categorical variables as percentages. Changes in LDL-C and dietary variables from baseline to 6 months were calculated as 6-month minus baseline values.

Percent change in LDL-C was calculated as the difference between 6-month and baseline values divided by the baseline value and multiplied by 100. PGSLDL performance in the study population was evaluated using regression coefficients and incremental R² (coefficient of determination), with 95% confidence intervals estimated via percentile bootstrapping. Adequate ancestry normalization of the PGSLDL was assessed using a chi-squared test to confirm that the PGSLDL was not associated with genetically inferred ancestry.

Multivariable logistic regression was used to evaluate the associations between the PGSLDL and 6-month change in LDL-C, categorized as an increase versus decrease in the HLC arm and a decrease versus increase in the HLF arm. Among those with normal LDL-C at baseline, we also evaluated the association between PGSLDL and new-onset hypercholesterolemia, defined as LDL-C ≥130 mg/dL at 6 months. Models were adjusted for age, sex, baseline LDL-C, 6-month change in weight, 6-month change in saturated fat intake, and genetic principal components 1-3.

Multivariable linear regression was used to evaluate the associations of change in saturated fat intake (as percent of total daily energy) and PGSLDL with change in LDL-C (modeled continuously) and to assess whether the PGSLDL modified the association between change in saturated fat intake and change in LDL-C. Additive and interaction models were examined with PGSLDL modeled both continuously and categorically using tertiles. The additive models included main effects for change in saturated fat intake and PGSLDL, whereas the interaction models additionally included a multiplicative interaction term between these predictors. Interaction Pvalues were derived from likelihood ratio tests comparing models with and without the interaction term. To characterize the association between change in saturated fat intake and change in LDL-C within each PGSLDL tertile, tertile-specific slopes and 95% confidence intervals were estimated using the emtrends() function in the emmeans R package (version 2.0.3). All models were adjusted for age, sex, baseline LDL-C, change in weight, and genetic principal components 1-3.

The incremental R² was estimated to quantify the variance in LDL-C response explained by change in saturated fat intake, PGSLDL, and their interaction. For each main predictor (change in saturated fat intake and PGSLDL), the incremental R² was calculated as the difference in R² between models with and without the predictor of interest, with both models adjusted for the other predictor and base covariates (age, sex, baseline LDL-C, change in weight, and genetic principal components 1-3). For the interaction term, the incremental R² was calculated as the difference in R² between the interaction model and the corresponding additive model. The 95% confidence intervals for incremental R² were estimated via percentile bootstrapping.

We conducted several sensitivity analyses to assess the robustness of our findings. First, we re-ran all models with additional adjustment for changes in diet quality and dietary adherence.

Change in diet quality was assessed using a modified HEI-2010 that excluded the fatty acid and solid fat components, which capture saturated fat intake that was already included in the models. Dietary adherence was represented using a binary variable, with high adherence defined as a change in grams of carbohydrate intake (HLC) or fat intake (HLF) below the group-specific median (i.e., a greater decrease) and low adherence defined as a change greater than or equal to the group-specific median. The median 6-month changes, which served as respective cutoffs, were -139.4 grams of carbohydrate in the HLC group and -36.3 grams of fat in the HLF group. Second, we re-ran all models restricted to individuals with genetically inferred European ancestry, as this was the largest ancestry group in which the PGSLDL is known to perform best (18), to assess whether ancestry differences may have influenced the associations. Third, we examined interactions between the PGSLDL and changes in individual saturated fatty acids, stratified by chain length, focusing on saturated fatty acids that contributed more than 1% of total daily energy at baseline. To account for multiple testing burden, a significant interaction for this analysis was defined as a P < 0.017 (0.05/3 fatty acids tested). Fourth, we evaluated interactions between the PGSLDL and changes in other macronutrients, including changes in unsaturated fat, carbohydrate and protein. Similarly, a Bonferroni-correction was applied, with significant interactions defined as P < 0.0125 (0.05/4 macronutrients). Statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.3.3 (The R Foundation for Statistical Computing). Statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05 unless otherwise specified.

Results

Cohort characteristics

Of 609 randomized participants, 431 were included in this secondary analysis, with 223 in the HLC group and 208 in the HLF group. Baseline characteristics were broadly similar between diet groups (Table 1). The mean age was 40.2 (SD, 6.7) years in the HLC group and 39.2 (SD, 6.7) years in the HLF group, and mean BMI was 33.1 (SD, 3.4) and 33.5 (SD, 3.4) kg/m 2 , respectively. In both groups, more than half of the participants were female, self-identified as White, and had genetically inferred European ancestry, and more than three-quarters had a college degree or higher.

Detailed dietary intake at baseline and 6 months, as well as changes over this period, are shown in Supplementary Table 1. Baseline dietary intake was similar between diet groups, with approximately 44%, 35% and 17% of total daily calories from carbohydrate, fat and protein, respectively, in both. Consistent with diet assignment, at 6 months, mean carbohydrate intake decreased to 26% of calories in the HLC group, and mean total fat intake decreased to 27% of calories in the HLF group. Mean baseline LDL-C was 116 mg/dL (SD, 26.1) in the HLC group and 111 mg/dL (SD, 30.6) in the HLF group (Table 1). The PGSLDL explained 15-17% of the variance in baseline LDL-C (Supplementary Table 2), consistent with its expected predictive performance (18). The score was not predictive of genetically inferred ancestry group, supporting adequate ancestry normalization (Supplementary Table 3).

Association between the polygenic score and LDL-C response

At 6 months, LDL-C increased by 4.7% (SD, 19) in the HLC group and decreased by 2.3% (SD, 22) in the HLF group. However, individual LDL-C responses were highly heterogeneous within each diet group. Percent changes in LDL-C ranged from a 26% decrease to a 126% increase in the HLC group and from a 53% decrease to a 125% increase in the HLF group (Figure 2).

We evaluated whether the PGSLDL predicted the direction of LDL-C change in response to each diet, and among participants with normal baseline LDL-C (<130 mg/dL), we assessed whether the PGSLDL predicted the development of hypercholesterolemia. On the HLC diet, 127 (57%) participants experienced an LDL-C increase. The risk for LDL-C increase was 43% higher for each 1-SD increase in PGSLDL (OR, 1.43; 95% CI, 1.02-2.05; P = 0.04). Among 151 participants in the HLC group with normal baseline LDL-C (<130 mg/dL), 23 (15.2%) developed hypercholesterolemia. The risk for hypercholesterolemia on the HLC diet increased 2.27-fold for each 1-SD increase in PGSLDL (OR, 2.27; 95% CI, 1.18-4.81; P = 0.02). These findings remained consistent after additional adjustment for change in diet quality and dietary adherence, and when analyses were restricted to participants of European ancestry, including models with additional adjustment for these variables in the European ancestry subset (Supplementary Table 4).

On the HLF diet, 130 (62.5%) participants experienced an LDL-C decrease, but PGSLDL was not associated with odds of LDL-C decrease (OR, 0.85; 95% CI, 0.59-1.21; P = 0.38). Similar nonsignificant findings were observed after further adjustments for change in diet quality and dietary adherence, as well as when analyses were restricted to the European ancestry subset (Supplementary Table 5). As expected, new hypercholesterolemia was uncommon on the HLF diet, affecting 8 (5.1%) participants of 157 with normal baseline LDL-C, and the PGSLDL was not associated with this change (OR, 1.4; 95% CI, 0.5-4.9; P = 0.52). Of note, given the low incidence of new-onset hypercholesterolemia, this analysis was conducted for comparison purposes only, as the small number of cases limited the ability to meaningfully evaluate this association.

Interaction between the polygenic score and change in saturated fat intake

Given the well-established effect of saturated fat intake on LDL-C (30, 31), we examined the association between change in saturated fat intake and LDL-C response, and whether PGSLDL modified this association. On average, saturated fat intake increased by 3.2 percentage points of total calories in the HLC group and decreased by 3.6 percentage points in the HLF group (Supplementary Table 1). In both groups, change in saturated fat intake was associated with change in LDL-C, independent of PGSLDL (Table 2). However, an interaction between change in saturated fat intake and PGSLDL was observed only in the HLC arm. The interaction term explained 3.3% of the variance in LDL-C response, and the PGSLDL main effect and interaction together explained 10.4% of the variance in LDL-C response to the HLC diet (Table 2).

The observed interaction between change in saturated fat intake and PGSLDL in the HLC group was robust across several sensitivity analyses. First, the interaction remained significant after additional adjustment for change in diet quality and dietary adherence (Interaction β [SE] = 0.68 [0.23]; P = 0.003; Supplementary Table 6). Second, to account for potential ancestry-related differences, we additionally adjusted for ancestry-by-PGSLDL and ancestry-by-change in saturated fat intake interaction terms, which had minimal impact on the change in saturated fat intake interaction-by-PGSLDL estimate (Interaction β [SE] = 0.59 [0.24]; P = 0.01). Findings were also consistent when analyses were restricted to participants of European ancestry (Supplementary Table 7). The same sensitivity analyses in the HLF group consistently provided no evidence of an interaction (Supplementary Tables 6 7).

We then evaluated the change in saturated fat-by-PGSLDL interaction using PGSLDL tertiles.

Consistent with the continuous analysis, the interaction between PGSLDL tertiles and change in saturated fat intake was significant in the HLC group only (HLC P interaction < 0.0001 and HLF P interaction = 0.47; Figure 3). In the HLC group, the effect of increased saturated fat intake on LDL-C increase was attenuated in the lowest PGSLDL tertile and amplified in the highest PGSLDL tertile (Figure 3A). In the HLF group, the effects of decreased saturated fat intake on LDL-C did not differ across PGSLDL tertiles (Figure 3B). These results were consistent in sex-stratified analyses (Figure 3).

Lastly, in the HLC group, we further evaluated whether interactions between the PGSLDL and change in saturated fat intake differed by fatty acid chain length. Of the 11 saturated fatty acids available, we examined the three that each contributed at least 1% of total energy at baseline: myristic acid (C14:0), palmitic acid (C16:0), and stearic acid (C18:0), which together made up 88% of total saturated fat intake (at baseline and 6 months). Baseline and 6 month mean intakes are shown in Supplementary Figure 1A. All three fatty acids showed significant PGSLDL-bychange interactions after Bonferroni correction with similar directions and patterns of associations across tertiles for all three fatty acids (all P interaction < 0.01; Supplementary Figure 1B), consistent to those observed in the total saturated fat analysis (Figure 3A).

Interaction between the polygenic score and changes in other macronutrients

We further evaluated interactions between the PGSLDL and changes in other macronutrients, including unsaturated fat, protein and carbohydrate, on changes in LDL-C. Of these, none showed a significant interaction in either diet group (Supplementary Figure 2). These findings suggest that the observed gene-diet interaction was specific to changes in saturated fat intake in the HLC group.

Discussion

In this secondary analysis of the DIETFITS trial, we found that individuals with a genetic propensity towards high LDL-C were at increased risk for LDL-C elevation and new hypercholesterolemia after 6 months on a HLC diet, independent of baseline LDL-C, weight change, and other factors. Further, some of this risk may be attributed to an interaction between saturated fat intake and polygenic background. In contrast, the LDL-C lowering effects of the HLF diet were less impacted by genetics. Together, these findings suggest that individuals with a genetic propensity towards high LDL-C may be more sensitive to the adverse effects of saturated fat in the context of low-carbohydrate diets.

Our findings extend existing evidence in several important ways. In the UK Biobank, a self-reported low-carbohydrate, high fat dietary pattern (<25% of total energy from carbohydrate and > 45% from fat) was associated with higher LDL-C only among participants in the 20% of polygenic risk for hypercholesterolemia (16). Within this group, severe hypercholesterolemia (LDL-C > 190 mg/dL) was three times more prevalent among those reporting the lowcarbohydrate, high fat diet compared with those reporting a standard diet (16). In the present study, we extend these findings in a randomized dietary intervention that achieved a comparable macronutrient distribution (26% carbohydrate, 47% fat). We found that each standard deviation increase in the polygenic score was associated with more than twice the odds of developing hypercholesterolemia after 6 months on a HLC diet, independent of baseline LDL-C and weight change. Importantly, we further demonstrate that this genetic susceptibility was partly attributable to greater sensitivity to increases in saturated fat intake. These findings are consistent with our recent observational analysis in the Women's Health Initiative, in which we found that the association of statured fat intake and LDL-C was amplified among individuals with higher polygenic risk for elevated LDL-C, with similar patterns extending to future risk of atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (15). Collectively, findings across distinct populations and complementary study designs strengthen the evidence that polygenic background contributes meaningfully to interindividual differences in the LDL-C response to low-carbohydrate diets, in part through genetically driven differences in sensitivity to saturated fat.

The mechanism underlying the presence of genetic effects on the HLC diet is uncertain, but the interaction with saturated fat, together with the absence of interactions with other macronutrients, points to a plausible explanation. Mechanistic studies in animal models have shown that saturated fat intake raises LDL-C by suppressing hepatic LDL receptor activity, thereby reducing receptor-mediated LDL clearance (32) (33) (34). Human tracer studies have similarly reported slower LDL clearance with saturated fat compared to polyunsaturated fat (35), and controlled feeding studies show that reducing saturated fat or replacing it with unsaturated fat upregulates LDL receptor expression in circulating mononuclear cells (14, 36, 37). Polygenic scores for LDL-C capture, in part, common variation at loci governing LDL clearance. Individuals with a higher genetic burden may therefore be less able to compensate for diet-induced reductions in LDL clearance, resulting in a larger increase for a given rise in saturated fat intake. Future studies are needed to understand the pathways through which the PGSLDL operates. Saturated fatty acids vary in carbon chain length and have been reported to have distinct effects on LDL-C. Dietary intervention trials have shown that palmitic and myristic acids generally raise LDL-C when substituted for carbohydrate or unsaturated fat, whereas stearic appears to have little effect (38, 39). In our cohort, palmitic, myristic and stearic made up 88% of saturated fat intake at baseline and 6 months, with palmitic contributing to more than half. In the HLC group, changes in all three fatty acids were associated with increases in LDL-C in the top two tertiles of polygenic risk, but not among those in the lowest tertile. The association of stearic acid was unexpected based on prior evidence (38, 39), and may reflect the substantial overlap in food sources of these fatty acids, particularly meat and dairy, making their individual effects difficult to distinguish in this setting. Further investigation in controlled feeding trials that can better isolate the effects of the individual fatty acids is warranted. Alternatively, examining specific food sources of saturated fat may provide additional insight, as the food matrix in which the fatty acids are consumed may also contribute to their differential effects on LDL-C (40).

In contrast to the HLC arm, the polygenic score did not predict LDL-C response to the HLF diet. This finding is consistent with a prior analysis of a subset of 145 women in the intervention arm of the Dietary Modification Trial of the Women's Health Initiative, in which a main-effect polygenic score for LDL-C did not predict 1-year LDL-C response to a low-fat dietary intervention, whereas a diet-response polygenic score did, explaining 3.7% of the variance in LDL-C change (41). Together, these results suggest that the genetic variants that predispose individuals to higher LDL-C concentrations may not translate proportionally into responsiveness to fat reduction. This responsiveness may be better captured by scores specifically derived from gene-diet interactions. Dietary context may also play a role. Both low-fat dietary interventions were multifactorial and emphasized fiber-rich foods, including fruits, vegetables and whole grains. This dietary pattern is known to support favorable LDL-C levels (30) and may have attenuated genetically mediated responses. Additional research is needed to determine whether overall dietary context shapes the expression of genetic effects on LDL-C response, and whether diet-response polygenic scores offer greater predictive utility across different dietary interventions.

Our findings have important clinical implications. Substantial interindividual variability in LDL-C response to low-carbohydrate diets has been widely documented (4), and our results suggest that this variability is not random but is explained, in part, by polygenic risk for elevated LDL-C. Because conflicting nutrition messages can undermine dietary advice (42, 43), acknowledging that LDL-C responses differ for biologically meaningful reasons may help strengthen clinician-patient trust. Our findings also counter the notion that diet matters little for individuals genetically predisposed to elevated LDL-C. Rather, these individuals appear to be most susceptible to LDL-C increases on a low-carbohydrate diet, in part through heightened sensitivity to saturated fat intake. Because these diets are often adopted for weight loss or glycemic control, rather than lipid management, such LDL-C increases may go unrecognized. Although polygenic scores are not yet ready for clinical use, our findings reinforce recommendations to measure LDL-C before and after initiating low-carbohydrate, high-fat diets (4). For those whose preferences and lifestyles align with this dietary pattern, replacing saturated fat with unsaturated fat remains a guideline-recommended strategy (30, 31).

Our findings should be interpreted in light of several limitations. First, this study was a post hoc analysis of a secondary endpoint of the DIETFITS trial. Second, diet was self-reported, and variable adherence may have contributed to heterogeneity in response. However, adherence at 6 months was generally good, as supported by repeated, unannounced 24-hour recalls (17), and findings remained consistent after adjusting for adherence. Third, although diet assignment was randomized, changes in saturated fat intake within each arm were not. Nonetheless, evaluating within-person changes in the context of randomly assigned interventions reduced the potential for confounding by diet self-selection, time-invariant factors, and reverse causation. Fourth, the study population consisted of individuals with overweight or obesity, which may limit generalizability. This is particularly relevant given evidence that the effect of saturated fat on LDL-C differs between individuals with and without obesity (44). Fifth, both diet interventions emphasized high diet quality, allowing us to evaluate these associations within the context of relatively healthful dietary intake. Whether these findings extend to lower-quality or more extreme versions of these dietary patterns remains unclear. Sixth, most participants were of European ancestry, and the limited sample sizes in other genetic ancestry groups precluded ancestry-stratified analyses, limiting out ability to assess whether findings were consistent across all ancestry groups.

In conclusion, genetic effects help explain the heterogeneity in LDL-C response to a HLC diet. Individuals with a high polygenic score for LDL-C have increased risk for an adverse LDL-C response to this dietary pattern. This risk may be partly explained by heightened sensitivity to saturated fat intake in the context of a low-carbohydrate diet.
