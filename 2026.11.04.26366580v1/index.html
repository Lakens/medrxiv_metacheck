What we found

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). Statcheck might still miss one-sided tests that are described in another sentence, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Purpose: Visual acuity (VA) testing has expanded from examiner-guided to examinee self-guided assessments, yet measurement variability may limit clinical utility. This meta-analysis investigated VA-tests' intertest and test-retest accuracy and precision across different testing strategies. Methods: Following PRISMA guidelines, we searched PubMed, Google Scholar, and Scopus for VA validation studies published between 1 January 1983 to 02 December 2024. Eligible studies reported intertest or test-retest Bland-Altman mean bias and limits of agreement (LoA); objective VA assessments and correlation coefficient only studies were excluded. Testing strategies were classified by test site, optotype sampling methods, response input methods, and level of supervision. Accuracy outcomes were reported as mean biases. Precision outcomes were reported as intertest LoA half-width and test-retest coefficient of repeatability (CoR) for test-retest. Multilevel random-effects models accounted for multiple estimates per study. Risk of bias was assessed using QUADAS-2. Results: Across 82 included studies (10,873 participants), pooled intertest and testretest mean biases were both 0.01 logMAR. Intertest LoA half-width was 0.19 logMAR and test-retest CoR was 0.14 logMAR. Variability showed marginal difference by optotype sampling (adaptive: LoA half-width 0.20 logMAR, CoR 0.15 logMAR; fixed: 0.18, 0.13) or response input method (examiner: 0.19, 0.13; self-run: 0.19, 0.18). Only few studies reported results for test site and level of supervision, hence only intertest was compared (test site, clinic: LoA half-width 0.19 logMAR vs remote 0.27 logMAR and level of supervision, supervised: 0.19 logMAR vs unsupervised 0.26 logMAR). QUADAS-2 identified patient selection as the domain of greatest bias concern. Conclusion: Across testing strategies, VA assessments showed minimal systematic bias but substantial variability. Lack of precision is more notable in remote and unsupervised testing while no difference within optotype sampling and response-input methods. Future development should prioritize on improving precision of visual acuity tests with finer adaptive optotype resolution and remote/unsupervised testings through repeatability studies.

Introduction

Globally, 2.2 billion people have visual impairment, with a substantial impact on development and quality of life (1). Visual acuity (VA) is the primary measure of vision in ophthalmology and optometry (2, 3), underpinning disease monitoring, treatment evaluation, and clinical trial endpoints (4). The Earyl Treatment Diabetic Retinopathy Study (ETDRS) chart is widely used as a reference standard in rigorous clinical research (5). However, routine VA testing remains heterogenous, ranging from conventional charts, such as Snellen and LEA charts, to examiner-guided and selfadministered adaptive digital tests. Software and mobile platforms(6-10) have further extended testing beyond traditional clinical settings, including patient's homes and emergency departments (11) (12) (13). This diversity in chart design, administration, testing environment, and patient characteristics raises a fundamental question: do these approaches yield VA measurements that are reproducible and comparable?

Methodologies in Assessing VA

VA is the primary outcome measure in optometric and ophthalmic clinical care and science. Various ways to measure it have been established since its earliest application (14). Conventionally, VA is measured at a dedicated eyecare facility using a wall-mounted or retro-illuminated chart 6 meters from the observer to reduce the influence of near-accommodation and pupil constriction reflex. The administrator then proctors the exam and scores the performance as smallest legible line as logMAR, decimal, or Snellen notation (5). Modifications in psychophysical procedures, optotypes, viewing distances, and administration strategies permit VA tests to be performed within a clinical environment, or remotely, without administrator's proctoring and scoring (8, 10) This expansion in where VA is measured has been matched by variation in how, namely the psychophysical procedure and scoring convention. Fixed presentation shows every optotype on every line and has fixed size change steps between line of optotypes, e.g., ETDRS 0.10 logMAR. Adaptive procedures instead select each subsequent optotype based on prior response using staircase procedures, Bayesian algorithms, or adaptive charts (10, 15). These tests enable estimations of threshold in a customized way and may have finer test resolution (i.e. smaller step sizes between rendered stimuli (10, 16)) compared to ETDRS's 0.10 logMAR step size. Moreover, these tests may provide additional information about the psychophysical performance compared to chart-based tasks (e.g., slope of psychometric function) (17).

Sources of measurement noise

Test Site

Routinely VA is measured in a dedicated eye-care facility where the testing environment is usually held constant. Testing distance is determined by the room set-up (e.g., fixed distance between the examination chair and eye chart) with ambient illumination and chart luminance maintained within recommend ranges per ISO 8596 guidelines (18) or ETDRS protocol (5, 19). Additionally, glare and visual distractions were usually minimized. Since these conditions are unlikely to change within the same clinic, patients who consistently return to the same clinic for follow-up visits are less subjected to variability introduced by the different environments. However, if test were performed on different clinical sites, variation in any of these settings could contribute to measurement variability.

However, not all VA assessments were performed in an eye-clinic. For example, patients in the emergency department with visual concerns will need to be evaluated for visual acuity. In the United States, passing a driver's license test also require checking of VA at the business office, instead of a dedicate eye clinic. During COVID-19, a surge in development of remote VA testing further allows at-home testing (20, 21). Unlike eye clinics, these testing environments are less consistent. Viewing distance can be established with facial recognition apps (7). Viewing angle, ambient lighting, and screen glare also varies. Screen size, resolution, brightness, and calibration may also vary across devices.

Optotype Sampling

In visual acuity tests, optotype sampling determines how subsequent optotypes are selected. Fixed sampling refers to predetermined sequences of optotypes e.g., letter sequence in ETDRS across lines. In adaptive sampling methods, sequences of letters or orientations of optotypes are randomized, thereby eliminating memorization bias.

Different optotypes used to test patients may require demographic (e.g., symbols for young children) and regional (e.g., charts using letters in Roman and in Arabic letters), hence inducing another source of variability due to their differences in meaning, physical spatial contents. Orientation judgement paradigms using Landolt C or tumbling E optotypes avoid these intertest confounders as only optotype size and gap orientation is varied. In the context of intertest comparison, letter identification and orientation judgments undergo different neural computations, thus adding additional variability.

Secondly, adaptive sampling uses various algorithm to select the next stimulus or set of stimuli based on previous responses. Additionally, some adaptive tasks do not have a fixed step size between varying optotype sizes, which allows finer resolution of acuity tests (6, 7, 10, 22, 23). The current gold-standard logMAR chart, such as ETDRS, includes 5-same sized-stimuli per line scaled at 0.10logMAR step size at a given distance using a 10 alternative forced choice task (24). The scoring is recommended such that all letters need to be identified correctly to receive the score, or stop at the line if this criterion is not met (24). Threshold or visual acuity is then determined as the last line that had no errors. However, some users may deploy a more lenient criterion, such as identifying 3 or 4 letters correctly before accepting the line, to account for various sources of noise (25). Scoring by letter-counting is another method (0.02 logMAR per correct letter) often applied. However, it discounts that the letter size within each line is the same. Additionally, it also dismissed the effect of a 60% correct criterion measures at a different point of the psychometric function than at 100% correct. Hence, these scoring methods are misleading and contributing to variance in VA measurements.

Response Input

The method by which an examinee's responses are recorded is another source of variability. In conventional testings with letter-based charts (e.g., ETDRS, Snellen, and Bailey-Lovie) or symbol-based charts (e.g., LEA symbols), the examinee typically name each optotype out loud and the examiner judges whether the response is correct in order to continue to the next line or terminate the test. For orientation-based optotypes (e.g., Landolt C or Tumbling E), responses may be given verbally or by gesture. In each case, the examiner records the response manually or enters it into a computer system. An examiner's judgement on scoring and error in calculation could introduce variability in VA measurements.

Alternatively, some VA assessments that enable examinee to enter their own responses using methods such as touchscreens (6, 10, 22, 26), keyboards (16, 23, 27, 28), or voice recognition (29). Each method has its own potential sources of error and may therefore affect the accuracy and precision of measurements in different ways.

Level of Supervision

Whether a VA assessment is supervised or not could be another variable that impact measurement accuracy and precision. In routine clinical testing, a trained technician is present in the room and administers the test. Their role may include providing instructions, ensuring consistent viewing distance, and scoring the results. The degree of examiner involvement, however, based on design of the assessment. Some tests heavily rely on the examiner, who is responsible for response input, optotype selection, and scoring. Others, such as automated tests with examinee self-input, require minimal involvement, and the examiner only serve to oversee the test and provide technical assistance as needed. Regardless of how involved the examiner is, it remains important to determine whether supervision is associated with accuracy and precision of VA testings.

Other factors: Viewing distance

Viewing distance is a further source of variation. The standard 6-meter (20 feet) distance approximates optical infinity, minimizing accommodation's and pupil constriction's contributions to threshold(5) whereas near-and intermediate VAs are typically measured at 40cm (16 in) and 67cm (26 in), respectively (30) (31) (32). Many tests use shorter distances such as 4 meters (13 ft) in ETDRS protocols(33), 1-2 meters on tablets and smartphones (34, 35) for practical reasons. Distance can shift measured thresholds independent of device: one study attributed a systematic paper-versus-digital chart difference partly to differing test distances (3m or 10ft vs. 5m or 16ft) rather than display technology (36). Shorter distances can also alter crowding, since inter-optotype spacing does not always scale proportionally with letter size across displays, with downstream effects on both measured acuity and repeatability (37). Like procedure and scoring, distance is often confounded with modality, since remote and mobile tests skew toward shorter distances.

Validation of visual acuity tests

Validation of VA tests is commonly established by repeatability and intertest agreement experiments. A repeatability experiment consists of comparison two-measurements of the same test. For intertest agreement experiment, a reference test is compared against a newly developed test to estimate its agreement. Note that during such experiment, no ground truth exists. Nevertheless, the comparison between two-tests has clinical utility in determining whether tests results are interchangeable. Consequently, without clear estimates of a VA-test's repeatability and intertest-agreement, an interpretation of results within and between tests remains challenging and prevents meaningful judgement during clinical trials and in clinical practices. Agreement and repeatability can be evaluated using several statistical approaches, each with distinct limitations. The intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) measures relative reliability but is influenced by between-participants variability and does not directly show the magnitude or direction of paired differences (38). The coefficient of variation (CoV), which expresses variability relative to the mean, does not directly assess paired agreement and can be difficult to interpret when logMAR values approach or cross zero. Bland-Altman analysis instead characterized paired differences using the mean bias and 95% limits of agreement (LoA) (39) (40) (41) Van der Zee et al. investigated broader range of VA tests and reported the overall mean systematic bias of 0.03 logMAR and overall mean 95%LoA of ±0.20 logMAR in distance VA measured in adults (44). However, these previous reviews focused on digital and self-administered VA test in adults and did not separately characterize intertest and testretest outcomes across testing characteristics.

The current study's primary objective was therefore to evaluate the precision and accuracy of a visual acuity test's repeatability and intertest agreement along four factors of variability: (1) test sites (clinic vs remote), (2) optotype sampling (fixed vs adaptive), (3) response input method (examiner vs self), and (4) level of supervision (supervised vs unsupervised). We hypothesized that VA tests precision will decreases with added sources of test variability. Moreover, based on prior meta-analyses (42, 44), we hypothesized that accuracy outcomes are not significantly affected by increased sources of variability.

Primary Analysis -Testing Strategies

Each eligible study was classified according to their reported testing strategies based on testing sites, methodology of optotype sampling, methodology of response input, and level of supervision (Fig 3). The test site described where VA was assessed. Testing in a dedicated research or eye care facility was classified as "clinic," whereas testing elsewhere, such as home, was classified as "remote." Next, "Optotype sampling" describes how optotype sizes were generated and displayed. Since the optotype size sampling dictates what the actual optotype sizes in any given test are, it determines a VA task's resolution. For example, VA tests with 0.10 logMAR size change per line on the ETDRS chart have a resolution of 0.10 logMAR and thus cannot measure any smaller changes. Tests using "fixed sampling" presented sizes in a predetermined sequence and terminated according to a pre-specified stopping rule, such as maximum number of errors at a given size. Examples included retro-illuminated ETDRS charts, Rosenbaum near acuity card, and digital charts with fixed increments (54) (55) (56) (57) . "Adaptive sampling" were defined as test that adjusted the optotype size according to preceding response to estimate VA threshold. Examples include staircase (12, 16, 21, 35, (58) (59) (60) (61), adaptive charts(10), and Best-PEST(7,23,59) procedures.

Response input described who recorded the examinee's answers. Any study was classified as "examiner" when an examiner recorded a spoken or gestured response, and as "self" when an examinee self-entered a response using a touchscreen or keypad.

Testing was classified as "supervised" when an examiner was present during the assessment and "unsupervised" when no examiner was present. Each eligible study was assigned to a testing-strategy group defined by its combination of test site, optotype sampling, response input, and supervision. Studies were excluded from testing-strategy analysis if the reported methods did not permit classification into all four categories.

Three-level random-effects metaregression models were fitted using the metafor package. These models accounted for multiple effect estimates from the same study and distinguished sampling error, variation among effects within studies, and variation between studies. Effect estimates, k, refer to data points in the meta-regression contributed by a study. For example, a study evaluating 2 different index VA tests across the same population will be contributing to 2 effect estimates. For each outcome, separate three-level meta-regression models were fitted for test sites, psychophysical procedure, response input, and supervision. Discussion

Prior investigations have proposed benchmarks for interpreting variability in VA measurements. Siderov and Tiu suggested that a difference of at least 0.15 logMAR was needed to confidently identify a real change under routine clinical conditions(112).

Intertest agreement

In this meta-analysis, systematic accuracy and precisions of validated visual acuity assessments were quantified across testing strategies and participants' characteristics.

The overall index VA tests demonstrated pooled systematic intertest mean bias very close to 0.00 logMAR, indicating the VA measured with index tests provided very similar results as the reference test. However, with the pooled systematic LoA half-width of 0.19 logMAR, it indicates we are expected to see variation of 0.19 logMAR (approximately 2 ETDRS lines) when comparing results from different tests. When different charts are used at follow-up visits, a change of less than 0.19 logMAR may clinically indicate a change in vision, but mathematically, may be difficult to distinguish from the expected variability between the tests.

When dissecting into different testing strategies, we observed a statistically significantly wider (p=0.030) intertest LoA half-width between VA conducted in non-clinical test site ("remote") and those conducted in a controlled clinical test site ("clinic"). This observation was also present when comparing intertest precision across different combination of testing strategies. Though the comparisons did not reach a statistically significant threshold when evaluating testing strategies among different combinations, there was a trend toward greater variability (LoA half-width 0.27 and 0.25 logMAR, Figure 5) in testing strategies involving remote testings. One consideration for the observed difference in variability was the imbalance of sample size. The remote subgroup included only 6 studies and 8 effect estimates, compared with 61 studies and 99 effect estimates in the clinic subgroup, making the remote pooled estimate more sensitive to individual studies. Another aspect to consider was that remote testings often coincided with unsupervised and examinee self-inputted responses, making the contribution of these characteristics difficult to differentiate. However, on the paired-wise comparison within each testing strategy, LoA half-width did not differ significantly by input method (examiner vs self; p=0.925) and level of supervision (supervised vs unsupervised; p=0.108). One "Remote / Adaptive / Examiner / Supervised" combination had a LoA half-width of 0.48 logMAR, which is more than twice of the overall pooled value of 0.19 logMAR. This combination was omitted from the combination level analysis was it was represented by a single effect estimate (8). However, it could still contribute to the variability in overall pooled LoA half-width in the "remote" subgroup.

Regardless, it highlights the substantial variability in remote testing can still occur even when conducted with the examiner input and under supervision.

In intertest agreement, testing conditions with increase sources of variability were associated with widen LoA half-width across VA tests, while differences in bias were marginal. These findings suggest that precision is more susceptible than accuracy to potential sources of variability in VA testing strategies and highlight that VA measurements can be accurate on average yet imprecise at the individual level.

Repeatability

We also observed a similar result pattern for test-retest comparisons. The pooled systematic test-retest mean bias was very close to 0.00 logMAR, indicating no overall learning or fatigue effects that would yield a better or worse results upon repeated testing. However, the lack of precision was evident. The systematic pooled test-retest CoR of 0.14 logMAR indicates if the same test were to be repeated, a difference that's smaller than 0.14 logMAR would be mathematically considered as the measurement variability within the test, not a true meaningful change. While the solution for intertest variability would be addressed by using the same eye chart and testing strategies when following up with patients, the lack of precision in test-retest poses a greater limitation in advancing assessments with wide test-retest CoR for clinical use, especially when monitoring change in subtle VA is crucial in evaluating treatment outcome and influencing clinical decision making.

In evaluation of test-retest CoR within each testing strategies, we found a significant difference in CoR between tests that utilized examiner-input versus examinee self-input (p=0.030). The CoR for self-inputted tests to be wider than those of examiner-inputted (0.18 vs 0.13 logMAR; p=0.030), but the difference of 0.05 logMAR may not be clinically significant. The increased variability in self-inputted tests could be explained by the differences in how examinee self-inputted their responses. Some utilized keypads labeled with corresponding orientation of the optotype (23, 27, 28) or corresponding Kay Pictures (16) . Others utilized touchscreen devices(6,10,22,26) or voice recognitional system for response input (29) . In addition to the methodology of how the responses were recorded, another variable worth mentioning is whether different psychophysical procedures could influence the variability observed in self-inputted VA tests. In fixed procedure, the scoring rules were line-by-line or letter-by-letter with relatively coarse increment. In adaptive procedure, optotype sizes were adjusted based on preceding responses and enable finer increments of VA measurement. Finer scoring in adaptive procedure may capture smaller differences in response that would be rounded to the same acuity value with line-based scoring. Thus, increasing the observed measurement variability. This is consistent with the findings in our analysis, where adaptive psychophysical procedure has wider CoR than fixed, but the differences did not reach a significant level. (0.15 vs 0.13 logMAR; p=0.098).

Regardless, within self-inputted VA tests, 13 studies utilized adaptive and 2 studies utilized fixed psychophysical procedure. The predominant representation of adaptive procedure in self-inputted test may contribute to the variability in self-inputted VA tests.

In evaluation of CoR among different combination of testing strategies, we observed similar pattern as described above. Test-retest CoR was the least precise in the combination that included adaptive procedure and examinee self-inputted testing (CoR 0.19 logMAR) compared to those of fixed procedures and examiner-inputted testings (CoR 0.13 and 0.14 logMAR), though again, the differences were not significant. Overall, we observed decrease in precision in testings that involved adaptive psychophysical procedure and examinee self-inputted testing strategies.

The effect of test resolution in optotype sampling on precision during repeatability experiments

Of the 38 studies that involved adaptive sampling, majority of the studies (n=32) relied on ETDRS as prototype of their chart design, which confined their step-size resolution to 0.10 logMAR. Similarly, most testing with fixed procedure (i.e., either fixed chartbased or fixed adaptive tests) followed the design of the ETDRS chart. Furthermore, some adaptive sampling methods may have additional free parameters (e.g. slope, lapse rate, differing Bayesian prior settings, etc.) and thus have more degree of freedom for test variability. Consequently, future studies should investigate the relationship between test resolution and different of parameters in adaptive protocol.

Influence of age and ocular pathology on test accuracy and precision

Beyond testing strategies, we also evaluated accuracy and precision on who were tested on the VA tests. Previous work (44) excluded individuals younger than 18 years, in part because the use of age-appropriate charts and optotypes were expected to introduce additional heterogeneity. Most included studies involving children used charts or optotypes also commonly used in adults, including ETDRS, Snellen, or Landolt C.

One study used Baile-Lovie acuity chart (76), and another study used Kay Pictures (75).

In our meta-analysis, we found a statistically significant difference in intertest accuracy (mean bias) of VA measured between adults and children (0.02 vs -0.04 logMAR; p=0.008). The negative mean bias indicates that index tests yielded lower logMAR values, corresponding to better VA, than reference tests. In another word, index tests measured better VA than references tests by 0.04 logMAR in children and worse VA than references by 0.02 logMAR in adults. Although intertest mean bias differed significantly between children and adults, the absolute between-group difference was small at 0.06 logMAR, suggesting the difference may have limited clinical relevance. No significant difference between children and adults were detected in intertest precision, test-retest accuracy, or test-retest precision. In both age groups, LoA-half width and CoR estimates were similar to the systematic pooled estimates. These findings provide no clear evidence that pediatric inclusion or use of age-appropriate testing reduced VA agreement or repeatability.

The presence of ocular pathologies may also influence accuracy and precision of VA measurements. Amblyopia and age-related macular degeneration (AMD) were the most represented conditions in this dataset. These conditions could theoretically affect performance through unstable fixation, impaired contrast perception, or fluctuating visual function (114). Nevertheless, test-retest CoR did not differ significantly between those without versus those with ocular pathologies (0.14 vs 0.13 logMAR; p=0.387).

Similarly, there was no significant difference in intertest LoA half-widths between either pathology subgroups (0.17 logMAR in without vs 0.18 logMAR in with pathology; p=0.467). Intertest and test-retest accuracies were both around 0.00 logMAR (Supplementary Material, Figure S7). In our meta-analysis, differences in accuracy and precision between age groups and ocular health status were generally small. However, these analyses are limited to broad comparisons and limit the generalizability of our findings to specific age ranges, ocular diagnosis, or level of disease severity.

Strength and Limitation

Several limitations should be considered. First, the imbalance in number of studies and effect estimates across testing strategies may have reduced the precision of subgroup comparisons and made sparely represented categories more sensitive to individual studies. Studies also contributed different number of effect estimates with varying sample size and sampling variance, resulting in unequal contribution to the pooled estimates. Although our multilevel models and cluster-robust inference accounted for dependence among effect estimates from the same study, uneven representation across testing strategies remains a limitation. This limitation was evident in the testretest analysis. Several VA assessments, including Odysight(103), DigiVis(21), and myVisionTrack(110), have been developed for home self-testing or remote monitoring between clinic visits, but remote and unsupervised testing were each represented by only one study in our test-retest analysis. This limits our conclusions about repeatability under these intended conditions and highlight the need for further validation for longitudinal monitoring. Secondly, the methodological quality and clinical applicability of included studies was variable. Common patient-selection concerns included convenience or nonconsecutive recruitment, and many validation studies predominantly enrolled healthy young patients, limiting their applicability to boarder range of patient and ocular conditions encountered in clinical practice. Additionally, studies often did not clearly report whether examiners were masked to the results of the alternative test, which could influence test scoring, interpretation and potentially overestimate the agreement. These findings, together with substantial heterogeneity in intertest and testretest estimates, highlight the need for rigorously designed validation studies using representative clinical population, prespecified testing protocols and masked assessment when feasible. Thirdly, we did not evaluate whether optotype design or the specific pairing of index and reference tests influence intertest agreement. Although ETDRS-based testing is widely regarded as the standardized reference for VA in clinical research(115), it was used as the comparator in only a little over half of the included intertest studies. Snellen, Landolt C, and Tumbling E charts were among other commonly used reference tests. The difference in optotype design, chart layout, crowding, scoring rules, and test-termination criteria may affect both systematic differences and the spread of differences between tests. Importantly, intertest LoA reflects the combined variability of the index and reference tests. Therefore, LoA halfwidth cannot be attributed solely to the performance of the index test. Consequently, heterogeneity in reference test selection may have contributed to the observed intertest variability in LoA half-width and potentially confounded comparisons across testing methodologies.

Conclusion

This systematic review and meta-analysis comprehensively evaluated the accuracy and precision of VA assessments for both intertest and test-retest comparisons across four testing strategies, namely test site, optotype sampling, response input method, and level of supervision. Our findings suggest that precision is more susceptible than accuracy to sources of variability, especially in remote and unsupervised testing, but this result needs to be solidified with more supporting studies. With precision being consistent in between optotype sampling methods and response input methods reassure that these testing strategies might not be the main source of measurement variability. Instead, future investigation and development should aim at development of VA charts with finer resolution and finding their association with precision.

While adaptive tests showed no meaningful difference than fixed-scale tests, their ability to sample smaller step size without compromising precision suggest their potential in detecting meaningful changes beyond measurement noise. Hence, future VA development should utilize adaptive stimulus sampling and are recommended in clinical trials and for drug developments.

Test-retest accuracy (mean bias)

Rosser et al. found that changes of at least 0.20 logMAR could reliability distinguished from no change using the ETDRS chart(113). More recently, Van der Zee et al. reported an overall mean random error of 0.20 logMAR across intertest and test-retest comparisons and proposed this values a provisional frame of reference(44). Building on this work, our meta-analysis provides separate pooled estimates of intertest and testretest variability as each has distinct clinical implications. Intertest variability addresses interchangeability between different tests, whereas test-retest variability helps distinguish true change in visual function from measurement error of the test itself when the same test was used repeatedly during longitudinal follow up. The 0.19logMAR in overall intertest precision implied that switching tests can introduce substantial variation and even more pronounced with assessments conducted in non-clinical test sites. These findings highlight the caution needed when integrating new VA tests into clinical practice or substituting them for established assessments. Although eye care facilities often have established protocol for routine VA testing, maintaining consistent testing conditions is particularly important when these assessments are extended beyond professional eye care settings. Examples include emergency departments, inpatient facilities and telehealth consultations, where rapid VA assessment is needed but testing environment may be difficult to standardize and testing instructions may be difficult to verify. Additionally, the 0.14 logMAR in test-retest precision reflects that small changes on repeated testing may be difficult to distinguish from expected measurement variabilities. Although the CoR of 0.14 logMAR fell within the 0.20 logMAR proposed by Rosser et al. and Van Der Zee et al., it remained greater than one ETDRS line (0.10 logMAR), suggesting that changes of this magnitude or smaller may still be obscured by measurement noise. Further reduction in test-retest variability could improve sensitivity to subtle longitudinal changes in VA.
