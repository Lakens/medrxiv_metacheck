Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Diabetic kidney disease is the leading cause of kidney failure worldwide, yet the molecular events underlying disease initiation and progression remain unclear. We measured 5,420 circulating proteins in 830 adults with type 1 diabetes and examined their longitudinal associations with onset and progression of kidney disease over 10 years of follow-up. We identified 130 proteins associated with at least one outcome independent of established clinical risk factors, of which 59 replicated in an independent cohort. HAVCR1/KIM-1, GFRA1, CD14 and GDF15 predicted albuminuria onset up to a decade before detectable disease. Albuminuria progression pointed to the kidney-liver axis (ASGR1, RBP4), whereas rapid kidney function decline and incident kidney failure pointed to immune and tumor necrosis factor receptor signaling. Mendelian randomization prioritized UMOD, SORT1, CLSPN, MAST4 and EGF as potential targets. These findings reveal molecular perturbations preceding clinical manifestations of diabetic kidney disease and identify distinct pathways associated with disease progression.

[div-01]

Diabetic kidney disease (DKD) is the leading cause of chronic kidney disease (CKD) and kidney failure (KF) worldwide, affecting a considerable proportion of the 537 million adults living with diabetes 1, 2 . Despite substantial advances, the clinical course of DKD is heterogeneous: some individuals maintain stable kidney function for decades whereas other progress rapidly to KF despite similar clinical risk factors 3 . Understanding this variability and identifying early markers for disease onset and progression is therefore needed.

Accumulating evidence suggests that DKD develops years before onset of clinically apparent disease. Alterations in lipid metabolism, central obesity and insulin resistance are detectable long before albuminuria develops in individuals with diabetes 4 . These systemic changes are likely accompanied by alterations in the circulating proteome, offering early insights into disease pathophysiology and risk. However, most existing proteomic studies of DKD have focused on its most advanced stages 5, 6 , identifying markers such as kidney injury molecule-1 (KIM-1, also known as HAVRC1) 7 and several members of TNF receptor family that predict progression to KF 5, 8 . Comparatively little is known about the proteomic signature of albuminuria onset, or the earlier transition from moderate to severe albuminuria. This lack of early markers is a critical gap, since clinical trials have consistently demonstrated that early intervention yields substantially greater long-term benefits than treatment initiated after severe kidney damage is established 9, 10, 11.

Defining these early molecular changes is particularly important in type 1 diabetes where diabetes onset typically occurs early in life and complications therefore disproportionately contribute to loss of healthy life-years. Despite declining incidence rates of severe DKD in type 1 diabetes, a considerable burden of kidney disease persists 12.

Recent advances in high-throughput proteomics enable comprehensive and unbiased profiling of the circulating proteome, providing new opportunities to systematically identify biomarkers of DKD risk at all stages of disease to uncover biological pathways.

In one of the most comprehensive proteomic studies of DKD to date, we quantified 5,420 circulating proteins in 830 adults with type 1 diabetes from the Finnish Diabetic Nephropathy (FinnDiane) Study (Fig. 1). Revealing widespread cross-sectional associations between kidneys and the circulating proteome, importantly, we also identified proteins predicting albuminuria onset, albuminuria progression, progression to KF and rapid kidney function decline. Lead proteins were independently validated in 249 additional participants with type 1 or type 2 diabetes. Calculating protein quantitative trait loci (pQTLs) and integrating these genome-wide findings with Mendelian randomization (MR) provided for causal evidence. Altogether, we identified both established and previously unrecognized markers, including several proteins detectable before clinical onset of albuminuria. They further implicate inflammation and the kidney-liver axis connecting DKD stages that have previously been studied in isolation.

RESULTS

Study population and proteomic landscape

The discovery cohort included 830 adults with type 1 diabetes from the FinnDiane Study (Supplementary Fig. 1): 449 individuals had a normal albumin excretion rate (AER) and estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) above 60 mL/min/1.73m 2 , while 149 had moderate and 232 severe albuminuria. Compared to those with a normal AER, individuals with albuminuria were more frequently obese, had higher systolic blood pressure and more chronic inflammation (Table 1). The Olink Explore HT assay covered 5,420 circulating proteins. Unsupervised clustering identified nine protein clusters with partially distinct functional enrichments (Extended Data Fig. 1). The largest cluster (cluster 7, n=1,467 proteins) did not represent any significant enrichment, whereas the other clusters were more specific, ranging from immune system and lipid metabolism (cluster 1,2, and 9), chromatin organization (cluster 6), to cellular stress responses and cell cycle regulation (cluster 4, 5).

Cross-sectional protein associations with cardio-kidney-metabolic traits

Circulating protein associations with baseline clinical traits were dominated by AER and eGFR which showed the largest number of significant associations and the strongest statistical evidence (Extended Data Fig. 2, Supplementary Table 1 and https://finndiane.shinyapps.io/Protein_Association/). Proteins involved in lipid metabolism, including LDLR were strongly associated with triglycerides and FABP4 with measures of obesity. In addition, inflammatory makers such as LRG1 and FGL1 were associated with hs-CRP (high-sensitivity C-reactive protein).

In binary analyses, consistent with above continuous measures of kidney health, severe albuminuria and CKD (eGFR<60 mL/min/1.73m²) were associated with 28% (n=1,535) and 29% (n=1,576) of all tested proteins, respectively (P<7.85×10 -5 ; Supplementary Table 2). Of these, 9% (n=484) remained significant after adjustment for eGFR in severe albuminuria, and 18% (n=959) after adjustment for severe albuminuria in CKD (Fig. 2a,b). Of note, 49% of individuals with severe albuminuria had a preserved eGFR, whereas 92% of those with CKD had severe albuminuria. Protein associations between both group were moderately correlated (Spearman ρ=0.37, P<2.2×10 -16 ), suggesting partially distinct signatures. Eight Reactome pathways were enriched in both, including extracellular matrix organization, TNF receptor binding and neutrophil degranulation (Fig. 2c, Supplementary Table 3).

Survival analysis identifies 130 proteins associated with albuminuria onset, its progression, progression to KF and rapid kidney function decline

We next conducted competing risk survival analysis accounting for death to identify proteins associated with albuminuria onset (N=449, 165 events, follow-up: 10.2 ± 5.4 years), progression of albuminuria (N=149, 60 events, 8.5 ±5.0 years), and progression to KF (N=232, 104 events, 9.0 ± 5.2 years; Supplementary Table 4). In addition, we used Firth's bias-reduced logistic regression to identify proteins associated with rapid kidney function decline (annual eGFR loss >3mL/min/1.73m 2 , N=730, 145 cases).

In minimally adjusted models, 38 proteins were proteome-wide significantly (P<7.85×10 -5 ) associated with albuminuria onset, 50 with albuminuria progression, 955 with progression to KF and 205 with rapid kidney function decline (Supplementary Table 5). Functional enrichment analysis revealed four pathways enriched in all outcomes (false discover rate (FDR) <0.05) with similar enrichment scores, namely extracellular matrix organization, innate immune system, immunoregulatory interactions and regulations of insulin-like growth factor (Fig. 3e, Supplementary Table 3). Onset and albuminuria progression shared three further pathways (diseases of metabolism, neutrophil degranulation, and plasma lipoprotein assembly remodeling and clearance), while albuminuria progression, progression to KF and rapid decline shared the pathway cell surface interactions at the vascular wall. The biggest overlap was found in pathways for progression to KF and rapid decline with altogether 11 pathways including TNF receptor binding, cytokine signaling in the immune system and interleukin 10 signaling.

Representing putative biomarkers independent of known clinical risk factors, we identified 130 proteins that were proteome-wide significantly associated with at least one of the four longitudinal outcomes after adjusting for sex, age, age at diabetes onset, eGFR, HbA1c, triglycerides, systolic blood pressure, waist-height ratio, hs-CRP and current smoking status. We further evaluated these proteins in an independent replication cohort of 122 additional participants with type 1 diabetes with their plasma proteome measured using the same platform. Discovery cohort outcomes were matched to their closest available one: albuminuria onset to baseline albuminuria, albuminuria progression to severe baseline albuminuria, rapid function decline to baseline CKD stages 3-4, and KF progression to a composite, longitudinal endpoint of incident KF or ≥40% eGFR decline. Overall, 59 out of 130 proteins showed evidence of replication (Supplementary Table 6).

Regarding albuminuria onset, five proteins (KIM-1, GFRA1, CD14, GDF15, REN) were proteome-wide significant after adjusting for clinical risk factors (Fig. 3a). KIM-1, GFRA1, CD14 and GDF15 replicated (P<0.01, Table 2). Among them, KIM-1 was consistently associated with all four kidney outcomes. Of note, CD14 and GDF15 were specifically associated with albuminuria onset and not with the later stages of DKD progression, whereas GFRA1 was also associated with rapid decline. Investigation of the associated cardiometabolic intermediate traits showed that serum CD14 abundance correlated most strongly with hs-CRP, GFRA1 with type 1 diabetes duration; and GDF15 with age (Extended Data Fig. 3).

Twenty-one proteins were proteome-wide significantly associated with the progression of albuminuria after adjustment for the clinical risk factors, of which 11 were replicated (Table 2, Fig. 3b). KIM-1 was the most significant predictor (P=1. 13×10 -8 ) and the protein with the largest effect size was ASGR1 (sHR=9.27, P=2.81×10 -8 ). Stratification by ASGR1 abundance produced clearly separated cumulative incidence curves (Supplementary Fig. 2). While correlated with triglycerides (Extended Data Fig. 3), it showed predominant expression in the liver as opposed to the kidneys in tissue expression data from the Human Protein Atlas (Extended Data Fig. 4b). Similarly, RBP4 was associated with triglycerides and expressed in the liver, but also in the kidneys (Extended Data Fig. 4b) with additional evidence of overexpression in CKD especially in injured proximal tubule cells (P=1.3×10 -31 ) in kidney single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNAseq) data 13 (Supplementary Table 7). In general, hierarchical clustering in the Human Protein Atlas clustered kidney and liver together, consistent with a potential involvement of the kidney-liver axis in albuminuria progression.

Of the 98 proteins proteome-wide significantly associated with progression to KF in the clinically adjusted model (Fig. 3c, Supplementary Table 6), 30 were replicated (P<5.1×10 -4 ; Fig. 4). Furthermore, of the 34 proteins associated with rapid kidney function decline (Fig. 3d), 30 replicated (P<0.0015; Supplementary Table 6, Fig. 4). Of note, 59% of the proteins associated with rapid decline were also associated with progression to KF. Significant proteins for both outcomes showed a strong correlation with baseline eGFR, except for MMP13 which correlated with sex (Extended Data Fig. 3).

To assess whether replicated associations were robust to sample storage conditions, we evaluated protein stability in a subset of 20 individuals with parallel serum samples stored at -20 °C and -80 °C. The 59 replicated proteins showed high correlations across storage conditions (median Pearson ρ=0.94 [interquartile range 0.91-0.96]; Supplementary Table 8).

Independent validation of replicated proteins in kidney disease in type 2 diabetes

To assess the generalizability of the findings, we tested whether the replicated 59 proteins were also associated with eGFR or advanced DKD (eGFR < 45mL/min/1.73m 2 ) in 127 NICOLA study participants with type 2 diabetes and eGFR <60mL/min/1.73m 2 . Of the 59 replicated proteins, 53 (90%) were associated with eGFR and 51 (86%) with advanced DKD, while 49 (83%) were associated with both outcomes (FDR<0.05; Supplementary Table 9). Effect directions were highly consistent across diabetes types, demonstrating substantial overlap between diabetes types.

Kidney expression evidence

Based on kidney biopsies from individuals with DKD (N=17 and 12 for tubular and glomerular, respectively) and healthy living donors (N=30 and 31 for tubular and glomerular, respectively), we investigated altered kidney gene expression in the European Renal cDNA Bank (ERCB) 14- 16 . Out of 15 genes, 6 (40%) were significantly differentially regulated (P<0.05/15) in either glomerular or tubulointerstitial compartment and four of them (CD14, CXADR, MMP7, WFDC2) in both compartments. (Supplementary Table 10). This suggests that the expression and regulation of these genes in the kidney are broadly consistent with those of their corresponding proteins in circulation.

To find further evidence (FDR<0.05) for kidney expression for the 59 replicated proteins, we queried scRNAseq data in in healthy controls and individuals with CKD from the Human Kidney Single Cell Transcriptome dataset 13 (Susztak & KPMP merged, https://susztaklab.com/hk_genemap_kpmp/scRNA, Supplementary Table 7) where 53 out of the 59 proteins showed evidence of cell type specific expression in the kidneys including immune cells. Furthermore, 38 of those were additionally differentially expressed in CKD including earlier stage markers such as CD14, GDF15, CXADR, RBP4 and later stage markers such as MMP7, WFDC2, PIK3IP1 and UMOD.

Interaction network of replicated proteins associated with deteriorating kidney health

Protein-protein interaction (STRING database) networks of the 59 replicated proteins (Extended Data Fig. 5, Supplementary Table 11) indicated TNFRSF1B as the most central protein in the network, exhibiting the highest degree (degree of 8), betweenness and eigenvector centrality. TNFRSF14 and BM2 had a degree of 7, followed by GDF15 and TNFRSF8 (degree of 6), and NECTIN2 (degree of 5). Overall, the largest connected component was dominated by immune-related proteins, particularly the members of the TNF superfamily, whereas two smaller separate networks consisted of NOS1 in its center and one with two mitochondrial proteins CHCHD6 and CHCHD10.

Genetic architecture of proteins and kidney disease-associated regulatory networks

To characterize the genetic regulation of circulating protein levels measured on the Olink Explore HT platform, we performed genome-wide analyses of both local (cis-pQTL; within 1 Mb) and distant (trans-pQTLs) genetic effects. We identified altogether 325,772 cis-pQTLs for 3,231 proteins (60.0%), and 97,709 trans-pQTLs for 3,617 proteins (67.0%), at FDR<0.05, demonstrating extensive genetic control of the circulating proteome (Extended Data Fig. 6a, Supplementary Table 12). Among proteins with significant pQTLs, nearly half (47%) showed evidence of both local and distal genetic regulation (Extended Data Fig. 6b-d). Several proteins showed particularly strong cis effects, with lead variants explaining more than 50% of protein level-variance (r 2 >0.5;). Notably, 1,300 proteins with cis-pQTLs, and 1,830 proteins with trans-pQTLs proteins were unique to the Olink Explore HT platform compared to earlier Olink panels, indicating that newly assayed proteins are also under strong genetic control 17.

To explore potential regulatory mechanisms among the 130 DKD-associated proteins, we inferred interactions based on cis-and trans-pQTL associations (FDR<0.1, minor allele frequency (MAF)≥0.05). Specifically, when a cis-pQTL variant for the lead protein acts as a trans-pQTL variant for another protein, the trans-regulated protein was considered a potential downstream target of the cis-regulated protein. Notably, this approach identified endothelial protein C receptor (PROCR) as an upstream regulator of the albuminuria progressionassociated PROC through the shared pQTL variant rs6579210. This variant was also reported as a cis-eQTL for PROCR in eQTLGen 18 . Furthermore, among others, we identified TRIAP1, associated with progression to KF, as a putative upstream regulator of PPIB and MECR (Extended Data Fig. 7).

Mendelian randomization

To evaluate genetic support for potential causal effects of circulating protein abundances, we performed MR analyses using identified pQTLs as instruments for the proteins and summary statistics from a genome-wide association study (GWAS) meta-analysis on eGFR in diabetes as outcome 19.

Among 2,447 proteins with available pQTLs (MAF≥5%), higher genetically predicted levels of UMOD were associated with lower eGFR, and higher SORT1 levels with higher eGFR (FDR<0.05, Supplementary Table 13). In analyses restricted to individuals with diabetes but without severe albuminuria, CLSPN, MAST4, EGF and SORT1 were additionally associated with eGFR (FDR<0.05). Comparison with observational findings demonstrated both concordant (EGF, SORT1) and discordant effects as UMOD demonstrated opposing effect directions (Supplementary Table 13). In contrast, CLSPN and MAST4 exhibited significant MR associations despite limited observational evidence.

Within the 130 lead proteins, 76 had suitable pQTL instruments for causal evaluation (Supplementary Table 14). In addition to significant MR evidence for UMOD, nominally significant (P<0.05) and directionally consistent evidence was observed for five of the 59 replicated proteins, including CD300A, ATRAID and INHBB for progression to KF and CD300A, CD55 and UMOD for rapid kidney function decline. Further nominal MR evidence was found for ADAMTSL2 and SPINK4.

DISCUSSION

In this large-scale prospective study, covering 5,420 proteins measured in 830 adults with type 1 diabetes, we identified distinct molecular signatures across the continuum of DKD development and progression, including 130 proteins associated with onset of albuminuria, albuminuria progression, progression to KF, or rapid kidney function decline independent of well-established clinical risk markers. In an independent cohort of 122 adults with type 1 diabetes, 59 were replicated and 55 of those associated with advanced DKD or eGFR in 127 individuals with type 2 diabetes showing substantial transferability across diabetes types. By integrating proteomics data with pQTLs through MR, we provide some genetically anchored evidence of causality.

Notably, our study shows that proteomic changes are detectable well before albuminuria and eGFR become informative, when intervention could still alter the disease trajectory. Beyond KIM-1, a well-established marker [20] [21] [22] , we identified and replicated three proteins that predicted albuminuria onset independently of established risk factors: CD14, GFRA1, GDF15.

CD14, a co-receptor for bacterial lipopolysaccharide (LPS) 23 , supports a role for inflammatory processes at disease onset consistent with earlier findings of elevated circulating LPS in individuals with normal AER who subsequently developed albuminuria 24 . Soluble CD14 has been previously associated with DKD severity in type 2 diabetes 25 , and our study demonstrates its relevance at earlier stages. In our interaction network, CD14 was linked to CD55, a protein with potentially causal evidence (P<0.05) for rapid eGFR decline. CD55 regulates innate immunity 26 , one of the four pathways shared across all four longitudinal outcomes.

GFRA1 and GDF15 which were linked in the interaction network showed both expression in injured proximal tubule cells (P<1.5×10 -66 ), and GDF15 additionally in principal and connecting tubule cells (P<10 -310 ) with significant underexpression in CKD (P≤5.5×10 -89 ). However, importantly, GDF15 is a ubiquitously expressed stress-response cytokine 27 . Previously, it has been linked to incident and worsening albuminuria in type 2 diabetes 28 , and to kidney function decline among individuals with type 1 diabetes and severe albuminuria 29 . GFRA1, implicated in kidney development 30 , was associated with albuminuria onset and rapid eGFR decline. An association with declining eGFR was also found in youth-onset type type 2 diabetes 31 . Together, these findings suggest that some of the proteins previously linked to established kidney dysfunction can be detected before albuminuria onset and that early molecular alterations may reflect both renal and systemic processes, indicating that key pathways are activated long before clinical disease.

The kidney-liver axis emerged as a prominent feature of earlier-stage outcomes, particularly albuminuria progression. For both albuminuria onset and progression, proteins in the plasma lipoprotein-and metabolism-related pathways were enriched. At the protein level, asialoglycoprotein receptor 1 (ASGR1), which had the largest effect size for progression, is predominantly expressed in liver hepatocytes and strongly correlated with triglycerides, consistent with its role as a putative drug target for hypercholesterolemia 32 . Its correlation with hs-CRP may reflect its reported link to coronary artery disease through inflammation 33 . RBP4, retinol-binding protein 4, which transports retinol from the liver to peripheral tissues, is expressed in both liver and the kidneys including proximal tubule, descending thin loop of Henle and parietal epithelial cells (P≤4.9×10 -43 ). Although RBP4 is lost to urine in proteinuria, elevated circulating RBP4 has been observed in CKD linked to altered kidney-liver axis signaling 34 . This observation was supported in scRNAseq data as RBP4 was overexpressed in injured proximal tubule cells (P=1.3×10 -31 ). RBP4 contributes to obesity-related inflammation, inhibits insulin signaling, promotes muscle insulin resistance, and its hepatic expression is linked to increased glucose production 35 . Although independent replication failed, PCSK9, predominantly liver-produced 36 , was among the lead signals for progression. Together, these findings point to a coordinated kidney-liver axis. Given the causal link between obesity and DKD 37 and rising rates of obesity in type 1 diabetes 38 , the kidney-liver axis merits further exploration.

The albuminuria progression signals also link to kidney biology. Both WFDC2 and MMP7 were overexpressed in various tubular cells in CKD in the scRNAseq data, have been implicated in early renal fibrosis in type 2 diabetes and were collectively proposed as a kidney fibrosis panel 39 . Both markers were also associated with advanced DKD in our type 2 diabetes validation set. Furthermore, in mouse models, higher tubular expression of Mmp7 lead to glomerular injuries 40 . Soluble coxsackievirus and adenovirus receptor (CXADR) may promote albuminuria progression by inhibiting tight junction formation 41 . Consistent with this, Cxadr is expressed during podocyte injury in mice 42 . However, in the scRNAseq data, in CKD, CXADR was underexpressed in podocytes (P=2.2×10 -4 ) and overexpressed in injured proximal tubule cells (P=9.3×10 -17 ). Additionally, in our interaction network, it was directly linked with CD55 which had a potential causal role. Progression to KF and rapid decline showed substantial overlap including immune and TNFreceptor signaling pathways. The shared lead signals converged with the 21-protein Joslin panel 8 of which 14 proteins (CD160, CD27, DLL1, EFNA4, EPHA2, GFRA1, KIM-1, LAYN, LTBR, NECTIN4, PI3, TNFRSF1A, TNFRSF1B, WFDC2) were proteome-wide significant in our late-stage phenotypes. TNFRSF1B was the most central node in our interaction network, closely rivalled by onset marker GDF15, absent in the Joslin panel. NECTIN2, among the strongest later-stage associations, was central in a subnetwork that linked to NECTIN4, TNFRSF14, NCR1, CD55 and CD46, the latter two protecting podocytes from complementmediated damage 43 . The urinary complement proteome has been associated with incident KF 44 . NECTIN2 itself was overexpressed in podocytes in individuals with CKD (P=1.2×10 -11 ). Its expression during injury-repair states of the thick ascending limb of Henle's loop 45 , together with its presence in the leading edge of the immunoregulation pathway shared across all four longitudinal outcomes further support immune system involvement. Moreover, we found both a novel candidate biomarker and a candidate regulator. PIK3IP1, a lead signal in rapid decline and progression to KF, encodes a negative regulator of PI3K signaling 46 . Given the established role of PI3K/AKT signaling in podocyte insulin responsiveness and glucose uptake 47 , PIK3IP1 is a plausible but previously unexamined regulator of PI3K activity in DKD. Furthermore, in CKD, it was both overexpressed in connecting tubule cells (P=2.2×10 -13 ) and underexpressed in endothelial cells of the glomerular capillary tuft (P=5.7×10 -7 ). In the pQTL networks, TRIAP1 (TP53-regulated inhibitor of apoptosis 1), associated with progression to KF, was predicted as an upstream regulator of PPIB (Cyclophilin B) and MECR. Mouse models indicate that Triap1 protects against high glucose-induced podocyte apoptosis 49 , suggesting a protective response to kidney damage. PPIB plays a role in inflammation and fibrosis by modulating the response of tubular epithelial cells 48 , and MECR is involved in the mitochondrial fatty acid synthase pathway, regulated by ATP in hepatocytes, and associated with insulin resistance 51 . Together, these suggest TRIAP1 as a novel, central regulator of kidney health and PIK3IP1as a novel candidate biomarker. Uromodulin (UMOD), the most abundant protein in urine, is produced in the thick ascending limb of Henle's loop 48 . Consistent with previous reports 49 , lower serum UMOD was independently associated with rapid decline of kidney function, but not with the earlier albuminuria-based outcomes. Given its role in CKD 50 , UMOD may capture a distinct, albuminuria-independent component of rapid decline. In contrast, genetically predicted UMOD levels were associated with lower kidney function in MR. This may reflect differences between life-long genetically determined protein levels and circulating protein concentrations prior to kidney function decline.

In general, the integration of proteomic and genetic data provided orthogonal support of the biological specificity of measured protein measurements 17 . Whereas the longitudinal analyses identified a broad set, MR prioritized five proteins (UMOD, SORT1, CLSPN, MAST4 and EGF), whose genetically predicted levels were associated with eGFR in diabetes. The limited overlap between these likely reflects the different biological questions addressed by these approaches, the lack of robust genetic instruments for some proteins of interest and limited statistical power and availability of GWAS on eGFR and other DKD outcomes in diabetes. Longitudinal observational associations may capture early molecular perturbations or compensatory mechanisms, whereas MR identifies proteins whose genetically determined levels are linked to kidney function. Among the genetically prioritized proteins, the two kidneytissue enriched proteins, UMOD and EGF, have established roles in kidney biology, and higher genetically predicted EGF levels were associated with higher eGFR in diabetes consistent with experimental studies on progressive renal injury and fibrosis 51.

To our knowledge, the current study represents the largest, longitudinal proteomics study on diabetes outcomes with 5,420 proteins measured in 830 participants with type 1 diabetes and further validated in 249 independent individuals with either type 1 or type 2 diabetes; and one of the largest diabetes cohorts profiled at this scale. Further strengths include the dense clinical characterization and the long follow-up time. Our study has some limitations. The Olink platform provides relative protein quantification without transferrable thresholds for abnormal protein levels. For translation, targeted, quantified assays are needed. Although we detected cis-pQTLs for 60% of the proteins, without genetic instruments for many proteins, and with limited statistical power in the GWAS studies for eGFR in diabetes, most associations remain observational. Finally, both the discovery cohort and the main replication cohort consisted of Finnish individuals with type 1 diabetes. While we further investigated the lead protein associations in individuals with type 2 diabetes from Northern Ireland, these findings might not generalize to other ethnicities.

In conclusion, this large-scale proteomic analysis revealed that while there is a small number of shared, enriched pathways, distinct molecular processes characterized the successive stages of DKD progression in diabetes. Early disease was marked by inflammatory, kidney-associated and systematic stress-related signals (CD14, GFRA1, GDF15) detectable before clinically apparent kidney injury; albuminuria progression highlighted the kidney-liver axis (ASGR1, RBP4) linked to metabolic and inflammatory dysfunction; and advanced disease, namely rapid kidney function decline and KF, was characterized by proteins enriched for immune and TNF receptor signaling. Together with genetic support from pQTL and MR analyses, these findings extend our understanding of the underlying mechanisms of DKD and highlight opportunities for earlier risk stratification and therapeutic intervention.

METHODS

Study cohort and outcomes

This study included 830 adults with type 1 diabetes from the FinnDiane Study. T1D was defined as T1D diagnosed by their attending physician, age of diabetes onset below 40 years of age, and requiring insulin in the first year after diagnosis. All participants underwent a thorough clinical examination at the study visit and provided blood and urine samples. Baseline albuminuria status was assigned based on agreement in two out of three measurements of the AER. We selected for proteomics analysis 449 participants with no kidney disease at the study baseline (normal AER and eGFR > 60 mL/min/1.73m 2 ), 149 participants with moderate albuminuria, and 232 with severe albuminuria at baseline. Within each of these three subcohorts, we included individuals with confirmed stable albuminuria status for at least 5 years of follow-up and individuals with deteriorating kidney health during the follow-up, and matched these two groups based on sex, baseline age and diabetes duration, and the storage time and number of thaws of baseline serum samples (all p ≥ 0.05). Individuals with normal AER and moderate albuminuria were additionally matched for baseline eGFR (p > 0.05). Among those with normal AER at baseline, we further required preservation of eGFR (> 60 mL/min/1.73m2) at the end of follow-up, for inclusion in the stable group. Additionally, we created a fourth sub-cohort of 730 adults with available data on eGFR slopes (Supplementary Fig. 1). Slopes were calculated from the study visit forward based on serum creatinine measurements using the CKD-EPI 2021 formula 52 . We included those individuals with a first eGFR observation within three months of the study visit and least three eGFR observations in at least two years of follow-up. Slope calculation stopped at CKD stage 5 (defined as first eGFR<15mL/min/m 2 and a confirmatory eGFR within 30 to 365 days), at KF (with a presumed final eGFR of 10mL/min/m 2 ), or at the last available measurement, whichever came first. All slopes were calculated based on calendar month median eGFRs. All slopes were visually inspected and one slope was removed due to quality issues.

We investigated the following longitudinal outcomes: • For those without kidney disease at the time of sampling (N=449), onset of albuminuria (N=165) over a follow-up time of 10.2 ± 5.4 years • For those diagnosed with moderate albuminuria at the time of sampling (N=149), progression to severe albuminuria (N=60) over a follow-up time of 8.5 ± 5.0 years • For those diagnosed with severe albuminuria at the time of sampling (N=232), progression to KF (N=104) over a follow-up time of 9.0 ± 5.2 years • For all adults with eGFR slopes available (N=730), rapid decline of kidney function after baseline, defined as an annual eGFR loss of more than 3 mL/min/1.73m 2 (N=145).

The study protocol was approved by the HUS Regional Committee on Medical Research Ethics (491/E5/2006, 238/13/03/00/2015, and HUS/3313/2018), and the study was performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from each participant.

Serum sample pre-processing and proteomics measurements

The median number of thaw cycles was 1 (IQR 1 -2) with 20 % of samples that have never been thawed before. The maximum number of thaw cycles was 5. The samples were randomized on plates based on sex, age, baseline albuminuria status, kidney disease progression, storage time and thaw cycle number. Altogether 840 serum samples that were stored in -20° Celsius were sent for proteomic analysis at SciLifeLab, Uppsala, Sweden. Serum protein levels were quantified using the Olink Explore HT, which combines proximity extension assays with next-generation sequencing and measures 5,420 proteins. The protein expression data was intensity normalized by the SciLifeLab. Of the samples, three failed the laboratory quality control and were removed. A further seven samples were removed as they were considered outliers with either their median or IQR protein concentrations exceeding 3.5 standard deviations of the overall median or IQR protein concentration (using the OlinkAnalyze R package). Three of those were also considered outliers in a Principal Component Analysis (PCA) plot (Supplementary Fig. 3). Due to careful sample randomization for the plates, the protein expression data was not batch adjusted.

Multiple testing corrections

To account for multiple testing in the proteome-wide analyses, we used Bonferroni correction applied to the independent numbers of tests. The effective number of independent tests was calculated using PCA, which showed that the first 637 principal components cover 99% of the variance in the protein abundance data. The P value threshold for multiple comparisons was therefore adjusted by dividing 0.05 by 637 resulting in a proteome-wide significant threshold of P=7.85×10 -5.

Assessment of protein stability during storage

The serum for the 830 samples was stored in -20° Celsius for a median of 20.0 years (IQR 16.2 -22.3 years). The maximum storage time was 25.2 years. Protein stability was evaluated in a subset of the participants (n=20). For each participant, two aliquots collected from the same study visit were stored in parallel at either -20 °C or at -80 °C. For each protein, protein abundance measured in samples stored at -20 °C was compared with that in paired samples stored at -80 °C with Pearson and Spearman correlation coefficients used to assess the linear relationship and rank order correlation, respectively.

Proteomic clustering All 5,420 proteins were assigned to different clusters based on the NPX levels. UMAP (version 0.5.12) algorithm was used for dimension reduction, followed by Hierarchical Density-Based Spatial Clustering of Applications with Noise (min_cluster_size=120, min_samples=15; HDBSCAN version 0.8.44) clustering based on UMAP coordinates. The silhouette score was used to determine clustering performance. Reactome pathway enrichment analysis and visualization were performed with the clusterProfiler (version 4.14.6), ReactomePA (version 1.50.0), and enrichplot (version 1.26.6) R packages. All proteins were mapped to Entrez Gene identifiers with org.Hs.eg.db R package (version 3.20.0) and were used as background set.

Significant enriched pathways were defined as pathways with Benjamini-Hochberg-adjusted P < 0.05. Up to five most significant pathways were visualized for each cluster.

Cross-sectional outcomes

Associations between protein abundance (NPX levels: dependent variable) and baseline clinic characteristics (independent variables) were assessed using multiple variable linear regression models adjusted for sex, age, storage time and thaw number. Clinical characteristics included hemoglobin A1c (hba1c), duration of type 1 diabetes (T1D duration), body mass index (BMI), waist-height ratio (WHtR), high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (Hs-CRP), total cholesterol, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL Chol), blood triglyceride level (Triglycerides), current smoking status (smoking), and systolic blood pressure (SBP). We repeated the association analysis with baseline eGFR as an additional covariate.

Given some evidence of quasi-complete separation (when using standard logistic regression models) and the smaller sample size, the associations between baseline NPX and severe albuminuria (normal AER or moderate albuminuria vs. severe albuminuria) and additionally with baseline CKD stage 3 or 4 (eGFR <60mL/min/1.73m 2 ) were assessed with Firth's biasreduced logistic regression models (penalization of the log-likelihood by Jeffreys prior) using the logistf R package 53 . The minimal models were adjusted for sex, storage time and thaw numbers whereas the adjusted models additionally also for either eGFR (albuminuria analysis) or severe albuminuria (CKD analysis).

Longitudinal kidney outcomes

Albuminuria onset, its progression, and progression to KF were assessed using a competing risk framework. For all those three outcomes, death from all causes was treated as a competing event. Participants who experienced neither event were censored at the date of last follow-up. Associations were evaluated using the Fine-Gray sub-distribution hazard model (sHR) (survival R package 54 ). The proportional hazard assumption was tested for all models and if a specific protein violated the assumption, the follow-up time was reduced to 10 years and if necessary, further reduced to 5 years. The protein associations with rapid decline of kidney function were assessed with Firth's bias-reduced logistic regression. Minimal models in all longitudinal analyses were adjusted for storage time, thaw numbers and sex. For the progression to KF, minimal models were additionally adjusted for eGFR, and the minimal models for rapid kidney function decline were additionally adjusted for eGFR and severe albuminuria. The fully adjusted models were adjusted for storage time, thaw number, sex, age, age of diabetes onset, eGFR, HbA1c, triglycerides, systolic blood pressure, waist-height ratio, hs-CRP and current smoking status. Fully adjusted models for rapid decline of kidney function were additionally adjusted for severe albuminuria. We report the results of fully adjusted models in the main text. The tidycmprsk R package was used to create cumulative incidence curves.

Functional enrichment analysis

Gene set enrichment analysis (fgsea package 55 ) using the proteins' gene names was performed to infer altered pathways. For each cross-sectional and longitudinal regression analysis, proteins were sorted according to their t-statistics (β coefficient divided by standard error). In the cross-sectional analyses, the t-statistics were based on the results of the adjusted analyses and for the longitudinal analyses, they were based on minimal models. Reactome 56 gene sets for humans (N=1839) were retrieved from MSigDB (release 2026.1. Hs) using the msigdbr package (version 2026.1.0) 57 . The minimum number of overlaps between the tested set and a Reactome gene set was set to 15, the maximum to 500 and the number of permutations to 100,000. Pathways with an FDR below 0.05 were collapsed into independent ones.

All analyses were performed in R using version 4.4.2.

Tissue expression and correlation with clinical variables

Using the HPAanalyze R package 58 , we downloaded tissue expression data from the Human Protein Atlas (Version 25, accessed 2026-08-09) 59, 60 and removed entries at the level Uncertain. For each tissue and protein, we reported the expression level of the most highly expressed cell type in that tissue and for that protein (levels are ordered from lowest to highest: not detectedlowmediumhigh). From the 59 replicated top proteins, 41 had detectable protein expression. We used hierarchical clustering for both the tissues and the proteins in the heatmap (Extended Data Fig. 4).

Additionally, we created heatmaps for the Spearman correlation between baseline clinical markers and the 59 replicated, top proteins. Again, we used hierarchical clustering for both the proteins and the clinical markers (Extended Data Fig. 3).

Protein-protein interaction network analysis

Protein-protein interaction (PPI) data were retrieved from the STRING database (Version 12, accessed 2026-08-09) restricted to Homo sapiens using the STRINGdb R package 61 . Interactions were queried for the 59 replicated proteins using a minimum required interaction confidence score of 400 (medium confidence) in this exploratory analysis. Prior to network construction, the statistical significance of protein-protein interaction enrichment was assessed using STRING's PPI enrichment test. A network was considered to reflect greater connectivity than expected by chance at p < 0.05. Network visualization was performed using ggraph. Node size was scaled to reflect degree centrality, and edge width was scaled to the STRING interaction confidence score (Extended Data Fig. 5).

Replication analyses in type 1 diabetes

Replication analyses were performed in an independent validation cohort consisting of 122 individuals with type 1 diabetes from the FinnDiane study. Proteins were quantified using the same Olink Explore HT platform as in the discovery cohort, but in plasma instead of serum and in a different laboratory at the Austrian Institute of Technology (Austria). The plasma samples had been stored at -80 degrees Celsius and were never thawed previously. The proteomics data was processed in the same way as the data in the discovery cohort.

Replication analyses were performed using minimally adjusted (sex and storage time cut into tertiles) Firth's bias-reduced logistic regression models due to the limited size of the replication cohort. As longitudinal follow-up data were only available for a subset of participants (64 of 122), proteins associated with the longitudinal outcomes in the discovery cohort (albuminuria onset, albuminuria progression, rapid KF), were evaluated against analogous baseline kidney phenotypes in the replication cohort. Specifically, proteins associated with progression to albuminuria onset (n=5), were evaluated against prevalent albuminuria at baseline (moderate or severe vs no albuminuria). Proteins associated with albuminuria progression (n=21), were evaluated against severe albuminuria, whereas proteins associated with rapid kidney function decline (n=34), were evaluated against baseline CKD stage 3 or 4 (eGFR < 60mL/min/1.73m 2 ) due to a lack of longitudinal slope data. Among the 64 individuals with longitudinal follow-up data available, a composite endpoint of KF or ≥40% eGFR decline occurred in 12 individuals during a median follow-up time of 2.9 years (233 person-years). The proteins associated with progression to KF (n=98) were evaluated against this composite endpoint using minimally adjusted (sex and storage time) Fine Gray analyses, with death included as a competing event.

The proportional hazard assumption was assessed and satisfied for all replication models.

For each outcome, the Bonferroni-corrected P value threshold was calculated by dividing 0.05 by the number of proteins evaluated in the corresponding replication analysis, i.e. P<0.01 for moderate or severe albuminuria; P<0.0024 for severe albuminuria; P<5.1×10 -4 for albuminuria progression to KF; and P<0.0015 for rapid decline.

Kidney tissue transcriptomic analysis

Differential kidney gene expression in DKD was analyzed using microdissected glomerular (n=31 and 12) and tubulointerstitial compartment data (n=30 and 17) from healthy living donors and patients with DKD, respectively, of the European cDNA biobank [14] [15] [16] for 15 proteins (Supplementary Table 10). The limma method (Linear Models for Microarray Data) was used to identify differentially regulated genes.

Evidence in kidney single-cell RNA sequencing data

To find further evidence for kidney expression, we queried single cell RNA transcriptomics data in in healthy controls and individuals with CKD from the Human Kidney Single Cell Transcriptome dataset 13 (Susztak & KPMP merged, https://susztaklab.com/hk_genemap_kpmp/scRNA, Supplementary Table 7) for the 59 replicated proteins. Log and normalized average expressions are reported across all samples, control samples, and CKD samples. Wilcoxon rank-sum test was used to assess if each gene expression is differentially expressed for each cell type, and differentially expressed in CKD samples as compared to control samples. P-values were corrected for multiple testing using the Benjamini-Hochberg FDR method (adjusted for testing 34,733 genes).

External validation in type 2 diabetes cohort

We evaluated the 59 replicated proteins in 127 individuals with type 2 diabetes and eGFR<60mL/min/1.73m 2 from the NICOLA cohort (Supplementary Table 9). Protein measurements were generated simultaneously with the 122 samples from the type 1 diabetes replication cohort, and protein measurements were generated using the same Olink platform and analytical workflow. Associations between protein levels and kidney function were assessed using two complementary outcomes: 1) continuous eGFR and 2) advance DKD, defined as eGFR <45 mL/min/1.73m 2 (CKD stage 3b or worse). Associations with eGFR were analysed using linear regression, whereas associations with advanced DKD were evaluated using logistic regression. All models were adjusted for age, sex and Olink plate. Multiple testing was performed using the Benjamini-Hochberg FDR method.

pQTL analysis

To identify the genetic determinants for proteins on the Olink Explore HT panel, we performed a pQTL analysis with MatrixEQTL software (v 2.3). We analyzed both local (cis) and distant (trans) effects, using a threshold of 1 Mb for detection of cis-pQTLs. We used imputed genome-wide genotyping data genotyped in four batches at the University of Virginia using HumanCoreExome Bead arrays (versions 12-1.0, 12-1.1 and 24-1.0, Illumina, San Diego,CA) and imputed using the population-specific Finnish reference panel (SISu version 3). and included only variants imputed with high imputation quality (INFO>0.80) and MAF>0.01.

A pQTL-supported protein regulatory network was then constructed for the 130 proteome-wide associated proteins. Trans-pQTL variants was restricted to those located on a different chromosome or more than 5Mb from target gene TSS. For each protein, downstream targets were defined as proteins associated in trans with SNPs with leading cis-pQTL variant (FDR<0.1, MAF>=0.05). Upstream regulators were defined as proteins lead cis-pQTL variant were associated in trans with other proteins (FDR<0.1, MAF>=0.05). Network is visualized with Cytoscape (version: 3.10.2).

MR analysis

We performed MR analyses to investigate potential causal relationships between circulating protein levels and diabetic kidney disease. Proteins with at least one significant pQTL identified in our pQTL analysis (cis-pQTLs at FDR<0.05 and trans-pQTLs at FDR<0.05) and a MAF≥ 0.05 were considered eligible instrumental variables. When only a single genetic instrument was available, causal effects were estimated using the Wald ratio method; otherwise, inverse-variance weighted (IVW) MR was applied.
