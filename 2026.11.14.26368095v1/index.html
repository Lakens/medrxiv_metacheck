Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Background

Shwachman-Diamond Syndrome (SDS) is a rare disease that affects 1 in 75,000 live births in the United States (1). This condition is caused by biallelic pathogenic mutations in the Shwachman-Bodian-Diamond Syndrome (SBDS) gene in approximately 90% of cases (1, 2).

Less commonly, SDS or an SDS-like syndrome can also be caused by pathogenic biallelic mutations in the DNAJC21 and ELF1 genes, or heterozygous mutations in the SRP54 gene. SDS can have diverse clinical presentations and symptoms, including, but not limited to, exocrine pancreatic insufficiency, skeletal abnormalities, and hematologic manifestations, notably neutropenia and bone marrow failure (3). One of the more serious complications of SDS is the high risk for myelodysplastic syndrome (MDS) and acute myeloid leukemia (AML) with about 1 in 3 patients developing AML by the age of 30 years (1), and the risk continues to rise with age. Since its discovery in 1964, most research on SDS has focused on understanding the disease mechanisms and exploring treatment options rather than describing the lived patient experience.

Qualitative research can identify new concepts of interest missed in previous quantitative studies, contributing to a more holistic understanding of the patient experience (4) (5) (6). An understanding of patient needs is essential for optimizing future care, research, and advocacy initiatives to ultimately improve the quality of life for individuals affected by SDS. Research in other disease communities has allowed for communication of the complex experiences of patients and specific recommendations for change (5) (6) (7). For example, previous qualitative research on other inherited bone marrow failure syndromes (IBMFSs) has revealed insights into the patient experience beyond what is seen in clinic, such as increased anxiety about future risks, exhaustion with time-consuming treatment regimens, and changes made to patients' future life plans (8) (9) (10) (11). Additionally, many people with rare diseases express feeling pressured to be experts in their condition and emphasize the importance of connecting with the disease community for support (8).

One of the only studies exploring qualitative data from an SDS cohort utilized the Italian SDS registry. They distributed a questionnaire, consisting of open-ended, multiple choice and analogue scale questions, to analyze quality of life. They compared this to data from Cystic Fibrosis patients and found that SDS patients had increased rates of physical pain, cognitive impairment, and social struggles compared to Cystic Fibrosis patients (12). While this study provided insights into the unique physical and cognitive challenges SDS patients may face, the findings were limited due to the single country sample and scope of the questionnaire. A better understanding of holistic patient experiences, including the management of their care and psychological support needs, is necessary to improve care and research specific to Shwachman-Diamond Syndrome.

In recognition of the value of patient perspectives, the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) created patient-focused drug development meetings. These meetings were create with the goal of hearing directly from patients and caregivers, and to systematically gather patient experience data (13). Through these meetings, the FDA has gained access to a deeper understanding of specific disease burdens and has allowed patients to have a greater role in their own treatment development (13). SDS Alliance (SDSA) hosted an Externally-Led Patient-Focused Drug Development Meeting (EL-PFDD) in June 2025. The goal of this meeting was to provide patient perspectives to the FDA and those involved in SDS research and drug-development.

Understanding the SDS patient perspective through the SDS EL-PFDD meeting and associated research activities is critical to informing not only researchers' and medical product developers' insights into the unmet needs and risk tolerance of the SDS community, but also the FDA's decisions during the drug development and approval processes. This study was designed to expand on the information received at the EL-PFDD meeting. The meeting included specific stories shared in a short format from panels of patients and caregivers. The meeting and subsequent Voice of the Patient Report focused on understanding the specifics of people's symptoms and experiences with current treatments (14).

We aimed to capture the nuance of living with SDS through interviewing a wider range of participants and caregivers and giving them a platform to share stories in a less structured format. Through semi-structured qualitative interviews, we aimed to provide a space for patients and caregivers to share their experiences living with SDS. We focused on understanding the social and emotional impacts of SDS, including both the impact of the condition on day-to-day life and experiences navigating healthcare systems. were contacted by program staff via email and/or phone to invite them to participate in this qualitative study. Eligible participants were English-speaking individuals over 18 years of age who had either a personal diagnosis of SDS or who were caregivers for someone with SDS. A total of 29 eligible participants consented to the study; 9 were lost to follow-up during scheduling attempts, and 20 people completed the study. No compensation was provided for participation in this study.

Methods

This

Interviews followed a semi-structured interview guide developed by a multidisciplinary team with backgrounds in qualitative research, genetic counseling, and SDS. Questions covered a broad range of topics, including diagnostic journey, symptoms and treatments, experiences in healthcare, and hopes for future treatments. Participants were encouraged to share any notable challenges or experiences that they have had related to SDS. All interviews were conducted in English over Zoom between February 2025 and July 2025, each lasting between 30 to 70 minutes. All participants gave informed consent for study procedures, including recording of the interview.

Audio recordings of interviews were transcribed verbatim and analyzed using the Dedoose platform. Filler words, stutters, or other verbal markers that did not impact meaning have been removed from quotes for clarity. A team of 3 coders developed a codebook through an inductive, open-coding approach. The first third of transcripts were independently read by at least two coders, who did line-by-line coding to capture the meaning of each statement. The coding team then consolidated codes, collaboratively refined codebook definitions, and resolved any coding discrepancies between team members (16). Once the codebook was finalized through this process, a single coder applied codes to the remaining transcripts. The team met periodically to review coding throughout analysis in order to address any content with unclear meaning and ensure consistency in code application. Thematic analysis was then performed through grouping of similar codes and identification of underlying concepts and experiences captured within each code (17). Themes and subthemes were created to capture patterns in the data with exemplary quotes to represent each idea.

Results

A total of 20 participants were interviewed (Table 1). Nine participants were adults with SDS, and 11 participants were caregivers of someone with SDS. Nine of the caregivers were parents of one child with SDS; one caregiver was a parent to two children with SDS, and one caregiver was a bereaved caregiver of a spouse with SDS. Twelve participants lived in the United States, and there was one participant each who lived in Australia, Canada, Ireland, Romania, Netherlands, Philippines, Poland and the United Kingdom.

Affected individuals had a wide range of symptoms. As outlined in Table 2, of the three key features of SDS, most participants reported pancreatic insufficiency (90%) and neutropenia (85%). Three of the adult participants had undergone bone marrow transplants.

Theme 1: SDS Impact on Daily Life

Participants described how numerous aspects of SDS, including management of the condition and treatment regimens, impacted their participation in various life activities, such as career development, extracurriculars, and socialization. This trend was seen in adult patients, pediatric patients, and caregivers of patients. As one adult patient explained: ". . . as I've learned, and as many other people with Shwachman-Diamond have learned, is that [SDS] doesn't just affect your bone marrow. It affects all facets of your life."

Inhibition of Career and Education Opportunities

Both caregivers and adults with SDS commonly described ways that SDS limited career development. Adults with SDS experienced challenges in holding the jobs that they want, frequently attributed to limited physical capacity to perform a job due to energy levels or physical pain. Others were limited by the need for decreased levels of social interactions in a work environment due to their compromised immune system. As a result, people were restricted in terms of the type of employment they could pursue. Many employed participants had to adjust the job type and/or amount of work they perform; as such, many sought positions that allowed for flexibility and accommodations. Many caregivers of children living with SDS shared that they also had to adjust work schedules to accommodate the extensive care required for their children. One stay-at-home mom shared: "Our lifestyle is, schedule-wise, very demanding. [Child's name] has a lot of appointments, and if I had a regular [full-time] job, it would be really hard for me to be able to take him to all of his appointments."

Other parents expressed how, even with accommodating employers, they were still limited in their opportunities for career growth. One participant shared the largest impact on her personal life as: "I would say a delay of career advancement. I have a fantastic boss. He gives me all this time off I could possibly need for my son, and flexible schedule and all that stuff. But I've been passed over for several promotions for people who definitely have less knowledge than I do, [and/or] been with the company less [time] than I have."

Other participants expressed a similar sentiment about absences from educational opportunities. Caregivers of children with SDS discussed needing to take their child out of school often. They felt this impacted their child both academically and socially. Despite these challenges in maintaining consistent attendance in primary and high school, all adult patients in our sample pursued higher education. They often had to consider many factors when deciding where to go, such as staying near home or near adequate medical facilities to accommodate consistent appointments and potential hospitalization.

Many affected adults highlighted their limited ability to participate in sports or physical activities.

People were impacted by fatigue, physical pain, and/or exacerbation of symptoms during exercise. Those who underwent hematopoietic stem cell transplantation reported interrupted activities for an extended period of time such as the inability or limited ability to work, attend school, and/or simply leave their homes or the hospital. Participants expressed frustration at "missing out" on social or personal activities that they wanted to participate in but were prevented from doing so due to their physical limitations. "I think just in general, I'm not normal anymore, I mean, I don't have the energy. Many caregivers took precautions to minimize exposure to pathogens due to their child's compromised immune system, such as avoiding high-touch, indoor environments (e.g., playgrounds and daycares). As a result, caregivers expressed concern that their children missed out on opportunities for play and socialization.

"Right now, I have to-he probably can't-I don't take him to any indoor play zones or play lands. And he would love it. He's super social…some of his social opportunities and opportunities for other children are limited and hindered [because of SDS]."

The symptoms and treatments of SDS also impacted the ability of both individuals and families to travel. Patients and caregivers had to consider daily treatment needs, health risks, and access to care before planning any trips. In many cases, participants were cautious about leaving their trusted clinicians for an extended period of time. Some participants reported planning vacations around locations with nearby hospitals that could manage any potential emergencies. Feelings of Loneliness

Participants described how barriers to socializing contributed to feelings of loneliness. Adult participants reflected on past feelings of isolation from classmates in primary school due to their condition. Caregivers of young patients similarly expressed concerns about their child feeling different or left out. Affected individuals described feeling "othered" by classmates who didn't understand why they were sick or missing class for appointments. As one adult participant with SDS described: "I do remember spending a lot of time in the hospital and then in the [healthcare facility] and then at home recovering. I am very glad that the school that I went to was very supportive and very understanding. Even though, you know, I was a kid, I felt isolated from my friends, I did feel a tad lonely at times." Having a condition that the general population did not know about also made many adult patients feel more emotionally isolated. In many cases, people were hesitant to open up to friends about what they were going through because they did not have the energy to explain SDS or thought that people would not understand. Having experiences that greatly contrasted with their peers' made people feel less known. As one participant shared: "Having that constant feeling of being different, it can definitely take a toll on your mental health and make you feel either emotionally strained or lonely at times." Theme 2: Navigating Healthcare as an SDS Patient or Caregiver People also expressed a wide range of challenges navigating the healthcare system as they sought to diagnose and treat their/their loved one's SDS symptoms. These experiences often took an emotional toll on participants. Most patients and caregivers were heavily involved in aspects of the medical management of SDS, including advocating for necessary procedures, seeking insurance coverage, and educating providers about SDS. Many participants expressed that they felt they would not have received the same level of care if they had not been active advocates for themselves or their loved ones.

Understanding an SDS Diagnosis

Upon receiving a diagnosis, many families reported being given a brief description of SDS, but most did not have extensive conversations with providers about what the future could look like.

Because of this, many participants reported conducting independent research on SDS. Most individuals turned to the internet as their primary source of information. This led to some helpful outcomes, such as finding simplified information on SDS or connecting with SDS organizations.

However, participants also felt responsible for sorting through the extensive information they found on the internet to learn about SDS. In numerous cases, participants faced barriers to accessing information due to paywalls on peer-reviewed research, with some paying out of pocket to access articles. Participants described increased anxiety with the amount of information they found when trying to research and understand SDS.

"When you go on the Google search, and there's a bunch of stuff you see that does cause-create a lot of fear. So, it was a lot of fear in the beginning." Many people turned to online SDS communities through Facebook or other various organizations for information about SDS and others' direct experiences with it. Since the symptoms and severity of those symptoms present on such a large spectrum in patients with SDS, many participants reported feeling overwhelmed with the possible outcomes for themselves or their child. They often reported reading stories of other SDS-impacted families and described not knowing how to make sense of the large range of cases described online.

One participant explained her experience reading social media posts about other children with SDS: " I think it just panicked me even more or kind of, you know, drilled into my brain that all these things that happened to her were going to happen to my son." At the same time, affected individuals and their families frequently found community and reassurance through online groups of people and families affected by SDS. Often, there was a sense of validation as participants commonly reported never meeting someone else with SDS prior to finding an online group. This fostered a strong community of people looking to provide hope and support to each other. "It's actually really cool because there are a few really close friends of mine who have [Shwachman-Diamond syndrome] who I've been able to reach out to and talk about my syndrome with. So, they've been really helpful in either comforting me or just staying in communication just so I know that, hey, I don't have to go through this alone." Since SDS is commonly diagnosed in childhood, caregivers also have to navigate establishing an understanding of SDS for both their affected and unaffected children. Caregivers expressed struggles in determining how much information to tell their children at different ages. Adult patients expressed that many of them did not understand the diagnosis fully until their adult years. "It's not really until…after I went to university that I truly understood what [SDS] was because there was a consultant hematologist that sat me down and went exactly through it. They didn't skirt around the issues of it. They laid it all on the line for me, and I was like, actually, that's quite helpful, really."

Impact of Providers' Knowledge of SDS

Participants described feeling that many healthcare professionals were not knowledgeable about SDS. This impacted the patient experience in all stages of care beginning with the process of receiving a diagnosis. Many participants underwent a diagnostic odyssey in which multiple providers went through a lengthy process of ruling out other diagnoses before SDS. In some cases, parents were the ones to mention SDS as a possible diagnosis after conducting their own research on the symptoms they were seeing in their child. Caregivers described the need to be proactive in requesting specific tests for a potential SDS diagnosis and exploring treatment options even without a genetic diagnosis to improve quality of life.

"We started immediately looking, learning about Shwachman-Diamond [syndrome]. As a matter of fact, the gastroenterologist wasn't going to put him on [pancreatic enzyme replacement therapy] until they got the genetic confirmation. We convinced [his team] to go ahead and put him on the enzymes even without that, while we were waiting for that one month." Many patients and caregivers recounted experiences with doctors who had never heard of SDS.

Participants commonly felt like the "expert in the room" and faced the burden of educating healthcare professionals on the condition, which, in some cases, led to mistrust when receiving care from doctors who admitted they only recently learned what SDS was. As a result, many individuals described becoming strong advocates for themselves or their children. They learned how to quickly educate medical providers on the most serious aspects of SDS and their care. Participants expressed the importance of being trusted and believed by providers, as they sometimes felt like they were questioned when expressing their experiences and needs. People often had to strongly push for their specific needs in emergency situations.

"Having to explain [SDS] and having to advocate to say, 'I need to get a room. I am not able to sit in the waiting room for too long. I cannot risk being exposed to this and that' I think that was definitely an obstacle, a challenge."

In some cases, the lack of knowledge about SDS from medical providers led to denial of care.

Participants shared situations where a medical center did not want to admit or treat them due to providers' lack of familiarity with their condition. Often, people relied on a doctor who was knowledgeable about SDS to help advocate for them in these situations. In countries outside of the US, participants described facing similar barriers to care, as SDS was sometimes not nationally recognized as a disease. In one case, a family was required to internationally ship their child's pancreatic enzyme replacement therapy since this medication was not available in their home country, requiring additional effort and money. Some international participants also highlighted that lack of coverage seemed to result from insurance companies' unfamiliarity with the condition. "[SDS] is not recognized because say if you have cystic fibrosis, which is the nearest comparable thing, people who have that diagnosis get their [pancreatic enzyme replacement therapy medication]. They're exempt from paying."

In some cases, the treatment itself presented barriers. Many caregivers of children with SDS faced difficulties in ensuring their child took the proper medications. Whether it was timing the administration of pancreatic enzyme replacement therapy capsules with their child's mealtimes or trying to get their child to swallow pills, parents reported having to find creative solutions, often leading to an increased physical and emotional toll on the caregivers. "Every doctor we talked to wanted to give us another suggestion of how to get [his medication] down his throat. We tried everything, and it felt like we were tormenting our child all the time, and nobody could understand how difficult that was on a daily basis."

Navigating Multiple Providers and Specialties

People had differing experiences navigating care across multiple specialties. When providers were flexible in scheduling appointments and communicated promptly with other specialists, participants expressed an increased sense of support. People also felt more reassured when their providers were aware of and agreed with their care plan.

In other cases, participants described being responsible for communication between providers to schedule and coordinate care. People reported feeling obligated to confirm with multiple providers that the treatments they, or their child, were receiving were the safest and best option, placing additional burden on participants to communicate their/their child's symptoms and care plan to all providers. As one parent shared: "A lot of advocating and fighting for him, and pushing to get him the best therapies and services, and making sure I'm happy with all the doctors in his care. It's tough. It's like a full-time secretary job" In some cases, patient intervention was necessary for coordinating appropriate care, particularly if patients felt like their specialists weren't considering the different body systems impacted by SDS. One mother explained their experience coordinating a procedure in one department while ensuring they follow guidelines set by another doctor. She explained how her intervention in coordinating care impacted management : "I'm like, 'Wait a minute, hold on. We couldn't use a rectal thermometer on our son because of the potential for an infection. And you want to do what now?' So luckily, we had a hematology oncology appointment the next week… she said, 'Oh, we can do this [procedure], but he needs to go on the granulocyte colony-stimulating factor (GCSF)] to boost his bone marrow before he has [a procedure]'…the GI doctor would never have done that. He didn't think of that."

Discussion

This qualitative study explored experiences of people in the Shwachman-Diamond syndrome community, specifically outlining the impact of an SDS diagnosis on patients' and caregivers' daily lives and the difficulty of navigating the healthcare system as an SDS patient or caregiver.

Participants highlighted how SDS symptoms and the extensive effort required to manage the condition influenced many aspects of their lives, including work/school, socialization, and leisure activities.

Participants reported facing multiple forms of social isolation through both limitations in their ability to participate in social activities and feelings of alienation from people who did not have shared experiences or understanding. In many cases, people connected with SDS community groups through social media or advocacy organizations to address this social isolation and gain knowledge about the condition. These groups gave participants a forum to ask questions and learn about people's direct experiences, yet the wide range of clinical presentations of SDS often could cause increased anxiety and uncertainty in participants. Previous research has shown that these feelings are echoed by people in other rare disease communities. Caregivers and families of those with a rare disease have expressed feeling misunderstood by their friends and families who did not share similar experiences (18) (19) (20). Rare disease groups, often found through online settings, can provide a source of comfort and support from people who have had similar experiences (21, 22). At the same time, research has shown how online groups can require people to spend time sorting through what information is relevant to them and ensuring what they are accessing is reliable (22). Along with this, our research shows how online communities may also increase anxiety when people are seeing a large range of the possible impacts and outcomes of a disease.

Overall, our participants found self-advocacy necessary to find providers who are familiar with SDS or educate those who were unfamiliar about the condition. Qualitative research on other rare diseases has similarly reported that many aspects of patients' and caregivers' lives, including career, education and socialization, are impacted by the symptoms and management of their condition (8, 10, 20). In a systematic literature review comparing experiences of adults with various rare diseases, similar themes were found with difficulties navigating the healthcare system (23). Not unique to the SDS community, many rare disease patients have also reported feeling their care is impacted by the lack of knowledge about their condition in the medical field (23, 24). The SDS participants reported how this also impacted insurance coverage and medication availability, as SDS only recently received an ICD-10 code in the US in 2023 (25).

Additionally, research within the rare disease community has highlighted experiences of "expert patients" who are tasked with researching and understanding their condition and subsequently explaining it to providers (23, 26, 27). These common experiences highlight the need for broader changes in how care is provided to those affected by rare disease (28, 29).

Clinical Implications

As many of the challenges faced by SDS patients and caregivers were related to the lack of providers knowledgeable about SDS, increased awareness of SDS in the medical field and more resources for providers to quickly and easily understand the condition could greatly benefit SDS families. One method that has been implemented in the United Kingdom to aid in this process is a patient passport (26, 30). This document, developed through collaboration between providers and patients, is given to new providers as a quick outline of the most important information about a patient and their rare condition. This resource may incorporate information from the most up-to-date consensus guidelines for SDS while focusing on the specific patient's symptoms and treatments (31). A similar resource has been developed for SDS to help kids in the school setting better explain their diagnosis to teachers and classmates (32). Applying this resource in the medical setting may be especially beneficial for people with SDS, as the condition has a wide range of symptoms and severity that impact each patient differently.

Research shows that implementing this type of resource can benefit both providers and patients in communicating care history and needs (26).

Often, participants were put in the position of ensuring that the care they were receiving across different hospitals and/or specialties was aligned. Regular meetings between the healthcare team and collaboration with experts could enhance care coordination and provide space to discuss ways to improve various treatment plans. Patient care navigators and social workers also serve as a resource to aid in the delivery of care. Research has shown that the involvement of primary care providers (PCPs) in coordination of care has been beneficial to those with complex care needs (33). In other areas of disease, such as oncology, multidisciplinary frameworks have been used to improve care such as through multidisciplinary programs or conferences (34) (35) (36). Additionally, the integration of holistic care pathways has been used to facilitate rare disease care in many healthcare systems, primarily in Europe (37) (38) (39). Care pathways develop evidence-based care recommendations through consensus guidelines and national expertise, and translate them into the context of local healthcare systems and patientspecific needs (35). While care pathways require integration at multiple levels, they have been shown to be beneficial to patients with conditions that require high levels of care coordination (38, 39). Implementation of innovative approaches to care coordination for SDS patients could release the burdens of navigating care from patients and caregivers, ultimately reducing their stress and the level of time required to keep up with care management.

Study Limitations and Future Directions

As recruitment occurred solely through SDS Alliance's SDS-GPS study platform, all participants were actively involved in this community and seeking opportunities to participate in future research. The experiences of individuals who haven't had the capacity to access care or seek out patient communities might differ from the reports of our participants. Additionally, as we only conducted interviews in English, there was limited capacity to understand the full range of experiences for international and non-English-speaking participants. However, through the inclusion of participants from nine different countries, we were able to identify and describe shared experiences of SDS across various healthcare systems. Future research with international participants could identify healthcare-system-specific vulnerabilities and strengths to support optimal implementation of clinical programs to improve SDS care. Additionally, our inclusion criteria for participants was limited to caregivers and adults with SDS. As many patients with SDS are children, assessing the perspectives of pediatric patients could provide more insight into the understanding of SDS and its impact on lives in childhood. Previous research focused on the views of children with rare diseases has been able to provide insights into their feelings and day-to-day experiences that may not be captured as fully through interviews with caregivers (40).

Conclusions

In recent years, there has been an increased emphasis on learning directly from patients about their experiences with rare disease and hopes for future change (13, 41, 42). This study provides a foundation for understanding the lived experiences of people with SDS and highlights the various ways that care can be improved to better support this community. Future research can focus on gaining a deeper understanding of specific experiences within the SDS community, such as experiences with a hematopoietic stem cell transplant, the transition from pediatric to adult care, and the cancer journey of those who developed MDS, AML, and/or another malignancy. SDS families face numerous challenges living with SDS and navigating care, but collaborative efforts from providers, healthcare systems, and patient advocacy groups to improve awareness, coordinate care, and innovate treatments hold the promise of beginning to address their needs.
