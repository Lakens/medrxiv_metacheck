Reporting p values imprecisely (e.g., p < .05) reduces transparency, reproducibility, and re-use (e.g., in p value meta-analyses). Best practice is to report exact p-values with three decimal places (e.g., p = .032) unless p values are smaller than 0.001, in which case you can use p < .001.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (e.g., “decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

What we found

We found 3 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Metacheck does not yet analyze automatically whether sentences which include non-significant p-values are correct, but we recommend manually checking the sentences below for possible misinterpreted non-significant p values.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. Abstract

Background: Residential nature-based programs may support resilience and wellbeing among people experiencing substantial psychological distress. We examined changes following an independently organized retreat.

Methods: This prospective observational study followed 18 adults attending a five-day resilience retreat in Finland. The study did not undertake diagnostic assessments or assign retreat participation. The primary outcome was CD-RISC-10 resilience at 21 days after the retreat. Secondary outcomes included perceived stress, affect, wellbeing, self-efficacy and posttraumatic stress symptoms. Analyses used mixed-effects models and paired Wilcoxon tests.

Results: At 21 days, the mixed model showed a 5.08-point increase in CD-RISC-10 (95% CI 2.51 to 7.65; p = 0.00011). The estimate persisted on a small-sample reference (p = 0.00034) and among the 14 participants with an intake reading (+4.59 points; p = 0.0014). Perceived stress, positive and negative affect, well-being and selfefficacy also registered improvement (each paired p < 0.01). Well-being rose by 21.66 points on the 0 to 100 WHO-5 index (95% Wald CI 14.29 to 29.03). Score improvements were largest at retreat completion, partly subsided by 14 days where measured but were sustained to the 21-day mark. Evidence for improvement in posttraumatic stress symptoms was less definitive.

Limitations: Uncontrolled design and a small sample limit causal interpretation.

Conclusions: Higher self-reported resilience and improved well-being persisted three weeks after the retreat. The findings support controlled evaluation of the program.

1. Introduction

Exposure to collective trauma can have enduring effects on mental health. In a nationwide Israeli cohort assessed before and after the attacks of October 7 (2023), about one in five adults followed a vulnerable or chronic course of posttraumatic stress symptoms (Levin et al., 2025). Resilience, the capacity of one to adapt, recover and maintain sustained functioning under stress and adversity, is increasingly understood as an adjustable capacity rather than a fixed one. Scores on the Connor-Davidson Resilience Scale can increase following treatment (Connor and Davidson, 2003). Supporting resilience capacity improvement alongside symptom management is therefore a relevant and effectual public-health objective.

Several intervention paradigms address resilience capacity, among them resilience training, nature-based programs and paced-breathing practices. Meta-analyses of resilience training report pooled effects of about 0.4 to 0.5 standard deviations, across 25 randomized trials (Leppin et al., 2014), 11 randomized trials (Joyce et al., 2018) and a multilevel analysis of 268 studies, though many of them were below practical significance (Liu et al., 2020). Across 50 studies of nature-based outdoor activities, 16 randomized trials showed reduced depressive and anxiety symptoms and increased positive affect, with uncontrolled studies largely agreeing (Coventry et al., 2021).

Nature-based mindfulness training showed a medium pre-post effect across 25 studies of mostly low quality (Djernis et al., 2019). Forest bathing reduced depression and anxiety across 36 studies (Siah et al., 2023). Additionally, paced breathing near six breaths per minute, the resonance frequency of the cardiovascular system (Lehrer et al., 2000), reduced stress and anxiety against control when training was combined with biofeedback instrumentation, in a meta-analysis of 24 studies (g 0.83; Goessl et . al., 2017). A meta-analysis of 12 randomized breathwork trials found a smaller effect on stress (g -0.35; Fincham et al., 2023).

Residential retreat programs combine these paradigms over several days away from daily routines. A systematic review of 23 retreat studies found benefits over every retreat program but limited by small samples, poorly reported methodological rigor and scarce follow-up measurements (Naidoo et al., 2018). Improvements measured at the end of a recreational break can fade after the return to routine (de Bloom et al., 2009).

Determining the enduring change is further complicated by the outcome measure. The CD-RISC is among the best-rated resilience scales (Windle et al., 2011), although a systematic review of its measurement found the evidence on its responsiveness limited (Sharif-Nia et al., 2024).

In this study, we report a prospective observational evaluation of an independently organized five-day nature-based retreat in Finland. The research assessed the existing program without appointing participation or diagnostic assessments. This resilience-focused retreat combined guided nature exposure, paced breathing training, mindfulness training, moderate physical activity and group reflection sessions, with elective sauna and cold-water immersion sessions. The primary outcome was self-reported resilience on the standardized CD-RISC-10 questionnaire at 21 days post-retreat. Secondary outcomes were perceived stress, affect, well-being, self-efficacy and posttraumatic stress symptoms; future-event specificity analysis was assessed in a subset of participants as an exploratory measure. Supplement S1 documents the departures from the pre-specified plan that apply to the analyses reported here, while section 2.5 describes the analyses. The physiological-domain OSF plan also specified a secondary analysis of the questionnaire outcomes, reported . in Supplement S2; the physiological measurements are the subject of a companion paper.

2. Methods

>Methods

2.1 Design and registration

This prospective observational study evaluated an existing retreat program under a Ben-Gurion University of the Negev (BGU) umbrella protocol. Participation in the retreat was not assigned by the investigators. The umbrella analysis plan designates the CD-RISC-10 as the primary outcome, assessed here at the end of the 21-day follow-up period (section 2.5). The autonomic-neuroscience plan was registered on the Open Science Framework on June 17, 2026, before data collection began (DOI 10.17605/OSF.IO/7WKM5; Amendments 1 to 6). The pre-registration governs the secondary analysis, detailed in Supplement S2. The ethics approval procedure is described in section 2.6.

2.2 Participants

All participants were over 18 years old. Participants self-reported significant exposure to stress. No diagnostic assessment was performed in this study. At intake, 13 of 15 PCL-5 scores were at or above the screening cutoff of 33 (Bovin et al., 2016), and 13 of 14 WHO-5 index scores were at or below 50, the index's cutoff for poor well-being (Topp et al., 2015). Pre-specified exclusion criteria were acute suicidality or a recent suicide attempt, active untreated psychosis, active severe substance-use disorder along with any condition making participation unsafe, pregnancy where thermal exposure was included, and inability to consent or to wear the measurement device (Empatica EmbracePlus bracelet). Declining research participation did not affect retreat participation. Age and sex, the registered covariates, were held on the intake record apart from the research data and were not linked to the coded dataset, to limit identification risk in a small cohort (Deviation 75). Age on the intake record averaged 35.7 years (SD 8.9; range 22 to 49).

The recruitment period was February 15 to May 17, 2026. Intake batteries were completed on July 29 and August 2, 2026, with one late completion on the first retreat day. The retreat ran from August 9 to 13, 2026. Follow-up concluded at 21 days postretreat, on September 3. The collected dataset was frozen on September 5. Four of the 18 participants failed to complete the baseline battery. At day 21 post-retreat, 13 participants had paired CD-RISC-10 scores and 14 had paired PCL-5 scores. Table 1 and Supplement S3 document the collection cascade and instances of missing data.

2.3 Intervention

The intervention was a five-day, four-night residential nature-based resilience retreat in Finland, delivered by facilitators of the operator, Amoreh Oy. The program combined guided nature exposure, resonance-frequency paced breathing training, mindfulness training and meditation practice sessions along with moderate physical activity and group reflection sessions, with optional thermal contrast (sauna and cold-water immersion). Participants were asked to be phone-free during sessions, with brief inapp check-ins at session boundaries. The program by day and the app's schedule are given in Supplement S6.

2.4 Measures

Questionnaires were administered in a dedicated companion app, offered in Hebrew and English, at intake, upon retreat completion (Day 5), and 14 and 21 days postretreat. The licensed Hebrew CD-RISC-10 was used throughout. All 18 participants completed the Day-5 battery in the app (Supplement S3). On retreat days, the app preceded each self-report assessment except the sleep diary with a guided pacedbreathing exercise that could not be skipped. A visual pacer set 26 breaths at 6 breaths per minute (5 s in, 5 s out; 4 min 20 s), with brief on-screen guidance instructions with no sound.

The primary outcome, the CD-RISC-10 questionnaire (Campbell-Sills and Stein, 2007; Connor and Davidson, 2003), has 10 items scored 0 to 4 (total 0 to 40) over the past month. The secondary outcomes were the Perceived Stress Scale test (PSS-10; Cohen and Williamson, 1988): 10 items, 0 to 4, items 4, 5, 7 and 8 reversed; total 0 to 40; past 7 days. The Positive and Negative Affect Schedule test (PANAS; Watson et al., 1988): 20 items, 1 to 5, momentary; positive and negative affect 10 to 50 each.

The PTSD Checklist test for DSM-5 (PCL-5; Blevins et al., 2015; Bovin et al., 2016): 20 items, 0 to 4; total 0 to 80; past month. It was administered at intake, at home about 7 days after the retreat, and at +21 days. In this study, the PCL-5 was utilized as a symptom characterization instrument and not as participant screening. The Well-Being Index test (WHO-5; Topp et al., 2015): 5 items, 0 to 5, past two weeks; scaled 0 to 100. The New General Self-Efficacy Scale test (NGSE-8; Chen et al., 2001): 8 items, 1 to 5; total 8 to 40 points.

The exploratory outcome, the autobiographical future-thinking task (AFTT; optional), comprised five cued future-event narratives typed at intake and the last day of the retreat (Day 5). They were scored for episodic specificity, the proportion of details that are internal (specific to the imagined event), under the Autobiographical Interview classification (Levine et al., 2002) adapted for future events (Addis et al., 2008). Its rating, the second rater's agreement and its result are detailed in Supplement S2. At 14 and 21 days, participants who did not complete the scheduled in-app battery received the same closed-response items through a linked question sheet and returned their answers by message, for the reasons given in Supplement S3. Both routes were self-administered. Mood check-ins and sleep diaries are reported descriptively (Supplement S5). Physiological data were collected alongside the questionnaires and are reported in a separate companion paper. The registered on-site physiological primary reported no change (N = 10; 95% BCa CI -0.20 to +0.03), informing the plan to measure wholenight heart rate variability at home after the next implementation.

2.5 Statistical analysis

To make use of every available measurement, change from intake was calculated for each battery instrument (the PANAS by subscale) with a linear mixed-effects model.

The model had timepoint as a categorical fixed factor (with intake as reference) and a random intercept per participant. It was fitted by restricted maximum likelihood to every available observation at four timepoints (the NGSE-8 without +14; the PCL-5 at intake, the early at-home reading and +21), with Wald 95% CIs and p values shown. Two sensitivity analyses of the same model are reported in Supplement S4. In the first, each timepoint contrast was tested against a t distribution instead of the normal reference, with the between-within degrees of freedom: the number of observations minus the number of participants minus the number of timepoint terms (the smallsample reference). In the second, because the model assumes that observations are missing at random given the participant, the model was refitted on the participants who had an intake reading of the instrument. The registered family analysis is reported, with within-family false-discovery-rate control, in Supplement S2.

Paired contrasts against intake used every complete pair. They reported the median change with a bias-corrected and accelerated (BCa) bootstrap 95% CI (DiCiccio and Efron, 1996; Efron, 1987) (10,000 resamples, seed 20260809) with the percentile bootstrap in place of BCa in cells with N below 7, as registered. They also reported Cohen's dz (Lakens, 2013) with Hedges' g and paired small-sample factor J = 1 -3/(4N -5) (Hedges, 1981). Its interval was taken over the resamples in which g could be computed and not reported when more than 1% failed (in the forest plot, the dz interval is the g interval divided by the same factor). They used two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test, zero differences discarded. Where a median interval is not computable, the mean change and its BCa interval are reported beside it. Wilcoxon p below 0.05, 0.01 and 0.001 is marked *, ** and ***. To test the robustness of the results, we calculated the largest Cook's distance of each contrast and reported it against the screen of 4/N. No participant was removed.

The OSF plan's pre-registered secondary analysis (Supplement S2) groups CD-RISC-10 and PCL-5 at +21 and PSS-10 and PANAS at Day 5 under Benjamini-Hochberg false-discovery-rate control at q < 0.10 (Benjamini and Hochberg, 1995), together with AFTT specificity as a second family and leave-one-subject-out sensitivity.

This study was approved by the Ben-Gurion University of the Negev Human Subjects Research Committee under the umbrella protocol (#1295-3). All participants in this study signed written informed consent before any data were collected. No adverse event was reported.

3. Results

>Results

3.1 Participants and follow-up

All 18 participants provided an end-of-retreat assessment. At 14 days post-retreat, all 18 provided follow-up data; at 21 days, 17 did so (Fig. 1). Baseline availability and paired sample sizes varied by instrument (Table 1; Supplement S3).

3.2 Primary outcome

From intake to +21 days post-retreat, the CD-RISC-10 change in the linear mixed model over the four timepoints was +5.08 points (95% Wald CI 2.51 to 7.65; p = 0.00011; 67 observations from 18 participants), with p = 0.00034 on the small-sample reference and +4.59 points (p = 0.0014) among the 14 participants with a complete baseline reading (Table S4b; Table S4d). In the paired contrast, the median CD-RISC-10 change was +4.0 points (N = 13; 95% BCa CI 0.0 to 7.0; Cohen's dz = 0.83; Hedges' g = 0.78, 95% BCa CI 0.31 to 1.26; Wilcoxon p = 0.0098, q = 0.012 in the registered family; Table 1; Fig. 2A). One participant exceeded the pre-declared Cook's distance screen of 4/N and was retained. Every leave-one-subject-out fold kept p below 0.05 (with the largest being 0.020; Supplement S2).

3.3 Secondary outcomes

All six outcome measures were assessed at both Day 5 and 21 days. Paired changes from baseline persisted to day 21 post-retreat (each unadjusted Wilcoxon p ≤ 0.0098), although changes in score units were smaller than at Day 5 (Table 1; Fig. 2). Perceived stress and negative affect were lower, and positive affect, well-being and self-efficacy scores higher than baseline. In the mixed model, the secondary changes at +21 days of the PSS-10, both PANAS scales, the WHO-5 and the NGSE-8, all but the PCL-5's, . kept p below 0.05 on the small-sample reference and among the participants with a baseline reading (Supplement S4; Fig. 3; Fig. 4). Notably, four members of the registered family, the CD-RISC-10, the PSS-10 and both PANAS scales, met its criterion under every single omission (Supplement S2).

The change in posttraumatic stress symptoms on the +21-day contrast (Supplement S1) was small and uncertain (PCL-5; N = 14; median change -6.0 points; Hedges' g = -0.24, 95% BCa CI -1.08 to 0.41; Cohen's dz = -0.26; Wilcoxon p = 0.065). Applying the registered family's correction gives q = 0.065, below the 0.10 family threshold, but the effect-size interval included zero, and single participant omissions moved p from 0.0059 to 0.11, with 1 of 14 crossing 0.05 (Supplement S2; Fig. 5).

The median change in AFTT specificity, the exploratory measurement, from intake to the last day of the retreat was +0.092 (N = 6; 95% percentile CI -0.008 to 0.195; p = 0.16; Supplement S2; Fig. S1B).

4. Discussion

>Discussion

4.1 Principal findings

In this study we have found the resilience score was higher at 21 days post-retreat than at baseline. The protocol's mixed model estimated a change of +5.08 points on the CD-RISC-10 (95% Wald CI 2.51 to 7.65; p = 0.00011; 67 observations from 18 participants). Perceived stress, positive and negative affect, well-being and general self-efficacy had also improved at +21 days (Table 1). For each of these scores the change in score points was largest upon retreat completion, partly subsided by +14 days post-retreat where measured, but with sustained improvement to +21 days postretreat compared to baseline.

The evidence for improvement in posttraumatic stress symptoms was less decisive.

The effect was small with an interval that included zero, although the result passed the 10% threshold. Nevertheless, the principal signal in this cohort applies to resilience and broader psychological well-being, rather than claiming amelioration of PTSD symptoms in particular.

4.2 Comparison with previous studies

Direct benchmarks for CD-RISC-10 change are limited. A randomized resilience trial in adolescents and young adults with cancer reported a 3.0-point benefit over usual care at six months (Rosenberg et al., 2018). A four-week heart rate variability biofeedback program also improved resilience relative to a wait-list, with follow-up maintenance (Cortez-Neavel et al., 2026). These controlled estimates cannot be directly compared with the within-group changes reported (Morris and DeShon, 2002) and therefore do not claim a clinical pertinence threshold for this cohort. A closer design analog, an uncontrolled week-long wellness retreat, reported improvements maintained at six weeks among participants followed up (Cohen et al., 2017).

Follow-up improvements have also been reported after related programs. Studies with recreational intervention comparison groups illustrate that improvement during a residential stay can occur in both groups, highlighting the importance of a comparator when attributing change to specific program components (Epel et al., 2016), as in one trial reported twice (Hwang et al., 2018; Kwak et al., 2019). Meta-analyses report maintained gains following meditation retreats (Khoury et al., 2017) and partial persistence after positive-psychology interventions (Carr et al., 2024).

4.3 Strengths and limitations

The strengths of this study include longitudinal follow-up through 21 days post-retreat, high follow-up completion, and rigorous reporting of individual trajectories and effect sizes with uncertainty intervals with its secondary analysis publicly pre-registered.

Nevertheless, this study has several limitations: the absence of a comparison group and the small sample size are chief among these. Age and sex were not linked to the research dataset, to preserve participants' privacy in a small group, which limits generalizability. The questionnaire administration route varied: where some participants could not complete the follow-up batteries through the app, a messagebased follow-up route was served. Four end-of-retreat assessments were delayed due to return voyage connectivity issues and Shabbat considerations. The study measured perceived resilience, not subsequent functioning under a new stressor. The Hebrew CD-RISC-10 has previously been used in an Israeli sample of 161 physicians, in which it demonstrated good internal consistency (Cronbach's α = 0.869; Joachim et al., 2024). The findings in this study are pre-registered as hypothesis-generating.

4.4 Implications

The findings compel controlled evaluation of nature-based residential programs. The partial attenuation after return to routine supports testing post-retreat impact and follow-up. A future study could compare participants with a prospectively defined waitlist, with allocation and assessment procedures designed to limit selection bias.

Standardized assessment conditions, documented activity and sleep, and resting or home-night physiological measurements would help distinguish psychological change from short-term physical load.

4.5 Conclusions

In this cohort, self-reported resilience remained higher three weeks after a five-day nature-based retreat, alongside lower perceived stress and negative affect and higher positive affect, well-being and self-efficacy. These consistent within-group changes support a prospective controlled evaluation.
