Reporting p values imprecisely (e.g., p < .05) reduces transparency, reproducibility, and re-use (e.g., in p value meta-analyses). Best practice is to report exact p-values with three decimal places (e.g., p = .032) unless p values are smaller than 0.001, in which case you can use p < .001.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (e.g., “decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 5 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Background: Psychiatric disorders show substantial overlap across diagnostic categories while differing markedly in clinical expression. Whether these shared abnormalities and individual differences are embedded in stable functional brain architecture rather than in state-specific connectivity remains unclear. Methods: We integrated functional connectomes across one resting-state and six task-based fMRI conditions in 377 participants from the YaleNeuroConnect cohort, including 223 healthy controls and 154 individuals with psychiatric disorders. Principal component analysis was applied to each participant' s functional connectivity data across the seven states to derive a state-independent connectivity representation. Network-based statistics then identified components showing significant case-control differences. Cross-state consistency was assessed, and findings were validated in an independent UCLA cohort of 177 participants. Associations between component expression and clinical measures were further examined within the Yale psychiatric group.

Results: Cross-paradigm connectivity revealed two reproducible patterns of state-independent network alteration. One pattern showed reduced connectivity among limbic, somatomotor and salience systems, whereas the other showed stronger cerebellar coupling with limbic and somatomotor systems. Both patterns showed consistent case-control differences across functional states and generalized to an independent UCLA cohort. Their clinical relevance, however, differed. Greater expression of the cerebello-cortical connectivity pattern was associated with lower positive affect, greater psychopathology and more everyday executive difficulties, whereas the reduced limbic-somatomotor-salience connectivity pattern showed no significant clinical associations.

Conclusions: These findings delineate a stable reorganization of large-scale functional architecture across psychiatric disorders and suggest that broadly expressed psychiatric abnormalities and individual clinical variation may be differentially represented within the altered connectome.

Introduction

Psychiatric disorders show substantial overlap across diagnostic categories while also varying markedly in their clinical expression, posing a challenge for understanding their underlying neurobiology. Symptoms, cognitive difficulties and functional impairments frequently extend across conventional diagnostic boundaries, while individuals with the same diagnosis can differ considerably in symptom severity and everyday functioning (1, 2). In line with this clinical heterogeneity, psychiatric neuroscience has increasingly moved beyond disorder-specific abnormalities toward transdiagnostic and dimensional approaches that seek neural features shared across disorders as well as neural variation related to individual differences in psychopathology (2) (3) (4) (5). These lines of work suggest that psychiatric disorders may be better characterized by distributed abnormalities in large-scale brain systems than by isolated changes confined to individual diagnoses.

Functional connectivity has provided an important systems-level framework for this effort. Altered interactions among large-scale networks involved in salience processing, affective regulation, sensorimotor function and higher-order cognition have been reported across schizophrenia, bipolar disorde and other psychiatric conditions (6) (7) (8) (9). Resting-state connectome studies have further identified transdiagnostic patterns that generalize across diagnostic groups and relate to dimensional psychiatric symptoms (2, 10). These findings together indicate that functional connectivity captures both common psychiatric dysfunction and individual clinical variation.

Despite this progress, existing studies have been limited in several respects. First, most transdiagnostic connectome studies have examined psychiatric abnormalities within a single functional state. Functional connectivity, however, varies across cognitive contexts and reflects both relatively stable features of individual brain organization and state-dependent modulation (11, 12). It therefore remains unclear whether reported transdiagnostic abnormalities represent persistent features of functional architecture or are partly specific to the state in which they were measured. Second, studies identifying shared case-control abnormalities and studies examining dimensional brain-clinical relationships have often pursued these aims separately. Whether the neural features that broadly distinguish psychiatric populations from healthy individuals are also those that account for variation in clinical expression remains insufficiently understood. Third, although cross-paradigm connectivity has been developed to isolate functional organization that is shared across cognitive states, its psychiatric applications have so far focused largely on schizophrenia and psychosis-spectrum phenotypes (13) (14) (15). Whether state-independent functional architecture reveals reproducible abnormalities across a more diagnostically heterogeneous psychiatric population remains unknown.

Cross-paradigm connectivity provides an opportunity to address these limitations by integrating connectivity acquired under multiple functional conditions and attenuating paradigm-specific variation while retaining the connectivity structure that is consistently expressed across contexts (12). Previous work has shown that such state-independent connectivity patterns can capture clinically relevant features of psychosis. Cerebello-thalamo-cortical hyperconnectivity across paradigms has been associated with psychosis vulnerability and subsequent conversion (15), while other cross-paradigm signatures have been linked to schizophrenia polygenic risk and individualized antipsychotic response (13, 14). These findings highlight the utility of state-independent connectivity for stable psychiatric brain features. Applying this approach transdiagnostically may reveal which abnormalities persist across states and relate to clinical heterogeneity.

In this study, we addressed these questions using the YaleNeuroConnect dataset, which combines clinical phenotyping with resting state and task conditions. We derived participant-specific state-independent connectivity representations from one resting-state and six task-based fMRI condition. Specifically, we asked three questions. First, can state-independent connectivity identify reproducible network alterations that differentiate a diagnostically heterogeneous psychiatric population from healthy controls? Second, are these alterations consistently expressed across distinct functional states and generalizable to an independent transdiagnostic cohort with a different diagnostic composition and task battery? Third, how do individual differences in these state-independent connectivity alterations relate to clinical and cognitive functions? By addressing these questions within a single framework, we sought to characterize the stable functional architecture of psychiatric disorders and its relationship to individual clinical variation. Methods

Participants

We analyzed the publicly available YaleNeuroConnect dataset (16), which provides multimodal imaging and extensive clinical phenotyping in participants recruited from the local community and from Yale clinical programs. All participants gave written informed consent under a protocol approved by the Yale Institutional Review Board (HIC #2000020891).

The present analysis used the functional connectivity data released with the YaleNeuroConnect resource. Of the 410 participants in the original cohort, 33 were excluded for imaging artifacts, excessive head motion (>30% of frames with framewise displacement >0.5 mm), incomplete fMRI runs or incomplete demographic data. The final discovery sample comprised 377 participants: 223 healthy controls (112 females, 111 males; age 30.63 +/-11.06 years) and 154 individuals with psychiatric disorders (86 females, 68 males; age 31.70 +/-11.25 years), who did not differ in age (t = -0.91, P = 0.362) or sex distribution (chi-square = 0.94, P = 0.332). Diagnostic overlap was substantial, with 121 of 154 individuals (78.6%) meeting criteria for two or more diagnostic categories. Demographic characteristics are summarized in Table 1 and diagnostic frequencies in Table S1.

Clinical and cognitive assessments

Clinical and cognitive functioning was assessed using a comprehensive battery of self-report questionnaires and standardized neuropsychological tests. Clinical measures included the Adult Temperament Questionnaire (ATQ), Behavior Rating Inventory of Executive Function-Adult Version (BRIEF-A), Brief Symptom Inventory (BSI), Interpersonal Reactivity Index (IRI), Positive and Negative Affect Schedule-Expanded Form (PANAS-X), Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI), and Perceived Stress Scale (PSS). Cognitive assessments included the Boston Naming Test (BNT), Wide Range Assessment of Memory and Learning (WRAML), Wechsler Adult Intelligence Scale (WAIS), Delis-Kaplan Executive Function System (D-KEFS), and Wechsler Abbreviated Scale of Intelligence (WASI). Overall, individuals with psychiatric disorders exhibited greater symptom burden and poorer cognitive performance than healthy controls (Table S2).

MRI acquisition

See Supplementary materials for scanning parameters (17). Each participant completed two resting-state and six task runs of 6 min 49 s each, providing more than 48 min of functional imaging within an approximately 90-min session.

fMRI conditions

During rest, participants kept their eyes open and viewed a fixation cross. Only the first resting-state run, acquired before the task battery, was analyzed, so that resting data were not influenced by preceding tasks. The six task runs comprised Card Guessing (18) (19) (20), Reading the Mind in the Eyes (21), the gradual-onset continuous performance task (22), movie watching, n-back working memory (23) (24) (25) and the stop-signal task (26, 27). Task order was counterbalanced across participants and stimuli were presented using Psychtoolbox.

Together, the seven states sampled reward processing, social cognition, sustained attention, naturalistic perception, working memory, inhibitory control and unconstrained task-free activity; detailed task procedures are described in the YaleNeuroConnect data descriptor (16).

MRI preprocessing and functional connectivity

We used the preprocessed connectivity matrices released by the YaleNeuroConnect investigators. The pipeline included skull stripping, motion correction, nonlinear normalization to MNI space, functional-to-structural registration, nuisance regression, temporal filtering and correction for head-motion effects (see Supplementary materials); scripts are available in the YaleNeuroConnect processing repository.

Functional connectivity was defined using the Shen 268-node functional atlas (28). Regional mean time series were extracted for each participant and condition, and pairwise Pearson correlations were Fisher r-to-z transformed, yielding a 268 x 268 matrix for each run. As described above, only the first resting-state run and the six task runs were analyzed.

Cross-paradigm estimation of state-independent functional connectivity

State-independent functional connectivity was estimated using the cross-paradigm connectivity framework described by Cao (12). For each participant, the seven Fisher z-transformed matrices were vectorized across 35,778 unique edges and entered into a participant-level principal component analysis across functional states. The first principal component (PC1) was retained as the dominant pattern shared across the seven conditions and reshaped into a symmetric edgewise connectivity matrix.

We also retained the variance explained by PC1 and the contribution of each state to PC1, to verify that the dominant cross-paradigm representation was expressed similarly in both groups and was not driven by a single paradigm. The resulting participant-specific PC1 matrix is referred to as the state-independent connectivity representation.

Case-control alterations in state-independent connectivity

To identify alterations associated with psychiatric status, we compared participant-specific state-independent matrices between groups using edgewise general linear models with age, sex and race as covariates. This analysis identifies distributed patterns that differentiate the diagnostically heterogeneous psychiatric sample from controls; diagnosis-level analyses would be required to establish how uniformly each pattern is shared across individual disorders.

Network-based statistics (NBS) controlled family-wise error over connected sets of altered edges (29). Edges surviving a primary threshold of P < 0.001 (uncorrected) were grouped into connected components, which were then evaluated using 10,000 permutations of group labels and the maximal component-size distribution (P FWE < 0.05). Increased and decreased components were assessed separately, and significant edges were assigned to Shen large-scale networks for descriptive summaries.

To assess whether NBS effects were present in the original connectivity space rather than specific to the PCA representation, each significant edge mask was projected back onto the seven Fisher z-transformed matrices. Mean connectivity across the edges of each mask was computed for each participant and state, and group differences were assessed separately for each state with false-discovery-rate correction across the seven paradigms.

Independent validation of the identified connectivity components in the UCLA CNP cohort

Generalizability was evaluated in the UCLA Consortium for Neuropsychiatric Phenotyping cohort (CNP) (30), a public dataset (OpenNeuro ds000030) that includes multiple psychiatric diagnoses and functional imaging across rest and several task conditions. Recruitment, phenotyping and consent procedures are detailed in the original publication.

After quality control, the validation sample comprised 177 participants (Table S3): 74 healthy controls and 103 individuals with psychiatric disorders (33 with schizophrenia, 38 with bipolar disorder and 32 with attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder). Data were preprocessed with fMRIPrep 24.1.1 using procedures comparable to those applied in the discovery cohort (see Supplementary materials). Seven states were analyzed: resting state, Balloon Analogue Risk Task, paired-associate memory encoding and retrieval, stop-signal task, spatial capacity working memory and task switching (26, (31) (32) (33) (34).

The same cross-paradigm PCA was applied independently in the UCLA cohort. The two Yale components were carried forward as fixed edge masks, and mean state-independent connectivity within each mask was calculated for each participant. Case-control differences in these component-expression scores were tested with age, sex and education as covariates, and the masks were also projected onto each UCLA functional state to evaluate the consistency of effect direction.

Clinical relevance of the identified connectivity components

For each significant NBS component, a component-expression score was calculated as the mean state-independent connectivity across its edges, yielding one score per component for each participant. Within the psychiatric group of the discovery cohort, partial correlations related these scores to the available clinical and cognitive measures, controlling for age, sex and race; two-sided P values were false-discovery-rate corrected across the tested associations.

Results

State-independent functional architecture across seven functional states

Across the seven analyzed functional states, PC1 accounted for 56.16% of the total variance in the discovery cohort. This dominant component therefore captured more than half of the cross-state variation in functional connectivity and was retained as the participant-level state-independent connectivity representation. The proportion of variance explained by PC1 did not differ between the psychiatric and healthy-control groups (t = 1.084, P = 0.279). Contributions from the individual paradigms were also comparable between groups; no paradigm-specific PC1 loading survived correction for multiple comparisons (all FDR-adjusted P > 0.35). These results indicate that subsequent group differences were not attributable to a gross difference in the overall contribution of one functional state to the cross-paradigm representation.

State-independent connectivity alterations in the transdiagnostic discovery cohort

NBS identified two distributed connectivity components that differed between the psychiatric and healthy-control groups. One component showed lower state-independent connectivity in the psychiatric group, whereas the other showed higher connectivity. The two patterns involved different large-scale network configurations rather than a uniform whole-brain shift in connectivity.

The decreased-connectivity component comprised 66 edges connecting 35 nodes (P FWE < 0.05), with alterations predominantly involving limbic, somatomotor and salience systems (Fig. 2a). The most prominent connections linked somatomotor and limbic regions, with additional involvement of limbic-salience and somatomotor-salience coupling.

The increased-connectivity component comprised 112 edges connecting 97 nodes (P FWE < 0.05) and was primarily characterized by enhanced cortico-cerebellar connectivity, particularly involving limbic and somatomotor regions (Fig. 2b), with additional but smaller contributions from other network pairs. Expression of the increased and decreased components was strongly inversely related across individuals (r = -0.78, P < 0.001).

Cross-state consistency of the identified connectivity alterations

We next projected the two NBS-derived edge masks onto the original Fisher z-transformed matrices. Because these masks were defined in the discovery analysis, this analysis assesses cross-state consistency rather than providing an independent validation. For each participant and state, component-expression scores were calculated as the mean connectivity across the predefined edges of each component.

The increased cerebellar component showed higher mean connectivity in the psychiatric group in all seven states (all FDR-corrected P < 0.001; Fig. 3 and Table S4). The decreased limbic-somatomotor-salience component showed the opposite pattern, with lower connectivity in the psychiatric group in every state (all FDR-corrected P < 0.001).

The preservation of effect direction across cognitive contexts indicates that these components reflect cross-state features of functional organization rather than effects confined to a single task.

Sensitivity analyses

The principal connectivity alterations in the YaleNeuroConnect cohort were robust across analyses restricted to White participants, non-smokers, medication-free participants, and individuals with a single psychiatric diagnosis, with patients consistently showing higher expression of the increased cortico-cerebellar component and lower expression of the reduced limbic-salience-somatomotor component. These findings support the robustness of the observed effects across demographic, smoking, medication, and diagnostic-comorbidity sensitivity analyses (Supplementary Table S5).

Independent validation of the state-independent connectivity components

Next, generalizability of the state-independent connectivity alterations was evaluated independently in the UCLA cohort. PC1 explained 68.84% of the variance across seven functional states. The proportion of variance explained did not differ between the psychiatric and healthy-control groups after adjustment for age and sex (t = -0.782, P = 0.435). Using the Yale-derived edge masks, the psychiatric group showed higher expression of the increased cerebellar component (t = 4.063, P < 0.001, Fig. 4a) and lower expression of the limbic-somatomotor-salience component (t = -3.235, P = 0.0015; Fig. 4b). The same directional differences were also observed across individual UCLA functional states (Fig. 4c and Table S6).

Clinical relevance of the state-independent connectivity components

Within the psychiatric group in the Yale discovery cohort, the increased cerebellar component was associated with multiple clinical domains after FDR correction (Fig. 5 and Table S7). Greater component expression was associated with lower positive affect and with greater psychopathology and everyday executive difficulties. By contrast, the decreased limbic-somatomotor-salience component showed no significant associations with the available clinical or cognitive measures after FDR correction.

The two components therefore differed not only in anatomical organization and direction of case-control alteration, but also in their relation to clinical variation. In the discovery cohort, stronger cerebellar coupling with limbic and somatomotor systems indexed a clinically varying dimension of affective, psychopathological and executive dysfunction, whereas the reduced limbic-somatomotor-salience component was expressed at the group level without detectable associations with the measured symptom dimensions.

Discussion

There is growing recognition that psychiatric disorders share substantial neurobiological features despite considerable heterogeneity in their clinical manifestations. However, most functional neuroimaging studies have characterized these abnormalities under a single brain state, leaving it unclear which alterations represent stable features of psychiatric brain organization and how such features relate to individual clinical variation. By integrating functional connectivity across resting state and six task conditions, we identified two reproducible patterns of state-independent connectivity alteration in a diagnostically heterogeneous psychiatric population. One pattern was characterized by reduced connectivity among limbic, somatomotor and salience systems, whereas the other was dominated by increased cerebello-cortical connectivity, particularly along cerebellar-limbic and cerebellar-somatomotor connections. Both patterns were consistently expressed across functional states and generalized to an independent UCLA cohort. Importantly, only the cerebellar pattern was significant associated with affective, psychopathological and everyday executive difficulties. Together, these findings suggest that psychiatric disorders are associated with a stable reorganization of large-scale functional networks, within which different connectivity features may relate differently to common psychiatric abnormalities and individual clinical expression.

The reduced connectivity pattern mainly involved interactions among limbic, somatomotor and salience systems. These systems support affective processing, representation of bodily states and the detection and selection of behaviorally relevant information (35, 36). The salience network, in particular, plays an important role in identifying biologically and behaviorally relevant internal and external events and coordinating responses across other large-scale systems (37, 38). The limbic and somatomotor systems provide complementary representations of affective value, bodily states and action-related information. Reduced coordination among these systems may therefore indicate impaired integration of affective, interoceptive and sensorimotor information. Consistent with this interpretation, abnormalities involving salience, limbic and somatomotor networks have been reported across schizophrenia, bipolar disorder and other psychiatric conditions (6-8). The present findings extend this literature by showing that this pattern of dysconnectivity is not restricted to a single state, but remains evident across substantially different functional contexts.

The state-independent nature of this pattern may be particularly relevant for understanding psychiatric pathophysiology. Functional connectivity observed under a single condition reflects both relatively stable organization and transient responses to the current cognitive state. In contrast, an alteration that persists during reward processing, social cognition, sustained attention, naturalistic perception, working memory, inhibitory control and rest is less likely to be explained by abnormal engagement with one specific task (14). Reduced limbic-somatomotor-salience connectivity may therefore reflect a relatively stable disturbance in systems that coordinate internal states with ongoing environmental demands (39, 40). Interestingly, this component did not show significant associations with the available affective, psychopathological or executive-function measures. This finding does not indicate that the component is clinically irrelevant. Rather, it raises the possibility that some neural abnormalities are expressed relatively broadly across psychiatric populations and may therefore be more closely related to common aspects of psychiatric dysfunction than to variation in current symptom severity (41) (42) (43).

A second pattern was characterized by stronger cerebellar coupling with limbic and somatomotor systems. The cerebellum has traditionally been associated with motor control, but increasing evidence indicates that it contributes to cognitive, affective and social processes through extensive interactions with cortical and subcortical systems (44, 45). Cerebellar circuits are thought to support the coordination and adaptive adjustment of behavior by integrating ongoing internal and external information (46, 47). In this context, enhanced cerebellar connectivity with limbic and somatomotor systems may reflect altered regulation of affective and bodily-state information in psychiatric disorders (47) (48) (49). Previous studies have reported cerebellar dysconnectivity in schizophrenia and bipolar disorder and demonstrated associations with clinical and cognitive abnormalities (9, 50). State-independent cerebello-thalamo-cortical hyperconnectivity has also been linked to psychosis vulnerability and subsequent conversion (15). Our findings extend these observations to a diagnostically heterogeneous psychiatric population and indicate that abnormal cerebellar integration may represent a more general feature of psychiatric brain dysfunction.

Notably, the cerebellar pattern was also the component that showed consistent relationships with clinical variation. Stronger cerebellar coupling was associated with lower positive affect, higher depression, anxiety and psychoticism scores, greater global symptom burden, and more difficulties in everyday executive functioning (51) (52) (53). These associations extended across several clinical domains rather than corresponding to one particular diagnosis. This result is consistent with an emerging view that clinically meaningful neural variation may follow dimensional features of psychopathology that cut across conventional diagnostic categories (5, 54). The cerebellar component may therefore capture a feature of functional brain organization that is particularly sensitive to variation in clinical expression (48, 52, 55). Importantly, the present findings do not establish whether stronger cerebellar connectivity represents a pathogenic process or a compensatory response to dysfunction elsewhere in the brain. Nevertheless, its association with multiple dimensions of clinical impairment suggests that cerebellar reorganization deserves greater attention in transdiagnostic models of psychiatric neurobiology.

The relationship between the two connectivity patterns is also informative. Their individual expression was strongly inversely correlated, suggesting that they should not be regarded simply as two independent abnormalities. Rather, reduced integration among limbic, somatomotor and salience systems occurred together with stronger cerebellar coupling to limbic and somatomotor systems (56, 57). One possibility is that these changes reflect a coordinated redistribution of large-scale functional organization (58) (59) (60). Weaker integration among cortical systems involved in affective, salience-related and bodily processing may be accompanied by altered recruitment of cerebellar regulatory systems (56, 61). Such reorganization could represent an attempt to maintain functional coordination in the presence of disrupted cortical network integration, although an alternative possibility is that excessive cerebellar coupling itself contributes to the persistence of maladaptive network states (47). The present findings suggest that psychiatric brain dysfunction may involve changes in the balance of interactions among large-scale systems rather than isolated increases or decreases in individual connections.

This distinction also has implications for understanding the relationship between psychiatric neurobiology and clinical heterogeneity. Neuroimaging studies frequently identify reproducible differences between psychiatric and healthy populations, yet these abnormalities often show only modest relationships with symptom severity or everyday functioning (62, 63). The current results suggest that group-level abnormality and individual clinical variation may not be represented uniformly within the same altered connectome. Thus, some network features may reflect relatively broadly expressed psychiatric dysfunction, while others may provide more information about how that dysfunction is expressed in individual patients (64) (65) (66). These findings support a move from searching for a single transdiagnostic neural abnormality toward characterizing multiple interacting features of brain organization that contribute differently to psychiatric status and clinical variation.

Another important finding is the consistency of these connectivity patterns across functional states. Previous cross-paradigm studies have shown that connectivity shared across tasks and rest can capture stable individual characteristics and clinically relevant features of psychosis, including vulnerability, genetic risk and treatment response (12) (13) (14) (15). Here, both psychiatric connectivity patterns showed the same direction of group difference across seven functional conditions in the discovery cohort and generalized to the UCLA cohort despite differences in diagnostic composition and task structure. These results suggest that the identified alterations are not strongly dependent on a particular cognitive context. The present study therefore extends cross-paradigm connectivity from previous disorder-focused applications to a broader transdiagnostic setting. In this context, state-independent connectivity may provide a useful way to identify functional abnormalities that are sufficiently stable to remain detectable as the brain moves between different cognitive states.

These findings may also have implications for biologically informed approaches to psychiatric classification. Current diagnostic systems remain essential for clinical practice, but considerable symptom overlap and within-diagnosis heterogeneity suggest that diagnostic categories alone provide an incomplete representation of underlying neurobiology (5, 67, 68). The present results do not suggest replacing conventional diagnosis with connectivity-based classification. Rather, state-independent functional organization may provide an additional level of characterization that complements diagnosis (15, 61, 69). In particular, the coexistence of broadly expressed network abnormalities and clinically varying connectivity features may provide a neurobiological basis for considering both common dysfunction and individual variation within psychiatric populations (64, 65).

Strengths of this study include integrating multiple functional states, using a deeply phenotyped, diagnostically heterogeneous discovery cohort, and validating findings in an independent dataset with a different task battery and diagnostic composition. Several limitations remain. First, the state-independent architecture was derived from only one resting-state and six task conditions, and future studies incorporating broader cognitive and affective paradigms may yield a more comprehensive characterization of state-independent brain organization. Second, we focused on BOLD functional connectivity alone; future research should integrate multimodal imaging, electrophysiological, molecular and genetic measures to clarify the biological basis of the identified network alterations. Third, clinical analyses relied on the available affective, psychopathological and executive-function measures; more comprehensive phenotyping will better delineate the clinical relevance of these connectivity patterns. Fourth, both cohorts mainly comprised adults, and future studies across the lifespan are needed to establish developmental generalizability. Finally, this cross-sectional design cannot establish causality; longitudinal studies are required to determine whether these connectivity alterations precede, accompany or follow changes in psychiatric symptoms and functioning.

Conclusion

In summary, we identified and independently validated two coordinated patterns of state-independent connectivity alteration in diagnostically heterogeneous psychiatric populations. Reduced connectivity among limbic, somatomotor and salience systems was consistently expressed across cognitive states, whereas increased cerebellar coupling with limbic and somatomotor systems additionally showed systematic associations with affective, psychopathological and everyday executive difficulties. These findings indicate that stable functional brain organization contains both broadly expressed psychiatric alterations and connectivity features more closely related to individual clinical variation, and that state-independent connectomics can help characterize how common neurobiological dysfunction and clinical heterogeneity coexist across psychiatric disorders.

BRIEF correlation coefficients were sign-reversed for visualization so that poorer executive functioning is displayed in the same direction as greater symptom burden.
