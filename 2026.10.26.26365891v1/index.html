What we found

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Chronic graft-versus-host disease (cGvHD) remains a major cause of morbidity after pediatric allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT), but the early plasma molecular features associated with primary immune tolerance (PIT) versus cGvHD are incompletely understood. We performed longitudinal LC-MS plasma proteomics in 32 pediatric allo-HSCT recipients sampled at Months 1, 3, 6, 9, and 12 after transplantation, quantifying 4,702 proteins.

Across timepoint-specific differential-abundance analyses, Month 3 showed the strongest observed PIT-cGvHD proteomic separation and was selected for deeper exploratory characterization. At Month 3, eight proteins were higher in PIT at BH-FDR <0.05, including the 19S proteasome ATPases PSMC1, PSMC3 and PSMC5, the chaperonin subunit CCT3, the ubiquitin-conjugating enzyme UBE2O, and APEH, RAN and NME1, pointing to differences in protein degradation and folding pathways. Time-resolved pathway analysis showed PITassociated immune, immunoglobulin, and proteostasis programs at distinct post-transplant intervals, whereas Month-3 cGvHD-associated profiles were enriched for platelet activation, cell adhesion, transmembrane transport, and metabolic processes. Outcome-blinded clustering of the 500 most temporally variable proteins resolved three descriptive trajectory patterns: a transient Month-3 peak, a Month-3 nadir with late recovery, and an early-high/latedecline pattern. The distribution of Month-3 PIT-cGvHD protein effects differed across outcome-blinded trajectory clusters, providing an internally consistent temporal framework for the Month-3 signal. These exploratory findings nominate Month 3 as a potentially informative interval of molecular divergence after pediatric allo-HSCT and identify pathway-level features of PIT and cGvHD for prospective validation.

Introduction

Allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (allo-HSCT) is a potentially curative treatment for a range of pediatric malignant and non-malignant disorders. However, its longterm success is substantially limited by graft-versus-host disease (GvHD), particularly the chronic form (cGvHD), which remains the primary driver of non-relapse morbidity and mortality in long-term survivors. 1 cGvHD is a multisystem immune-mediated disorder arising from dysregulated donor T-and B-cell responses targeting host tissues, and its clinical presentation is highly heterogeneous, ranging from mild organ involvement to severe, therapy-refractory disease. 2 In contrast, a subset of patients achieves primary immune tolerance (PIT), a state characterized by full donor chimerism, absence of GvHD, and progressive immune reconstitution (IR) without the need for prolonged immunosuppression. 3, 4 Understanding the molecular basis of this divergence between PIT and cGvHD has important implications for risk stratification, therapeutic targeting, and the design of transplant protocols that promote tolerance. 4 Immune reconstitution after HSCT is a complex, temporally ordered process involving the sequential recovery of innate and adaptive immune cell compartments, thymic output, and Bcell reconstitution, occurring over months to years post-transplant. 5 Both the pace and quality of immune reconstitution are substantially influenced by the occurrence of GvHD: cGvHD impairs thymic output, delays naïve T-cell recovery, and perturbs B-cell homeostasis, thereby perpetuating immunodeficiency and susceptibility to infections. 6 Conversely, early and robust immune reconstitution has been associated with reduced GvHD severity and improved overall survival. 7 The plasma proteome offers a minimally invasive window into systemic immune and inflammatory states. Longitudinal proteomic profiling by liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS) enables unbiased, high-resolution tracking of molecular changes across the post-transplant course and has been applied to identify biomarkers of transplantrelated complications, including acute GvHD. 8, 9 However, longitudinal plasma-proteomic studies specifically designed to characterize PIT and cGvHD over the first year after HSCT remain limited. Many studies emphasize cross-sectional or timepoint-specific comparisons, underscoring the need to integrate such analyses with approaches that describe temporal protein patterns across follow-up.

Here, we present an integrated longitudinal analysis of plasma proteomics in a pediatric allo-HSCT cohort. We first characterized the temporal structure of the proteome using outcomeblinded trajectory clustering, then identified Month 3 as the strongest window of PIT-cGvHD divergence. Finally, we tested whether trajectory-defined protein programs are systematically associated with clinical outcome, providing a non-circular framework for interpreting posttransplant proteomic dynamics.

Materials and Methods

Patient cohort and clinical classification

This study included 32 pediatric patients who underwent allo-HSCT from the Children's Hospital, Helsinki University Hospital, Finland, between 2016 and 2022. Patients were followed prospectively with longitudinal plasma sampling at months 1, 3, 6, 9, and 12 posttransplant. Written informed consent was obtained from the parents or legal guardians of all patients, and from the patients themselves when age-appropriate, and the study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Helsinki University Hospital (HUS/2306/2016). Patients were classified into three outcome groups based on standardized clinical criteria: (1) Primary Immune Tolerance (PIT): no acute GvHD (grade 0) and no cGvHD (n=11); (2) Secondary Immune Tolerance (SIT): acute GvHD present but no cGvHD (n=7); and (3) cGvHD: cGvHD present regardless of acute GvHD status (n=14). Acute and chronic GvHD were graded according to NIH consensus criteria. 3, 10 Among the 14 patients who developed cGvHD, the median time from transplant to cGvHD onset was 3.8 months (range, 3.3-11.6 months); no patient had a documented cGvHD onset before or at the Month-3 sampling window, confirming that Month-3 proteomic sampling preceded clinical cGvHD diagnosis in all cases. Baseline demographics and clinical characteristics are detailed in Table 1 and Supplementary Figure S1. Primary comparisons were between PIT and cGvHD; SIT data are presented in supplementary analyses due to limited sample availability.

Plasma sample collection and LC-MS proteomics

Peripheral blood plasma samples were collected at each timepoint in EDTA-coated tubes.

Peripheral blood mononuclear cell isolation was done with gradient centrifugation (Ficoll-Paque PLUS, Cytiva, Buckinghamshire, UK). Plasma was separated by centrifugation at 2000 × g for 15 min and stored at -80°C until analysis. Patients were scheduled for longitudinal sampling at Months 1, 3, 6, 9, and 12 post-transplant; actual sampling completeness varied, with 14 of 32 patients sampled at all five timepoints and 18 patients sampled at 1-4 timepoints (median, 4 timepoints per patient). Month-1 samples were most frequently unavailable. In total, 120 samples were collected and analyzed. Plasma proteins from 20 µl of plasma were enriched with an automated Mag-Net plasma enrichment workflow and the resulting peptides were analyzed with liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS). The fully automated Mag-Net workflow and the LC-MS untargeted proteomics method with Evosep One (Odense, Denmark), employing the 60 samples per day method, connected to Orbitrap Astral (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) have been previously reported in detail 11 (Supplementary Methods). Method robustness was ensured by processing 2 quality control (QC) samples per plate (Supplementary Figure S2), consisting of pooled commercial reference plasma (Research Donors Ltd, London, UK) processed alongside patient samples on the same plates.

Clinical flow cytometry

Blood immune cell phenotyping was performed on whole blood in the HUS Diagnostic Center clinical laboratory using its validated extended immunophenotyping panel and published gating strategy. 12 T-cell subsets (CD3, CD4, CD8, CD45RA, CCR7, CD31, TCRαβ, TCRγδ) included naïve (CD45RA⁺CCR7⁺) and recent thymic emigrant (CD45RA⁺CD31⁺) CD4⁺ T cells; B-cell subsets (naïve, memory, switched and non-switched memory, transitional, plasmablast); and NK cells (CD56⁺CD3⁻) were also defined. Samples were acquired on FACSLyric cytometers and absolute counts were calculated using BD Trucount tubes.

Trajectory clustering and representative proteins per cluster

For each protein, a cohort mean log2-transformed intensity was calculated at each of the five post-transplant timepoints (Months 1, 3, 6, 9, and 12), averaging across all patients with an available measurement at that timepoint, irrespective of outcome group. Proteins were retained only if a non-missing cohort-mean value was obtained at all five timepoints; no imputation was required, as timepoint-level averaging inherently accommodated per-patient missingness. From the retained proteins, each protein's five-timepoint cohort-mean trajectory was first z-score standardized (mean 0, SD 1 across the five months), and 500 proteins were selected for downstream clustering based on this standardized representation. The 500 selected proteins were then subjected to k-means clustering (k=3, nstart=100) without using outcome group labels. Cluster-level mean trajectory was computed as the mean z-score per cluster per month. To illustrate the proteins underlying each trajectory pattern, we computed a cluster-specific shape score for each protein quantifying conformity to the cluster-typical profile: for the Month-3-peak cluster, the Month-3 z-score minus the mean z-score of the remaining timepoints; for the Month-3-nadir cluster, the mean of Months 6-12 minus the Month-3 z-score; for the early-high/late-decline cluster, the Month-1 minus Month-12 z-score. The top 12 proteins per cluster by shape score were visualized as a z-score heatmap.

Differential expression and enrichment analyses

Differential abundance between PIT and cGvHD was assessed independently at each timepoint using limma, with empirical-Bayes moderated t-statistics from an unpaired twogroup model (PIT vs. cGvHD); topTable-derived p-values are two-sided. Benjamini-Hochberg FDR correction was applied within each timepoint, and proteins with FDR < 0.05 were considered significant. Proteins were tested if detected in ≥70% of samples at that timepoint; this yielded 1,564, 1,554, 1,678, 1,806, and 1,955 proteins tested at Months 1, 3, 6, 9, and 12, respectively (at Month 3, n_PIT=9, n_cGvHD=13 with available paired data). Missing values among tested proteins were median-imputed per protein before model fitting; for each of the eight FDR-significant Month-3 proteins, at most 2 of 22 samples were imputed (none in PIT).

Complete Month-3 results for all tested proteins are provided in Supplementary Table S2. To test whether protein trajectories diverged by outcome group across the full follow-up period rather than at isolated timepoints, we additionally fit generalized estimating equation (GEE) models (exchangeable correlation structure, patient-clustered robust standard errors) with a group×time interaction term for each protein with ≥60% completeness across the five timepoints (1,999 of 4,702 proteins). Unlike the per-timepoint limma analyses above, which required ≥70% detection within a single timepoint, this criterion required ≥60% completeness pooled across all five timepoints combined. Benjamini-Hochberg FDR correction was applied across all 1,999 tested proteins. Prior acute GvHD status was not included as a covariate because it is confounded with outcome group (no PIT patients had acute GvHD; 71% of cGvHD patients did). Month 3 was identified as the timepoint of strongest PIT-cGvHD separation in this screen (Fig. 2A) and was then characterized in depth (module scores, leaveone-patient-out (LOPO) cross-validation, trajectory-cluster association); pathway enrichment was also examined at Month 1 and 6. Because Month 3 was the maximum of five timepoints, its effect sizes may be modestly inflated.

Gene Ontology Biological Process over-representation analysis (ORA) and gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) were performed using clusterProfiler (v.4.12.6). 13 For ORA, the background gene universe was restricted to the set of proteins tested in the corresponding timepoint-specific differential-abundance analysis (rather than the default whole-genome background), ensuring that enrichment was assessed relative to the proteins actually detectable on this platform. For GSEA, the full ranked list of tested proteins at each timepoint (ranked by moderated t-statistic) was used, such that the same timepoint-specific protein universe was implicitly represented.

Cluster-level association with Month-3 outcome effects

To test whether trajectory-defined programs are associated with clinical outcome without circularity, we compared the distribution of Month-3 log2 fold changes (cGvHD -PIT) across the three trajectory clusters using a linear model (ANOVA), restricted to proteins present in both the trajectory set and the Month-3 differential analysis (n=178). Cluster labels were derived independently of outcome data.

Results

Cohort characteristics and proteomic profiling

The study cohort comprised 32 pediatric allo-HSCT recipients (PIT n=11, SIT n=7, cGvHD n=14), sampled longitudinally at Month 1, 3, 6, 9, and 12 after transplantation (Fig. 1A; Table 1). Median age at transplant was 10.8 years (range, 2.1-17.2). Donor source, conditioning regimen, and HLA-matching status are shown in Table 1. Acute GvHD occurred in 17 patients (53%); 10 of them progressed to cGvHD, and 4 further patients developed cGvHD without preceding acute GvHD (total cGvHD n=14, 44%). White blood cell counts at diagnosis were 1.0, 1.95, and 3.6 x10³/µL in PIT, cGvHD and SIT, respectively (Table 1), mainly reflecting underlying diagnosis (non-malignant 4.4 vs. malignant 1.7 x10³/µL). LC-MS profiling quantified 4,702 plasma proteins across 120 samples from 32 patients. The median number of protein identifications per sample was 2,336 (range, 1,157-3,915), corresponding to a median persample detection rate of 49.7% of the total quantified proteome. Identification depth was consistent across timepoints (median, 2,240-2,533 proteins per sample; Supplementary Figure S2).

Global proteomic landscape is dominated by inter-individual and temporal variations

To characterize the overall proteomic structure, we performed principal component analysis (PCA) on the 577 proteins quantified in all 100 PIT and cGvHD samples (Fig. 1B). PIT and cGvHD samples overlapped substantially at every timepoint, and neither outcome group, timepoint nor patient identity explained appreciable variance in PC1 or PC2 (adjusted R² ≤ 0.04; PC1 and PC2 explained 46.7% and 14.9% of total variance). PC1 tracked the number of proteins identified per sample (Spearman ρ = -0.92), indicating a depth-related axis. In a PCA of the 200 most variable proteins PC1 55.0%, PC2 17.2%; Supplementary Fig. S3A, B), same-patient samples were not significantly closer than different-patient samples in PC1-PC2 space (median distance 12.5 vs. 12.9; Wilcoxon P = 0.41; Supplementary Fig. S3C). The absence of outcome-related structure in the leading components supported targeted longitudinal analyses rather than relying on unsupervised separation alone.

To define temporal protein patterns independently of clinical outcome, proteins were z-score standardized across timepoints and the 500 most variable proteins were clustered according to their longitudinal profiles. Outcome-blinded k-means clustering with three prespecified clusters resolved three principal trajectory patterns (Fig. 1C): a transient Month-3 peak (Cluster 1, n=179), a Month-3 nadir with progressive late recovery (Cluster 2, n=159), and an early-high, late-decline pattern (Cluster 3, n=162).

We subsequently projected samples from patients with secondary immune tolerance (SIT) onto this proteomic framework. PCA, signature-score, and cluster-trajectory analyses were used descriptively to compare SIT with PIT and cGvHD across follow-up (Supplementary Fig. S4). These analyses showed broad overlap among the three outcome groups and were not used to identify additional FDR-significant proteins or to establish a definitive SIT-specific proteomic phenotype, given the exploratory design and limited SIT sample size.

PIT and cGvHD diverge most strongly at Month 3, with higher abundance of proteasome-, chaperonin-and ubiquitin-related proteins in PIT

We examined when PIT-cGvHD proteomic differences were greatest and which biological programs characterized the divergence. Timepoint-specific differential-abundance analysis showed Month 3 as the strongest window of PIT-cGvHD separation (Fig. 2A; volcano plots for all timepoints in Supplementary Fig. S5). In a group×time interaction model, 7 of 1,999 proteins with sufficient longitudinal completeness showed a significant interaction (FDR<0.05; Supplementary Table S1, Supplementary Fig. S6), all declining over follow-up in PIT and rising in cGvHD. Because time was modeled linearly, this analysis detects sustained average differences and is complementary to, not confirmatory of, the transient Month 3 signal (Fig. 1C, 2, and 4).

At Month 3, 240 of 1,554 tested proteins differed between cGvHD and PIT at nominal P < 0.05, and eight remained significant after Benjamini-Hochberg correction (BH-FDR < 0.05; Fig. 2B; Supplementary Table S2). All eight were higher in PIT: PSMC1, PSMC5 and PSMC3 (19S proteasome ATPase subunits), CCT3 (a chaperonin subunit), UBE2O (an E2/E3 ubiquitin ligase), and APEH, RAN and NME1. Abundance was 2.2-to 6.4-fold lower in cGvHD. Further chaperonin and proteasome proteins, including CCT5, CCT2 and PSMD2, ranked just below the threshold (BH-adjusted P = 0.050). No protein met BH-FDR < 0.05 at Months 1, 6, 9 or 12.

To examine whether this signal extended beyond individual proteins, we summarized the abundance of curated protein modules. The Month 1 and Month 6 modules were selected from GO-BP categories, whereas the Month 3 module comprised the eight FDR-significant proteins from Fig. 2B together with IGHA1 and IGHG3; these circular heatmaps are therefore descriptive summaries and not independent tests (Fig. 2C). The predominant group-level pattern changed over time: complement/coagulation proteins were displayed at Month 1, a proteostasis/humoral-immunity set at Month 3, and antigen-presentation/immune-activation proteins at Month 6. In particular, the Month-3 circular heatmap showed higher relative abundance of multiple proteostasis-associated proteins in PIT, including PSMC1, PSMC3, PSMC5, CCT3, UBE2O, RAN, NME1, and APEH. These descriptive module summaries support temporally structured molecular differences between PIT and cGvHD, rather than a single invariant group signature across follow-up.

As an unbiased complement to the curated modules above, we performed timepoint-specific, unbiased GO-BP gene set enrichment analysis (Fig. 2D). At Month 1, PIT-associated enrichment was dominated by adaptive immune response (NES = -1.88, BH-FDR = 3.68 x 10 -23 ), immunoglobulin-mediated immune response (NES = -1.86, BH-FDR = 1.03 x 10 -11 ), and complement activation 16 (NES = -1.65, BH-FDR = 1.96 x 10 -4 ). In contrast, the strongest cGvHD-associated Month-1 terms were synaptic vesicle cycle (NES = 2.09, BH-FDR = 0.0083), regulation of macroautophagy (NES = 1.94, BH-FDR = 0.035), and post-Golgi vesicle-mediated transport (NES = 2.04, BH-FDR = 0.037).

At Month 3, the timepoint of strongest divergence, cGvHD-associated enrichment included platelet activation 17 (NES = 2.79, BH-FDR = 8.11x10 -7 ), proton transmembrane transport (NES = 3.30, BH-FDR = 6 x 10 -6 ), monocarboxylic-acid catabolic process (NES = 2.85, BH-FDR = 4.2 x 10 -4 ), and platelet aggregation (NES = 2.49, BH-FDR = 1.88 x 10 -4 ). In contrast, and consistent with both the individual-protein (Fig. 2B) and module-level (Fig. 2C) results above, the PIT-associated profile showed strong enrichment of proteasome-mediated ubiquitindependent protein catabolism (NES = -1.89, BH-FDR = 2.45 x 10 -5 ), erythrocyte development (NES = -2.15, BH-FDR = 4.79 x 10 -5 ), and ubiquitin-dependent protein catabolism (NES = -1.78, BH-FDR = 5.43 x 10 -5 ).

At Month 6, PIT-associated immune signatures remained prominent, including adaptive immune response (NES = -1.97, BH-FDR = 1.15 x 10 -5 ) and immune response (NES = -1.59, BH-FDR = 0.0032). Conversely, cGvHD-associated enrichment included proteasomemediated ubiquitin-dependent protein catabolism (NES = 1.92, BH-FDR = 0.0177) and extracellular-matrix assembly (NES = 2.27, BH-FDR = 0.0258). Together, these three complementary lines of evidence (individual proteins (Fig. 2B), pre-specified modules (Fig. 2C), and unbiased pathway enrichment (Fig. 2D)) converge on the same conclusion: functional separation between PIT and cGvHD is time-dependent, with the most coherent and extensive opposing pathway signals observed specifically at Month 3.

Given this convergent evidence for Month 3 as the clearest window of coordinated divergence, we examined the corresponding pathway architecture in detail. At this timepoint, the PITassociated ranking was enriched for protein-catabolism and proteostasis-related processes, including proteasome-mediated ubiquitin-dependent protein catabolic process, erythrocyte development, protein ubiquitination, and hydrogen peroxide catabolism (Fig. 2E; Supplementary Table S3). In contrast, the cGvHD-associated ranking was enriched for proton transmembrane transport, tricarboxylic-acid-cycle and monocarboxylic-acid-catabolic processes, platelet activation and aggregation, cell-cell adhesion, and integrin-related signaling. 17 Together, these findings indicate that the Month-3 proteomic contrast reflects coordinated differences between PIT-associated protein-quality-control programs and cGvHDassociated transport, metabolic, platelet, and cell-adhesion programs.

Month 3 PIT proteomic and immune reconstitution

We next asked whether the Month-3 PIT proteomic program related to immune reconstitution.

A composite PIT pathway score, built from standardized abundance of proteins in the top PIT-enriched GO biological-process pathways at Month 3, was higher in patients subsequently classified as PIT than in those with cGvHD (Fig. 3A). To address the circularity, we used leaveone-patient-out (LOPO) cross-validation, re-deriving the signature without each patient before scoring that patient. The PIT-associated signature remained significantly higher in PIT (Mann-Whitney P=0.043, Fig 3E), whereas the same procedure applied to the Month 1 and Month 6 PIT-associated signatures was not significant (Month 1: P = 0.44, n = 19 [PIT n = 8, cGvHD n = 11]; Month 6: P = 0.21, n = 23; Supplementary Table S4). No reciprocal cGvHD-associated signature was available for Months 1 or 6, so this comparison is limited to the PIT direction.

The pattern suggests that the PIT-associated program is localized to the Month-3 window rather than reflecting a persistent baseline difference. In patients with paired Month-3 proteomic and flow-cytometry data, higher PIT pathway scores showed nominal positive associations with absolute counts of recent thymic emigrants (RTE) and naïve CD4⁺ T cells, and weaker or inconsistent associations with naïve CD8⁺ T cells, naïve B cells, and γδ T cells (Fig. 3B). Confidence intervals were wide and analyses unadjusted, so these findings are exploratory. Median trajectories of these subsets over Months 1-12 suggested numerically greater recovery of RTE and naïve CD4⁺ T cells in PIT, whereas the other subsets were inconsistent (Fig. 3C). Linear mixed-effects models (group, month and their interaction; patient random intercept; log₁₀(cells/µL+1)) pointed in the same direction, but confidence intervals were wide and generally included the null, so there was no definitive evidence of differential longitudinal reconstitution between clinical groups (Fig. 3D). To obtain an exploratory comparison in an independent cohort, we analyzed an external pediatric allo-HSCT cohort previously profiled using targeted Olink Inflammation and Immune Response panels. 18 None of the eight proteins prioritized by LC-MS was measured on these panels, and only nine of the 180 Olink proteins were also tested in the LC-MS Month 3 analysis, so this analysis could not replicate the LC-MS signal. At Month 3, 27 of 180 proteins had nominal P<0.05 (about nine expected by chance), with higher TRANCE, NCR1, CD83, and CD28 in PIT and higher AREG, OPG, IL-18R1, IL8, and IL10 in cGvHD (Supplementary Fig. S7; Supplementary Table S5).

No protein passed FDR<0.05 (smallest BH-adjusted P=0.074, TRANCE). These results are therefore hypothesis-generating and do not constitute replication, given the small cohort, targeted assay design, and differences protein coverage compared with untargeted LC-MS.

Trajectory clustering reveals a coordinated month 3 proteomic program

Because single-protein analysis at Month 3 has limited power, we defined trajectory clusters from temporal patterns across all timepoints, without clinical labels. This yielded three patterns: a transient month-3 peak, a month-3 nadir with later recovery, and an early-high, late-decline pattern (Fig. 4A). We next summarized these patterns at the pathway level. Mean PIT and cGvHD proteomic signature scores (z-scores) showed that the PIT signature was higher in patients subsequently classified as PIT, particularly around month 3, whereas the cGvHD signature showed the opposite pattern (Fig. 4B). Under the same LOPO validation (Fig. 3E), the PIT-associated signature remained nominally higher in PIT (P=0.043), whereas the reciprocal cGvHD-associated signature did not (P=0.42). Within each trajectory cluster, we ranked proteins by a trajectory shape score and selected the top 12 per cluster. These representative proteins recapitulated the cluster-level trajectories and spanned immune, complement, and metabolic functions, suggesting coordinated programs rather than isolated changes (Fig. 4C). Cross-referencing these representative proteins against Human Protein Atlas immune cell transcriptomic data showed no statistically significant enrichment for a specific immune cell lineage within any cluster (Supplementary Fig. S8, Supplementary Table S6), though several proteins (e.g., CEMIP, CFI, CHRD) are not expressed by circulating immune cells and may represent non-leukocyte-derived plasma components. We then tested whether trajectory cluster membership predicted Month-3 differential abundance between PIT and cGvHD. Cluster membership was associated with Month-3 log fold changes (ANOVA F(2,175) = 14.56, p = 1.4 × 10⁻¹⁰) (Fig. 4D), with Clusters 1 and 3 higher in PIT and Cluster 2 higher in cGvHD. Because cluster assignment incorporates each protein's Month-3 shape, this consistency is not independent replication of the Month-3 finding.

Discussion

In this longitudinal proteomic study, we identified a coordinated Month-3 program that distinguishes pediatric patients who later achieve PIT from those who develop cGvHD. The divergence preceded clinical cGvHD in every affected patient (median onset 3.8 months, close to the classical day-100 threshold), so it is unlikely to reflect established disease alone. Differential-abundance, pathway and trajectory analyses pointed to a PIT-associated signature of protein degradation and folding. Across the first year, complement/coagulation, proteostasis/humoral and antigen-presentation programs predominated at different intervals.

Together, these findings suggest that the plasma of the two groups already differs about three months after transplant, before clinical diagnosis.

These findings extend previous work describing impaired thymopoiesis, delayed naïve T-cell recovery, and B-cell dysregulation in cGvHD by placing these features within a longitudinal plasma-proteomic context. 19 Rather than identifying a single dominant biomarker, the PIT-cGvHD contrast spans several molecular programs whose relative prominence changes over time. The strongest PIT-associated signal at Month 3 involved proteins related to proteasome function, protein folding and ubiquitin-dependent protein turnover. All eight subunits of the TCP1 ring complex (CCT/TRiC), which folds actin and tubulin and supports TCR-driven immune-synapse organization, 20, 21 were also higher in PIT (Supplementary Table S2). The proteasome regulates antigen processing, NF-κB-dependent inflammatory signaling and lymphocyte survival, [22] [23] [24] and its pharmacological inhibition can modulate alloimmune responses. In mice, bortezomib given immediately after transplantation inhibited acute GvHD, whereas administration five or more days later accelerated GvHD-dependent morbidity, 24, 25 and it has been tested clinically as GvHD prophylaxis. 26 These observations concern proteasome activity within cells and do not predict the direction of plasma abundance.

Proteasomes are also detectable in plasma, where their abundance changes in several inflammatory conditions. 27, 28 Because these are intracellular proteins, their plasma abundance may reflect protein turnover or release from damaged or activated cells, rather than a secreted function. 27, 28 A more active, recovering hematopoietic and lymphoid compartment is one plausible explanation. It would fit the nominal association of the PIT pathway score with recent thymic emigrants and naïve CD4⁺ T cells, and it agrees with work linking thymic recovery and naïve T-cell restoration to favorable immune recovery after HSCT. 6,10 However, mixed-effects models did not show definitive differential reconstitution, and the correlations were exploratory, based on limited paired data and not adjusted for multiple testing. We therefore hypothesize that appropriately regulated protein turnover in this early window accompanies the establishment of immune tolerance. Because we measured plasma protein abundance and not activity, cell origin or function, this and the interpretation below remain hypothesis-generating. This pattern is consistent with a transient period of coordinated post-transplant recovery in PIT, rather than a stable proteomic state that persists throughout the first year after transplantation.

On the cGvHD side, enrichment of platelet activation, cell adhesion, and transmembrane transport pathways may be consistent with vascular inflammation and endothelial dysfunction implicated in GvHD. 29 Platelet-and stroma-derived growth factors, including PDGF and TGFβ, have been implicated in the later fibrotic phase of the disease, which makes early plateletand adhesion-related signals biologically plausible, 30, 31 although we did not measure these factors. Together, these findings suggest that, by Month 3, tolerance may be supported by increased protein turnover and quality control, whereas the path to cGvHD is supported by active early vascular and tissue stress. 17, 32 The value of these findings lies in timing and pattern, not in any single molecule. If confirmed, this would indicate that the plasma environment diverges before clinical diagnosis, when intervention might still be considered. However, the study was not designed for prospective prediction, and validation in an independent cohort with pre-specified predictive modelling and clinically relevant performance measures is required. 33, 34 The pathways also yield testable questions: whether early platelet-and adhesion-related signaling contributes to later fibrotic remodeling, and whether proteasome-and chaperonin-related abundance marks the establishment of immune tolerance. We do not propose a biomarker or therapeutic target; Month 3 may represent an early branching point between tolerance and chronic disease that warrants further study in larger independent pediatric cohorts.

Several analyses support the internal consistency of the Month-3 signal. The PIT-associated signature remained nominally higher in PIT when re-derived without the scored patient under LOPO cross-validation, indicating that separation was not solely due to using the same samples for construction and evaluation, although the margin was narrow. The reciprocal cGvHD signature did not retain significance, which may reflect heterogeneity in organ involvement, onset timing, and severity, 35, 36 or limited power and noise in a small cohort.

Month-3 fold changes differed across outcome-blinded trajectory clusters, showing that the group difference is embedded in broader temporal patterns and not attributable to a few isolated proteins. Because cluster assignment partly reflects each protein's Month-3 shape, this is consistency and not independent replication. A linear group×time GEE model detects sustained average differences and found seven proteins rising more steeply in cGvHD (Supplementary Table S1, Supplementary Fig. S6). Because time was modeled linearly, it was not designed to detect a transient Month 3 signal and neither supports nor contradicts it.

Month-3 was chosen after screening all timepoints, so its prominence may partly reflect selection. The same cross-validated procedure separated the groups at Month 3 but not at Month 1 or 6, which is consistent with a time-localized signal, although a difference in significance between timepoints is not a formal test of timepoint specificity.

The plasma signal should not be interpreted as a direct readout of circulating leukocyte expression alone. Several representative proteins (Fig. 4C), including CEMIP, CFI, and CHRD, were not detected in sorted immune-cell populations (Supplementary Fig. S8, Supplementary Table S6) and may originate from hepatic, stromal, endothelial, or other nonleukocyte sources. The Month-3 plasma proteomic program may therefore integrate immunecell activity with systemic tissue responses to transplantation, including vascular, metabolic, and tissue-remodeling processes. 37 This is particularly relevant in cGvHD, a multisystem disorder in which immune dysregulation, endothelial injury, and tissue damage may coexist. 36 Because proteasome, chaperonin and related proteins are also abundant in red cells and platelets, contributions from blood-cell-derived material cannot be excluded.

Several limitations merit consideration. The cohort was modest, especially for timepointspecific and paired proteomic-flow analyses, limiting power and covariate adjustment.

Sampling was incomplete, so residual bias cannot be excluded. Month 3 was selected as the timepoint of strongest separation, so effect sizes may be inflated. Prior acute GvHD could not be adjusted for because it is confounded with the outcome definition. The Month-3 signature therefore distinguishes complete primary tolerance from the trajectory leading to cGvHD and is not a cGvHD-specific signature, and cohorts including secondary acute GvHD are needed.

Donor type and conditioning intensity differed modestly across groups and were not covariates, so residual confounding cannot be excluded. LC-MS coverage misses low-abundance cytokines and chemokines that affinity-based assays quantify better, so non-detection of an established biomarker is not evidence against its relevance.

Despite these limitations, the study combines repeated plasma proteomics with longitudinal immune phenotyping in a pediatric allo-HSCT cohort, and integrates timepoint-specific, pathway-level, LOPO-validated, outcome-blinded trajectory and longitudinal analyses.

Together, these analyses nominate Month 3 as a potentially informative interval of early molecular divergence after transplantation, and an exploratory PIT-associated plasma signature of protein degradation and folding for prospective validation.
