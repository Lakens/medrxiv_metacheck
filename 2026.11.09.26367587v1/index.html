Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 128 papers with 246 instances of power statements, 203 were correctly detected (true positives), 22 were missed (false negatives) and 21 were incorrectly detected (false positives). Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Marginal Significance works

List all sentences that describe an effect as ‘marginally significant’.

The marginal module searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The list of terms is a subset of those listed in a blog post by Matthew Hankins. The module returns all sentences that match terms describing ‘marginally significant’ results.

Some of the terms identified might not be problematic in some contexts, and there are ways to describe ‘marginal significance’ that are not detected by the module.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 225 papers containing 405 instances of non-exact p-values, the module correctly detected 269 cases (true positives) and incorrectly identified 78 (false positives). It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Effect Sizes in t-tests and F-tests works

The Effect Size module checks if effect sizes are correctly reported in t-tests and F-tests.

The Effect Size check searches for regular expressions that match typical ways in which effect sizes are reported. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download files from additional data repositories reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. Full text

Abstract

Background: Consumer electroencephalography (EEG)-based neurotechnologies are rapidly expanding across commercial digital health delivery networks and wellness markets. Positioned at the intersection of nonmedical self-monitoring and clinical applications, these consumer devices introduce complex challenges for device governance and digital bioethics regarding product claims, data privacy, evidential substantiation, and user protection.

Objective: This qualitative study examined expert stakeholder perspectives on the ethical, regulatory, and data governance challenges of consumer EEG neurotechnology, focusing on the allocation of responsibility across product lifecycles.

We conducted semi-structured interviews with 19 expert stakeholders spanning research, clinical practice, engineering, industry, ethics, law, and regulatory policy across North America, Europe, and Latin America. Interview data were systematically analysed using reflexive thematic analysis to examine how governance challenges, evidence burdens, and responsibilities are conceptualized across the neurotechnology ecosystem.

Results: Five interrelated themes emerged: ( 1) Regulatory Category Boundaries and Oversight; (2) Evidential Credibility and Claims-Related Harm; (3) Neurodata Privacy, Security, and Inference; (4) Layered Governance and Explicit Accountability; and (5) Participation, Global Equity, and Contextual Responsiveness. Participants identified major friction in applying traditional medical oversight to nonmedical digital health uses, linked unverified claims to user vulnerability, and expressed concern over downstream neurodata processing. A connecting synthesis demonstrates that oversight responsibilities span multiple actors and development stages rather than resting on a single regulatory mechanism.

Conclusions: Effective governance of consumer EEG digital health devices requires explicit allocation of duties for claims substantiation, data practices, and user safety, backed by inclusive stakeholder participation and contextual responsiveness.

Introduction Background

Consumer neurotechnology has expanded from specialized research and clinical environments into commercial markets. EEG-based headsets and related non-invasive brain-computer interface applications are promoted for meditation, self-monitoring, cognitive enhancement, and entertainment [1, 2]. Some products are positioned as wellness technologies, while others make claims that approach health-related assessment or intervention. Their ethical significance therefore depends on their purposes, claims, and uses, alongside their technical characteristics.

Consumer EEG systems can combine recording hardware with applications, computational models, and dataprocessing services. These systems may translate physiological signals into feedback that users interpret as information about attention, relaxation, or other aspects of their mental functioning [3]. Evaluating such products requires distinguishing the ability to record a signal from the evidence supporting a particular interpretation. Brain-based feedback may influence users' expectations and self-understanding even when its reliability or practical significance is uncertain [1]. Accordingly, the relevant object of governance includes the hardware, software, product claims, data practices, and circumstances of use.

Prior Work and Research Gap

Existing scholarship has identified ethical concerns involving evidential quality, privacy, security, autonomy, and oversight in direct-to-consumer neurotechnology [1, 2]. Evidence and claims are closely connected: users may have limited means to assess whether advertised benefits or interpretations are adequately substantiated. These concerns are particularly consequential when products appeal to people seeking help with health-related difficulties. Ethical assessment must therefore consider both technical performance and how uncertainty is communicated. Data governance presents related challenges. Concerns extend beyond the initial collection of EEG signals to their processing, linkage with other information, sharing, and subsequent use [3, 4]. The significance of data can depend on the models applied to them and the purposes for which derived outputs are used. Research on BCI data governance has also highlighted questions about ownership, privacy, and responsibility [5]. These concerns do not imply that EEG provides unrestricted access to mental states. They concern the protection of information and the justification of interpretations within wider technological and commercial systems.

Several governance domains may address aspects of these products. Medical-device and general-wellness policies attend to intended purposes and claims, while data-protection and AI frameworks address aspects of processing and system use where applicable [6] [7] [8]. Technical standards and organizational processes offer additional approaches to ethical design and implementation [9]. However, the presence of multiple instruments leaves practical questions about how responsibilities connect across hardware, software, claims, and data pathways. Determining which rules apply is therefore only part of the governance problem.

Expert interviews offer a vital empirical tool for examining how these responsibilities are understood across the broader technology ecosystem. In digital health ecosystems, where hardware, cloud infrastructure, thirdparty applications, and user data streams intersect, governance cannot rely solely on static regulatory frameworks or proprietary closed-door practices. Instead, establishing effective oversight requires insights into how multi-sector actors conceptualize data privacy, open science principles, and practical technological implementation. Such accounts also require critical interpretation because professional roles inevitably shape judgments about risk and appropriate intervention. Responsible innovation scholarship further emphasizes the importance of including affected people in decisions about emerging technologies [10] [11] [12]. Expert perspectives can illuminate these governance questions but cannot substitute for users' experiences. However, prior research has rarely triangulated perspectives across the full spectrum of digital health stakeholders. An unresolved question is how stakeholders working across the neurotechnology ecosystem-integrating insights across industry developers, clinical researchers, and policy experts spanning diverse geographical and regulatory environments-understand the allocation of responsibility when product claims, data practices, and oversight involve multiple organizations.

Study Objective

This qualitative interview study investigated expert stakeholder perspectives on the ethical risks, data privacy challenges, and governance of consumer EEG-based neurotechnology. Drawing on semi-structured interviews with 19 cross-sector experts, including software and hardware developers, clinicians, researchers, bioethicists, and regulatory policy experts across North America, Europe, and Latin America, the study examined how stakeholders navigate tensions between open technology ecosystems and user protection, evaluating product claims, neurodata handling, and the allocation of governance responsibilities across the product lifecycle.

Methods

Study Design

We conducted a qualitative interview study to examine expert stakeholder perspectives on the ethical, legal, and regulatory challenges of consumer EEG-based neurotechnologies and non-invasive brain-computer interfaces. Semi-structured interviews systematically explored perceived risks, limitations of existing oversight, product claims, data privacy practices, and the allocation of governance responsibility.

Participants and Recruitment

Nineteen expert stakeholders participated in the study. Participants were selected for their professional expertise or experience in neuroscience, clinical practice, neuroengineering, device development, data science, philosophy, ethics, law, regulation, or policy. Recruitment combined purposive and snowball sampling across North America, Europe, and Latin America. Potential participants were identified through searches of institutional databases and recommendations generated through snowball recruitment. They were invited by email, and interview dates and times were confirmed through email correspondence. Recruitment sought diversity in professional background, gender, and seniority. Recruitment ended after 19 interviews when the researchers judged that additional interviews were no longer contributing new concepts or relationships relevant to the study aims.

Participants were organized into three broad categories according to their primary area of expertise: researchers and clinicians, including philosophers and data-informatics specialists; engineers, developers, and industry stakeholders; and stakeholders working in ethics, law, regulation, and policy. These categories contextualized participants' accounts and supported interpretation of how professional roles shaped their perspectives, functioning as qualitative context rather than statistically comparable groups.

Data Collection

Individual semi-structured interviews were conducted in English using licensed Zoom or Microsoft Teams platforms, according to participant preference. Interviews lasted approximately 30 to 45 minutes. A common interview guide was used across stakeholder groups, allowing participants to address the questions from their own professional perspectives.

The guide covered three broad areas: participants' professional background and work with neurotechnology; perceived benefits, risks, and barriers to application; and ethical, legal, and regulatory issues and potential governance responses. Topics included device safety, accuracy, efficacy, privacy, security, responsibility, policy gaps, standards, and mechanisms for preventing harm. Participants were also asked how ethical considerations informed their work and what barriers they encountered in practice.

Julie Moonga conducted all interviews. Interviews were audio-recorded, and transcripts were generated during the interviews using the videoconferencing platforms' transcription functions. The transcripts formed the textual dataset for analysis.

Data Analysis

Authors J.M. and L.S. analyzed the interview data using thematic analysis. Analysis followed an iterative process of familiarization, coding, theme development, review, and interpretation, drawing on the approach described by Braun and Clarke [13] [14] [15] [16]. Analysis focused on patterns of meaning across participants' accounts rather than fine-grained linguistic or conversational features.

Transcripts were read repeatedly to identify relevant passages and develop initial codes. Coding captured concerns about safety, accuracy, efficacy, misleading claims, privacy, security, vulnerability, responsibility, regulation, standards, transparency, and inequality. Participant responses were also condensed into progressively more abstract summaries to examine relationships among concerns and develop broader analytical categories. Codes and categories were compared across the dataset and revised as analysis progressed.

Related codes were grouped into candidate themes. These themes were reviewed against the coded extracts and the wider dataset, and their boundaries and definitions were refined to produce five final themes. Analysis examined both the concerns participants expressed and how they located responsibility for addressing them. Particular attention was given to relationships among product classification, evidential credibility, data practices, user protection, and governance mechanisms.

Coding discrepancies were reviewed to reach agreement on the interpretation of relevant extracts. Quotations were selected to illustrate recurring patterns, tensions, and analytically important positions. Minor edits removed repeated filler words or irrelevant fragments without changing meaning. Quotations are attributed using anonymized participant codes.

Interpretation and Reflexivity

Participants' accounts were interpreted in relation to their professional roles and responsibilities. The analysis considered how disciplinary and institutional perspectives could influence judgments about acceptable risk, adequate evidence, and appropriate oversight. Accounts were therefore treated as situated expert perspectives rather than definitive assessments of device performance, legal applicability, or consumer experience.

The interpretation was informed by neuroethics and governance scholarship, including attention to relationships among technologies, institutions, commercial actors, and users. Theme development remained responsive to the interview material, and candidate interpretations were reviewed against participants' accounts. The policy implications presented in the Discussion constitute the authors' synthesis of the findings in relation to the literature and are distinguished from the interview findings.

Ethical Considerations

The study received ethical approval from King's College London (United Kingdom) under reference LRS-22/23-39469. Participants provided verbal consent before interview recording. Participant identities are protected through anonymized quotation identifiers and broad reporting of professional categories. Full transcripts are not publicly available because of the sensitivity of the interviews and the restrictions associated with ethical approval. Deidentified quotations are presented to substantiate the analysis.

Theme 2: Evidential Credibility and Claims-Related Harm

Participants linked accuracy, precision, efficacy, and the substantiation of claims to protection of users. Technical performance was discussed in relation to what companies communicated and what users might expect from a device. Health-related claims were a particular concern because they could influence decisions by people seeking help.

One participant explicitly located responsibility for substantiating claims with companies: "The onus should be on the companies that are making claims.

"Cure your depression", for example, is one that I see all the time. If you are making that strong claim, it needs to be vetted." (P3)

The same participant emphasized that performance needed attention over time: "We need to get the right accuracy and precision, and then retrain over time to adjust to changing brain states."

(P3)

A further account connected vulnerability with limited information: "When someone is a patient, desperation and lack of information can lead to decisions that are not so good."

(P4)

Together, these extracts connected evidential quality with the circumstances in which users evaluated claims. Beyond whether a technology produced a raw output, the key analytical question was whether that output and its advertised meaning warranted users' confidence. Participants' concerns suggested that the burden of evaluating uncertain claims could fall on people who lacked sufficient information or were strongly motivated to obtain a benefit. This theme therefore linked technical credibility with interpretive risk: the possibility that users would place confidence in claims or outputs whose evidential basis they could not readily assess. The interviews documented concerns regarding these potential risks, though quantifying the actual incidence of user harm remains beyond the study scope.

Theme 3: Neurodata Privacy, Security, and Inference

Participants raised concerns about the collection, handling, interpretation, and potential reuse of brain-derived information. Their accounts connected privacy with tracking, dual use, the value of data, and the purposes for which information was processed.

One participant linked tracking with uncertainty about permissible uses: "Everything is tracking.

What are the rules that are going to govern these sorts of things? Because we also have to deal with dual use." (P5)

Another emphasized the importance of examining purposes and subsequent use: Concerns also extended to the interpretation of data through computational models: "There's no transparency into how the model after passing through all these hidden layers actually came to a particular conclusion." (P3)

The latter account concerned model interpretation more broadly and illustrated a related problem for systems that translated data into user-facing conclusions. Participants' concerns extended beyond initial data collection to encompass downstream processing, algorithmic conclusion generation, and secondary data uses.

The analytical interpretation was that data protection and interpretive accountability were connected. Decisions about collection, processing, and reuse could affect both privacy and the meaning attributed to an output. Rather than validating the empirical accuracy of consumer EEG in revealing sensitive mental states, these accounts focused on the governance of data and interpretations whose scope could expand beyond initial recordings.

Theme 4: Layered Governance and Explicit Accountability

Participants described combinations of governance mechanisms rather than a single regulatory solution. Their proposals included standards, professional guidance, company policies, and statutory regulation. The value of combining mechanisms was accompanied by concern about implementation and compliance.

One participant described the need for complementary instruments: "We have no choice but to fill the gaps using soft law and maybe more than one mechanism, maybe standards, professional guidelines, company policies.

It has to be some combination of those." (P7)

Another identified technical standards as an opportunity: "Opportunity is through standards.

IEEE is looking at standards in the neuroscience or neural tech area, so that would be one opportunity to have an official industry standard, like an IEEE or ISO standard of appropriate governance." (P8)

However, a further participant challenged the assumption that legally binding rules necessarily resolved compliance problems: "Non-compliance is a problem not just with soft law, but also with hard law.

There is tremendous noncompliance with hard law, so it is not unique to soft law." (P2)

These accounts revealed a tension between expanding the available governance instruments and ensuring that those instruments influenced practice. Standards and guidance were viewed as potential means of addressing gaps, while the account of noncompliance qualified any simple distinction between effective statutory regulation and ineffective voluntary approaches.

Read alongside the concerns about company claims and downstream data use, this theme raised the question of how responsibilities were allocated across governance arrangements. The analytical interpretation was that while multiple governance instruments may cover distinct technical domains, they risk obscuring underlying accountability unless explicit roles are defined. The interviews supported concern about the relationship between governance coverage, implementation, and accountability; they did not demonstrate that a particular combination of mechanisms would resolve that concern.

Theme 5: Participation, Global Equity, and Contextual Responsiveness

Participants connected governance with the inclusion of affected people and attention to the contexts in which technologies were used. Their accounts questioned whether professional and technical expertise alone could adequately address patients' perspectives and differences in regulatory protection.

One participant called for collaboration that included patients:

"There must be a collaborative effort, not only with the clinicians, the researchers, developers and manufacturers, also with the patient, to really understand their culture and their humanistic perspective."

Another connected the value of neural data with unequal regulatory protection: "There are more regulations in the Global North than there are in the Global South, and neural data has a lot of value."

(P3)

In a related extract, the same participant described differences in the perceived capacity to prevent misuse: "Global North are probably more aware of and can protect themselves in a way because there are regulations that are national regulations that can help prevent misuse of data."

(P3)

These accounts linked participation and equity through a common concern about whose circumstances informed governance. Patient involvement was presented as a way to understand cultural and human perspectives, while the accounts of regulatory inequality drew attention to differences in available protection.

The analytical interpretation was that governance arrangements could not be assessed solely through their technical requirements or formal existence. Participants also considered whether affected people were included and whether protections were meaningful across settings. These were expert perspectives on participation and inequality, rather than direct evidence of consumer experience or a systematic comparison of jurisdictions.

Discussion

Principal Findings

This study examined expert stakeholder perspectives on the ethical risks and governance of consumer EEGbased neurotechnology. Five interrelated themes connected regulatory boundaries, evidential credibility, neurodata practices, accountability, and participation. Across these themes, participants considered both the availability of governance mechanisms and the responsibilities associated with their implementation.

The central interpretation is that consumer neurotechnology governance extends far beyond determining whether a device fits a traditional medical regulatory category. Because consumer EEG devices function within open and interconnected digital technology ecosystems, governance must address the full lifecycle-from hardware engineering and algorithmic model design to third-party data flows and end-user marketing. A key strength of this study lies in its multi-regional, cross-sector empirical design. By triangulating perspectives across 19 expert stakeholders spanning industry developers, clinical researchers, and policy authorities across North America, Europe, and Latin America, our findings reflect the real-world friction between open digital innovation and user safety. This cross-sector alignment reveals that layering voluntary guidelines, technical standards, and statutory rules is ineffective unless specific responsibilities for data privacy, model validation, and user protection are explicitly assigned to identifiable actors.

This interpretation should be distinguished from a demonstrated accountability failure. The study examined expert accounts rather than product compliance, enforcement decisions, or consumers' experiences of harm.

It nevertheless identifies questions that existing governance arrangements need to address: who substantiates a claim, who remains responsible for downstream data uses, and who responds when product functionality or interpretation changes? Fig 1 brings together the five themes and illustrates how concerns about evidence, data practices, and oversight connect with responsibility across the product lifecycle.

Comparison With Prior Work

Previous scholarship has identified concerns about safety, privacy, autonomy, transparency, and evidential quality in direct-to-consumer neurotechnology [1, 2]. The present findings connect these concerns through stakeholders' accounts of governance and responsibility. Participants did not discuss evidence, data protection, and regulation solely as separate problems. Their accounts linked the credibility of claims with user vulnerability, the value of data with potential misuse, and the availability of standards with questions of compliance.

The theme of regulatory boundaries illustrates this relationship. Participants perceived difficulties when technologies moved between clinical, wellness, enhancement, and other nonmedical applications. These accounts should not be interpreted as evidence that consumer neurotechnology is categorically unregulated.

Rather, they suggest that the boundaries used to allocate oversight may not resolve all concerns associated with actual use. This is consistent with arguments that governance of direct-to-consumer neurotechnology requires attention to product claims and applications, alongside technical characteristics [2].

The findings also reinforce the ethical significance of evidential credibility. Participants located responsibility for substantiating claims with companies and connected limited information with users' vulnerability. This extends the discussion of safety beyond immediate physical effects to the confidence users place in product claims and feedback. Kreitmair [1] similarly identifies epistemic considerations as an important dimension of consumer neurotechnology ethics. In this study, the relevant concern was whether users could reasonably evaluate the meaning and reliability of information presented as brain-based. The interviews support attention to this interpretive burden, although they do not establish the frequency or consequences of misleading feedback.

The neurodata theme likewise connects privacy with the purposes and interpretation of information. Previous work has examined privacy and security risks in BCI ecosystems and the challenges associated with neural-data ownership and use [3] [4] [5] 17]. Participants' accounts emphasized collection purposes, subsequent uses, and transparency of computational interpretation. Their concerns therefore support examining the data pathway rather than treating the initial recording as the sole object of governance. This interpretation is consistent with Chiappone et al [18], whose comparative analysis of US and EU consumer data protections argues that governance should attend to the purposes and consequences of data use rather than focusing exclusively on whether information is classified as 'neural data'. This does not imply that EEG provides unrestricted access to mental states. The ethical question concerns the handling of information and the claims made about its significance, including when interpretations are uncertain.

Finally, participants' calls for patient involvement and attention to unequal regulatory protection align with scholarship on public participation and responsible innovation [10] [11] [12]. Their accounts suggest that governance depends partly on whose perspectives inform definitions of benefit, acceptable risk, and adequate protection.

Because the sample comprised experts, these findings establish the importance participants attributed to inclusion; they do not substitute for research with consumers or affected communities.

Implications for Policy and Practice

The findings support an approach that connects governance requirements with identifiable responsibilities.

The following implications are the authors' interpretation of the interview findings in relation to the literature. Finally, participation should contribute to identifying consequences and priorities that expert assessment may overlook. Users, patients, and affected communities can inform the interpretation of benefits, burdens, and acceptable data practices. This involvement should extend beyond consultation that has no influence on decisions. Cross-jurisdictional collaboration should also attend to differences in regulatory capacity and available protection, without assuming that frameworks developed in one setting will transfer unchanged to another [20]. The study identifies these considerations as governance priorities; their practical implementation requires work with the affected groups themselves. Limitations 413 While our sample (N = 19) provided deep qualitative triangulation across three distinct stakeholder sectors 414 (developers/industry, clinicians/researchers, and ethics/policy experts) across three major global regions (North America, Europe, and Latin America), it does not represent all regional regulatory environments or consumer end-user populations systematically. The multidisciplinary breadth supported robust cross-sector policy insights, but dedicated comparative studies across specific legal jurisdictions remain necessary.

The analysis examined participants' accounts rather than independently assessing device performance, legal compliance, enforcement, or harm. Proposed governance responses consequently remain implications for further consideration, rather than demonstrated solutions. In addition, product functionality and governance arrangements can change over time. Applying these findings to particular technologies or jurisdictions requires attention to the circumstances in which the interviews were conducted and subsequent developments.

Future research should examine consumer experiences, specific product and data pathways, and how responsibilities are allocated in practice. Evaluations of standards, claims review, and mechanisms for responding to concerns would help establish whether the proposed responses improve protection.

Conclusions

Expert stakeholders connected the governance of consumer EEG-based neurotechnology with regulatory boundaries, evidential credibility, neurodata practices, accountability, and participation. Their accounts suggest that ethical assessment should follow the relationships among products, claims, data uses, interpretations, and users. Combining governance mechanisms may address different concerns, but their effectiveness depends on clear responsibilities and implementation. The practical implication is to make responsibility for evidence, data practices, and user protection explicit, while involving affected people in determining what adequate protection requires.

A distinction between medical and nonmedical products remains important, but it should not be treated as a complete assessment of ethical significance. Manufacturers should clearly describe intended purposes and the limits of their claims, while relevant authorities should clarify how existing requirements apply. The findings do not justify applying identical requirements to all consumer EEG products. They support examining which obligations are appropriate to the consequences of particular claims and uses. Second, claims substantiation and communication of uncertainty deserve explicit attention. Developers and manufacturers are better positioned than consumers to provide evidence about the performance and limitations of their products. Disclosure should distinguish what has been evaluated from what remains uncertain and should identify the populations and conditions to which the evidence applies. Stronger claims, especially those directed toward people seeking health-related benefits, warrant closer scrutiny. This proposal follows participants' concerns about company responsibility and vulnerability; determining specific evidential thresholds would require further technical and regulatory work. Third, data governance must embrace an ecosystem-wide view that balances data privacy with transparent, open science practices. In consumer digital health, responsibilities for data handling are distributed across hardware manufacturers, app platforms, cloud analytics providers, and third-party software developers. Safeguarding user trust in open technology ecosystems requires clear documentation of data provenance, model transparency, and restrictions on secondary data monetization. Policy interventions must mandate explicit data pathways and organizational accountability rather than burdening end-users with deciphering complex consent agreements for derived neurodata outputs.Fourth, standards and professional guidance should be connected with implementation and accountability.Participants identified these mechanisms as useful complements to statutory regulation, while also noting that noncompliance occurs under both voluntary and binding arrangements. The existence of a standard is therefore not, by itself, evidence of adequate protection. Its value depends on what it requires, how adherence is assessed, and what follows when requirements are not met. Process-based approaches to ethical design and organizational governance may help make these responsibilities operational, but their effectiveness must be evaluated in practice[9,19]. These responsibilities also have a temporal dimension. Changes to software, processing, or product claims may alter what users receive after initial deployment. A lifecycle perspective therefore offers a practical extension of the findings: organizations should consider whether modifications require renewed evaluation, revised communication, or changes to safeguards. The interviews do not establish a specific monitoring regime, but participants' concerns about changing performance and compliance provide a rationale for examining continuing responsibility.
