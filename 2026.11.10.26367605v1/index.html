Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 225 papers containing 405 instances of non-exact p-values, the module correctly detected 269 cases (true positives) and incorrectly identified 78 (false positives). It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

Abstract

Background: The increasing use of genetic testing has identified thousands of KCNH2 variants associated with long QT syndrome type 2 (LQT2), yet most remain variants of uncertain significance (VUS). Translating functional abnormalities into clinically actionable evidence remains a major challenge. We established a nationwide functional genomics strategy to systematically characterize KCNH2 variants identified in the French LQT2 population and improve their clinical interpretation.

Methods: We characterized 303 KCNH2 variants, including 201 VUS, using standardized highthroughput automated patch-clamp electrophysiology and complementary membrane trafficking assays. Nine electrophysiological parameters were integrated using MeTAL to predict variant-specific repolarization phenotypes under homozygous and heterozygous conditions. Modeled QTc values were compared with patient electrocardiographic data and variant-specific penetrance estimates. Functional evidence was calibrated against established benign and pathogenic variants using ClinGenrecommended categorical OddsPath and continuous likelihood-ratio approaches.

Results: Multidimensional functional profiling revealed abnormalities not captured by current amplitude alone, identifying additional variants with clinically relevant repolarization defects. Among variants assessed under heterozygous conditions, MeTAL predictions showed 82.9% concordance with patient QTc phenotypes for ACMG class 5 variants, with apparent discordances partly consistent with incomplete penetrance. Continuous likelihood-ratio calibration demonstrated strong discrimination between established benign and pathogenic variants (AUC=0.936) and provided graded functional evidence for 76.1% of VUS. Integration of calibrated functional evidence into the ACMG/AMP framework enabled reclassification of 64 of 201 VUS (31.8%), including 7 as pathogenic, 46 as likely pathogenic, and 11 as likely benign.

Conclusions: This nationwide study establishes a scalable framework integrating high-throughput electrophysiology, computational modeling, and clinical phenotyping to translate complex KCNH2 channel dysfunction into clinically interpretable evidence. The resulting reclassification of nearly onethird of VUS, together with the open-access HAMMER database, provides a resource to improve molecular diagnosis and risk assessment in LQT2.

Introduction

Variants of KCNH2, the gene encoding the cardiac hERG potassium channel, have been implicated in congenital Long QT Syndrome type 2 (LQT2) and Short QT syndrome type 1 (SQT1), rare inherited arrhythmic disorders characterized by delayed or abbreviated ventricular repolarization and an increased risk of sudden cardiac death [1] [2] [3] [4] . The clinical relevance of LQT2 is further underscored by its distinct arrhythmic risk profile compared with LQT1, the comparatively limited protection afforded by beta-blocker therapy, and the particular susceptibility of hERG channels to pharmacological blockade by numerous drugs [5] [6] [7] . Early identification of individuals at risk is essential for optimizing clinical management and implementing preventive strategies. A major challenge in the era of genomic medicine is the rapid accumulation of KCNH2 variants in public clinical databases, from approximately 1,000 entries in 2021 to more than 3,900 in 2026 (ClinVar: https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/clinvar), driven by widespread adoption of high-throughput sequencing technologies. Among missense variants, approximately 80% remain classified as variants of uncertain significance (VUS) 8 according to the recommendations of the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics and the Association for Molecular Pathology 9 , and consequently represent a major focus of ongoing efforts toward clinical reclassification. Determining the pathogenicity of these VUS is a critical priority for accurate diagnosis and management of patients with inherited arrhythmic disorders, as well as for appropriate risk stratification within affected families 10.

In the context of KCNH2, patch-clamp electrophysiology is considered the gold standard for assessing the functional impact of genetic variants. Beyond functional characterization, electrophysiological studies provide important insights into genotype-phenotype relationships 8 . Importantly, the ClinGen Sequence Variant Interpretation Working Group has established recommendations for the use of wellvalidated functional assays as PS3/BS3 evidence, emphasizing validation against established pathogenic and benign controls and calibration of the resulting evidence strength 10 . Although functional interpretation of VUS has historically lagged behind the pace of variant discovery, coordinated international initiatives are now addressing this gap. Large-scale efforts to systematically characterize KCNH2 variants are underway, with hundreds of variants already assessed using standardized high-throughput in vitro platforms [11] [12] [13] [14] [15] [16] . These initiatives represent an important step toward translating variant detection into clinically relevant interpretation.

Despite their considerable value, these approaches remain subject to important limitations. First, electrophysiological studies lack standardized experimental protocols, making direct and reliable comparisons across studies challenging 8 . Second, because KCNH2-related disorders are inherited in an autosomal dominant manner, expression systems that strictly ensure equal expression of the wildtype (WT) and variant alleles are essential for accurately assessing variant pathogenicity in the heterozygous state. Third, functional analyses often focus on a restricted subset of biophysical parameters 11, 16 . Although hERG channel expression is an established determinant of channel function and disease risk, our recent systematic investigation demonstrated that other biophysical parameters, including channel inactivation, also play a role in determining the pathogenicity of individual variants 17 . Assessing channel conductance alone therefore fails to capture the full spectrum of functionally relevant properties and may lead to inaccurate estimates of the clinical pathogenicity of hERG VUS. Indeed, alterations in other hERG channel biophysical properties may either exacerbate or compensate for the pathogenic effects of altered channel expression levels. Individual variants often produce a mixture of gain-(GoF) and loss-of-function (LoF) features, rendering their clinical interpretation particularly challenging in the absence of an integrative analytical framework 18 . To address this complexity, we previously developed MeTAL 17 , a computational package specifically designed to integrate the full spectrum of hERG biophysical properties that provides QTc evaluations as clinicallyrelevant assessment of variant pathogenicity. MeTAL establishes a rationale linking channel phenotypes -including gating kinetics and voltage-dependence for activation, inactivation, recovery from inactivation and deactivation besides current density -to patient-level clinical manifestations.

Herein, using a standardized phenotyping pipeline, we systematically phenotyped, at the national level, 303 French variants including 201 VUS (corresponding to 56% of the 358 KCNH2 variants referenced in the national Cardiogen network (https://www.filiere-cardiogen.fr/)), 21 ACMG class 1 and 2 variants, 43 out of 78 ACMG class 4 and 38 out of 58 class 5 variants. 101 variants characterized were in common with an earlier large-scale phenotyping effort 11 . We further applied MeTAL as a binary classifier to identify functionally disruptive variants under homozygous conditions across all ACMG classes and subsequently to assess their predicted impact on ventricular repolarization under strict heterozygous conditions. Using this systematic and standardized approach, we stratified the pathogenicity of French KCNH2 VUS, including variants for which channel expression was preserved.

Whenever clinical data were available, MeTAL-based pathogenicity predictions were compared with electrocardiographic phenotypes from patients carrying the corresponding variants. Functional phenotypes were subsequently calibrated against established benign and pathogenic reference variants according to ClinGen recommendations, enabling the assignment of PS3/BS3 evidence strength for clinical variant interpretation. Integration of this functional evidence into the ACMG/AMP classification framework supported the reclassification of 58 VUS as likely pathogenic. Building on these complementary functional, computational, and clinical efforts, we established an open-access, web-based database integrating detailed genetic, clinical, functional, and patient-level data for all 303 variants. This resource provides a comprehensive systematic characterization of KCNH2 VUS undertaken at the scale of a national cohort and represents an important step toward resolving the clinical interpretation and management of KCNH2 VUS. It will support clinicians, researchers, and variant curators in the accurate diagnosis and risk stratification of LQT2 and will remain open to the integration and ACMG-based reclassification of newly identified VUS by other research groups adopting our standardized protocol. Under homozygous conditions, the resulting pseudoQTc values are not directly comparable with clinical QTc but have the advantage to classify the intrinsic pathogenicity of hERG variants. The reference dataset comprised 58 variants, including 20 benign/likely benign (B/LB; ACMG classes 1-2) and 38 pathogenic variants (ACMG class 5) (Figure 5a). Variants with normal functional phenotypes under homozygous conditions were represented by their pseudo-QTc values, whereas those with abnormal phenotypes were represented by their modeled-QTc values. This approach provided a common phenotypic readout for calibration across both experimental conditions.

Functional results were classified as normal or abnormal according to the modeled QTc phenotype. All 20 B/LB reference variants displayed a normal modeled QTc, whereas 31 of 38 pathogenic reference variants displayed an abnormal modeled QTc. Following application of the conservative add-one correction, normal and abnormal functional readouts yielded OddsPath values of 0.184 and 16.32, respectively, corresponding to BS3_moderate and PS3_moderate evidence (Figure 5b, Table S3).

To exploit the continuous nature of MeTAL-modeled QTc, we next developed a likelihood-ratio (LLR) calibration using the same reference dataset. MeTAL-modeled QTc showed high discriminatory performance between B/LB and pathogenic reference variants, with an area under the ROC curve of 0.936 (Figure 5e). The calibrated continuous LLR model translated modeled QTc values into graded strengths of evidence for pathogenicity or benignity, corresponding to PS3_supporting, PS3_moderate, PS3_strong and PS3_very strong, and to BS3_supporting, BS3_moderate and BS3_strong evidence. Modeled QTc values that did not reach the supporting threshold in either direction were considered functionally uninformative. The continuous calibration substantially refined functional evidence assignment compared with the categorical OddsPath approach (Figure 5f,g, Table S1). Among the 43 ACMG class 4 variants, 22 (51.2%) provided LLR-based evidence toward LQT pathogenicity, 13 of them reaching PS3_strong or PS3_very strong, whereas 16 (37.2%) provided evidence toward benignity and 4 (9.3%) were functionally uninformative (Table S1). One additional class 4 variant displayed an SQT-like modeled phenotype (<340 ms) and was therefore considered separately. Application of the continuous MeTAL-QTc calibration to the 201 VUS provided LLR-based evidence toward either pathogenicity or benignity for 153 variants (76.1%) (Figure 5f,g, Table S1).

Fifty-six variants (27.9%) provided evidence toward pathogenicity, 33 of them reaching PS3_strong or PS3_very strong, whereas 97 (48.3%) provided evidence toward benignity, 76 of them reaching BS3_moderate or BS3_strong (Table S1). Forty-four variants (21.9%) fell within the LLR-uninformative interval and therefore did not reach the supporting evidence threshold in either direction. Four additional VUS (2.0%) displayed an SQT-like modeled phenotype (<340 ms), bringing the total to five SQT-like variants across ACMG classes 3 and 4. Because marked QT shortening may represent a pathogenic GoF phenotype of KCNH2, these variants were not assigned benign evidence despite falling on the low-QTc side of the calibration. The present LLR model was calibrated along the LoF/long-QT spectrum and therefore does not quantify pathogenic evidence in the short-QT direction. These variants were consequently considered separately from both the benign and LLR-uninformative categories. Overall, whereas categorical OddsPath calibration assigned a uniform moderate level of functional evidence based solely on a normal versus abnormal phenotype, continuous LLR calibration captured the magnitude of the functional alteration, distinguishing variants providing strong or very strong functional evidence from those providing only supporting evidence or yielding functionally uninformative results. This substantially increased the resolution of functional evidence assignment, particularly among VUS.

Calibrated functional evidence enables reclassification of KCNH2 VUS -Finally, we assessed whether

the calibrated functional evidence generated in this study could contribute to resolving the 201 KCNH2

VUS according to the ACMG/AMP framework 9 . To quantitatively integrate the different lines of evidence, ACMG/AMP evidence strengths were converted into the Bayesian point-based framework developed by Tavtigian and colleagues 23, 24 . Pre-existing PS3 or BS3 criteria were removed before calculation of the baseline score to avoid double counting functional evidence, and the newly generated MeTAL-modeled QTc evidence was subsequently incorporated using either categorical OddsPath or continuous likelihood-ratio (LLR) calibration 10 (Tables S5 and S6). Notably, pre-existing functional evidence was available for only 2 of the 201 VUS; our systematic characterization therefore provided new functional evidence for 199 variants (99.0%). Using categorical OddsPath calibration, 63 of the 201 VUS (31.3%) fulfilled the criteria for PS3_moderate, whereas 138 (68.7%) met the criteria for BS3_moderate (Figure 5c,d). Within the quantitative framework, moderate evidence contributes +2 points toward pathogenicity or -2 points toward benignity 23, 24 , allowing the calibrated functional evidence to be integrated with the remaining ACMG/AMP evidence originally associated with each variant 9 . Incorporation of OddsPath-calibrated functional evidence reclassified 58 of the 63 variants assigned PS3_moderate (92.1%) from VUS to likely pathogenic, corresponding to 28.9% of the 201 VUS, whereas the remaining five retained their VUS classification (Figure 6a,b). Conversely, all 138 variants assigned BS3_moderate remained classified as VUS, despite a two-point shift toward benignity. Overall, 143 of the 201 variants (71.1%) therefore remained classified as VUS. These results illustrate the additive nature of the Bayesian point-based framework, in which the impact of functional evidence depends both on its empirically supported strength and on the evidence already available for each variant 10, 23, 24 . This asymmetric effect largely reflected the distribution of pre-existing nonfunctional evidence. Most variants were already positioned close to the likely pathogenic threshold, with 147/201 (73.1%) having a baseline score of +5 before incorporation of the newly calibrated functional evidence. Consequently, the addition of PS3_moderate (+2 points) was sufficient to move most functionally abnormal variants beyond the ≥6-point likely pathogenic threshold, whereas BS3_moderate (-2 points) was generally insufficient to reach the ≤-1-point likely benign threshold (Figure 6a). Thus, the differential impact of PS3_moderate and BS3_moderate on VUS resolution reflects their integration with the existing evidence profile of each variant, rather than differences in their numerical weight.

We next investigated whether the graded functional evidence provided by continuous LLR calibration could overcome the limitations of assigning uniformly moderate evidence and enable more definitive ACMG/AMP classifications (Figure 6a,c, Table S6). This approach enabled the reclassification of 64 of the 201 VUS (31.8%), including seven as pathogenic, 46 as likely pathogenic, and 11 as likely benign.

Among the 56 variants receiving evidence supporting pathogenicity, 53 were reclassified (seven as pathogenic and 46 as likely pathogenic), while three remained VUS. Notably, all seven variants receiving very strong pathogenic evidence reached the pathogenic classification, and all 26 variants receiving strong pathogenic evidence were reclassified as likely pathogenic. Conversely, among the 97 variants receiving evidence supporting benignity, 11 were reclassified as likely benign, all supported by strong benign evidence, while 86 remained VUS. An additional 44 variants received uninformative evidence, while 4 variants displaying SQT-like phenotypes were not assigned LLR-based evidence because the calibration was not applicable to QT shortening. These 48 variants retained their baseline ACMG/AMP classification. Although the overall reclassification rate was only modestly higher with LLR than with OddsPath calibration (31.8% versus 28.9%), the distribution of final classifications differed substantially (Figure 6b,c). Whereas OddsPath calibration exclusively reclassified variants as likely pathogenic, continuous LLR calibration enabled reclassification toward both pathogenicity and benignity, including seven pathogenic and 11 likely benign classifications. This demonstrates the added value of quantitatively graded functional evidence in refining ACMG/AMP classification outcomes beyond those achievable with uniformly moderate evidence.

Importantly, the non-functional ACMG/AMP criteria were incorporated at their originally assigned strengths and were not independently reassessed in this study 20 . Discussion

The increasing availability of clinical sequencing has revealed a large and growing number of KCNH2 variants, while functional characterization has remained comparatively limited. Large-scale functional studies have begun to address this gap, demonstrating the feasibility of multiplexed trafficking assays and high-throughput electrophysiology for generating functional evidence at scale.

Early studies established the value of massively parallel assays for identifying traffickingdeficient KCNH2 variants, initially focusing on selected regions of the channel 15, 31 . More recently, automated patch-clamp approaches have enabled direct measurement of channel function for hundreds of variants, providing a complementary readout to multiplexed trafficking assays and demonstrating their potential for clinical variant interpretation 11, 14, [32] [33] [34] . The recent study by O'Neill et al. 11 further demonstrated the value of combining large-scale functional measurements with clinical phenotyping and penetrance estimates for KCNH2-LQT2 variant classification and risk stratification.

A major strength of the present study is its population-based, national-scale design. Rather than selecting variants according to their location within the channel or their prior classification, we Comparisons with previously published functional datasets highlight the importance of integrating multiple biophysical properties when interpreting KCNH2 variants. Discrepancies across platforms may arise from differences in experimental conditions, particularly WT-to-variant expression ratios, but also reflect the limitations of approaches relying primarily on current amplitude or trafficking measurements. Indeed, alterations in gating properties may reinforce or compensate for changes in current density, ultimately modifying their impact on ventricular repolarization. By integrating these combined effects into a modeled QTc phenotype, MeTAL provides a complementary framework for refining functional evidence and emphasizes the importance of orthogonal validation in variant interpretation.

Variants were evaluated in two genotype contexts. Homozygous expression measures the intrinsic capacity of a variant to perturb hERG function, whereas heterozygous expression reproduces the molecular context of LQT2 carriers and tests whether the defect persists in the presence of WT channels, where dominant-negative effects and allele dosage operate. The two conditions were not interchangeable in our dataset: 69 variants showed a marked defect under homozygous conditions while retaining near-normal function in the heterozygous state, consistent with haploinsufficiency rather than dominant-negative suppression. Extending this to patients, MeTAL predictions showed high patient-level concordance for class 5 variants (82.9%), and most apparent mismatches occurred for variants for which other carriers displayed the predicted phenotype, consistent with the incomplete penetrance of LQT2 and with recent large-scale penetrance analyses 11, 22 . A normal QTc in a carrier therefore does not contradict a pathogenic functional effect, nor does an abnormal QTc establish that the KCNH2 variant is solely responsible: patient-level measurements contextualize functional predictions rather than serving as a gold standard against which they are scored. Penetrance itself increased with ACMG pathogenicity, from exclusively low estimates among class 1-2 variants to >50% penetrance for 72.2% and 77.8% of class 4 and class 5 variants, respectively, yet varied substantially within classes. ACMG classification estimates the probability that a variant is diseasecausing; penetrance estimates the probability that carriers express the phenotype. The expression system, although standardized and highly scalable, does not reproduce the molecular and electrophysiological environment of human cardiomyocytes, and the controlled WT/variant context used to model heterozygosity may not reproduce all aspects of endogenous allele-specific expression, subunit assembly or cellular regulation.MeTAL is an intermediate computational representation of ventricular repolarization and pseudo-ECG modelling rather than a complete model of ventricular repolarization, and therefore cannot capture all interactions among cardiac ionic currents. Clinical QTc is itself influenced by age, sex, heart rate, medications, autonomic state, and genetic and environmental modifiers, so a single ECG measurement may underestimate the functional contribution of a pathogenic variant. These limitations reinforce rather than diminish the importance of integrating functional and clinical evidence.Together, our findings establish a scalable framework in which standardized multidimensional electrophysiology defines channel dysfunction and MeTAL translates this dysfunction into a clinically interpretable repolarization phenotype. Continuous calibration then quantifies its evidentiary strength, allowing it to contribute directly to variant reclassification within the ACMG/AMP framework, while patient phenotypes and penetrance provide complementary clinical context. Combined with open dissemination through HAMMER, this framework provides a direct path from high-throughput functional genomics to evidence-based interpretation of KCNH2 variants in patients and their families.
