Power Analysis Check

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). Statcheck might still miss one-sided tests that are described in another sentence, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. Some differences come from the ground truth: it lacks p-values with a correction name (“p Bonf = .061”), and counts partial eta squared written with its subscript after it (“η 2 p = .05”), “p = .05”, and some table notes and figure legends (“† p < .10, *p < .05”), which this module leaves out, as non-significant p-values. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. Introduction

The number of reported dengue cases worldwide more than doubled from 2023 to 2024 1 . This increase was in part driven by the largest epidemic ever recorded in the Americas with Brazil reporting almost 5.7 million suspected cases, representing 79% of the total in the Americas.

Transmission is endemic in large parts of Brazil and large-scale outbreaks have occurred regularly; however, the 2024 outbreak was unprecedented.

Of all hypotheses being proposed to explain Brazil's unprecedented 2024 dengue outbreak, climate change is the most common. An El Niño event increased temperatures and altered rainfall, increasing mosquito density and expanding the vector's range 1, 2 . Temperature shapes dengue transmission through its effect on vector life-history traits and viral replication 3 . Mechanistic models based on mosquito thermal physiology predict that basic reproductive numbers (R0) increase nonmonotonically with temperature, reaching a peak before declining at higher temperatures 3, 4 . These temperature-dependent R0 curves have been used to project future expansion of suitable habitat and epidemic potential under climate change 3, 5, 6 . More recently, a global analysis found dengue incidence to similarly depend nonlinearly on temperature 7 . The nonlinear effects of temperature, combined with the multiplicative nature of transmission dynamics, means that even modest increases in temperature could lead to disproportionate case surges.

In addition to climate, other contributing factors, many related to immunity, have been put forward.

Serotype dynamics may have created local gaps in immunity: the co-circulation of multiple serotypes 8 , the re-emergence of DENV-3 in São Paulo [8] [9] [10] , and the DENV-2 Cosmopolitan genotype into the Western Amazon 11 all placed populations with limited serotype-specific immunity at greater risk. Prior heterotypic immunity may have enhanced disease severity through antibodydependent enhancement, potentially inflating case counts 12 . Increased human mobility may also have facilitated introduction into new areas. Urbanization and deforestation in the Central-West and Southeast may have expanded Aedes aegypti breeding sites 13, 14 , exposing populations with low immunity. Other factors unrelated to immunity have also been proposed: co-circulating arboviruses with similar symptoms (e.g., Oropouche, chikungunya, and Zika) have posed challenges to surveillance and likely inflated suspected dengue counts [15] [16] [17] [18] , while differences in reporting may have contributed to the elevated counts observed in 2024.

Climate change and immunity are often examined in isolation, with less attention given to how they may interact spatially. As climates warm, areas transition from unsuitable to suitable for sustained transmission, exposing fully susceptible populations to dengue. As seen in pandemics, initial outbreaks of immunizing pathogens in such populations greatly exceed subsequent outbreaks, as initial large outbreaks induce immunity that reduces or blunts future transmission. A recent exploratory analysis of municipality-level dengue incidence and temperature in Brazil observed a shift from a positive correlation in 2013 to a negative correlation in 2024, with population immunity proposed as a potential driver of this change. 19 Other authors have highlighted the expansion of dengue into areas with low historical transmission as possible drivers of incidence in recent years 20, 21 , but often the focus is on the impact of temperature on mosquito abundance and transmission alone 1, 2, [22] [23] [24] . Quantifying susceptibility from case data alone remains challenging: widespread asymptomatic infection 25 and multiple circulating serotypes and genotypes 26 , and limited serological surveillance obscure real immunity levels, making it difficult to disentangle temperature's direct effect on transmission from immunity-driven blunting of transmission driven by historical transmission that itself is temperature driven.

To investigate the drivers of Brazil's 2024 dengue epidemic, we analyzed 24 years of state and municipality-level case and temperature data. A striking pattern observed in Brazil requires explanation: many northern states with consistently high temperatures (previously associated with higher dengue transmission) experienced average or below-average case counts, while southeastern states with comparatively lower temperatures, saw unprecedented outbreaks. We fit models to quantify the importance of temperature and population immunity in explaining the 2024 outbreak and its spatial patterns. Using a mechanistic, temperature-dependent transmission model, we inferred state-level susceptibility indices, and tested whether accounting for spatial variation in immunity could resolve the apparent contradiction between temperature and incidence patterns.

Our study addresses how climate-driven range expansion interacts with immunity dynamics to shape dengue emergence and outbreaks, insights that have implications for anticipating future outbreaks as warming continues.

Results

Temperature is insufficient to explain the spatial patterns in the 2024 outbreak

The Brazil 2024 dengue season (defined as July 2023 to June 2024) was unprecedented in scale (Fig. 1A), with weekly case counts exceeding all prior seasons in our dataset. Spatially, the burden was concentrated in the Southeast and South (Fig. 1C); nine states, primarily in the Southeast, accounted for 97% of cases despite comprising 67% of the population. This concentration is a reversal of historic spatial patterns: the heatmap of case distribution relative to population (Fig. 1B) shows that states in the Southeast and South -historically less dominant -were disproportionately represented in 2024, while many traditional hotspots in the Northeast and North contributed relatively less compared with their historical profiles. Equivalent descriptive results using confirmed cases only are shown in SI Appendix, Fig. S1.

The geographic shift in incidence toward cooler southern states raises the question of whether those same states experienced unusually higher temperatures relative to the rest of the country.

Most states recorded higher-than-average temperatures in 2024, with only four exceptions (two in the North and two in the Northeast; Fig. 1D). We next systematically explored the relationship between temperature and incidence by calculating the Pearson correlation (adjusted for spatial autocorrelation) between log10 total incidence and mean temperature for each of the seasons 2001-2024. The Pearson correlation for the 2024 season was -0.72 (95% confidence intervals (CI): -0.96 to 0.09; Fig. 2), a large negative point estimate indicating that states with a lower mean temperature tended to have larger outbreaks in 2024, though the association is not statistically significant.

Correlations prior to the mid-2010s tended to be positive, and were significantly positive for the 2001, 2003-2009, 2011, 2012, and 2017 seasons (as per Pearson correlation coefficients; Fig. 2). We found a significant declining trend in the correlation over the study period, fitted on the Fisher-z scale (Methods): -0.059 per year for Pearson (95% CI: -0.082 to -0.035) and -0.044 per year for Spearman (95% CI: -0.062 to -0.026); the fitted trend crosses zero in the mid-2010s and its 95% band excludes zero over the most recent seasons. The corresponding confirmed-case correlations are shown in SI Appendix, Fig. S2. The same declining pattern was observed at different spatial resolutions (health regions and municipalities; see SI Appendix S4 and Fig. S3). This decline suggests that temperature changes alone did not determine outbreak size in 2024 and/or temperature has a non-monotonic relationship with incidence.

Transmission remains high at elevated temperatures, but declines with immunity

The shift in relationship between temperature and incidence, and particularly the shift towards negative correlations in recent seasons, could have been driven by temperatures in the northern states becoming too high for effective transmission 24 , or by spatial variation in immunity (higher in the north, lower in the south). Fig. 2 shows that incidence in warmer states did not noticeably drop during the 2024 season, arguing against temperature directly limiting transmission. Nevertheless, to test these hypotheses while accounting for epidemic dynamics, we estimated weekly reproductive numbers (Rt) for each state using time series of weekly cases, and then fit a generalized additive model (GAM) with temperature as a smooth predictor. This approach allows us to detect whether and to what extent transmission declines at high temperatures. We found that Rt increased with temperature up to 28.4°C (95% CI: 27.9 -28.9°C; solid line in the top panel of Fig. 3), and decreased thereafter. Though Rt decreased with temperatures increasing above 28.4°C, we found that they remained high (for example, Rt at 32°C was higher than 70% (95% CI: 66 -75) of observed values across the country and time). Notably, when the fitted temperature response is evaluated at each two-week window of the 2024 season and averaged across the season, its contribution to log(Rt) was higher for the northern and northeastern states (+0.08 to +0.11 for Amapá, Roraima, Amazonas, Piauí, Ceará, and Maranhão) than for São Paulo (-0.09), reflecting the north's sustained near-optimal temperatures through most of the year versus São Paulo's briefer warm season. Temperature alone would therefore predict greater transmission potential in the north than in São Paulo, making it difficult to attribute the north's comparatively modest 2024 incidence to thermal suppression. Note, however, that it is at the extremes where there is least data to inform the decline in Rt with precision.

Given that the fitted temperature contribution to log(Rt), averaged across two-week windows of the 2024 season, was higher in the northern and northeastern states listed above than in São Paulo, we next asked whether historical incidence could explain variation in transmission through the accumulation of immunity. We incorporated cumulative incidence at multiple time scales (within each season and over our entire dataset) as proxies for susceptible depletion. We found clear decreases in Rt as cumulative incidence increased at both timescales, supporting a strong role for susceptibility (Fig. 3). Including these cumulative incidence terms increased the deviance explained from 12.9% to 17.4%; a model including only the cumulative incidence terms explained less deviance (6.8%) than temperature alone (12.9%), though the combined model explained more than either alone, indicating that temperature and susceptibility capture largely independent components of variation in Rt. The inclusion of cumulative incidence did not appreciably change the estimated temperature response function (dashed vs. solid line in the top panel of Fig. 3): the two models' confidence envelopes overlapped across the full range of observed temperatures. Similarly, the patterns in Rt with cumulative incidences barely changed with and without the inclusion of temperature (dotted vs. dashed lines in the bottom panels of Fig. 3). The corresponding analyses using confirmed cases are shown in SI Appendix, Fig. S4.

Changing correlations between temperature and incidence reflect spatial variation in susceptible depletion

To understand the circumstances that might give rise to reversals in the relationship between temperature and incidence, while also including the effects of immunity, we ran simulations using synthetic temperature time series with varying spatiotemporal patterns. The four scenarios (constant, linearly increasing, step increase, and spatially homogeneous high temperature; Fig. S5) are deliberate idealizations, each constructed to isolate one feature, rather than to reconstruct Brazil's actual trajectory. These simulations show the correlation between temperature and incidence can change signs over time (SI Appendix, Fig. S6), as we observed in the data (Fig. 2).

At the time of introduction, when populations are fully susceptible, the correlation starts positive.

However, as susceptibles are rapidly depleted in warmer northern states but not in cooler southern states, later outbreaks shift southward, despite the lower temperatures. This causes the correlation to turn negative (the timing of the sign change depends on whether mean temperatures remain constant or increase linearly). Once susceptibles are also depleted in southern states, the correlation returns to positive. Similarly, a sharp increase in mean temperature across all states (due to, e.g., an unusually warm year) can also cause a positive correlation to temporarily turn negative (SI Appendix, Fig. S6). In contrast, removing spatial variation in temperatures and thus in immunity (assuming similarly high temperatures throughout Brazil) causes the correlation to turn negative just after dengue introduction, but it returns to positive much sooner, and remains positive throughout the simulation for most parameters explored. These simulations show that the shifting temperature-incidence correlation observed in Brazil may arise from spatial variation in immunity that results from spatial variation in temperature, rather than from temperature's direct effect on transmission alone.

Mechanistic estimates of susceptibility illustrate the temperature-immunity trade-off

Our GAM analysis, which revealed strong dependencies between reproductive numbers, temperature, and proxies for immunity, is a phenomenological approach that cannot distinguish how temperature affects transmission (whether directly, via effects on the thermal biology of the vector, or indirectly, through past transmission shaping immunity levels). To more explicitly estimate susceptibility and examine how it interacts with temperature in driving transmission and the role it played during the 2024 season outbreak, we estimated an initial susceptibility index (𝑆 !" #$% !"! & ; an estimate of the proportion susceptible at the start of a season) in each state for each season using a published temperature-dependent mechanistic SEIR-SEI model. Our estimates of 𝑆 !" #$% !"! & show significant variation over time, particularly in southern states (SI Appendix, Fig. S7). Spatially, susceptibility indices were higher in the south, particularly in São Paulo, Goiás, Distrito Federal, and Minas Gerais, and lower in northern states (SI Appendix, Fig. S8). The relationship between inferred initial susceptibility and observed incidence is shown in SI Appendix, Fig. S9. While numbers cannot be directly compared, these broad patterns are consistent with regional dengue seroprevalence in participants in a vaccine trial in 16 sites across Brazil: similar seroprevalence was observed in the North, Northeast, and Central-West, with lower seroprevalence in the Southeast and substantially lower seroprevalence in the South 27 . As the correlations between mean temperature and incidence demonstrated, outbreak sizes are not solely driven by higher temperatures. In 2024, the larger outbreaks occurred in states with a relatively low mean temperature but a comparatively high susceptibility (e.g., Minas Gerais, São Paulo, and Distrito Federal; Fig. 4; SI Appendix, Fig. S10). Conversely, locations with low susceptibility required a particularly high temperature (and thus transmission) to generate a larger-than-normal outbreak (e.g., Amapá). Discussion

Brazil experienced an unprecedented dengue season in 2024, coinciding with record high mean temperatures across much of the country. This prompted climate change to be proposed as a primary cause, yet the largest outbreaks occurred in southeastern states with comparatively lower temperatures than northern states (Fig. 1), culminating in the shift to negative spatial correlations (Fig. 2). We show this apparent contradiction is explained by spatial variation in population immunity: the 2024 surge resulted from an increasingly suitable climate interacting with areas of high susceptibility. Significant clusters of incidence anomalies aligned with states we inferred as having relatively high susceptibility (e.g., São Paulo, Minas Gerais, Distrito Federal), while states in the North and Northeast, which also experienced warming, had lower susceptibility and reported more limited outbreaks (SI Appendix, Fig. S8). We hypothesized this pattern could arise either from temperatures in northern states becoming too high for efficient transmission, or from geographic variability in susceptibility. Analysis of effective reproductive numbers supported the latter hypothesis: transmission at high temperatures remained substantial (70th percentile of the Rt -temperature curve), while models incorporating susceptibility proxies explained significantly more variation and showed clear declines in Rt with cumulative incidences (Fig. 3). Finally, we used a mechanistic SEIR-SEI model to estimate a susceptibility index for each state and season, quantifying the spatial variation in immunity (Fig. 4). Out-of-sample predictions for 2024 showed that both temperature and the susceptibility index were necessary to adequately predict the statelevel 2024 incidences (while susceptibility and temperature, in isolation, performed comparatively poorly; Fig. 5). The significant interaction between temperature and susceptibility suggests that temperature's association with incidence is modulated by susceptibility: the effect of temperature is greater with high levels of susceptibility, and similarly, the effect of susceptibility on incidence is greater at higher temperatures.

The observed shift from positive to negative fitted correlations between incidence and temperature could serve as a signature of range expansion into immunologically naive populations. The relationship between dengue incidence and temperature is mediated by immunity, and can be expected to evolve, from when a virus is initially introduced, to endemicity. In the initial phases post-introduction to climatically suitable areas and immunologically naive populations, a positive correlation between incidence and temperature might be expected, though some variability may arise due to differences in the timing of introduction of DENV to new areas. However, as susceptible hosts are depleted in these optimal environments, the spatial patterns of incidence would change.

Climate-driven expansion of the vector's range into new, immunologically naive areas, particularly large metropolitan centers, could compound this effect. Together, these processes would lead to an increasingly heterogeneous immune landscape where low susceptibility blunts outbreaks despite more optimal temperatures, resulting in the observed negative correlations. In the future, as dengue becomes endemic and Brazil becomes more immunologically homogeneous, we might expect the correlation between temperature and incidence to become positive again.

The role of population immunity in explaining dengue outbreaks is often overlooked, but crucial given the role played by range expansion in the 2024 outbreak in Brazil. In a recent analysis of 21 mostly dengue-endemic countries, using case data up to 2019, Childs et al. (2025) 7 estimated the average temperature-incidence relationship across locations and tested whether it varies with population immunity, using lagged incidence as a proxy. Finding it similar across immunity levels, they applied a single temperature response when projecting the effects of warming. Our Rt analysis is broadly consistent: the temperature response is similar with and without the cumulative-incidence terms (top panel of Fig. 3). That the response is largely immunity-invariant does not, however, mean immunity is unimportant for outbreak magnitude. The cumulative-incidence terms explain significant variation in Rt, and between states it is the susceptibility index that separates those that surged in 2024 from those that did not (Figs. 4 and 5). The results are compatible because they answer different questions. Childs et al. estimate an effect averaged across many endemic locations and years, not a single year or spatial anomaly, and attribute the immunity-invariance to endemic populations never reaching persistent herd immunity, through waning and serotype turnover. The 2024 outbreak in Brazil points to the opposite case: large populations with lower historical transmission undergoing a range expansion. This is a scenario their analysis explicitly sets aside. Consistent with this, our analyses suggest that accounting for immunity greatly improves predictions of the spatial patterns of the 2024 outbreak Defining R0, and especially the conditions that define the critical transmission threshold R0 = 1, remains critical for predicting dengue incidence. However, mechanistic expressions for the temperature dependence of R0 usually assume stationarity 28 , and rely on variables that are difficult to estimate in the field and are expected to be spatially heterogeneous, such as the vector-to-host ratio (on which R0 depends; see SI Appendix, Appendix S2). Studies deriving and applying temperature-dependent R0 models have therefore focused mainly on the shape of its temperature dependence, the transmissive range (R0 > 0), or the temperature that maximizes R0, rather than on absolute R0 values 3, 6 . This limitation bears directly on why the Southeast surged in 2024 and not earlier: without an absolute estimate of R0, we cannot identify whether or when its transmission potential first crossed the R0 = 1 threshold for sustained transmission, and so cannot fully account for the timing of the outbreak. It is also possible that preceding years saw widespread but largely unreported transmission (primary dengue infections are frequently mild or asymptomatic) with part of the 2024 reported surge reflecting secondary, more often symptomatic infections in populations primed in the year(s) before.

Our findings are subject to several limitations. Surveillance biases likely distorted incidence data: the misclassification of Oropouche virus (OROV) or Chikungunya cases as dengue in the South (e.g., Santa Catarina) may have inflated reported incidence, while systemic underreporting of DENV-3 in the Northeast may have masked earlier resurgences, thereby skewing our susceptibility estimates for these regions 9, 15 . Interaction with health care resources or attention may result in time-and incidence-varying reporting rates of dengue (e.g., large outbreaks may increase reporting due to broader recognition of dengue risk or decrease reporting due to saturation of reporting resources). Our estimates of reproductive numbers assume a fixed generation interval, which instead would be expected to vary with temperature 29, 30 . Our modeling framework makes several simplifying assumptions. The single-serotype SEIR-SEI model does not account for serotypespecific immunity; our 'initial susceptibility index' is therefore an aggregate measure that may mask complex inter-serotype immune dynamics. Because each state-season is fit independently, with no constraint linking successive seasons, a serotype introduction that reset susceptibility would be absorbed as a jump in 𝑆 !" #$% !"! & ; the fit is agnostic as to whether a given shift reflects a serotype introduction, demographic turnover, or waning cross-protection.

We have not addressed several alternative mechanisms that could have contributed to the pattern of dengue observed in 2024 in Brazil. Introduction of new serotypes into some areas may have contributed to greater levels of incidence. State-level serotype composition for 2024 and regional serotype composition for 2007-2024 among typed surveillance records are shown in SI Appendix, Figs. S14 and S15, respectively. These observations caution against assuming uniform serotype circulation across Brazil, but do not support large spatial differences across Brazil in serotypes circulating. The contribution of pre-existing immunity to higher rates of apparent illness was not explicitly addressed here, though could also contribute to variation. Finally, differences in reporting, though estimated in our models and not found to contribute to systematic differences in cases in southern states compared to northern states, could contribute to differences in incidence.

All analyses were conducted at the state level. We chose this scale because over a third of Brazil's municipalities have a median of zero cases per season (five or fewer, for almost two-thirds), and 39% of all municipality-seasons record zero cases, a degree of sparsity that destabilizes both reproductive-number estimation and susceptibility inference. Confounding requires that an omitted factor be correlated with temperature: sub-state heterogeneity in incidence that is uncorrelated with temperature cannot bias the state-level association, and averaging over many such independent local departures suppresses their contribution to the state-level estimate. The risk aggregation cannot address is heterogeneity that is correlated with temperature at the sub-state level, e.g., municipality-level gradients in reporting rate or vector-control investment that correlate with local climate, which could bias the state-level associations in either direction. The Rt GAMs include state and season fixed effects, which absorb time-invariant between-state differences and nationwide inter-seasonal variation, but not state-specific time-varying confounds or sub-state heterogeneity.

The correlation analysis (Fig. 2) and the regression models (Fig. 5) make no such adjustment, because the between-state contrast is itself the quantity of interest. We therefore cannot rule out residual confounding at the state level. Moving to a municipality scale would not in itself resolve this: separating such confounding from the associations of interest would still require either measured covariates for the suspected confounds (health-system capacity, vector-control measures, reporting rates), or a design that removes variation at the confound's own scale (e.g., within-municipality, over time), which reintroduces the data sparsity that motivated the state-level choice in the first place. In inferring the initial susceptibility index, we also assumed a spatiotemporally uniform vector-to-host ratio, reporting rate, and number of infections introduced to each location at the start of a season, which are significant oversimplifications. Spatial differences in vector control act through the vector-to-host ratio: historically low-burden states such as São Paulo may never have built the dengue-specific control capacity of more affected states, and any such difference would be absorbed into the susceptibility index, producing a spatial pattern much like the one we attribute to immunity. We therefore treat the susceptibility index as a relative rather than absolute measure of population susceptibility, a use supported by the stability of indices within and across states when estimated from total versus confirmed cases, despite their substantially different reporting rates (SI Appendix, Fig. S7). The assumptions made in these compartmental models also lead to unrealistically fast dynamics, potentially inconsistent with spatial heterogeneity within states, making the estimation of susceptibility more difficult. Finally, beyond the inherent assumptions of our SEIR-SEI model, we highlight three potential temperature-related biases. First, the temperature dependence of vector life-history traits, derived from laboratory experiments under constant conditions, may bias rate estimates in fluctuating real-world temperatures, with the expected bias dependent on the response functions' curvature at a given temperature 31 . Second, the gridded temperature dataset we use here is interpolated and averaged over relatively large spatiotemporal scales, which may not reflect the microclimatic temperatures actually experienced by mosquitoes, nor the precise temperatures in the laboratory experiments used to parameterize the model. Third, as demonstrated by Childs et al. 7 , temperature datasets can also exhibit significant discrepancies for a given location, potentially leading to substantial variations in estimated vital rates solely based on the dataset used.

We used total reported dengue cases in our analyses, which may introduce biases due to misclassification of other febrile illnesses. However, when we repeated analyses using only confirmed cases, results remained qualitatively unchanged (see SI Appendix, Appendix S3).

Confirmed cases have their own limitations: there can be less testing during large epidemics due to resource constraints, potentially leading to greater underreporting in high-incidence years and states. Thus, while neither measure is perfect, the consistency of results across both definitions strengthens confidence that the spatial and temporal patterns we identify, particularly with regards to the roles of temperature and immunity, likely reflect true epidemiological dynamics rather than surveillance biases.

As noted above, we do not distinguish between primary and secondary infections, limiting our ability to project past 2024. The 2024 outbreak in southeastern states could constrain transmission through accumulated immunity. However, populations with monotypic immunity remain vulnerable to secondary infections, which could lead to increased cases if other serotypes were to circulate in the near future. At the same time, continued range expansion into southern states (Paraná, Santa Catarina, and Rio Grande do Sul), and potentially other large, immunologically naive metropolitan areas like Buenos Aires, could drive substantial future outbreaks.

Our results suggest avenues for further research to improve our understanding of the spatiotemporal evolution of dengue epidemics in emerging and re-emerging settings. First, reconstruction of population susceptibility even at crude scales (e.g., statewide and annual) will provide information about areas at risk of increased transmission and with higher sensitivity to climatic changes. This reconstruction could be achieved using case data where available 32 , bolstered by population representative serological surveys 27, 33 , although methodological development is necessary to integrate data sources and project these estimates countrywide.

Second, our models using reconstructed susceptibility indices outperformed models using mean temperature, suggesting that current models using temperature-dependent reproductive numbers to predict changes in dengue transmission would also benefit from integration of susceptibility estimates informed by transmission models. One step towards this could be the use of effective reproductive numbers in assessing climate associations (rather than incidence) as this accounts, at least in part, for changes in susceptibility.

Consideration of population immunity will likely also be critical in improving the accuracy and increasing the time horizon of models forecasting dengue burden, as noted by Wu et al. 34 . The majority of published forecasting models use only climate data as predictors 35 ; evaluation of models on Brazil's 2024 epidemic highlights the limitations of such models, as observed by Araujo et al. 36.

Whether a measure of immunity is needed for prediction in other settings will depend on regime: it is likely to matter most during susceptibility regime shifts such as range expansions into newly suitable areas, or in the aftermath of a serotype introduction or unusually large outbreaks, and least where susceptibility is more stable, as in long-established endemic areas. Our results also note the weakness of using only historical case numbers to project future burden. Integration of historical climate and estimates of immunity (including serotype specific estimates) into forecast models would represent significant progress in this field and increase the value of these models to public health.

Materials and Methods

Study design

We conducted a retrospective ecological time-series analysis to investigate the drivers of the 2024 dengue epidemic in Brazil. Our analysis compared state-level incidence, temperature, and their 2024 season anomalies against a 23-year historical baseline (2001-2023).

Descriptive and statistical analyses

To quantify the relationship between temperature and incidence, we calculated Pearson's correlation coefficients (r) and associated 95% confidence intervals, across states (one value per state), between (i) the 2024 season temperature anomalies and the 2024 incidence anomalies, and To summarize how the season-by-season correlation series changed over time, we regressed the coefficients on season by generalized least squares on the Fisher-z scale (atanh r), with inversevariance weights and a first-order autoregressive term across seasons, to account for the coefficients' unequal uncertainty and serially correlated residuals. Modeling framework

Variation in reproductive numbers with temperature and immunity

To understand how spatiotemporal patterns in dengue cases in Brazil are related to temperature and population susceptibility, we first analyzed an outcome of estimated effective reproductive numbers (Rt) derived from reported case data. Rt are more robust to differences in reporting than raw case numbers, and reflect a combination of transmissibility and population immunity (Rt=R0*St, where St is the proportion of the population susceptible). Reproductive numbers were calculated using the EpiEstim v2.2.5 41 package in R from weekly state-level time series of cases over a twoweek moving window, assuming a mean serial interval of 12 days and a standard deviation of 3 days. Estimates based on fewer than five cases within a window were deemed unreliable and excluded from subsequent analysis, and Rt values equal to or greater than 10 were also deemed unreliable and excluded (as they were often estimated for the start of the time period, or in states that reported few cases). The log-transformed Rt values were the dependent variable in a generalized additive model (GAM) from the mgcv v1.9.4 package 42 in R. The GAM included a smooth term for mean temperature for the same two-week moving window, and smooth terms for the lag-1 log-cumulative incidence per 100,000 (both within-season and overall, state-level) as a proxy for the build-up of immunity prior to the time window over which Rt was estimated, and thus accounting for changing susceptibility driven by annual outbreak dynamics and historical transmission. We used a contemporaneous temperature window rather than distributed lags deliberately: the aim is to isolate temperature's direct effect on transmission from its lagged, indirect effect through accumulated immunity, which the cumulative-incidence terms (themselves a historical function of temperature) already capture. Adding lagged temperature would induce collinearity between it and the lagged incidences, blurring this decomposition. More fundamentally, a temperature lag cannot represent the path-dependent depletion of susceptibles -whether an outbreak actually occurred -that the cumulative incidence terms carry. We note that "direct" is approximate here, since temperatures ultimately act on mosquito life history at multiple lags.

Season and state were included as categorical fixed effects. To assess the relationship between temperature and Rt when not accounting for historic incidence, the same model was fit excluding the cumulative incidence smooth terms. To minimize the risk of overfitting, the GAM was fitted with double penalization and the smoothing parameter estimation was made using restricted maximum likelihood. To further reduce the risk of overfitting, we performed 10-fold cross-validation to select the optimal basis dimension (k) from a range of 3 to 30 (the same value used across all smooth terms). To account for temporal autocorrelation in the residuals, models were fitted using the 'bam' function with an AR(1) error structure. The autocorrelation parameter ρ was estimated from the mean lag-1 autocorrelation of residuals from an initial model fit, calculated separately for each state-year series. Performance was measured by the RMSE and Pearson correlation coefficient between predicted and observed values on the out-of-sample data.

Simulations to study relationships between temperature and incidence over the transition from emergence to endemicity

To explore the conditions under which correlations between temperature and incidence (estimated in the Descriptive/statistical analyses section above) might change over time, we used the same SEIR-SEI model described above, here including human birth and death rates. The observed temperature inputs were replaced with sine curves parameterized for each state to remove dataspecific confounding effects and noise, and to focus on the effects of specific changes in temperature on the resulting correlations. The scenarios are not calibrated to Brazil's observed warming; they are idealized to isolate individual mechanisms. The simulations were run for a period of 50 years. Four distinct temperature scenarios were modeled (see SI Appendix, Fig. S5): • "Constant temperature" scenario: the mean temperature for each state was held at its observed value, while the amplitude of the sine curve was set to roughly match the observed seasonal temperature variation (see SI Appendix, Fig. S16).

• "Linearly-increasing temperature" scenario: the mean temperature was set to decrease linearly from year 50 back to year 0 at a rate of 0.73 degrees per decade.

• "Step-increase in temperature" scenario: the mean temperature was held constant (as in the "constant" scenario) until year 40, at which point the mean was increased by one degree for the remainder of the simulation.

• "Constant high temperature" scenario aimed to remove spatial variation in temperature (for instance, simulating conditions in a location where the disease is endemic). The mean and amplitude of temperature for each state was sampled from narrow normal distributions (for the mean, μ = 27℃, σ = 0.5℃; for the amplitude μ = 3℃, σ = 0.1℃), approximately corresponding to the mean and amplitude of the temperatures observed in the warmest states (SI Appendix, Fig. S16).
