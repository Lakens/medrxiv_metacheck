Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). Statcheck might still miss one-sided tests that are described in another sentence, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Surveys can reach large samples but capture only pre-defined answers, with identical responses often concealing very different reasoning. We tested whether AI-moderated interviews capture this reasoning at scale. In a pre-registered, randomized, counterbalanced design, 1,953 US adults from a probability-based panel and an opt-in panel completed three AI-moderated interviews as well as a section with closed-ended and open-text survey items. Topics were extracted from transcripts inductively, without a predefined codebook, and validated by a confirmatory follow-up. Participants endorsed 94% of topics as fair descriptions of what they said. 21-51% of topics from the AI interview had no counterpart in the closed-ended battery, and 17-37 percentage points net coverage over open text answers. Among respondents who gave identical closed-ended responses, their reasoning diverged widely in valence and content. AI-moderated interviews surface reasons that closed-ended and open-text items miss, and show how reasoning differs among people who answer alike.

[div-01]

Population research on health attitudes and behaviors uses two main types of instrument. Standardized surveys built on predetermined constructs scale to large samples at low marginal cost, and produce responses that are comparable across people and over time. However, they only capture fixed, pre-defined response options, and identical answers can conceal very different underlying reasoning 1, 2 . Open-text items relax that constraint by letting respondents answer in their own words, but responses are typically limited in length and depth. Qualitative interviews, on the other hand, give respondents the ability to speak freely, and interviewers can probe, clarify, and adapt to respondents' answers. Yet they are time-consuming, costly, and do not scale well, often limiting the generalizability of findings.

Large language models (LLMs) have opened a third option. Their rapid improvement on tasks involving language and reasoning suggests they could conduct fluent, adaptive interviews at scale while analyzing the resulting transcripts automatically [3] [4] [5] . One early application has been to improve the measurement properties of standardized surveys, for example by clarifying question wording or prompting respondents to expand on a rating 6 . A more ambitious direction uses LLMs to conduct qualitative-style interviews in their own right 5, 7 , and a growing number of commercial platforms now offer such interviews. Early evidence for this approach is encouraging, suggesting that LLM-led interviews can follow good interviewing practice, are received well by respondents, and yield data that captures considerations a fixed survey might miss [5] [6] [7] [8] . Yet, rigorous evidence that such LLM-led interviews capture more than standard surveys at scale remains limited.

Existing work leaves four questions of evidence unanswered. First, to our knowledge, no previous study provides a randomized, counterbalanced comparison of AI-moderated interviews against multi-item comprehensive survey batteries, designed to match each subject of the interview prompts. Second, the analysis of AI-moderated interview transcripts has generally relied on researcher-defined codebooks that restrict analysis and cannot, by design, quantify what falls outside them 4, 9, 10 , reintroducing the very constraints that motivated the interview over a standard survey. Recent work comparing AI systems has begun to surface and quantify topics that emerge beyond a predefined guide, but only in small samples and without comparing to a matched survey 11 . Third, this work has been conducted almost entirely in opt-in panels, with a notable exception 6 , even though it is well established that findings can differ by sampling frame 12, 13 . This is a problem because current evaluations of these approaches do not account for sample-noise. Finally, little is known about how the configuration of the interviewer, such as the prompting strategy that governs follow-up questions, affects the number and range of topics surfaced. Early work suggests that different probing strategies are better suited to different contexts, for example idiographic prompts (asking for personal examples) perform better, but this has only been tested with small samples and with probes attached to individual survey questions, rather than in interviews at scale 14.

This study addresses these gaps in a preregistered, randomized design using a mixed-methods survey platform that allows responses elicited from AI-moderated, multi-turn interviews to be compared directly with standard survey questions. We chose healthcare trust as the test case because trust is contextual, conditional, and shaped by personal experience and political beliefs 1 , This kind of nuanced evaluation is difficult to capture with closed-ended questions. The topic is also timely: trust in US physicians and hospitals fell from around 71% in 2020 to 40% in 2024 15 yet people still trust individual clinicians more than the system as a whole 16 , and fifty years of trust research has relied heavily on cross-sectional surveys.

Participants provided responses about two healthcare trust domains. Domain 1 examined trust in decisions, covering past experience, and weighing professional advice against social media. Domain 2 examined how trust forms and changes over time, covering how and why trust had shifted in recent years, and what is needed to rebuild it. Each participant completed one domain as an adaptive AI-moderated interview and the other as a standard question battery of closed-ended and open-text items, counterbalanced by domain and order (see Study Design). The battery was built to best-practice standards from published instruments. Interviews were coded using an LLM-based pipeline that extracted labels inductively, repeated ratings, reconciled discrepancies, and clustered the results into a unified topic set 4, 17, 18 . We fielded the same instrument in two independent samples of US adults, one from a probability-based panel (AmeriSpeak by NORC) and one from an opt-in panel (Prolific), for a combined N = 1,953, allowing us to assess whether AI-derived insights hold when compared to a different sampling method from the same population This design lets us ask five questions. First, do interviews capture more topics than closed-ended and open-text items? Second, do identical closed-ended ratings conceal different underlying reasoning? Third, as an exploratory question, does the interviewer's probing strategy (the rules that govern when and how it asks follow-ups) relate to what interviews reveal? Fourth, how do participants experience an AI-moderated interview compared with a standard survey? And fifth, do the topics surfaced hold across probability-based and opt-in sampling frames?

Results

Interviews cover topics that matched survey items do not

We needed to derive structure from our interviews, so we applied an inductive topic modeling pipeline to the AI interview transcripts (see Methods) to extract topics, i.e. the reasons underlying participants' beliefs/behaviors, from transcripts. Each participant's interview was given a set of labels, each linked to the relevant passage of the interview. We then validated these topics with a sub-sample of 403 participants, who marked 94% of topics as being an acceptable description of what they said or meant Having established that the topic labels extracted from AI-moderated interviews were faithful representations of interview content, we compared our AI interviewer topics to both a typical multiple/single choice battery, and similarly worded open-text box questions. These survey questions were written against the same interview specifications the AI received and were grounded in 17 published instruments and reviewed by two survey methodologists (Methods).

We quantified coverage in two ways. First, against the closed-ended battery, where 21-51% of reliably estimated interview topics (median 19 per subdomain, range 12-28) had no matching item (Figure 1a; matching matrix is in the supplement). Secondly, we applied the identical topic modeling pipeline to open-text boxes which asked the same question as the opening question asked by the AI interviewer. The resulting labels were matched to the interview labels by embedding similarity (see Methods).

Against these matched free-text answers, interview topics had a net advantage of 17-37 percentage points (Figure 1b): 60-75% of interview topics had no counterpart in free text, against 32-53% in the reverse direction (ie. free text topics with no equivalent in the AI interview topics), a reference for how much two groups of people say differently by chance. Both formats surface some unique topics, but the interview consistently surfaces more, and the advantage holds at every matching threshold tested (Methods, Extended Data Fig. 1).

AI-moderated interviews capture heterogeneity in the underlying reasoning for identical survey responses

We wanted to explore whether AI-moderated interviews could expose different reasoning processes obscured by identical answers in closed-ended batteries. We looked at a single battery which all participants completed after the main study (described in Methods). This focused on trusting different sources of information for health decisions, with each source rated on a likert scale. In this battery, among participants who gave the same closed-ended trust rating, the emotional valence of their interview reasoning ranged widely (interquartile ranges 0.43-1.34 on a -1 to +1 scale; Figure 2).

As an example, within the group of participants who all said they trusted public health authorities "a fair amount" , the AI interviewer elicited distinct topics and negative opinions about certain bodies. These included concerns about the CDC: "The CDC has become skewed politically, and needs to return to backing what research is actually saying" (topic: Political influence on health agencies, valence=-0.67, topic prevalence 18%), and people distrusting motives due to COVID "We had bad experience with COVID vaccine so it would be difficult to earn my trust" (topic: Distrust of institutional motives, valence=-0.67, topic prevalence 30.4%). The interviews also revealed potential intervention pathways to increase trust: "I look for facts such as clinical studies that give quantitative results" (topic: Scientific evidence validation, valence +0.67, prevalence 37.5%). People who gave the same rating held different, sometimes opposing, reasons, which a closed-ended item cannot show.

Interview probing strategies associated with topics revealed

The AI interviewer had discretion to deploy different probing strategies. We explore two examples: counterfactual (asking what the participant would do under different conditions) and reframing (asking them to reconsider through a different lens). The interviewer chose when to probe, so these are descriptive associations rather than causal estimates.

Counterfactual probes (16-17% of interviewer turns) were associated with about 0.1 more topics from Turn 2 onward in both panels, a small effect (Cliff's δ ≤ 0.08; Figure 3b, Extended Data Table 1).

Reframing probes (38-42% of turns) were associated with up to half a topic more by Turn 5 in the probability panel and a smaller, later difference in the opt-in panel (Figure 3c, Extended Data Table 1). Interviews that went on to be reframed already held more topics at Turn 1, so the gap partly reflects which conversations the interviewer chose to reframe.

Topic findings replicate across panels

We used the same similarity-based matching approach from our earlier open-text vs AI interviewer comparison to quantify the overlap between the topics identified across the two panels. At the same similarity threshold (t=0.7), 59% (58 out of 98) of the Probability panel's distinct labels and 51% (65 out of 128) of the non-probability panel's distinct labels had a similar counterpart in the other panel; weighted by how many participants raised each label, coverage reaches 71% and 61% of participant-mentions respectively. This overlap is visualized in Figure 5b. We also find that the non-probability panel generated more topics. This is likely because they seemed to engage more with the questions (323 vs 162 median words per interview).

Accordingly, the non-probability panel covered the probability panel's topics more completely than the reverse (Figure 5).

The sampling noise level associated with survey research across different panels can lead to differences of 8-10pp 19 in response patterns. We can also expect an online non-probability panel to replicate around 72.5% 20 of results in an identical study on a representative sample. Taking this into account, our findings here show a reasonable level of overlap (mention-weighted 61-71%)

To further understand how different the panels may be in their perceptions, we also compared the panels on their trust in health information sources (Figure 6). We generally find strong agreement between both panels, with one notable exception: the probability panel is much less trusting of AI (25 percentage point difference). As such, studies exclusively working with non-probability panels may overestimate respondent's willingness to engage with AI, whether for healthcare or in AI interviews themselves.

Discussion

We show that AI-moderated interviews can surface reasoning that matched survey items miss. We were able to capture individual reasoning patterns that surveys often fail to capture while retaining the scale and reach. Specifically, we show that across subdomains, between 21% and 51% of interview topics from the interviews had no equivalent in the matched closed-ended survey, and that we could identify these inductively, without a predefined codebook.

The coverage findings were consistent in both a probability-based panel and an opt-in panel of US adults, to our knowledge the first test of an AI-moderated interviewing method in independent probability-based and opt-in samples. This matters because sample source is known to affect questionnaire and survey outcomes, and because most work in this area has been conducted on a single opt-in panel [5] [6] [7] [8] . The main divergence between panels was greater distrust of AI and greater dropout during the AI interview section in the probability panel, which is worth noting for researchers working in this area, since it suggests panel choice may shape findings on AI-related questions more than on other domains.

Our findings on trust largely replicate previous findings from the literature, such as participants trusting their own clinician more than the health system as a whole, and reporting a general erosion of trust since the pandemic. But the AI-moderated interviews were able to surface some of the specific individual experiences driving those patterns. For example, participants who gave the same closed-ended trust rating (trusting public health authorities "a fair amount") described very different experiences behind this, and their reasoning spanned barriers and facilitators of trust. A survey would place these people in the same group, yet they hold different underlying attitudes and it is possible these people would respond to attempted interventions in a different way.

Interestingly, we also found that the probing strategy adopted by the AI interviewer was associated with how much information surfaced. Specifically, turns using a counterfactual or reframing probing strategy were associated with more topics being surfaced, though this was observational rather than experimental in the sense that the AI interviewer chose when to deploy each technique. This suggests that interviewer design plausibly affects what an interview can uncover, consistent with theoretical accounts of why probing deepens qualitative data 21 , though establishing a causal effect requires further controlled studies. It also suggests AI interviewing tools should not be treated as interchangeable: how an AI interviewer is configured may have substantial impact on the quality of the results.

These findings could have wide application across several settings. Qualitative research is expensive, slow, and limited by the availability of trained interviewers. An AI interviewer running over the web or by phone could collect open, probed accounts at survey scale on questions like vaccine hesitancy, care-seeking, or maternal health, and could work across languages without recruiting and training local interviewer teams. For health systems trying to improve patient experience, this method could recover the nuance behind closed-ended ratings for an entire patient population, rather than just from small numbers recruited into focus groups. Similarly, in pharmaceutical and market research, this method could help to uncover the reasoning behind important behaviors such as treatment adherence and trial participation, which are usually only studied through small qualitative samples, and which might not generalize to larger populations. In public health and policy, attitudes can change faster than validated instruments can be built. For example, attitudes towards vaccination shifted rapidly during the COVID-19 pandemic, and a similar pattern can be seen today with regards to the use of AI in healthcare. Since our topic extraction method does not rely on a predefined codebook, it allows novel topics to be studied and reported without having to fully anticipate those topics in advance.

There are several important limitations of our study. First, what we compare against. This study did not compare AI interviews against human-led interviews, and further work is needed to test how closely these insights match human-level depth. Our comparison is based on an instrument that was designed for this study. We designed the quantitative sections using a careful process of literature review, evaluation of validated scales, and thorough testing. The domains did not allow exclusive use of psychometrically validated scales, so coverage figures come from our instrument rather than fixed and purely validated properties. A longer battery might have captured more of what the AI interviews surfaced.The closed-ended comparison also covers four of six subdomains and the open-text comparison three (due to design constraints detailed in the Methods). Finally, modality was compared between subjects to avoid contamination, so the comparison rests on randomization rather than paired within-person data. This choice is supported by recent experimental evidence that answering AI interview probes changes subsequent closed-ended responses 22.

Secondly, there are limitations related to how topics were generated, validated, and used. The interview model, our prompting scaffold, and our analytics pipeline may have limited the topics that surfaced. Because our member checking sub-study showed participants only their own topics, we cannot separate accurate labeling from acquiescence. Previous labeling confirmation has shown this only leads to a slight false positive rate 6 , but we flag this as a limitation. Foil topics from other participants would separate the two. Finally, the association between probing style and topic coverage is observational because the interviewer chose when to probe, so we cannot separate greater engagement leading to more labels against the method leading to more labels.

Thirdly, there are limitations regarding participants and generalizability. Even though people rated the AI interviews as acceptable, we did see a cost, higher early drop-out, slightly lower experience ratings, and a preference for human interviewers in the probability panel. This pattern matches prior work 6 , which also found higher drop-out and slightly lower ratings for AI-moderated follow-ups. The impact is small and order-dependent: ratings fell only when the interview came second, so part of it reflects fatigue late in the survey rather than the format itself. We interpret this as a trade-off, a small loss in experience against a substantial gain in topic coverage, and one that improvements to the interviewing interface are likely to narrow. Lastly, the study covers one subject area, trust in healthcare, in one country. while this is a broad domain, touching health, trust and politics, other subjects may vary in findings on insights and overlap.

Future work could address some of these limitations and further strengthen support for AI interviewers. For instance, Experimentally manipulating probing strategies, validating topics against foil labels and human coders, and testing other subject areas and countries, would improve evidence. And because interviewer and analysis models are improving quickly, we expect the capabilities to increase and evaluations like this one should be repeated as the tools change rather than treated as fixed.

Our findings show that AI-moderated interviews can reveal a substantial amount of the reasoning that standard surveys leave uncaptured, at survey scale. Across public health, health services, and clinical research, the reasoning behind a response has rarely been measured in large samples, despite being crucial to identifying an intervention target, or a change to a protocol or policy. Recovering such reasoning in large samples could substantially extend what survey research is able to deliver in these settings

Methods

AI-moderated Interviewer engine

The AI interview was executed based on a configuration set up by the authors. The configuration fully specified the sequence of quantitative and AI-driven questions for a given arm, and four such configurations were created to serve the four arms (Extended Data Fig. 2). It also specified an overall set of instructions for the role the AI was to play, akin to a "system prompt" in other AI products. Finally, it specified which content safeguards were active to stop the interview for participant safety, specifically we used Azure AI Content Safety, which flagged conversations covering topics like self-harm & violence. As people were asked to speak about sensitive topics we applied a higher threshold of 6 on Azure's eight-level severity scale (0-7). We set this after manual testing, to reduce false-positive interview terminations found at lower thresholds. The model used in this configuration was OpenAI's gpt-5.4-mini-001.

For the AI-moderated interview sections -of which each participant completed three (Extended Data Fig. 2)we specified the objective of the interview, the number of turns available to the AI, the large language model endpoint to use, the fixed opening question, what context from previous questions should be considered, and few-shot examples of good and bad follow-up questions identified during testing. When a respondent answered the opening question, the LLM was prompted with all the aforementioned information, and a followup question was formulated and transmitted to the web interface.

Study design

Sample and recruitment

The instrument was fielded to two independent samples of US adults aged 18 and over, drawn from panels with deliberately different recruitment logics so that the findings could be tested against a sampling frame.

The probability sample was collected using a nationally representative sample drawn from the AmeriSpeak Panel, a probability-based panel operated by NORC at the University of Chicago. This panel is recruited using a rigorous multi-mode strategy (mail, telephone, and face-to-face non-response follow-up), to ensure comprehensive U.S. household coverage. Panel recruitment is by invitation to a sampled address only, with no self-nomination into the panel, so selection probabilities are known and the resulting sample supports design-based inference to the US adult population. Our sample was drawn from this panel using sampling strata based on age, race and ethnicity, education, and gender.

The non-probability sample was recruited via Prolific, an online participant pool in which adults enroll themselves and opt into individual studies. To ensure our findings were generalizable to the broader United States population, we utilized Prolific's "Representative Sample" allocation feature. This methodology employs a cross-stratified quota sampling approach designed to mirror national demographic distributions based on U.S. Census Bureau data. Specifically, the sampling frame was stratified across a narrower set of factors: sex, age bracket, and ethnicity (using simplified census categories). Prolific is stratified from a self-selected pool rather than a probability sample. Throughout the paper the two samples are labeled Probability and Non-probability, are fielded with the identical instrument, and followed the same randomization rules.

The analyzed sample sizes were 930, and 1023 for the probability and non-probability panels respectively. The studies overrecruited to account for dropouts within the survey, 304 (probability) and 246 (non probability) were not included in the final data set for this reason. We also checked for more than 5 copy-past events (as a proxy for copying from an AI-chatbot), 4 (probability) and 39 (non-probability) were not included for this reason. Demographic attributes of both samples are given in Extended Data Table 2.

Weighting

The AmeriSpeak panel provided participant weights, which are raked 23 to US adult benchmarks on age, sex, education, race and ethnicity, housing tenure, household telephone status, Census division, and the interactions age × sex, age × race and ethnicity.

Prolific supplied no weights, so we raked the sample by iterative proportional fitting 24 to the weighted margins of the probability panel on the same dimensions, omitting household telephone status, which Prolific does not collect, and trimmed the resulting weights to the range 0.2-6.0. Within each panel, weights were rescaled to a mean of 1 over analyzed respondents.

Instrument Design

Instrument development involved three stages, which were domain selection, interview specification, and item writing. Domains were selected on the basis of a narrative review of research and media coverage on trust in healthcare in the United States published between 2019 and 2026, drawing on approximately 30 academic and policy sources alongside national news reporting. The review asked where trust in healthcare currently stands, how it varies across people and contexts, what drives it up or down, and how academic and media accounts of these trends diverge. It identified four areas the literature has not resolved. These are how trust is repaired after it erodes, why people switch health information sources and how they justify switching, how risk calculus interacts with trust when people follow health guidance they doubt or reject guidance they trust, and how trust operates where human and AI sources are combined. Two domains were selected out of the four: trust and risk trade-offs, and personal history and trust formation. Both were selected because there are many potential reasons how people arrive at their current beliefs and understanding the reasoning in more depth is valuable. The initial literature and media scan was generated using ChatGPT Deep Research in January 2026. All findings were subsequently reviewed and cross-checked against a selection of systematic reviews and media sources by the authors, who produced the final synthesis and domain recommendations.

The interviews were specified next, setting out what each interview should cover and probe, and the survey items were derived from those specifications. Each interview aim was translated into one or more closed-ended or open-text items targeting the same content. Where an aim covered several distinct ideas, a battery was written rather than a single item, so the survey had room to cover necessary ground albeit within the constraints of closed question types.

To ground the items in existing measurement, 17 published instruments, national survey programs, and theoretical frameworks were reviewed. These were the Health Care System Distrust Scale (HCSD) and its revision, the Medical Mistrust Index (MMI), the Medical Mistrust Scale for Medical and Healthcare Students and Providers (MMtS-MHC-SP), the Trust in Physician Scale (TPS), the Wake Forest Physician Trust Scale short form (WFPTS), Trust in the Medical Profession (TMP), the Trust in Multidimensional Healthcare Systems Scale (TIMHSS), Trust in Public Health Authorities (TiPHA), the Decisional Conflict Scale (DCS), shared decision-making scales (e.g., SDM-Q), the Autonomy Preference Index (API), the Multidimensional Health Locus of Control Scale (MHLC), the Theory of Planned Behavior, the Ipsos PX Pulse, the Health Information National Trends Survey (HINTS), the KFF Health Tracking Poll, and the NORC/ABIM Foundation trust survey. Where possible, items were adapted from these scales, or written against constructs those scales define. To maximize equivalence between the two arms, however, most items were constructed new, since established measures did not cover the domains or framing that a fair comparison required.

An example of an adapted item is "Important medical decisions should be made by the doctor, not the patient", adapted from the API (original: "The important medical decisions should be made by your doctor, not you"), and an example of an item based on a validated construct is "I distrust the healthcare system overall, but still trust a doctor", written to capture the distinction between system-level and providerlevel trust that the HCSD and the TPS treat separately. Batteries were largely constructed from mixed sources. For example, in the battery asking what drove uncertainty in a past health decision, the four statements correspond respectively to competence, drawing on TiPHA and TIMHSS; benevolence, drawing on the HCSD; risk and uncertainty, drawing on the DCS; and normative pressure, drawing on shared decision-making scales and the Theory of Planned Behavior.

Response formats followed standard conventions. Closed-ended items used four-point Likert or single-choice formats. Rating batteries were paired with a forced-choice follow-up asking which factor mattered most, thus distinguishing endorsement from priority. Critically, we added open-text items where relevant, as these are a standard part of traditional surveys, and allowed analysis of what a multi-turn interview adds over a single open-text response.

The instrument was reviewed internally by two coauthors with extensive experience in quantitative survey design, who assessed wording, response format, and coverage against the interview specifications. Three quality assurance pilots were run in March 2026, with 10, 25, and 50 participants, prior to full fielding.These were used to review the quality of the interviews and to inspect survey responses descriptively. No formal analysis was conducted on the pilot data.

Analytics Topic labeling extraction

Interview transcripts were converted into per-participant topic labels using a four-stage LLM pipeline, combining concepts from the LLM-evaluation literature on grading rubrics 25, 26 , and modern conversation topic harmonization tools like Athropic's CLIO 17 1. independent multi-rater extraction 2. consensus adjudication 3. embedding-based harmonization of labels into a per-question vocabulary of topic families 4. generation of a short description for each family.

These steps are detailed below.

Stage 1: Multi-rater label extraction

Each transcript was coded independently by three LLM raters (Claude Sonnet 4.6) using the same structured content-analysis rubric (Supplementary Note 1). Single rubric 'autograders' have been shown to be the most reliable approach for this type of extraction 26 . The rubric defined the target construct as a topic, i.e. an underlying reason that explains why the participant holds a belief or makes a decision, and required topics to be grounded inductively in the participant's own words rather than in a predefined codebook. For each topic an LLM rater returned: a lowercase label of at most five words; a one-sentence belief statement; an effect on the target behavior (barrier, facilitator, mixed, unclear); an importance score from 0 to 1, representing the importance of that topic to the person based on the transcript; a valence score on a seven-point scale from -1 to +1 capturing the participant's feelings toward the topic; the User turn indices where it appeared; and one or two verbatim supporting quotes. Rank was derived from importance rather than assigned independently.

Stage 2: Consensus adjudication

A fourth LLM call (Claude Sonnet 4.6) acted as consensus grader 25 over the three rater outputs for each transcript (Supplementary Note 2). A topic was retained only if at least two of the three raters identified the same underlying reason, allowing close synonyms. Synonymous labels were merged to one canonical label, importance and valence were averaged across agreeing raters. The averaged valence was then rounded to the nearest point of the seven-point rubric scale (-1 to +1), and averaged importance was retained as a continuous 0-1 score. Quotes and turn indices were unioned and deduplicated, and rank was recomputed from importance. The adjudicated output for each transcript formed the raw topic set carried into harmonization.

Stage 3: Label harmonization across participants

Raw labels vary in wording across participants, so labels were harmonized into a compact vocabulary of topic families. This was adapted from the method used in Anthropic's CLIO tool 17 . Harmonization was run strictly per question, so a label string occurring under two questions was harmonized independently in each.

Unique raw labels were embedded with Cohere embed-v3-multilingual. Embeddings were L2-normalized, reduced with UMAP, and clustered with HDBSCAN (settings in Table M1). HDBSCAN noise points were reassigned to their best cluster only when their soft membership probability met a minimum threshold; otherwise they were carried forward as their own candidate so that rare but distinct topics were not absorbed into unrelated clusters.

Clusters were then canonicalized by an LLM judge (Claude Opus 4.7) in three passes (Supplementary Note 3). In Pass 1, labels within each cluster were presented in chunks alongside their frequency and up to two example belief statements and one quote, and the judge mapped every label to a canonical label, merging only labels expressing the same underlying reason. In Pass 2, the per-chunk candidates within a cluster were consolidated to a small set. In Pass 3, candidates from all clusters and unassigned noise labels were pooled with cumulative counts and collapsed into a final family vocabulary with a target of about 20 families. Before Pass 3, near-duplicate candidates were pre-merged by embedding cosine similarity, and large pools were consolidated in chunks followed by a final merge. Every judge prompt forbade collapsing labels into vague umbrellas (for example "concerns" or the subdomain itself) and required family labels to remain specific reasons and at most five words. In practice, some conversations still had un-merged small number labels, we chose to leave these as distinct but uncommon topics that are qualitatively interesting.

The output was a per-question mapping from each raw label to a harmonized family, with provenance (cluster id, noise status, assignment probability) and two diagnostic scores: each raw label's cosine similarity to its family centroid, and to the embedded family label.

Stage 4: Family descriptions

For each harmonized family, an LLM (Claude Sonnet 4.6, temperature 0.3) wrote a one-to two-sentence neutral description synthesized from its child labels, belief statements, and quotes (Supplementary Note 4). These descriptions were used for reporting and were shown to participants in the member-checking exercise.

Final output Each participant-question pair -i.e. a single turn in an AI interview -was now enriched with a list of topics, each with its raw label, harmonized family label, belief statement, effect on target, importance, valence, turn indices, and validated quotes.

Validation of topic labels

The labels are crucial to claims about the subject coverage and panel comparison. We conducted a follow up exercise with 403 participants from the non-probability panel. We targeted 100 participants from each study arm (actual numbers per arm 101-105), to ensure the interview subject did not confound the follow-up results.

Participants judged their own answers against one topic at a time and answered two questions about each: whether the label was "a fair description of something you said or meant" (binary) and "how accurately does it capture what you meant" (1 = not at all accurate to10 = completely accurate). The accuracy rating was requested whether or not the participant endorsed the topic.

Each participant had completed 3 interviews so across the 403 samples there were 4,102 topic label ratings, with a mean of 10.2 labels each (range 1-18). Ratings were clustered within participants, so we calculated confidence intervals with a participant-level cluster bootstrap with 5,000 iterations, whereby ratings were resampled within each respondent.

Participants marked 94% of topics as being an acceptable description of what they said or meant, and rated the accuracy of topic descriptions an average of 8.7/10 (95% CI 8.6-8.8). 77.8% (95% CI 73.5-81.6) of the 401 debrief respondents selected only "nothing missing" when asked whether anything they had wanted to say was absent from the topics.

This value may be inflated by acquiescence, the tendency to agree with statements put to respondents 27 . However, we do see some level of rejection (5.9% of topics), and accuracy ratings variability (median 9 of 10, 49% of topics at the maximum), and previous work shows acquiescence is relatively small in participant evaluation of AI labels 6.

AI interview vs. standard overlap quantification

Closed-ended questions To capture the overlap between closed-ended standard questions and the labels extracted from AI interviews (Figure 1) we built a mapping table of labels to each possible closed-ended answer.

For each label we classified it by LLM as: • Covered: an in-scope label judged to map onto a battery item from the matched subdomain for that question; • Unanticipated: an in-scope label judged not to map onto any battery item in that subdomain (the plotted quantity in Figure 1); • Not evaluated: a label below the reliability floor and excluded

The LLM was given the list of items and response options in the matching battery, and the topic label, and told to classify each alongside a rationale. This matching table was then reviewed and corrected by an author for accuracy.

Calculating the reliability floor

We included a label as a candidate for coverage only when its prevalence within a subdomain can be considered reliable for that sample size. We excluded any labels present for <12 respondents, based on a requirement to have a relative standard error ≤ 30%, the conventional threshold below which a survey proportion is treated as reliable in US federal health statistics 28

Calculating mention-weighted overlap

The number of times a label comes up across participants is important, because labels differ widely in how much of the conversation they represent. Counting labels equally would give one raised by 12 people the same weight as one raised by 200. The mention-weighted share reflects what fraction of participants' total set of labels concerns something the closed-ended battery does not ask. A mention is defined as a respondent raising one label within a subdomain; a label raised by the same respondent on several turns counts once.

Across the eight combinations of sample and subdomain, 1,015-1,593 mentions came from 409-514 respondents (2.4-3.1 distinct topics per respondent). Labels clearing the reliability floor accounted for 91.4-94.5% of mentions, so 5.5-8.6% were not evaluated.

For comparison, the unweighted share of labels judged unanticipated was 27.8-63.2% across cells, against 20.8-50.6% mention-weighted. Unanticipated labels therefore tend to be raised by fewer respondents than covered ones, and mention-weighting therefore provides a more conservative approach than failing to weight for number of mentions.

Confidence Intervals

To estimate intervals for the closed ended battery-coverage share, we used a respondent-level bootstrap. The shares are computed over topic mentions, but each participant can contribute several mentions, so mentions from the same person are not independent, and an interval that treated them as independent observations would be too narrow. Resampling respondents rather than mentions correctly reflects this clustering.

For each sample × subdomain cell, we recorded for every respondent how many of their distinct topics fell into each coverage category (covered, unanticipated, or not evaluated), counting a topic once per respondent. We then drew 2,000 resamples of respondents with replacement, independently in each cell and each the size of that cell (409-514 respondents), summed the category counts over the drawn respondents, and recomputed the share. Because the total number of mentions changes from one resample to the next, the interval reflects variability in the denominator as well as the numerator. The 95% confidence interval is the 2.5th to 97.5th percentile of the 2,000 resamples. Each topic's coverage category was held at its observed value in every resample, so the reliability floor (≥ 12 respondents) was not re-applied. The interval therefore describes sampling precision given the observed set of evaluated topics.

Open text questions

Three subdomains had a matched free text question (T1-1, T1-2, T2-1), so we were able to apply the same topic extraction pipeline to these free text questions and compare the labels.

Matching labels between open-text questions and AI interview

Both label sets were embedded with Sentence-BERT 29 using sentence-transformers/all-mpnet-base-v2, an MPNet-based model 30 fine-tuned for semantic textual similarity 31 , pinned to hub revision e8c3b32edf5434bc2275fc9bab85f82640a19130 (768dimensional embeddings; derived/label_coverage_provenance.json). Embeddings were L2-normalized so the dot product is the cosine similarity. For each sample × subdomain the full AI interview × free-text cosine matrix is computed; an AI interview label is covered when its row maximum reaches a given threshold (τ), a free-text label when its column maximum does. This is the maximum-similarity matching construction used in BERTScore, applied here to whole-label sentence embeddings rather than individual tokens; BERTScore similarity correlates well with human judgments 32 Setting a threshold for matching Label matching requires setting a similarity threshold, tau (τ), above which two labels are treated as mapping on to the same semantic context. Because there is no absolute recommended value, we set our τ using two complementary empirical approaches.

First, we explored at what level of similarity two topics would typically look the same. For each topic generated by the AI interview, we computed its cosine similarity to the most similar other topic in the same panel and subdomain. We pooled across each panel and subdomain (648 topics), and as each panel's topics were derived separately, this forms a dataset with a mixture of similar labels and different ones to test our matching values on. Indeed, we see a bimodal distribution in Extended Data Fig. 3a (Hartigan's dip test, D = 0.037, p < 0.001), most topics have a near neighbor that is clearly different (lower mode), and a second group has a closely related or reworded sibling (upper mode). We fitted a two-component Gaussian mixture model to these values, identifying a boundary between the two components where a topic is equally likely to belong to either group. Setting a threshold within this boundary means we are best discriminating between clearly similar topics and clearly different ones. Bootstrapping this process gives us a boundary point of 0.706 (95% bootstrap CI 0.678-0.756).

We then took the same approach on a mixture of free-text and AI interview topics. For each topic in one format, we computed its cosine similarity to the best-matching topic from the other format in the same panel and subdomain, in both directions, across the three subdomains with a matching free-text question (Extended Data Fig. 3b). Its fitted boundary, 0.709 (95% bootstrap CI 0.648-0.754), closely matched the one estimated among AI interview topics.

Second, to further validate this boundary we calibrated τ against labeled reference pairs. To estimate false-match rates, we evaluated distinct topic pairs separated by the extraction pipeline within single interviews, which are designed to be distinct (n=2,721). We found that false matches remained below 5% for τ≥0.54 (95% CI: 0.51-0.56). To evaluate recall -i.e. the ability of this approach to identify all topic pairs that are matches -we used LLM-generated semantic paraphrases (with Claude Sonnet 5) that altered phrasing while preserving core meaning (n=871), achieving at least 90% recall for τ≤0.80 (95% CI: 0.78-0.82). At τ=0.70, the matcher identified 97.8% of paraphrases while matching only 1.4% of known distinct pairs.

Based on these two exercises, we set τ at 0.70, which sits firmly inside the ranges identified above. In the interests of further transparency we computed matching across a range of similarity thresholds (0.5-0.9) which showed that our conclusions are not sensitive to the selected matching thresholds (Extended Data Fig. 1). Across the range of thresholds, AI interviews surface more unique topics than do single free text boxes.

We incorporated both Gaussian mixture model uncertainty and participant sampling variability into the error bars in Figure 1b. For each of 500 bootstrap threshold estimates, we split participants in each panel and subdomain into four non-overlapping halves. We recomputed the share of unique AI-interviewer topics at that threshold, using the scaled difference between halves (divided by √2) as the sampling error estimate. Adding these half-sample deviations to the full-sample share generated 2,000 replicate values per bar, with the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles defining the confidence bounds. Half-samples were used instead of standard respondent bootstrapping to avoid dropping rare, participant-discovered topics, preserving realistic sampling variability.

Comparing topics surfaced by participants with identical closed-ended answers

We wanted to explore how participants who answer identically to a set of closed ended questions, might differ in the reasoning behind these answers. To do this we turned to two 'universal' question batteries that were given after the main study, the 'trust-source battery' and the health-system item. For each of these items, we selected participants discussing them in two steps: first by topic, then by what each person actually said.

We identified the most common responses for each questionnaire item, pooled across both samples. We created one participant group per questionnaire item who gave an identical closed-ended answer (trust healthcare provider "A great deal"; trust public health authorities "A fair amount"; trust AI chatbots "A fair amount"; trust social media and podcasts "Not much"; "Agree" the health system acts in patients' best interests).

We then sought to identify, for each of these universal items, the subgroup of respondents who reasoned about the item's subject in the AI interview section. This would allow us to compare reasoning among participants who answered the item identically. To do this we first used an LLM judge. We used Claude Haiku 4.5 with the prompt in Supplementary Note 5, who sequentially screened every item against every interview conversation, judging whether each topic label held by at least 2% of a conversation's participants was relevant.

Not all of these universal question items had reasonable coverage in the AI interview results. To avoid looking at low participant number-groups, only closed ended items which had at least 15% of topics judged as relevant, and at least 10 participants holding one of these labels were included for analysis. Five of the nine questionnaire trust items met this criterion; the other four (pharmacists, friends and family, pharmaceutical companies, political figures) were not substantially addressed by any conversation and were excluded.

Because the first judge selection operates at the level of topics, extracted from multiple participants, we wanted to be sure each person was actually talking about something relevant to the respective question item. For example, "institutional trust" could be mapped to the question item on trusting healthcare providers, but for some people it could refer to the government instead. So we used a second LLM judge (Claude Haiku 4.5; Supplementary Note 6). This judge read the participant's own topic-relevant quotes and asked whether it was about the thing being rated, rather than about another source, their own health, or the interview. Of 6,177 mentions, 5,004 passed. Participants were included if at least one of their mentions passed. Each participant's valence was the mean of the extracted valence across their relevant mentions, weighted by extracted importance (range -1 to +1). Participants were also classified by the effect on target of their relevant topics as naming barriers to trust only, facilitators only, or both, with mentions coded as mixed counting as both. Figure 2 plots the distribution of valence within each group in 21 equal-width bins, and we summarize its spread with the interquartile range. The analysis is descriptive, and no hypothesis tests were performed.

Evaluating prompting techniques

In an observational analysis, we explored the association between the prompting technique the AI interviewer deployed, and the rate in which topics surfaced in the interview.

In counterfactual prompting (CF), participants were encouraged to explore what they might do or feel when a different condition applied 33 . One example from this study: "If the same guidance had come with a stronger warning or a more forceful explanation, would you have taken it any differently?". In reframing 34 , the interviewer prompts participants to reconsider a topic through a different lens, question threshold, or level of specificity -for example, asking what evidence would change a participant's stated view 21 , or asking them to make a general answer more concrete 35 . An example of this from the current study: "Is that line really about AI itself, or whether you'd be just as unwilling to use the web, forums, or any other non-doctor source for a health question?"

We identified the use of techniques across all the interviews with an LLM classifier. The classifier was given the turn, the preceding participant question, and the original first seed question. The prompt included a taxonomy for each of these methods, and a 2-step task decomposition (available in supplementary). This type of deductive classification with LLMs has shown to be reliable with recent frontier models 36, 37 . An interview counted as exposed to a technique if any interviewer turn within it used that technique. At each turn we report M diff , the difference in the mean number of topics accumulated so far between exposed and unexposed interviews (exposed minus unexposed, in topics), with a Welch 95% confidence interval and a two-sided Mann-Whitney U test, Cliff's δ as a distribution-free effect size, and Benjamini-Hochberg correction across the five turns within each panel and technique.

Counterfactual probes appeared in 15.9% (probability) and 17.0% (non-probability) of classified interviewer turns. Exposure to at least one counterfactual probe was not associated with a statistically significant difference in cumulative topic count at Turn 1 in either sample, but by Turn 2 produced more topics even after Benjamini-Hochberg correction in both samples (Figure 3b; Extended Data Table 1). Differences are small with an additional 0.1 topic and Cliff's δ never exceeding .08. This small effect suggests that CF may produce responses with more topics compared with conversations that don't show the technique, but this pattern is inconsistent at an individual level.

Reframing probes were associated with a greater number of topics, particularly in the probability sample. Reframing probes appeared in 37.6% (probability) and 41.6% (non-probability) of interviewer turns, and here the two samples diverged (Figure 3c; Extended Data Table 1). In the probability sample, exposed interviews already held more topics at Turn 1 and the difference persisted at every subsequent turn, reaching roughly half a topic by Turn 5, which was the largest contrast of either technique in either sample. In the non-probability sample the difference was smaller and emerged later, reaching statistical significance only from Turn 4.

The probability sample's Turn-1 difference indicates that conversations the interviewer went on to reframe already differed before most reframing had occurred, perhaps because conversations already needed to be detailed enough for the AI moderator to reframe. So the later gap should not be read as the technique's effect alone, and experimental manipulation of probing strategies is required to estimate causal effects.
