Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 225 papers containing 405 instances of non-exact p-values, the module correctly detected 269 cases (true positives) and incorrectly identified 78 (false positives). It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. Abstract

Objectives: To examine which measure of socioeconomic position and socioenvironmental conditions has the strongest relationship with subjective cognitive decline (SCD) among midlife and older lesbian, gay, bisexual, transgender, queer, intersex, asexual, and other sexual and gender minority (LGBTQIA+) adults.

Methods: We conducted a cross-sectional analysis of 3869 LGBTQIA+ adults aged 50 years or older participating in a community-engaged survey in California. Using logistic regression, we examined overall associations between each indicator of socioeconomic position and socioenvironmental conditions and SCD. We used a dominance analysis to assess the relative importance of each indicator based on the proportion of variance in SCD it explained.

Results: Overall, 14.7% of the sample reported SCD. Midlife and older LGBTQIA+ adults in a lower socioeconomic position (i.e., lower education, unemployment, lower household income, fewer assets, financial insecurity, food insecurity, and unstable housing) had higher odds of SCD than those in a higher position, with significant odds ratios (OR) ranging from 1.28 to 2.55. In contrast, perceiving one's community as safe for LGBTQIA+ people was associated with lower odds of SCD (OR 0.47; 95% CI 0.38, 0.58). Dominance analysis indicated that household assets was the most important factor, explaining 4.4% of the variance in SCD (95% CI 3.5%, 5.4%), followed by household income (2.9%; 95% CI 2.2%, 3.8%) and perceived community safety (2.2%; 95% CI 1.4%, 3.1%).

Discussion: Supporting the cognitive health of LGBTQIA+ people may require attention to both financial security across the life course and the broader social environments.

INTRODUCTION

Subjective cognitive decline (SCD) is characterized by self-reported experiences of worsening or frequent confusion or memory loss (Jessen et al., 2014). While not all individuals who report SCD will progress on to develop cognitive decline, it remains an important early indicator associated with an elevated risk of mild cognitive impairment and Alzheimer's disease and related dementias (ADRD) (Jessen et al., 2014; Rabin et al., 2017; Rostamzadeh et al., 2022). SCD can also interfere with individuals' capacity to perform activities of daily living, thereby compromising their quality of life (Hill et al., 2017). Because interventions to delay or prevent ADRD may be most effective at the earliest stages of disease progression, there is scientific and public health interest in identifying factors that contribute to SCD (Jessen et al., 2020; Wong et al., 2025).

Approximately 11% of U.S. adults aged 45 years or older report SCD (Taylor et al., 2018). This burden has been shown to be disproportionately pronounced among lesbian, gay, bisexual, queer, intersex, asexual, and other sexual and gender minority (LGBTQIA+) populations (Flatt et al., 2021; Fredriksen-Goldsen et al., 2022; Huo et al., 2024). According to the minority stress model, higher rates of physical health problems experienced by LGBTQIA+ people are a result of distinct and additive stressors beyond those experienced by the general population, such as experiences with rejection, expectation of stigma, and internalized negativity (Flentje et al., 2020; Hendricks & Testa, 2012; Meyer, 2003). Among LGBTQIA+ adults aged 45 years or older, 15.7% reported SCD, a prevalence 50% higher than their non-LGBTQIA+ counterparts (Flatt et al., 2021). Further compounded by the intersection of gender and race, midlife and older transgender adults, particularly those from minoritized ethnoracial communities, reported having 1.7 to 2.5 times greater odds of SCD compared to their White cisgender counterparts (Cicero et al., 2023).

A substantial body of research has identified social and economic factors as upstream determinants of cognitive aging and ADRD risk in the general population (Glymour & Manly, 2008; Lövdén et al., 2020; Luo et al., 2025; Rosso et al., 2016; Singh-Manoux et al., 2005; Adkins-Jackson et al., 2023), yet their role within LGBTQIA+ populations remains poorly understood. Existing studies among LGBTQIA+ populations have primarily focused on social isolation and depression as key contributors to SCD (Flatt et al., 2018; Fredriksen-Goldsen et al., 2018; Hsieh et al., 2021; Kim et al., 2025). However, LGBTQIA+ adults also face elevated prevalence of poverty and economic insecurity and are more likely to experience employment loss, food insecurity, and inadequate financial resources to support daily living compared with non-LGBTQIA+ people (Fredriksen-Goldsen et al., 2013; Nguyen et al., 2024). Occupationbased socioeconomic position, such as precarious employment and loss of employment, has been associated with greater depressive symptoms in LGBTQIA+ adults (Kinitz, Tran, Shahidi, et al., 2026; Kinitz, Tran, Soltani, et al., 2026), a proximal factor for SCD (Correro II & Nielson, 2020; Flatt et al., 2018; Hsieh et al., 2021). These conditions may become increasingly consequential for LGBTQIA+ people as they age and underscore the need to better address the social and economic needs that support healthy cognitive aging in this population.

In this study, we aimed to characterize the prevalence of SCD and its relationship to respondents' socioeconomic position and socioenvironmental conditions in a community-based sample of midlife and older LGBTQIA+ adults in California. We focused on California given its rapidly growing population of both older adults and LGBTQIA+ people (Johnson, 2024; Johnson et al., 2025), and because addressing economic insecurity is a key priority among these groups (University of California, San Francisco, 2024). We then aim to quantify the relative contribution of each social and economic factor that shaped SCD among midlife and older LGBTQIA+ adults to understand which factors may be among the most important in relation to SCD.

METHODS

Study Design and Participants

This study is a secondary analysis of data collected through an online cross-sectional survey conducted from January to March 2024 to assess the health, well-being, and service needs of midlife and older LGBTQIA+ adults living in California (University of California, San Francisco, 2024). Participants were recruited using convenience sampling through a variety of outreach strategies led by Openhouse, a nonprofit organization that supports older LGBTQIA+ people. These strategies included the creation of a statewide coalition of LGBTQIA+ service providers, distribution of flyers in community spaces, social media posts, newsletters, a dedicated website hosted by the California Department of Aging, and word-of-mouth among community members, senior service organizations, and events. Additional details regarding community outreach are described elsewhere (University of California, San Francisco, 2024).

The eligibility criteria for the study were self-identifying as LGBTQIA+, being 50 years of age or older, and residing in California including community-dwelling older adults and those in senior living facilities. The survey was administered online through Qualtrics (Qualtrics LLC, Provo, UT) and was available in Chinese, English, Spanish, and Tagalog, the most widely spoken languages in California. Eligible participants were asked to provide informed consent for themselves, and those who completed the survey could choose to enter a drawing to win a $25 gift card. The study was approved by the [redacted] Institutional Review Board.

Several steps were taken to ensure the quality of survey responses during data collection. These included fraud and bot detection tools embedded in Qualtrics and manual reviews of potentially invalid responses. Those identified as likely fraudulent or bot-generated (n = 406) or flagged as invalid during manual review (n = 2) were excluded from the dataset. In addition, we excluded 168 participants with missing outcome data for the current analysis.

Measures

Subjective Cognitive Decline

Subjective cognitive decline was assessed with a single self-reported question based on the Behavioral Risk Factor Surveillance System Cognitive Decline module, "During the past 12 months, have you experienced confusion or memory loss that is happening more often or is getting worse?" Response options included "yes", "no", and "I don't know/I'm not sure". Those who responded affirmatively were considered to have experienced SCD.

Socioeconomic and Socioenvironmental Conditions

We included nine indicators that captured participants' socioeconomic position and socioenvironmental condition. 1) Education level was categorized into four groups: high school diploma/GED or less, some college, 4-year college degree, and graduate degree. 2) Unemployment was dichotomized as a binary measure. Participants were classified as unemployed if they did not report any form of employment (employed, working 1-39 hours per week; employed, working 40 or more hours per week; self-employed; or temporarily employed) and were not retired. All others were considered unemployed. 3) Household income was categorized into five groups ranging from $30,000 or less to more than $120,000.4) Household assets were assessed by asking participants to estimate the total value of all assets owned by their household. Responses were grouped into four categories from less than $10,000 to more than $500,000.5) Financial insecurity was assessed by asking whether participants had struggled to pay bills due to insufficient income in the past 12 months (yes or no). 6) Food insecurity was measured by asking how often participants worried about having enough money for nutritious meals in the past 12 months (yes or no). 7) Housing stability was assessed based on participants' current housing situation and categorized as stable if they reported having a steady place to live and unstable if they reported being worried about losing housing in the future or not having a steady place to live (e.g., in a shelter, living outside on the street). 8) Transportation was assessed by asking participants to select the methods they typically use to get where they need to go. Participants who selected public transportation were categorized as relying on it; all others were categorized as not relying on public transportation. 9) LGBTQIA+ community safety was assessed through two questions asking how safe participants perceived their current community to be for 1) transgender, genderqueer, and non-binary individuals (gender minority safety) and 2) lesbian, gay, asexual, bisexual, and queer individuals (sexual minority safety), using a 5-point scale (1=extremely safe to5=extremely unsafe). We then combined these responses to create a composite measure with four categories: safe for both gender and sexual minority people, safe for gender minority people only, safe for sexual minority people only, and unsafe for both groups.

Participant Characteristics

Participants self-reported their ethnoracial identity, current gender identity, current sexual orientation, intersex status, place of birth, prior military service, and health insurance. Participants could select multiple options for their ethnoracial identity, gender, and sexual orientation. Sexual orientations were combined to create non-mutually exclusive groups: asexual, bisexual, gay/lesbian, queer, straight, and another sexual orientation. Gender identities were cross stratified with sex assigned at birth to create mutually exclusive groups: cisgender men, cisgender women, gender diverse individuals assigned female at birth, gender diverse individuals assigned male at birth, transgender men, and transgender women. Urban residence (yes or no) was based on self-reported ZIP code. Age groups (50-59, 60-69, ≥70 years) was based on self-reported birthdate and categorized prior to data release to support deidentification. HIV status was dichotomized as living with HIV for those reported "positive" and not living with HIV if otherwise.

Mental Health and Substance Use

Mental health measures included loneliness assessed using the UCLA 3-item Loneliness Scale (UCLA-3, range: 3-12) (Hughes et al., 2004), depressive symptoms in the past two weeks assessed using the Patient Health Questionnaire-2 (PHQ-2, range: 0-6) (Kroenke et al., 2003), and PTSD symptoms in the past month assessed using the 6-item PTSD Checklist (PCL-6, range: 6-30) (Lang & Stein, 2005). For all three scales, higher scores indicate greater symptom severity. Substance use included current cigarette smoking and alcohol consumption, with the latter assessed using the Alcohol Use Disorders Identification Test-Consumption (AUDIT-C, range: 0-12), where higher scores indicate greater alcohol consumption and potential hazardous drinking (Bush et al., 1998).

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive analysis

We summarized the socioeconomic and socioenvironmental conditions, sociodemographic characteristics, health conditions, and substance use for the overall sample and by SCD. Differences were assessed using t-test for continuous variables and chi-square or Fisher's Exact test for categorical variables. Correlations were calculated between each socioeconomic and socioenvironmental indicator.

Regression model specification

To evaluate associations between socioeconomic and socioenvironmental conditions and SCD, we fitted separate logistic regression models to estimate the unadjusted and adjusted odds ratios (OR) and associated 95% confidence intervals (CI). We used a directed acyclic graph (Supplemental Figure 1) to identify potential confounders and mediators based on the published literature of correlates of cognitive decline and ADRD risk in both the general population and LGBTQIA+ people (Adkins-Jackson et al., 2023; Alzheimer's Association, 2025; Correro II & Nielson, 2020; Flatt et al., 2018; Fredriksen-Goldsen et al., 2013 , 2018; Hsieh et al., 2021; Kim et al., 2025; Livingston et al., 2020; Lövdén et al., 2020; Luo et al., 2025). For our main analysis, we considered the hypothetical timing of covariates in relation individuals' socioeconomic and socioenvironmental conditions from a life course perspective in order to identify a minimally sufficient set of confounders for adjustment. Adjusted models accounted for age groups, gender groups, ethnoracial groups, US birth, urban residence, prior military service, and living with HIV. While mental health symptoms and substance use may be important confounders, in the context of our study, we have conceptualized them as potential mediators based on prior literature and our hypothesized framework and therefore did not adjust for them in the main analysis (Zwieten et al., 2022). As a sensitivity analysis, we assessed the robustness of the estimated associations in the main analysis to additional adjustment for mental health symptoms (loneliness, depressive symptoms, PTSD symptoms) and substance use (current smoker and alcohol use).

Handling of missing data

Approximately 18% of the study sample had any missing data, with non-response rates ranging from 0.1% to 8.7% across all variables. Thus, we addressed missingness using multiple imputation by chained equations, using the mice R package to generate 20 imputed datasets (van Buuren & Groothuis-Oudshoorn, 2011). Analyses for our main models were conducted on each imputed dataset, and the results were combined using Rubin's rules to obtain overall estimates and their standard errors (Rubin, 1978).

Dominance analysis

To calculate the relative importance of each socioeconomic and socioenvironmental condition on SCD in LGBTQIA+ midlife and older adults, we used an extension of the dominance analysis procedure that allows for the use of logistic regression (Azen & Traxel, 2009). This approach assesses the change in variance for all possible subset models, allowing assessment of the relative importance of each predictor both alone and in the presence of all other combinations of predictors -that is, how much variance in the observed outcome can be attributed to one factor versus another. However, unlike linear models that use the traditional R² , we used pseudo-R² measures, specifically McFadden's R² , to determine relative importance. We opted for McFadden's R² due to its desirable statistical properties (Azen & Traxel, 2009), including boundness (values vary between 0 to 1), linear invariance (remains unchanged to linear transformation), monotonicity (value does not decrease when variables are added to the model), and intuitive interpretability.

We first fit a multivariable logistic model that included all socioeconomic and socioenvironmental factors while controlling for potential confounders as outlined in our main analysis, representing the total variance explained by our model. Because the dominance analysis software does not support multiply imputed data, we used complete cases for this analysis. We also refitted the same model with imputed data to assess the robustness of the results to the assumed missing data mechanism. We then used the dominanceanalysis R package to decompose the total variance and evaluate the relative importance of each socioeconomic and environmental factors while keeping potential confounders constant across all models (Navarrete & Soares, 2025). A bootstrap procedure with 1000 resamples was used to obtain general dominance values (i.e., proportion of variance explained for a given variable averaged across all model sizes) and the corresponding 95% percentile CI. We report general dominance for our analysis to identify which socioeconomic and environmental factors that, on average, contribute most to explaining the variance in SCD among LGBTIQIA+ midlife and older adults. We also reran the dominance analysis to determine whether the relative importance of each socioeconomic and socioenvironmental condition would change after accounting for mental health symptoms and substance use. All analysis were conducted in R version 4.5.1.

RESULTS

Descriptive Analysis

The final sample included 3,869 midlife and older LGBTQIA+ respondents (Table 1), of whom 34.0% (n=1317) were aged ≥70 years, 11.2% (n=424) were transgender and gender diverse, 21.8% (n=844) reported at least 1 minoritized ethnoracial identity, 86.3% (n=3340) identified as gay or lesbian, and 1.0% (n=40) were intersex. Educational levels were high in the study sample; 73% (n=3813) held at least a 4-year degree, including 42% (n=1624) with graduate degrees (Table 2). However, unemployment was at 56.6% (n=2189). Income distribution varied widely, with 32.2% (n=1246) reporting household incomes of >$120,000, while 16.9% (n=655) earned ≤$30,000. Similarly, 41.3% (n=1597) reported household assets of >$500,000, contrasting with 13% (n=502) having <$10,000 in assets. Respondents reported various forms of insecurity: 25.9% (n=1001) experienced financial insecurity, 34.9% (n=1352) faced food insecurity, and 12.9% (n=500) dealt with housing instability. Additionally, 34.0% (n=1314) relied on public transit for their transportation needs. Half (51.3%, n=1986) of respondents felt their community was safe for both sexual and gender minority people, while 20.8% (n=803) perceived it as unsafe.

Overall, 14.7% of participants reported experiencing SCD. Compared to participants who did not report SCD, those reporting SCD were more likely to be aged 70 years or older (38.7% vs. 33.2%, p=0.04), gender diverse assigned male at birth (5.6% vs. 2.6%, p=0.004), bisexual or pansexual individuals (11.8% vs. 8.7%, p=0.02), to have prior military service (12% vs. 9.1%, p=0.04), and to be currently living with HIV (23.2% vs. 15.1%, p<0.001).

Pairwise correlations of each socioeconomic and contextual factor of interest are shown in Figure 1. Education levels showed positive correlations with household income (r=0.32, p<0.001) and assets (r=0.29, p<0.001), while negatively correlating with financial insecurity (r=-0.23, p<0.001) and food insecurity (r=-0.26, p<0.001). Household income and assets were moderately positively correlated (r=0.54, p<0.001) and both negatively correlated with financial insecurity, food insecurity, and housing instability (all p<0.001). Financial and food insecurity were strongly correlated (r=0.65, p<0.001) and both positively associated with housing instability (r=0.35 and r=0.36, respectively, both p<0.001). Lack of LGBTQIA+ community safety was positively correlated with financial insecurity (r=0.19, p<0.001), food insecurity (r=0.23, p<0.001), and housing instability (r=0.15, p<0.001). Reliance on public transportation showed weak but significant positive correlations with various insecurity measures.

Regression modeling

In unadjusted and adjusted models, nearly all socioeconomic and socioenvironmental conditions were significantly associated with SCD (Figure 2 and Supplemental Table 1). In models that regressed SCD on education, household income, household assets, being in a lower position for any of these socioeconomic factors was associated with higher odds of SCD, with progressively stronger associations at lower socioeconomic positions. For example, in adjusted models, respondents with the lowest household income and assets had 2.03 (95% CI 1.25, 3.31) times and 2.55 (95% CI 1.96, 3.32) times higher odds of SCD compared to the highest income and asset groups. At moderate household income ($30,000-$60,000) and assets ($10,000-$100,000), respondents had 1.76 (95% CI 1.34, 2.31) times and 2.02 (95% CI 1.56, 2.62) times higher odds of SCD compared to the highest income and asset groups. Similarly, unemployment, financial insecurity, food insecurity, unstable housing, reliance on public transportation were all associated with higher odds of SCD, with adjusted ORs ranging from 1.28 to 2.30. Regarding LGBTQIA+ community safety, those who reported their community as safe for both sexual and gender minorities had lower odds of SCD (adjusted OR 0.47; 95% CI 0.38, 0.58) compared to those who felt their community was not safe. In sensitivity analysis that additionally adjusting for mental health symptoms and substance use, we observed attenuation of the estimated associations (Supplemental Table 1). Several associations remained statistically significant (e.g., education, household income, financial and food insecurity, and perceived LGBTQIA+ community safety), whereas others (e.g., unstable housing and reliance on public transportation) were reduced in magnitude and their 95% CIs included the null.

Dominance analysis

Estimates of the multivariable logistic model that included all socioeconomic and socioenvironmental conditions while controlling for potential confounders, as outlined in our main analysis, are shown in Supplemental Table 2; results from complete cases and multiply imputed data indicated minimal differences. The dominance analysis suggested that among midlife and older LGBTQIA+ respondents, household assets were the most important factor influencing SCD, explaining 4.4% of the variance (95% CI 3.5%, 5.4%; Figure 3). This was followed closely by household income, which explained 2.9% of the variance (95% CI 2.2%, 3.8%), and perceived community safety, which explained 2.2% of the variance (95% CI 1.4%, 3.1%). Unemployment, financial insecurity, housing instability, food insecurity, education levels, and reliance on public transit each explained <1% of the variance in SCD among respondents. Sensitivity analyses that additionally accounted for mental health symptoms and substance use in dominance analysis showed attenuation in the variance explained in SCD by each factor (Supplemental Figure 2). Reduction in the variance explained in SCD for assets, household income, and perceived community safety were all by at least 1 percentage point.

DISCUSSION

Consistent with prior studies among midlife and older LGBTQIA+ adults from population-based samples (Cicero et al., 2023; Flatt et al., 2021; Fredriksen-Goldsen et al., 2022; Hsieh et al., 2021), nearly 15% of midlife and older LGBTQIA+ respondents in this California sample reported SCD with higher percentages reported in certain subgroups such as bisexual and pansexual people as well as gender diverse people assigned male at birth. Midlife and older LGBTQIA+ respondents with SCD were more likely to experience social and economic disadvantage compared to those without SCD, even after accounting for age, gender, ethnoracial groups, US birth, urban residence, prior military service, and HIV status. The most important socioeconomic and socioenvironmental contributors to SCD were household assets (explaining 4.4% of the variance), followed by household income (2.9% of the variance) and community safety for LGBTQIA+ people (2.2% of the variance). Other factors, such as unemployment, financial insecurity, housing instability, food insecurity, and education level, were found to be less important, with each factor explaining less than 1% of the variance in SCD. Because our sample was predominantly cisgender and gay or lesbian, and include relatively more socially and economically advantaged respondents, these aggregate estimates may primarily reflect the experiences of these groups and mask subgroup differences in association of economic and social conditions with SCD for transgender and diverse people, bisexual people, and racially minoritized LGBTQIA+ people. Thus, the observed associations may underestimate the potential magnitude of social and economic disparities in SCD among midlife and older LGBTQIA+ adults.

Our result is consistent with the broader literature on social factors and SCD among the general population (Singh-Manoux et al., 2005; Lövdén et al., 2020; Luo et al., 2025) and on research focusing on the economic marginalization LGBTQIA+ individuals experience and the proposed biological pathways between minority stress and physical health (Correro II & Nielson, 2020; Flentje et al., 2020 Flentje et al., , 2022;; Hendricks & Testa, 2012; Kinitz et al., 2025; Meyer, 2003). Chronic minority stress may be related to epigenetic changes and alterations in the hypothalamicpituitary-adrenal axis functioning associated with accelerated cognitive decline in LGBTQIA+ individuals (Correro II & Nielson, 2020; Flentje et al., 2020; Hatzenbuehler & McLaughlin, 2014). Complementary work has documented a higher prevalence of SCD among LGBTQIA+ people, identified social isolation and depressive symptoms as proximal mediators of cognitive decline in this population (Correro II & Nielson, 2020; Fredriksen-Goldsen et al., 2018; Hsieh et al., 2021; Kim et al., 2025), and highlighted poorer economic conditions as a potentially important socially patterned exposure that may result from minority stress (Kinitz et al., 2025; Kinitz, Tran, Shahidi, et al., 2026; Kinitz, Tran, Soltani, et al., 2026; Nguyen et al., 2024). Notably, in our study, associations between lower socioeconomic position and SCD remained robust after adjusting for mental health symptoms and substance use, though we observed attenuation of the explained SCD variance after accounting for mental health and substance use in the dominance analysis. This suggests the potential importance of these factors for SCD in LGBTQIA+ individuals and highlight the need for future longitudinal research to better understand the mechanism between economic marginalization, mental health and substance use, and later-life cognitive decline.

An important contribution of our study is the identification of community safety as a socioenvironmental correlate of cognitive health in LGBTQIA+ populations. Respondents who perceived their community as safe for sexual and/or gender minority individuals had 53% lower odds of reporting SCD. Although research directly examining the association of community safety and SCD among LGBTQIA+ is limited, this result aligns with evidence examining the association of perceived community safety and physical health (Flentje et al., 2022). Relatedly, area-based characteristics that capture aspects of community safety, such as place-based disorder (e.g., signs of neglect, violence, or lack of perceived safety), have been associated with higher stress perceptions among LGBTQIA+ adults and greater depressive symptoms among transgender and gender diverse adults (Tran et al., 2026). These findings underscore the broader influence of the social environment on cognitive health in LGBTQIA+ populations and highlight the need for further research into the mechanisms through which community safety may shape SCD.

Results from dominance analysis further suggest that both financial security across the life course and the broader social environments may be the most relevant socioeconomic and socioenvironmental condition to SCD. Household assets, which represent accumulated wealth over the life course (Krieger et al., 1997), were shown to explain the most variance in SCD. One's assets (or lack thereof) may reflect the long-term consequences of earlier financial strains that leave LGBTQIA+ people unable to build their savings or possess the necessary financial resources to support cognitive aging and care in later life. This is consistent with work suggesting that LGBTQIA+ individuals experience heightened employment precarity and economic marginalization related to minority stress (Kinitz et al., 2025; Nguyen et al., 2024), which may accumulate over time to shape cognitive health. Because assets reflect long-term financial trajectories, they may be difficult to modify later in life, particularly for economically marginalized groups. This highlights the potential value of future research focusing on interventions that promote financial security earlier in the life course, such as stable income opportunities and retirement and financial planning programs tailored to the needs of LGBTQIA+ communities. By contrast, household income and perceived community safety highlight the potential relevance of policies and programs that address the current social and economic circumstances for midlife and older LGBTQIA+ people, especially those with SCD. Such efforts may include addressing economic marginalization that LGBTQIA+ people face, such as unemployment, poorer job quality, and gender-based discrimination (Fredriksen Goldsen et al., 2022; Kinitz et al., 2025; Nguyen et al., 2024), which may contribute to fewer financial resources that would allow LGBTQIA+ people to live in communities with more supportive resources.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal interpretation, as cognitive impairment in older age may itself contribute to greater financial insecurity and poorer living conditions. Second, the outcome was self-reported. Prior work has noted that SCD is an imperfect indicator of objective cognitive impairment (Romanelli et al., 2024), and future studies would benefit from incorporating cognitive testing to better understand how socioeconomic and socioenvironmental conditions relate to cognitive decline. Relatedly, the cross-sectional design does not allow us to examine changes in cognition over time, which may be more biologically relevant for understanding the influence of social and economic factors. Third, selection bias is possible if LGBTQIA+ individuals experiencing greater economic marginalization and SCD were less likely to participate. Fourth, our analyses assume no model misspecification and that included covariates were sufficient to control for confounding, though unmeasured confounding may be present. Fifth, while minimal differences were observed between the multiple imputation and complete-case analysis, these methods may still be biased under missing-not-at-random or misspecification of the imputation model. Sixth, participants included in this study were derived from a convenience, with an overrepresentation of White, cisgender, and gay/lesbian respondents and may not be representative of LGBTQIA+ individuals in California. Furthermore, while the survey was offered in four languages, nearly all participants completed it in English. These factors limit the generalizability of our findings. Finally, our analyses considered LGBTQIA+ adults as an aggregate population and were not designed to examine heterogeneity in these associations across sexual orientation, gender identity, or intersecting social identities. Prior research suggests that experiences of SCD may differ across sexual and gender minority subgroups and at the intersections of gender and ethnoracial identity (Cicero et al., 2023; Flatt et al., 2021). Future studies with larger samples of transgender, gender-diverse, and other underrepresented LGBTQIA+ populations should examine whether socioeconomic and socioenvironmental correlates of SCD differ across these groups.

CONCLUSION

Notwithstanding these limitations, the current analysis contributes to the literature on aging and

LGBTQIA+ health by offering new evidence on the relative importance of social and economic conditions that may support cognitive aging in midlife and older LGBTQIA+ adults. Using regression and dominance analysis, our study identified both economic security and community safety as factors that can both inform future research and guide focused interventions. Future research examining interventions to improve financial literacy and retirement planning may help support LGBTQIA+ people as they age.
