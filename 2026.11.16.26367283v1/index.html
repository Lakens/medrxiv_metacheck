Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

INTRODUCTION

Crohn's disease (CD) is a chronic inflammatory disorder of the gastrointestinal (GI) tract with increasing prevalence and significant financial burden worldwide (1). CD is transmural across the GI tract, causing manifestations that vary from mild abdominal pain and inflammation to severe bowel injury and/or obstruction, occasionally leading to surgical resection (2).

Disorders of gut-brain interaction (DGBI), including irritable bowel syndrome (IBS) and functional dyspepsia, are functional gastrointestinal disorders marked by recurrent pain, altered bowel habits, bloating, and/or dyspeptic symptoms, symptoms that can mimic those of active inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) (3, 4). Among children with IBD in remission, IBS and DGBI with abdominal pain occur in up to 16.1% and 29.5%, respectively, further complicating the differentiation between a diagnosis of CD and DGBI (5). Due to these challenges, diagnosis requires extensive non-invasive and invasive testing (6), including endoscopy, histology, and imaging (6) (7) (8). Still, these diagnostic methods are not 100% reliable (9).

Currently, there is no cure for CD, so clinical management aims to minimize severity by inducing and maintaining disease remission (10). A widely utilized method to track pediatric CD disease activity clinically is the Pediatric Crohn's Disease Activity Index (PCDAI), which ranges from 0 to 100 based on a combination of questionnaire responses and biological measurements, enabling severity-aware assessment in practice and research (11, 12). Many of the questions within the PCDAI, however, are also relevant to DGBIs, further complicating the diagnoses.

CD pathogenesis is generally characterized by dysregulated innate and adaptive immunity, epithelial barrier dysfunction, intestinal microbiome alterations, and genetic risk factors (13). Bulk RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) CD studies suggest prominent roles for neutrophil activation/degranulation and innate-immune signaling pathways including NOD-like and Fcγ-receptor-driven pathways, alongside Th1/Th17 cell responses (14), all of which are associated with disease activity (15). Several of these pathways, including TNF-and IL-12/23driven inflammation, are targets for current therapies, yet non-response and loss of response remain common in clinical practice (16, 17). These features are thus driving efforts to develop biomarkers that can distinguish CD from DGBI, as well as aid clinicians in facilitating earlier diagnosis and treatment stratification. However, most studies utilize tissue biopsy samples (18) (19) (20) (21). Whole blood offers a less-invasive alternative that is obtainable at presentation, captures systemic immune activity, and is amenable to scalable clinical testing. Most whole-blood studies, however, are limited to non-symptomatic healthy controls and/or assess disease activity at time of surgery (22) (23) (24) (25). There are few studies investigating whole-blood gene expression at (a) time of CD diagnosis and (b) utilizing symptomatic controls (26, 27). Because many individuals with suspected IBD initially present with functional gastrointestinal symptoms, we used DGBI as a symptomatic, non-IBD comparison population to minimize bias inherent to healthy control designs.

We therefore performed whole-blood RNA sequencing in a prospective, two-center cohort of treatment-naïve children and young adults presenting with undiagnosed gastrointestinal symptoms, comparing those ultimately diagnosed with CD with those diagnosed with DGBI. To our knowledge, this is the largest transcriptome-wide whole-blood study of treatment-naïve pediatric CD and the first pediatric study to use a Rome IV-defined DGBI comparator. Because patients present across a range of disease activities, we also modeled PCDAI within CD. This allowed us to ask whether the signal that separates CD from DGBI is the same signal that tracks clinical severity. Our aims were to (1) define transcriptional and immune cell-composition differences between CD and DGBI and their relationship to PCDAI; (2) organize these differences into co-expression and protein-protein interaction networks to characterize the 111 biological programs involved at a systems-level; and (3) identify, within each network, the genes 112 that most reproducibly discriminated CD from DGBI, combine them into a minimal cross-113 module diagnostic panel, and evaluate its predictive performance in an independent pediatric 114 cohort composed of CD and symptomatic control whole-blood samples. 115

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Complete methods are provided in the Supplementary Materials.

Study design, sample collection, and ethical approval

All participants (and guardians, where appropriate) provided written informed consent, and study procedures were approved by the Institutional Review Boards of the NYU Grossman (28). Individuals with biopsy-confirmed ulcerative colitis, celiac disease, or CD who received treatment prior to sample collection were excluded. The site study team independently reviewed all CD diagnoses using established criteria that incorporated clinical history, physical examination, laboratory testing, and endoscopic and/or radiologic evaluation. Disease activity in CD was assessed with the PCDAI, and patients were stratified into minimally-active (PCDAI ≤ 10), mild (10 < PCDAI ≤ 30), or moderate-severe (30 < PCDAI) categories based on accepted thresholds from previous studies (29, 30). We used the term minimally-active instead of remission because individuals presenting to the clinic for the first time with gastrointestinal symptoms cannot have achieved remission. These groups were used to summarize the CD population and visualize results but were not used as model inputs.

Across the study period, 438 peripheral blood samples were collected during (a) first presentation to the clinic or (b) follow-up for the diagnostic tests. Thirteen samples were excluded at quality control, as described below, yielding a final analytic cohort of 425 samples (112 CD, 313 DGBI) used for all downstream analyses. C-reactive protein (CRP) was measured in a subset of our participants, and demographic data were collected by the study team, which included age, BMI percentile, secondhand smoke exposure, and sex. Missing secondhand smoke exposure (n = 31) was coded as a separate category in downstream models. Demographic and clinical characteristics were summarized by study group and compared using Wilcoxon ranksum tests for continuous variables and Pearson chi-square tests for categorical variables.

Whole-Blood RNA-seq

Total RNA was extracted from whole blood, and quality was verified by Bioanalyzer trace. Library preparation was performed using the Tecan RNA library preparation kit. The protocol included ribosomal RNA (rRNA) depletion and integrated DNase treatment. For each sample, 5 ng of total RNA was used as input, and 10 PCR cycles were applied during library amplification. Library concentrations were quantified by quantitative PCR, and samples were pooled to achieve uniform sequencing depth. Sequencing was performed on the Illumina NovaSeq X Plus platform, generating 2 × 51 bp paired-end reads, with one plate loaded per flow cell.

Bioinformatic Analyses

Bulk RNA-seq reads were processed with a standard nf-core/Nextflow pipeline (doi:10.5281/zenodo.1400710) (31), which included STAR alignment to GRCh38, and transcript quantification with salmon. Thirteen samples with STAR alignment < 85% were removed.

Quantification files were imported into R v4.4.1 (32), where transcripts were summarized to the gene level, and low-expression genes were removed with edgeR. Batch effects were investigated using principal component analysis (PCA). Bulk deconvolution (CIBERSORT/LM220) (33) was performed to estimate immune cell fractions between CD and DGBI groups, where the trimmed mean of M-values (TMM) normalized counts per million (CPM) matrix was used as input.

Estimated immune cell fractions were compared between CD and DGBI with Wilcoxon tests. Differential expression analysis (DEA) was performed with the limma-voom pipeline to identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs). We fit two models: To characterize shared pathway-level perturbations across both CD activity and diagnosis, we took the overlapping DEGs from both models and performed over-representation analysis using Gene Ontology Biological Process (GOBP) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG).

Weighted Gene Co-Expression Network Analysis (WGCNA) was performed using the normalized residual counts (batch-corrected) to identify co-expressed gene modules (34). The expression profile of each module was summarized by its module eigengene (ME), the first principal component of the module's gene expression, which was then correlated with CD diagnosis and PCDAI group variables. Module genes were then overlapped with DEGs from both models to identify differentially co-expressed modules (DCMs) enriched for DEGs associated with CD diagnosis and CD severity. The DCMs' gene sets were further refined by filtering the modules into respective protein-protein interaction (PPI) networks with Search Tool for the Retrieval of Interacting Genes/Proteins (STRING v12.0). Combined score ≥ 500 was used to filter for interactions between protein-coding genes. Each differentially expressed PPI network was pruned to the largest connected component (LCC) and summarized by proportion of network nodes retained, the global clustering coefficient, and Louvain modularity. Reactome pathway over-representation analysis was performed separately for each module's LCC using all genes tested from the two models as the background, with significance defined as FDR < 0.05.

Nodes were annotated by their DEA results from both models (log 2 FC and FDR significance), and we defined a composite centrality as the mean rank across unweighted degree, betweenness centrality, PageRank centrality, and k-core. We used the composite centrality and DEA results to identify differentially expressed hub genes. Community structure within each module's LCC was inferred with a nested stochastic block model (NSBM), fit by minimizing the model's description length. The resulting finest-level partition was used for network visualization and to test blocklevel concentration (clusters) of DEGs.

Supervised Machine Learning

DEGs within each DCM's LCC were the candidate features. Samples were split 70/30 into training and held-out test partitions, stratified by diagnosis and CD activity group. In Stage 1, each module was analyzed on the training partition by repeated nested cross-validation (CV; 10 folds, 10 repeats), with least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) gene selection followed by LASSO, random forest (RF) and support vector machine (SVM) classifiers. Genes selected in at least 90% of 1,000 bootstrap LASSO fits formed each module's stable genes. In Stage 2, stable genes were pooled across modules, and genes selected in at least 85% of a second round of bootstraps formed the final panel. Because the panel genes were fixed before CV, the final-panel CV was not nested with respect to gene selection. The panel was evaluated once on the held-out test partition and, after refitting on all 425 samples, once on 39 208 CD and 37 symptomatic control (SC) whole-blood samples from GSE126124 (26), with 209 expression z-scored within each cohort. This external test cohort included participants aged 8 to 210 18 years, and the SC population consisted of individuals with juvenile polyps, infectious colitis, 211 and functional intestinal disorders (two controls who were later diagnosed with CD were 212 excluded). Thresholds were Youden-optimal cutoffs from the internal out-of-fold predictions. 213

We report area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC-ROC) with 95% CIs 214 alongside area under the precision-recall curve (AUC-PR), sensitivity, specificity, positive and 215 negative predictive value (PPV, NPV), F1 score, balanced accuracy, and Brier score. RESULTS

Study participants

In total, 425 high-quality whole-blood samples were collected from untreated participants in our study, which comprised 112 CD and 313 DGBI participants (Table 1). The median age was 13 years, and 78% of our population was enrolled from NYU and 22% from CHOP. The median PCDAI in the CD group was 28, with 11 individuals classified as minimally-active, 59 as mild, and 42 as moderate-severe. The single largest subclassification in our DGBI group was IBS, which comprised 34% of the DGBI samples. Functional abdominal pain-not otherwise specified, functional dyspepsia, and functional constipation were the next most common classifications, comprising 28%, 18%, and 14%, respectively. Functional nausea and vomiting, nonretentive fecal incontinence, abdominal migraine, aerophagia, and rumination syndrome represented less than 6% of our DGBI population. CD participants were more often male than DGBI participants (P = 0.017), whereas secondhand smoke exposure did not differ across CD and DGBI groups. Both CRP (mg/L) and BMI percentiles were significantly different across groups, with medians of 13 vs 0 mg/L for CRP and 30th vs 58th percentile for BMI for CD and DGBI groups respectively (both P < 0.001). CRP was unable to be measured in 102 of our participants.

Whole-blood gene expression profiles separate CD from DGBI and capture within-CD disease activity

To assess transcriptomic differences between children newly diagnosed with CD and DGBI, we performed RNA sequencing of 425 high-quality whole-blood samples. The median library size was 16.9 million reads and the median STAR alignment rate was 89%. Pseudogenes, hemoglobin genes, and low-signal genes were filtered out, yielding 15,444 highly expressed genes for downstream analysis. Batch effects from covariates were assessed using PCA, where sequencing batch was the largest contributor of unwanted variance (Supplementary Figure S1).

We regressed out the covariate effects of sequencing batch, sex, secondhand smoke, recruitment site, and age and re-ran PCA on the batch-corrected normalized matrix. The batch-associated separation, specifically the sequencing batch split, was eliminated (Figure 1A). Separation between CD and DGBI samples increased following correction. PC1 and PC2 accounted for 53.4% and 16.1% of the variance, respectively, and revealed two distinct clusters primarily defined by diagnosis of CD (purple) or DGBI (orange). More specifically, the moderate-severe samples (pluses) clustered distinctly from DGBI samples (circles), whereas minimally-active CD samples (triangles) mostly overlapped with the DGBI group. Likewise, the mild CD samples (squares) tended to fall between these clusters. Altogether, this suggested a dual gradient between DGBI and CD samples, as well as from minimally-active CD to moderate-severe CD states.

Immune cell deconvolution and differential gene expression analyses reveal broad inflammatory program separating CD and DGBI samples and that intensifies with CD severity

To assess whether leukocyte composition differed between CD and DGBI samples, we utilized CIBERSORT to estimate the proportions of 22 immune-cell types. As shown in Figure 1B, several cell populations differed significantly between the groups based on a Wilcoxon test with an FDR < 0.05. CD samples exhibited higher proportions of monocytes, neutrophils, activated mast cells, plasma cells, activated dendritic cells, and M0 macrophages compared with DGBI samples, but lower proportions of CD8+ T cells, naïve CD4+ T cells, resting memory CD4+ T cells, activated memory CD4+ T cells, and both naïve and memory B cells compared with DGBI.

To further investigate transcriptional differences between CD and DGBI samples, we implemented two DEA models. The first comparison between CD and DGBI samples identified 1,095 DEGs, with 757 up-regulated and 338 down-regulated (Figure 1C, Supplementary Table S1). Prominently up-regulated genes in CD samples compared to DGBI samples included PSTPIP2, CD177, GYG1, TLR5, SLC26A8, CACNA1E, and MAPK14. The top downregulated genes in CD included CACNB4, LRRN3, NELL2, GZMK, and TCF7. The PCDAI model using only CD samples identified 634 total DEGs, with 365 upregulated and 269 downregulated (Figure 1D, Supplementary Table S2). PCDAI-associated upregulated DEGs included CD177, MAPK14, MAP3K20, ADAM9, and TRPM2, with down-regulated genes KLRC3, LOC101929698, LINC02915, and AK5. Several genes like MAPK14 and CD177 were ranked in the top 10 of both models, which motivated an investigation into their overlapping signature.

Comparing DEG sets of CD-vs-DGBI and within-CD differential expression models revealed 377 overlapping genes, while 718 and 257 genes were unique to the CD diagnosis and within-CD models, respectively (Figure 1E). All 377 overlapping DEGs exhibited directionally concordant gene coefficient effect sizes, indicating that the molecular signatures distinguishing CD from DGBI intensified in alignment with CD severity (Supplementary Table S3). A hierarchical clustering heatmap of the 377 overlapping DEGs (Figure 1F) showed clear CD/DGBI separation with gradients that aligned to PCDAI groups. Like the PCA plot, most CD samples that were clustered with the DGBI samples were minimally-active and mild participants, whereas the divergent cluster of CD samples was largely comprised of the moderate-severe and mild samples. Pathway enrichment using KEGG and GOBP of the overlapping DEGs (Figure 1G) highlighted inflammatory programs and response to microbes. Specifically, response to bacterium, phagocytosis, regulation of inflammatory process, Transcriptional misregulation in cancer, humoral immune response, Cornified envelope formation, Starch and sucrose metabolism, response to fungus, and innate immune response in mucosa were the top unique pathways enriched (full list of enriched pathways is in Supplementary Table S4). Our results suggest that the antimicrobial and immune system responses underlying disease activity in CD are also shared with those that differentiate CD from DGBI.

WGCNA identifies CD-associated co-expression modules

To contextualize the results from our differential expression models, we assessed the coexpression programs related to CD diagnosis and PCDAI severity by constructing a signed coexpression network with WGCNA. The scale-free topology fit increased monotonically and exceeded 0.80 for soft-threshold power = 8, while mean connectivity declined as expected (Supplementary Figure S2A, S2B). Thus, power = 8 was used for WGCNA module construction, which identified a total of 16 modules, plus the unassigned grey module. The eigengene dendrogram/heatmap showed blocks of strongly co-varying modules (Supplementary Figure S2C). Positively co-regulated modules clustered together, while anticorrelated modules segregated to opposing branches, suggesting distinct biological groupings. Module sizes spanned a broad range (Supplementary Figure S2D). The unassigned grey module contained 3,400 genes.

Among colored modules, turquoise (2,645) was largest, followed by blue (2,287), brown (1,603), yellow (1,510), green (1,117), red (901), and black (688). Smaller modules comprised pink (224), magenta (184), purple (174), greenyellow (145), tan (144), salmon (133), cyan (127), midnightblue (83), and lightcyan (79).

Differential module expression analysis and integration with differentially expressed genes

To assess how the co-expression modules related to disease diagnosis and severity, we correlated MEs with DGBI, minimally-active CD, mild CD, or moderate-severe CD groupings (Figure 2A). The most significant positive correlations for diagnosis were blue (r = 0.48, P < 0.001), black (r = 0.36, P < 0.001), cyan (r = 0.36, P < 0.001), and red (r = 0.30, P < 0.001).

Weaker positive correlations (r < 0.30) were observed in the midnightblue (r = 0.24, P < 0.001) and green (r = 0.14, P < 0.001) modules. Positive correlation indicated that each module's respective gene sets are generally upregulated in CD-vs-DGBI samples. The most significant negative associations were turquoise (r = -0.52, P < 0.001), salmon (r = -0.37, P < 0.001), tan (r = -0.32, P < 0.001), and greenyellow (r = -0.30, P < 0.001), indicating the genes in these modules were downregulated as a whole in CD versus DGBI. Weaker negative correlations were observed in the magenta (r = -0.29, P < 0.001), brown (r = -0.29, P < 0.001), yellow (r = -0.24, P < 0.001), and lightcyan (r = -0.24, P < 0.001) modules. Accordingly, the strength of module-trait correlations decreased progressively from the moderate-severe CD subgroup to the mild and minimally-active CD subgroups, reflecting a gradient in transcriptional association of each coexpression module's set of genes with CD disease severity (Supplementary Figure S2E).

To directly evaluate DCMs, we assessed the overlap of our DEGs from our differential expression results with each module's gene sets and recorded the proportion of DEGs in each module. We observed the highest overlap in the blue, red, magenta, turquoise, and cyan modules, which we deemed the DCMs (Figure 2B, 2D, 2F, 2H, 2J respectively). The greenyellow, purple, black, brown, green, salmon, yellow, tan, pink, and lightcyan modules had less than 5% overlap with the CD-vs-DGBI and within CD DEG sets, so these remaining modules were discarded from downstream interaction analyses. Additionally, the midnightblue module was dropped from downstream network analysis as it was predominantly comprised of immunoglobulin variableregion genes not represented in the STRING database.

PPI network analysis of differentially co-expressed modules

To resolve our 5 DCMs into interaction networks, we mapped the genes in each module to STRING and retained high-confidence interactions to construct module-specific PPI networks.

Mapping rates ranged from ~80% to 95% across modules since STRING only contains proteincoding gene interactions (Supplementary Table S5).

Across the 5 DCM networks, the LCC varied in size, from 39% of nodes in magenta to 87% in blue, with the remainder of nodes forming singletons and small disconnected components (Supplementary Table S5). Each LCC nonetheless contained >98% of the edges in its full network, indicating that each module's interactions were concentrated within a single core component (Figure 2C, 2E, 2G, 2I, 2K). Because the LCC captures this densely interacting core, we restricted downstream analysis to the LCC and dropped disconnected nodes. LCC topology differed across modules (Supplementary Table S5). Clustering coefficients were highest in cyan (0.75) and magenta (0.66) and lowest in blue (0.24) and turquoise (0.36), while red exhibited moderate clustering (0.52). Modularity showed the inverse pattern, highest in turquoise (0.69), red (0.63), and blue (0.50) and lowest in cyan (0.11) and magenta (0.17). Thus, the larger blue, red, and turquoise LCCs resolved into distinct sub-blocks with high modularity and lower global clustering, while the smaller cyan and magenta LCCs behaved as denser cohesive clusters with less sub-clustering.

Module-specific classifiers effectively discriminate CD and DGBI samples

We hypothesized that a subset of genes from each co-expression module's LCC could be used to classify CD from DGBI samples. To test this, we built a 2-stage pipeline (Supplementary Figure S7) composed of (1) identifying the most diagnostic genes in each module; (2) distilling a final panel across each module's diagnostic subsets. For Stage 1, the candidate classifier genes for each module were 328 DEGs for blue, 158 for red, 96 for turquoise, 28 for magenta, and 21 for cyan. We employed LASSO to select a subset of each module's candidates, and then LASSO, RF, and SVM models were trained on those selected genes. We refer to these as the locked module panels.

Locked panels were substantially reduced by LASSO, comprising 9 of 328 candidate DEGs for blue, 9 of 158 for red, 14 of 96 for turquoise, 15 of 28 for magenta, and 5 of 21 for cyan, for a total of 52 DEGs across the modules retained (Supplementary Table S8). Positive coefficients indicate higher odds of CD, and negative coefficients indicate lower odds. All 9 blue coefficients were positive, led by PSTPIP2 (0.479). All 14 turquoise coefficients were negative, led by CACNB4 (-0.669). Red had 8 positive coefficients and 1 negative coefficient (RNF152, -0.325) and was led by VCL (0.475). Magenta was mixed, with 9 positive and 6 negative coefficients, and was led by GZMK (-0.869). Among the 5 cyan genes, CD274 had the largest coefficient (1.072), and LAMP3 (-0.736) was the only negative coefficient.

All 5 modules discriminated CD from DGBI (Supplementary Table S8). Turquoise performed best, with CV AUCs of up to 0.915 and test AUCs up to 0.955 across the 3 classifiers.

Blue followed, with CV AUC up to 0.895 and test AUCs up to 0.914. Red reached CV AUCs of 0.886 and test AUCs of 0.897. Cyan had the lowest CV AUCs (up to 0.852), but its test AUCs reached 0.930 and were exceeded only by those of turquoise. Most magenta candidates were differentially expressed only in the within-CD severity comparison (23 of 28) and had CV AUCs up to 0.891. Its test AUCs were the lowest of the 5 modules and varied the most across classifiers, from 0.803 for RF to 0.869 for LASSO.

Stable-core genes, defined as those selected in at least 900 of 1,000 Stage 1 bootstrap resamples, were few relative to the number of candidates (Supplementary Table S8). Blue and turquoise each contributed a single gene, PSTPIP2 (stability = 0.902) from 328 blue candidates and CACNB4 (stability = 1) from 96 turquoise candidates. Cyan also contributed a single gene, CD274, with stability = 1. There were 4 genes contributed by both red (RNF152, CHPT1, RAP1GAP, and CLIC4) and magenta (GZMK, KLRK1, MATK, and KLRC1). All 11 stable-core genes formed the candidate pool for Stage 2. We found network centrality did not reliably predict reproducibility across bootstrapped models. KLRD1, the top-ranked hub by composite centrality in magenta, was selected in 631 of 1,000 resamples. In contrast, MATK, which was ranked 53rd out of 61 nodes in terms of composite centrality, was selected in 985 resamples. All 4 red stable-core genes ranked 415th or lower out of 660 nodes, and PSTPIP2, the only blue stable-core gene, ranked 949th out of 1,763.

Combined cross-module panel construction and evaluation

To favor sparsity for the final cross-module panel, we recalculated stability frequencies of the 11 module stable genes across another 1,000 bootstraps. Stabilities now ranged from 0.058 to 0.978 (Supplementary Table S9). Four genes exceeded the Stage 2 threshold of 0.85 and formed the final panel. These were PSTPIP2 from blue (stability = 0.978), CACNB4 from turquoise (stability = 0.934), GZMK from magenta (stability = 0.896), and CLIC4 from red (stability = 0.871), so 4 of the 5 modules were represented. The next-ranked candidates were both from the red module, CHPT1 (0.776) and RNF152 (0.744). No cyan gene was retained, because CD274, the only cyan stable-core gene, had a pooled stability of just 0.317 during stage 2 bootstrapping. The remaining magenta stable genes were also infrequently selected across bootstraps. This included KLRK1, which had been selected in 993 of 1,000 resamples at the module level (stage 1), but only 58 times in the cross-module (stage 2) bootstraps.

In the final-panel LASSO model, fitted with λ = 0.0565 and an intercept of -1.276, higher expression of PSTPIP2 (coefficient 0.637) and CLIC4 (0.415) increased the odds of CD, whereas higher expression of CACNB4 (-0.423) and GZMK (-0.308) decreased them (Supplementary Table S10).

In CV on the training partition of 297 samples, SVM achieved an AUC-ROC of 0.918, LASSO 0.909, and RF 0.901 as shown in Figure 5A and Table 2. AUC-PR ranged from 0.815 to 0.849, sensitivity from 0.769 to 0.795, specificity from 0.922 to 0.941, PPV from 0.785 to 0.822, and NPV from 0.920 to 0.927. On the held-out test partition of 128 samples, AUC-ROC was 0.962 for SVM, 0.961 for LASSO, and 0.927 for RF (Figure 5B). AUC-PR ranged from 0.900 to 0.926, sensitivity from 0.735 to 0.794, specificity from 0.957 to 0.968, PPV from 0.871 to 0.897, and NPV from 0.910 to 0.928.

All 4 panel genes were present on the external Affymetrix array from GSE126124. SVM and LASSO achieved the highest external AUC-ROCs, 0.932 and 0.931, respectively, while RF reached 0.879 (Figure 5C). AUC-PR ranged from 0.860 to 0.884. At the internally derived thresholds, specificity ranged from 0.919 to 0.946 and PPV from 0.903 to 0.933. Sensitivity was 0.718 for all 3 models, and NPV ranged from 0.756 to 0.761. RF had the highest external balanced accuracy, 0.832 (Table 2).

In our cohort, PSTPIP2 and CLIC4 expression were higher in CD compared to DGBI, whereas CACNB4 and GZMK expression were lower (Figure 5D). More specifically, the PCDAI groups revealed that expression within CD varied based on disease activity, particularly within the GZMK gene, which consistently decreased across the minimally-active to moderate-severe strata. CLIC4, CACNB4, and PSTPIP2 showed little difference across minimally-active and mild groups, with more separation evident in the moderate-severe subgroup. In the external cohort, all 4 genes differed between CD and SCs in the same direction as in the internal cohort (Figure 5E).

Single gene predictors, leave-one-out analysis, and misclassification rates

PSTPIP2 had the highest single predictor performance with a univariate CV AUC of 0.891. The other 3 genes performed less well alone (0.806 to 0.838), but still strongly. Removing any single gene from the 4-gene panel lowered the AUC-ROC in every model and partition.

During CV, the reduction was no greater than 0.029. On the test partition, it was no greater than 0.046, except for GZMK in the SVM model, whose removal reduced the test AUC-ROC by 0.139, to 0.823. Additionally, the PPV was more sensitive to gene removal. It decreased by up to 0.114 in CV after removal of PSTPIP2 from SVM, and by up to 0.210 on the test partition after removal of CACNB4 from RF (Supplementary Table S11). By majority vote across the 3 classifiers, CV misclassification was concentrated in samples with mild CD (15 of 41, 36.6%), compared with 12.5% in minimally-active CD, 3.4% in moderate-severe CD, and 6.8% in DGBI.

The held-out test partition showed a similar pattern, with minimally-active and mild CD exhibiting the highest misclassification rates, with 66.7% (2 of 3) and 22.2% (4 of 18), respectively (Supplementary Table S12).

DISCUSSION

In this prospective two-center cohort of 425 treatment-naïve children and young adults, whole-blood transcriptomes separated newly diagnosed CD samples from Rome IV-defined DGBI samples. The program that separated them intensified with disease severity, where all 377 genes shared by the diagnostic and PCDAI models changed in the same direction, and minimally-active CD was most transcriptionally similar to DGBI. A 4-gene panel drawn from 4 co-expression networks (PSTPIP2, CLIC4, CACNB4, and GZMK) discriminated CD from DGBI with an AUC of up to 0.96 in held-out samples, and up to 0.93 in an independent pediatric cohort composed of symptomatic controls and CD samples.

Prior whole-blood studies have largely compared IBD with healthy or nongastrointestinal controls, included treated patients, or were small. In the largest of these, which included children and adults with established IBD (most of whom had received prior therapy), discrimination was high for active pediatric IBD but weak for inactive disease (24). The most comparable study is that of Palmer et al, who prospectively sampled children at diagnostic colonoscopy, including 39 with CD and 39 non-IBD SCs with infectious colitis, juvenile polyps, or functional disorders (26). Their 6-gene blood model reached 80% accuracy (PPV 0.84, NPV 0.72) in an external set of peripheral blood mononuclear cells from healthy adults and adults with treated IBD. Our cohort is more than five times larger and restricts the comparison to CD versus Rome IV-defined DGBI. We used the Palmer et al cohort as our external test set due to its similar pediatric population and usage of whole-blood gene expression, after excluding 2 controls who were later diagnosed with CD. Our panel reached an AUC of up to 0.93, a PPV of up to 0.93, NPV of up to 0.76, and balanced accuracy of up to 0.83. Differences in platform, test population, and prevalence preclude a direct comparison, and we did not evaluate their model in our data. The two panels share no genes, yet all 6 of their panel genes fell within our CDassociated modules. S100A8, SLC2A3, and TXNIP were in the neutrophil (blue) module, where all 3 were indirectly connected to PSTPIP2 via IL1RN. IL7R was assigned to the housekeeping and T-cell receptor (turquoise) module and was indirectly connected with CACNB4 by 3 edges. UBB and ALAS2 were grouped in the hemostasis/platelet (red) module, but each gene was 3 edges away from CLIC4 and shared no direct neighbors. Taken together, both Palmer's and our diagnostic panels appear to represent the same neutrophil, lymphoid, and hemostatic pathways indexed through different genes.

The concordance between the diagnostic and severity signatures suggests that CD at presentation differs from DGBI mainly in the magnitude of a systemic inflammatory response.

CD samples had higher estimated neutrophil and monocyte fractions and lower lymphocyte fractions. The largest up-regulated network (blue) was significantly associated with neutrophil degranulation, the most reproducible blood pathway across IBD studies (15, 26). The red module added a platelet-activation signal, consistent with the hypercoagulability that peaks around diagnosis in active pediatric IBD (35). The magenta module behaved differently than the other modules, as it was the only one dominated by within-CD DEGs. Specifically, 23 of its 28 DEGs (mainly GZM-and KLR-family genes) declined with increasing PCDAI and were not differentially expressed between CD and DGBI samples. This suggests that loss of circulating effector lymphocytes tracks with severity once CD is established. GZMK was an exception, as it was the only magenta gene differentially expressed between CD and DGBI without being associated with the PCDAI, yet was still retained in the final panel. Within the blue, red, turquoise, and magenta networks, stochastic block modeling showed that DEGs were concentrated in discrete, interconnected sub-blocks rather than dispersed across the network. This suggests that CD perturbs specific subnetworks within each program rather than the program as a whole.

A notable finding was that sampling several pathways matched or improved predictions compared with sampling one pathway in depth. The blue and red modules had coherent functional identities, Neutrophil degranulation and Hemostasis, and the strongest differential expression signals. Yet neither module's own locked panel (9 genes each, with test AUC up to 0.91 and 0.90) matched the 4-gene cross-module panel with test AUC of up to 0.96. Moreover, the genes within a single program (ie, Neutrophil degranulation) were highly collinear, which was reflected by LASSO rotating among blue-module genes across bootstraps. Additionally, the PSTPIP2 model alone reached CV AUC of 0.89, while the full blue module only increased this by less than 0.01 CV AUC with 8 more genes. This pattern suggested that adding more genes does not necessarily add more information. Proxies for separate programs, by contrast, contribute complementary signal. The same logic may explain why turquoise, whose network lacked one dominant biological process, produced the best single-module panel (test AUC up to 0.95) which the 4-gene cross-module panel matched with less than a third as many genes. The final panel reflects this breadth. PSTPIP2 is a known myeloid adaptor that restrains neutrophil IL-1β and NADPH oxidase activity (36). The hemostasis (red) module contributed CLIC4, which is a lipopolysaccharide-inducible intracellular chloride channel that promotes macrophage inflammatory cytokine production (37). GZMK and CACNB4 both index the downregulated lymphocyte program, where GZMK+ CD8+ T cells were shown to accumulate in inflamed CD mucosa (38). Removing any single gene reduced AUC by no more than 0.03 during CV and 0.05 during testing, except for GZMK in the SVM model (0.14 drop in AUC). Our panel should therefore be interpreted as a set of pathway proxies rather than as candidate mechanisms. The panel's operating characteristics favor a rule-in role due to its high specificity. In the external cohort, specificity reached 0.95 but sensitivity did not exceed 0.72, and misclassified CD samples were concentrated in mild and minimally-active disease. A positive result could therefore justify expedited endoscopy, whereas a negative result could not exclude mild CD.

Thresholds derived on RNA-seq will need recalibration on a targeted assay, and the panel's value beyond fecal calprotectin and CRP remains to be established. This study has limitations as well.

Candidate genes were derived from all 425 samples from WGCNA and before the training/test split, so held-out test estimates are likely optimistic. The external cohort, which played no role in gene selection, provides the least biased estimate. The STRING database restricted our network analysis to protein-coding genes, so noncoding and poorly annotated genes were lost from every module, including those that were differentially expressed. PCDAI is weighted toward symptoms that partly overlap with DGBI, and endoscopic activity was not modeled. Additionally, only 11 patients had minimally-active CD, reducing power to detect differences in the mildest of CD cases. CRP was not collected systematically and was missing in 102 participants, so we did not benchmark our panel against it. Because participants with ulcerative colitis and celiac disease were excluded during study recruitment, the panel's performance against other inflammatory conditions, other than the small number of infectious colitis SCs in the external cohort, remains untested. Future work should test the panel against fecal calprotectin and CRP in a referral population that includes other inflammatory diagnoses, using a targeted qPCR assay for PSTPIP2, CLIC4, CACNB4, and GZMK. If its performance is confirmed, this panel would be suitable for a low-cost, point-of-care blood test. The findings presented in this study should be followed up using paired biopsies with endoscopic scoring, single-cell profiling, and longitudinal sampling to determine the panel's prognostic value.
