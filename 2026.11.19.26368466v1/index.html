Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. Abstract

Objective: To test whether a smart contract that admits hospital-trained models to a shared mortality model only after peer hospitals verify their performance can exclude poisoned or substituted models, and at what cost. Design: Retrospective simulation study of a federated learning protocol. Methodology: In the Blockchain-Enabled Federated Ensemble (BFE), hospitals committed SHA-256 digests of locally trained XGBoost and random-forest models to a Solidity contract; peers posted digest-bound validation AUROCs, and the contract admitted models with a median peer AUROC of at least 0.70. Admitted models were pooled by weighted logit averaging (BFE-PW) or stacking (BFE-Stack) with federated recalibration. Code and drafts were produced with an AI coding assistant under the authors' supervision. Setting: Four hospitals simulated from one academic centre with age, renal case-mix and laboratory-availability skew; a data-scarce regime keeping 5-20% of each hospital's training and validation patients; and a natural partition by care era. Participants: 425,215 adult admissions (186,103 patients; 2.35% in-hospital mortality) in MIMIC-IV v3.1, with predictors from the first 24 hours. Interventions: BFE against centralised, local-only, FedAvg, FedProx, median and Multi-Krum FedAvg and federated logistic regression, under seven attacks on training, attestation, recalibration and model delivery. Main outcome measures: AUROC and AUPRC (joint patient-clustered bootstrap), calibration, attack effects, gas and transactions. Results: Under gross label flipping, FedAvg lost 29% of its AUPRC while the contract excluded the poisoned models in every seed (Multi-Krum FedAvg was equally protected); all substituted models were detected, and collusion blocked release. 1. Introduction

Federated learning (FL) lets hospitals build a shared prediction model without pooling patient records, which dataprotection law such as HIPAA and the GDPR restricts [1] [2] [3] [4], and it can approach centralised accuracy for clinical outcomes [5] [6] [7] [8]. Two obstacles remain for the tabular electronic health record (EHR) data behind most hospital risk models. First, gradient-boosted and random-forest ensembles often outperform deep networks on tabular data [9, 10], yet they have no shared parameter space and cannot be combined by FedAvg-style averaging. Second, a consortium of independent hospitals needs a verifiable record of which contributed models entered the shared model, on what evidence, and whether the deployed artefact is the one that was validated. Without it, one poisoned or swapped contribution can degrade the shared model unnoticed [11, 12], and robust aggregation rules designed for neural-network updates protect only partly [13, 14]. Hospitals also differ in patient age, case mix and the laboratory tests they order, so their data are not independent and identically distributed (non-IID). Under such skew, iterative parameter averaging can drift and converge slowly [15] [16] [17] [18], and the clinical remedies proposed so far [17, [19] [20] [21] assume a gradient-trained model. Tree ensembles can be federated without averaging, by lossless federated boosting, which exchanges encrypted gradient statistics at every split [22], or by one-shot ensembling of locally trained models [23] [24] [25].

We therefore ask whether peer-attested admission of whole, locally trained tree ensembles, with every attestation and decision recorded on a smart contract, can give a consortium that verifiable record at a small cost in accuracy, and when collaboration through such a protocol pays off.

Ledger-based clinical FL already records updates, consent and site-level misconduct for neural or linear models [26] [27] [28] [29] [30] [31] (Section 2). We found no clinical study in which a contract admits ensemble members by a published rule over hash-bound peer validation scores, and none that tested postvalidation artefact substitution and collusion separately from training-time poisoning. The Blockchain-Enabled Federated Ensemble (BFE) studied here federates tree ensembles in one exchange and decides admission per committed artefact, and any party can recompute each decision from on-chain attestations.

We present BFE as a governance protocol for federating tree ensembles, and evaluate it in a simulation study on 425,215 adult admissions from MIMIC-IV v3.1 [32, 33]. Our contributions are: 1. A governance protocol for one-shot federation of tree ensembles. Hospitals commit SHA-256 digests of their XGBoost and random-forest members to a smart contract. Peers post validation AUROCs (area under the receiver operating characteristic curve) that must name the committed digest, and the contract admits a member whose median peer AUROC is at least 0.70. The admitted set and weights can be recomputed from the ledger, so a deviating aggregator is detectable, though not prevented; recalibration and stacking coefficients are committed only by digest. The protocol extends one-shot ensembling [23, 24] by giving member selection to a contract that applies a published rule to peer evidence. 2. Accuracy and calibration in simulated consortia.

Four hospitals were simulated by rule-based age, renal and laboratory-availability skew, with an IID control, and MIMIC-IV care eras (anchor-year groups 2008-10, 2011-13, 2014-16, 2017-22) gave a second, nonsimulated partition. With large hospitals BFE matched FedAvg or FedProx in AUROC but stayed below localonly and centralised XGBoost, and when each hospital kept only 5% of its patients it exceeded local models, as FedAvg did. Comparators include federated logistic regression; the primary inference is a joint patient-clustered bootstrap. 3. A threat evaluation that separates attack surfaces: full, partial and targeted label flips, poisoned recalibration and threshold steps, false attestation, post-validation artefact substitution and collusion by half of the consortium, against an oracle ensemble and robust FedAvg [13, 14].

It shows which surfaces the contract closes (gross poisoning by the gate, substitution by the digest check), where it fails closed (collusion: no model is released) and which it leaves open (targeted and recalibration poisoning): a gate certifies average discrimination, not subgroup safety or calibration. 4. Site-level calibration repair and ledger cost. A local site-specific intercept, which shares no data, corrected two-fold under-prediction at the hospital with a restricted laboratory menu (observed-to-expected ratio, O/E, 2.13 to 0.93), whereas a robust median-of-sites recalibration did not. Gas and latency are reported for 4 to 32 hospitals.

2. Related Work

>Related Work

2.1 Mortality prediction on tabular EHR

MIMIC-III [34], MIMIC-IV [32] and the multi-centre eICU database [35] made in-hospital and intensive care unit (ICU) mortality a standard prediction task, and EHR-based models exceed conventional severity scores [36] [37] [38]. On tabular features, tree ensembles often outperform deep networks [9, 10]. Performance falls when models are transported between units or institutions [39], and reporting guidance asks for discrimination, calibration and clinical utility [40, 41].

2.2 Federated learning under clinical heterogeneity

Clinical FL has ranged from sparse linear models [42] and patient clustering on eICU [43] to FedAvg-style mortality and oxygen-requirement prediction across hospitals [6, 44]. Fe-dAvg's convergence worsens with heterogeneity [15, 16], and remedies include proximal terms [17] and re-weighting or decoupling schemes [19] [20] [21]. Across real sites, heterogene-ity arises from demographics, medication use and site practice [45], and federated XGBoost across five ICU databases trailed centralised training [46, 47]. Reviews find decentralised models comparable to centralised ones in AUROC and better than local models [8, 48], but hospitals with large datasets gain little [49, 50].

2.3 Federating tree models and one-shot ensembles

Lossless federated boosting exchanges encrypted gradient statistics at every split [22], SimFL cuts that cost with locality-sensitive hashing [51], and FedXGBllr sends local ensembles once and learns their combination [52]. One-shot FL ensembles locally trained models after a single round, for example admitting a model that reaches a baseline AUROC on its own site's data [23]; FENS adds a federatedly trained aggregator [24], the analogue of BFE-Stack. Federated random forests improved on local models [53], helped small hospitals most [49] and were evaluated across eleven Austrian hospitals [25]. On MIMIC-IV split into five non-IID sites, inference-time ensembling recovered less area under the precision-recall curve (AUPRC) than FedAvg [54]. None of these studies screens contributions against adversaries; BFE keeps the one-shot structure and adds peer-attested admission executed by a contract.

2.4 Poisoning defences and verifiable contribution screening

Byzantine-robust aggregation bounds the influence of outlying updates by Krum and Multi-Krum [13], the coordinatewise median and trimmed mean [14] or a server-held root dataset [55], and ensembles of FL models give certified robustness to a few malicious clients [56]. On ledgers, committees validate updates by Multi-Krum, consensus or voting [57] [58] [59]. In health, Kalapaaking et al. verified models by secure multi-party computation before blockchain aggregation [60], FedSecure-Chain logged client trust on a ledger after anomaly detection and robust aggregation [61], and blockchain FL for retinal imaging resisted label flipping [62]. Kuo and colleagues screened submitted clinical models with auditing, coefficient and performance detectors [28] and, on a blockchain network, quarantined sites only when a misbehaviour budget ran out, to tolerate false positives [29]. These schemes screen updates or sites, using detectors, a committee or the server.

2.5 Blockchain for clinical model governance

Ledgers were proposed early for biomedical data provenance [63]. ModelChain proposed privacy-preserving predictive modelling on a private blockchain [64], GloreChain fitted logistic regression on one [65], and QuorumChain and We-bQuorumChain used smart contracts and a proof-of-quorum protocol to keep clinical model learning going when sites drop out [30, 31]. FedEnTrust used smart contracts for registration and role-based access over a distilled federated ensemble for diabetes prediction [66], and BlockFedMed governed consent, integrity and incentives for ICU mortality prediction [27]. Outside medicine, DFedForest built random forests from decision trees shared through a blockchain [67].

Reviews describe a growing field [68], yet a systematic review found only 11 implemented and quantitatively evaluated biomedical blockchain systems [69]. Table 1 compares the closest designs.

3. Materials and Methods

>Materials and Methods

3.1 Data source and cohort

We used the hospital (hosp) module of MIMIC-IV v3.1 [32, 33, 70], de-identified records of patients admitted to Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center (Boston, MA) in 2008-2022, accessed through PhysioNet under its credentialed data-use agreement; no further institutional review was required. We included admissions of adults (≥18 years) who stayed at least 24 h and were alive at 24 h, so that every prediction is made at a 24 h landmark. The outcome was in-hospital death. Of 546,028 admissions, 120,812 were excluded for stays shorter than 24 h and one for death within 24 h (Supplementary Fig. S1). The cohort comprised 425,215 admissions of 186,103 patients, with 9,984 in-hospital deaths (2.35%); patients contributed a mean of 2.3 admissions. Predictors. Predictors were restricted to information available at 24 h (Supplementary Table S1; 148 columns after one-hot encoding with a consortium-agreed vocabulary): demographics and admission context, utilisation history, twelve Charlson-type comorbidities [71] from previous admissions only (index-admission diagnoses are assigned after discharge), and the minimum, maximum and mean of 29 laboratory tests from 6 h before to 24 h after admission. Missingness was treated as informative. Two extended predictor sets were used in a sensitivity analysis (Supplementary Methods S-M1).

3.2 Partitions into hospitals Simulated hospitals (primary).

Patients (with all their admissions) were partitioned into four virtual hospitals with three forms of skew: • Covariate skew.

Hospital A, a geriatric referral centre, receives patients aged ≥70 years at first eligible admission.

• Case-mix skew. Hospital B, a renal centre, receives the remaining patients with kidney disease (creatinine ≥2.0 mg/dL in any admission, or a chronic kidney disease diagnosis).

• Feature-availability skew. The remaining patients are allocated 40:60 to Hospital C, a community hospital without lactate, blood-gas, troponin, albumin, liver, bilirubin, differential-count or coagulation panels and with 30% of other results deleted at random, and Hospital D, a general hospital.

Each patient follows the rule with probability p and is otherwise assigned at random, so p = 0 is an IID partition. The primary analysis used p = 0.85; sensitivity analyses used p = 0 (five seeds) and p ∈ {0.5, 1.0} (three seeds). Within each hospital, patients were split 70/10/20 into training, validation and test sets; no patient appeared in more than one hospital or split. Hospital C's missingness applied to every method, including centralised baselines.

Care-era partition (natural).

Each "hospital" was one MIMIC-IV anchor-year group (A: 2008-2010; B: 2011-2013; C: 2014-2016; D: 2017-2022). The eras differ in case mix, mortality (1.9% to 3.6%) and diagnosis coding (ICD-9 to ICD-10 in 2015); no missingness was added (five seeds).

3.3 Local ensemble members

Each hospital trains two members on its own data: XGBoost [72] (early stopping on the hospital's validation AUPRC) and a random forest [73], with hyper-parameters fixed in advance (Supplementary Methods S-M4). Each serialised member m is identified by its digest h m = SHA-256(bytes m ).

3.4 Ledger and admission rule

The audit layer is a Solidity contract, FederatedAudit-Registry, written for a permissioned Ethereum consortium and run here on a local single-process Ethereum Virtual Machine [74] (eth-tester), without consensus or network latency. A deployment would use a permissioned EVM network (for example Hyperledger Besu with Quorum Byzantine Fault Tolerance (QBFT) consensus) with one validator per hospital; such consensus tolerates f faulty validators when n ≥ 3f + 1, so four hospitals tolerate one, matching the gate's tolerance of one false score, but it protects the record, not the truth of a score. A governor (the data-governance board) registers hospital nodes; an aggregator opens and finalises rounds and commits the global model; hospital nodes submit models and attest to peers' models. Only digests, sample counts and integer scores are stored on-chain. Fig. 1 summarises the data flow, roles and trust boundaries, and Supplementary Fig. S2 the software architecture. A round proceeds in four steps (functions in Supplementary Methods S-M2). (i) Each hospital commits the digest of each member, a digest of its training manifest (in this prototype the model family and hospital; a deployment would add the feature schema and hyper-parameters) and its trainingset size. The contract accepts one submission per address per round, so each aggregation opens one round per model family; with four hospitals and eight members this takes 37 transactions. (ii) Each peer obtains the artefact off-chain, for example from a content-addressed store, checks its digest, evaluates it on its own validation split and posts the AUROC on-chain; the contract rejects the attestation if the digest the validator reports differs from the committed one, if the validator is the owner, or if it has already attested that model. (iii) At finalisation the contract computes the median peer AUROC S m of every member and admits the member if it has at least one attestation and S m ≥ τ. (iv) The aggregator commits the digest of the global specification. A view function verifyModel lets the aggregator and each hospital check that a delivered artefact matches an admitted commitment. In the simulation peers scored members in memory, so the delivery check was exercised through verifyModel (attack A3).

The threshold τ = 0.70, a conventional lower bound for acceptable discrimination [75], was fixed before the experiments. When all K -1 = 3 peer scores are present, their median tolerates one false score per model under an honest majority; the contract does not enforce such a quorum, but every model received all three attestations before finalisation. τ is set by the aggregator when it opens a round and is recorded on-chain; it was 0.70 in every round. The contract cannot verify that a validator actually ran the artefact or that the reported AUROC is correct; scores are signed, non-repudiable self-reports. commitGlobal does not check the specification against the admission decisions; the admitted set and weights are checked off-chain against the ledger, but recalibration and stacking coefficients only by refitting on validation data.

Recalibration variants.

Two further variants were evaluated. In robust recalibration, every hospital fits its own Platt pair (a k , b k ) on its validation split and shares only those two numbers; the consortium uses their coordinate-wise median, so no single hospital can move the calibration. In the sitespecific intercept, each hospital keeps the global model and shifts its intercept, pk (x) = σ (logit p(x) + c k ), with c k fitted on its own validation split; nothing is shared and the global model is unchanged.

Decision threshold.

One F1-maximising threshold per model was chosen from the hospitals' summed (TP, FP, FN) counts on a 400-point grid; the robust variant used the median of the hospitals' own thresholds and the site-specific variant each hospital's own.

3.6 Comparators

• Centralised XGBoost, random forest and multilayer perceptron (MLP), trained on the pooled training data (a privacy-violating upper bound). Centralised XGBoost also bounds the accuracy of lossless federated gradient boosting [22].

• Local-only XGBoost: each hospital applies its own model to its own patients.

• FedAvg-MLP [1] and FedProx-MLP (µ = 0.01) [17]: a two-hidden-layer perceptron trained for 40 rounds on federatedly standardised inputs with missingness indicators (Supplementary Methods S-M4).

• Byzantine-robust FedAvg: coordinate-wise median (FedMedian) [14] or Multi-Krum with one assumed attacker (FedKrum) [13]. With four hospitals the median equals the trimmed mean that drops one value at each end, so a separate trimmed-mean run (FedTrim) applies the same rule. Multi-Krum's guarantee needs at least 2f +3 = 5 clients, so with four it is a heuristic.

• Federated logistic regression (FedLR), fitted by federated Newton-Raphson.

• Uniform ensemble: the unweighted probability average of all eight tree members, without gate or recalibration.

• Oracle ensemble (attack scenarios): BFE with the attacker removed by hand, i.e. a perfect gate.

• BFE with MLP members (ablation), separating the aggregation rule from the model family.

3.7 Threat model and attack scenarios

The adversary is a registered hospital (D unless stated) that wants to degrade the shared model, globally or for a sub-group. Honest hospitals evaluate faithfully; the aggregator, governor and ledger are trusted in the experiments (a deviating aggregator is discussed in Section 5.2 but not tested), and privacy attacks are out of scope (Section 5.5).

• A1, label flipping. D trains its members on inverted outcome labels (under FedAvg, in every local round).

• A2, false attestation. As A1, and D also attests an AU-ROC of 0 for every peer model.

• A3, artefact substitution. D passes peer validation with an honest model but delivers a label-flipped model at aggregation.

• A4, collusion. Hospitals C and D both poison and smear their peers, breaking the honest-majority assumption; included to characterise the failure mode.

• A5, partial label flipping. D flips a random 20% or 40% of its training and validation labels, so its models stay informative and may pass the gate.

• A6, targeted label flipping. D relabels every death among its female patients as a survival, aiming to suppress risk for one subgroup while keeping overall discrimination.

• A7, recalibration and threshold poisoning. D's models are honest, but it computes its contributions to the federated Platt fit, the stacking fit and the threshold counts from inverted validation labels. These channels bypass the admission gate.

A tamper test applied 200 single-bit flips, 200 one-byte appends and 20 retrained substitutes to a small model.

3.8 Evaluation and statistical analysis

Discrimination was measured by AUROC and, given the 2.35% outcome rate, by AUPRC [77]. Calibration was assessed by the calibration intercept (calibration-in-the-large), calibration slope and integrated calibration index (ICI; mean absolute difference between predicted risk and lowesssmoothed observed risk) [78], pooled and per hospital, by the observed-to-expected (O/E) ratio, by the Brier score [41] and by the expected calibration error (ECE). Sensitivity and positive predictive value at the federated threshold, and decision curves [79], describe clinical use.

A seed re-draws the partition, splits and initialisation, so the SD across seeds reflects partition and training variability. Primary inference was a joint patient-clustered bootstrap: in each of 1,000 replicates, patients (with all their admissions) were resampled in each seed's test set, the betweenmethod difference was averaged over the five seeds, and the 95% percentile interval of that mean was reported. Planned comparisons were BFE-PW and BFE-Stack against FedAvg, FedProx, FedMedian, local-only and centralised XGBoost, for AUROC and AUPRC. As a secondary analysis, DeLong's test [80] compared BFE-PW per seed with FedAvg, FedProx, local-only and centralised XGBoost, the uniform ensemble and BFE-Stack, with Holm adjustment [81] over the 30 tests.

A subgroup audit covered sex, age band and race group (strata with ≥20 deaths). Ledger cost was measured as gas and latency per transaction for 4-32 hospitals. Software is listed in Supplementary Methods S-M3; reporting follows TRIPOD+AI [40].

4. Results

>Results

4.1 Cohort and induced heterogeneity

In the primary configuration (p = 0.85) the four simulated hospitals differed in size, case mix, mortality (1.36% at D to 3.83% at A), median age (53 years at C and D to 80 at A) and missing laboratory features (36.6% at B to 73.7% at C) (Table 2, Fig. 2 a-c); under the IID partition mortality ranged only from 2.32% to 2.38% (Supplementary Table S2). The pooled test set held on average 85,227 admissions and 1,956 deaths per seed.

The partition induced real distribution shift (Fig. 2 d): on Hospital C's sparsely measured patients, other sites' models reached AUROCs of 0.878-0.897 against 0.919 for C's own model.

4.2 Performance with large simulated hospitals

Table 3 summarises performance at p = 0.85 and Table 4 the primary inference. Centralised XGBoost reached the highest discrimination (AUROC 0.915, AUPRC 0.337), and with 71,000-131,000 admissions per hospital local-only XG-Boost was already strong (0.910). In the joint patientclustered bootstrap, BFE-PW's AUROC did not differ from FedAvg's (∆ +0.0001, 95% CI -0.0014 to 0.0018) and was lower than FedProx's, local-only XGBoost's and centralised XGBoost's; BFE-Stack was higher than FedAvg (∆ +0.0055, 95% CI 0.0042 to 0.0070), similar to FedProx and lower than centralised XGBoost. Both ensembles had lower AUPRC than FedAvg and FedProx (BFE-PW 0.301, BFE-Stack 0.309, FedAvg 0.315, FedProx 0.322; Table 4). The secondary per-seed DeLong analysis agreed (Supplementary Table S3).

With MLP members, BFE reached 0.894 against 0.902 for FedAvg with the same architecture, so one-round ensembling cost about 0.008 AUROC and tree members recovered it.

Pooled calibration was good for all federated methods (BFE-PW intercept -0.03, slope 1.01, ICI 0.002; Fig. 3 c), but it hid site-level errors. BFE-PW over-predicted deaths at Hospital B (O/E 0.64) and under-predicted them at Hospital C by about half (O/E 2.13); BFE-Stack (0.72 and 1.82), FedAvg (0.74 and 1.71) and FedProx (0.74 and 1.57) erred in the same direction, while centralised and local-only XG-Boost stayed within 0.89-1.09 at every hospital (Supplementary Table S4). With one consortium-wide threshold, BFE-PW therefore identified only 3.3% of deaths at Hospital C, against 27.5% for centralised XGBoost. Section 4.5 tests two corrections.

Per hospital (Table 5), BFE-PW matched each hospital's own model within 0.004 AUROC; at Hospital C, the ensembles and local XGBoost outperformed FedAvg (0.918 against 0.896), consistent with trees tolerating structurally missing panels.

4.3 Data-scarce hospitals and natural care-era sites

When each hospital kept only part of its patients, collaboration became useful (Table 6, Fig. 4). With 5% of the data (about 3,700 training admissions per hospital), local-only XGBoost fell to an AUROC of 0.844 ± 0.026, whereas BFE-PW reached 0.866 ± 0.005 and BFE-Stack 0.866 ± 0.007, similar to FedAvg (0.863) and FedProx (0.867); BFE-PW exceeded the local models in all five seeds (by 0.005-0.058). At 10% (about 7,400 admissions) the federated methods were 0.000-0.004 above local models, and at 20% (about 14,800 admissions) local models had caught up (0.890 against 0.887 for BFE-PW and 0.892 for FedProx). The gate admitted all eight honest members in every seed and regime. In AUPRC the ensembles stayed below FedAvg and FedProx in every data-scarce regime and with large simulated hospitals.

On the care-era partition, where sites differ by calendar period, case mix and diagnosis coding rather than by a rule, BFE-Stack reached an AUROC of 0.916 and BFE-PW 0.913, against 0.912 for FedAvg, 0.914 for FedProx, 0.911 for local-only and 0.918 for centralised XGBoost (Table 6). In the joint bootstrap (Supplementary Table S5), BFE-Stack exceeded FedAvg, FedProx and local-only XGBoost (by 0.0043, 0.0021 and 0.0050; all 95% CIs above zero) and was below centralised XGBoost; BFE-PW did not differ from FedAvg or local-only models. Local-only models no longer outperformed federation.

4.4 Poisoning, substitution and collusion

Table 7 and Fig. 5 report the attacks, with Hospital D as the adversary.

Gross label flipping (A1, A2).

FedAvg lost 29% of its AUPRC (0.315 to 0.224) and its AUROC fell to 0.868. Coordinate-wise median aggregation protected in part (AU-ROC 0.884, AUPRC 0.274; trimmed mean in a separate run 0.889 and 0.285), whereas Multi-Krum fully protected Fe-dAvg (0.901, 0.311 against 0.901, 0.314 without attack). The ungated uniform ensemble lost some discrimination (AU-ROC 0.900 to 0.885) and became badly miscalibrated (Brier 0.077 against 0.019). Under BFE, peers scored D's poisoned members at median AUROCs of 0.10-0.13; the contract rejected both members in every seed, admitted all six honest members, and BFE-PW was unchanged (AUROC 0.902, AUPRC 0.301), equal to the oracle ensemble that removes D by hand (0.902 and 0.301). False attestations of 0 by D (A2) lowered honest members' medians by at most 0.07 and changed no decision.

Partial flipping (A5).

When D flipped 20% of its deaths and survivals, its members still scored median peer AUROCs of 0.75-0.82 and were admitted in every seed. They did no measurable harm: BFE-PW (AUROC 0.902, AUPRC 0.300) and BFE-Stack (0.906, 0.307) equalled the oracle ensemble, because the poisoned members kept useful ranking and received lower weights. FedAvg lost 0.009 AUROC (0.892) and its calibration worsened (ECE 0.027 against 0.003). Targeted flipping (A6).

When D relabelled the deaths of its female patients as survivals, its XGBoost member scored median peer AUROCs of 0.71-0.73, just above the threshold, and its random forest 0.82-0.85; both were admitted in every seed. The attack lowered BFE-PW's pooled AUROC only from 0.902 to 0.896 (AUPRC 0.301 to 0.282) and left its AUROC in women almost unchanged (0.915 to 0.913), but at the shared threshold the sensitivity for deaths in women fell from 32.8% to 27.5% while that in men rose from 35.1% to 43.9% (Supplementary Table S7). FedAvg was affected similarly. BFE-Stack was more resistant (AUROC unchanged at 0.907; sensitivity in women 35.8% to 33.2%). A gate based on pooled discrimination therefore did not detect a targeted attack that shifted harm onto one group.

Recalibration poisoning (A7). When D's members were honest but D submitted flipped labels to the recalibration and threshold steps, which the gate does not inspect, the pooled Platt fit inverted the score (slope -0.27 to -0.31 in the five seeds): BFE-PW's AUROC fell to 0.098 and BFE-Stack's to 0.252, and BFE-PW flagged 54-60% of patients while finding 4.3% of deaths. Replacing the pooled fit with the median of the four sites' Platt coefficients removed the effect on discrimination (AUROC 0.902, AUPRC 0.301) and calibration (ECE 0.005), but D could still shift the median-of-sites threshold: sensitivity rose to 46.5% (35.7% without attack) and positive predictive value fell to 23.9% (34.6% for standard BFE-PW without attack).

Artefact substitution (A3).

All 10 substituted artefacts (two per seed) failed verifyModel and were excluded; without the digest check, the same ensemble would have deployed them and its AUROC would have fallen to 0.895 (AUPRC 0.287). In a separate tamper test, the ledger detected 200 of 200 single-bit flips, 200 of 200 one-byte appends and 20 of 20 retrained substitutes. These results confirm that the digest comparison works, and follow from SHA-256 collision resistance, not from a blockchain-specific property.

Collusion (A4).

With two of four hospitals poisoning and attesting zero for every honest model, no member reached the threshold and the contract released no global model in any seed. An ungoverned uniform ensemble in the same setting fell to an AUROC of 0.491. BFE therefore fails closed when its honest-majority assumption is broken.

4.5 Correcting site-level miscalibration

Letting each hospital fit its own intercept on its validation data, with no data shared, removed the site-level bias of BFE-PW (Supplementary Table S4): O/E moved from 2.13 to 0.93 at Hospital C, from 0.64 to 0.98 at Hospital B, and from 1.05 and 0.95 to 0.99 and 1.00 at A and D; the share of deaths identified at Hospital C rose from 3.3% to 35.5%. Per-site AU-ROCs were unchanged, and because the corrected risks are comparable across hospitals the pooled AUROC rose from 0.902 to 0.908 (AUPRC 0.301 to 0.312). The median-ofsites recalibration, which protects against poisoned recalibration (Section 4.4), did not help here: without attack it made Hospital C's under-prediction worse (O/E 3.12) and shifted pooled calibration (O/E 1.14, slope 0.89). The two correc-tions are complementary: the robust fit for the shared model, the local intercept at each hospital.

4.6 Subgroups

Discrimination varied more across age bands than across sex or race for every method (Fig. 6): BFE-PW's AUROC fell from 0.957 (18-49 years) to 0.832 (≥85 years), as did centralised XGBoost's (0.965 to 0.841), and the largest gap between sexes or race groups was 0.032. BFE-PW underpredicted mortality at ≥85 years (O/E 1.17) and in patients with unrecorded race (1.28), where FedAvg did not (0.99), and over-predicted it at 18-49 years (0.76) and in Black (0.81) and Hispanic (0.83) patients; BFE-Stack's O/E stayed within 0.89-1.15 (Supplementary Table S8).

4.7 Ledger cost and scalability

Deploying the contract cost 1,147,766 gas. One aggregation of eight members required 37 transactions and about 3.28 million gas (Supplementary Table S9), with 92-285 ms per transaction on the local EVM. The protocol added about 30 s to local tree training, and artefacts were exchanged once (0.56 MB per XGBoost and 8.9 MB per random-forest member) instead of weights over 40 rounds.

Cost grows with consortium size K because attestations scale as K(K -1) (Table 8, Supplementary Fig. S3): at K = 32 a round needed 1,027 transactions and 77.8 million gas, and one finalizeRound call 4.99 million gas. Because a permissioned network sets its own gas limit, these figures show relative scaling.

4.8 Sensitivity analyses

As skew increased from IID to deterministic referral, FedAvg (0.897 to 0.903) and BFE-Stack (0.906-0.907) were stable and BFE-PW fell to 0.896 at p = 1 (Supplementary Fig. S4, Supplementary Table S10). Adding ICU, medication and microbiology predictors raised every method's AUROC by 0.009-0.013, and the extended set plus vital signs by 0.013-0.018 (both relative to the primary predictors), with the same ordering (Supplementary Table S11); they narrowed but did not remove the under-prediction at Hospital C (O/E 1.38 with vital signs). Decision curves showed positive net benefit for all federated models at thresholds of 1-30% (Supplementary Fig. S5).

>Discussion

5.1 Principal findings

Four findings stand out. First, the protocol's accuracy cost was small but real: with large hospitals the ensembles matched FedAvg or FedProx in AUROC but stayed below local-only and centralised XGBoost, and below FedAvg and FedProx in AUPRC. Second, collaboration paid off for small hospitals: at 5% of the data every federated method beat local models by about 0.02 AUROC, whereas above roughly 15,000 training admissions local models were as good. Third, the gate excluded grossly poisoned and substituted models and failed closed under collusion, but attacks that kept validation discrimination high passed it, and two did harm. Fourth, good pooled calibration hid two-fold underprediction at the hospital with a restricted laboratory menu, which a local intercept removed without sharing data.

5.2 What the ledger adds, and what it does not

Most of BFE's protection comes from two mechanisms that need no blockchain, a median-of-peers gate and a digest comparison; a trusted server or a signed append-only log would reproduce every prediction in this study. The ledger adds three properties. The admission rule runs as code that no participant can change after deployment, and each round's threshold is recorded on-chain; every input to it (artefact digest, signed score, decision) is non-repudiable and replicated at every hospital; and any hospital can recompute the admitted set and the weights from the ledger alone, so a deviating aggregator that re-admits a rejected model or alters a weight is detectable. These properties matter when hospitals do not fully trust the aggregator or each other [68, 69]. The guarantees are narrower than they may appear. Scores are self-reported, so a validator can post a plausible false AUROC without running the model; only grossly deviant scores, such as the zeros in A2, are attributable. The contract does not check the global specification against the admission decisions, so a deviating aggregator is detected, not prevented. Recalibration coefficients (and, for BFE-Stack, the meta-learner) are committed by digest but can be reproduced only by refitting on the hospitals' validation data, so BFE-Stack, with more fitted coefficients, is less auditable than BFE-PW, although it was the more accurate variant. Finally, the attacks that passed the gate (Section 4.4) show that it certifies average discrimination on peers' validation data, not the absence of harm to a subgroup or to calibration, and it never inspects recalibration. A consortium that relies on BFE should therefore pair the gate with subgroup-level attestation, which the contract could store as further scores, and with robust recalibration.

5.3 Comparison with previous work

Ledger-coordinated clinical learning has so far recorded updates, consent and site-level misbehaviour for parametric or linear models [26] [27] [28] [29] [30] [31] 65]. BFE differs in recording one admission decision per model artefact, computed by the contract from hash-bound peer scores, and in testing substitution and collusion separately from training-time poisoning. Multi-Krum protected FedAvg as well as the gate did against gross flipping, but the gate leaves an auditable record of each decision. One-round ensembling is established [23, 24, 52], and model-level federation of random forests has been evaluated across real hospitals [25, 49, 53, 82]; our accuracy results agree with that work, including the findings that small hospitals gain most [49, 50] and that decentralised models match central ones in AUROC but lose on threshold-based measures [48].

5.4 Clinical deployment and generalisability

A deployment would follow the roles in Fig. 1: one validating node per hospital, a governance board holding the registration key (preferably as a k-of-n multi-signature), and rounds scheduled by the board, for example quarterly or after a coding or assay change. Before scoring its own patients, each hospital verifies the admitted artefacts against the ledger and fits its own intercept on local validation data. The ledger then records which model version was in use, on what evidence it was admitted and who attested to it, which supports the change control that clinical model governance requires [68].

Three results bear on transferability. The protocol accepts any model family that uses the agreed feature schema. Its advantage over local modelling appeared where hospitals had a few thousand admissions, as small and resource-limited hospitals do, although with about 7-25 deaths per validation split peer AUROCs near τ were noisy, and no attacks were tested in that regime. Hospital C, without lactate, blood-gas, troponin or liver panels, resembles many district hospitals in low-and middle-income countries; there a local intercept, which needs no data sharing, raised the share of deaths identified from 3% to 36%. And on the care-era partition, BFE-Stack had the highest AUROC among the federated methods and local-only models no longer outperformed federation. What does not transfer automatically is the threshold τ = 0.70, which assumes outcomes on which a useful model clearly exceeds it; for harder outcomes the threshold, and the number of validators needed for a stable median, must be set from pilot data.

5.5 Limitations

The hospitals were carved from one US academic centre, and their heterogeneity was imposed by rule; the care-era partition adds unengineered shift but still comes from one institution, with one feature vocabulary. Real inter-institutional differences in coding, assays and record systems were absent, and no external validation was performed, for example on eICU-CRD [35]. The ledger ran on a single-process EVM without consensus or node failure, so latencies are simulator timings and gas is the transferable quantity. The contract is a prototype without an attestation quorum, deadlines, commitreveal of scores, a multi-signature governor or key revocation; it blocks self-attestation by address rather than by hospital, and finalizeRound cost grew about as K 2.5 between 16 and 32 hospitals, so one call would exceed a 30-milliongas block at about 65 hospitals. A deviating aggregator was argued to be detectable but not tested.

Privacy rests on data locality, not on a formal guarantee [4]: here "privacy-preserving" means only that no patient record leaves its hospital. Every hospital receives every peer's full tree models, whose split thresholds and leaf values encode training data; leaves held at least 20 training rows (random forest) or a Hessian weight of 5 (XGBoost), which limits but does not prevent membership inference [83], and a recent attack reconstructed much of clients' training data from federated tree models [84]. The federated recalibration shares exact per-site gradients and Hessians, and threshold selection shares per-site counts of true and false positives. No differential privacy, secure aggregation [85] or membership-inference audit was applied, and because a model trained on personal data may itself be personal data under the GDPR, sharing it needs its own legal basis. Random forests were exchanged as Python pickles, which a malicious hospital could use to run code on its peers; a deployment needs a non-executable format. The attacks did not include backdoor triggers or adaptive attacks optimised against the gate [11, 12, 86]. Comparisons mix aggregation rule and model family; the MLP-member ablation separates them only in part, and lossless federated XGBoost was represented only by its centralised ceiling [22]. Each hospital's validation split served for early stopping, attestation, recalibration and threshold selection, which may make recalibration slightly optimistic. Finally, the outcome was in-hospital death at a 24-hour landmark only, and several analyses were added after the first results were known (see Reporting).

6. Conclusion

A smart contract that admits locally trained tree models by hash-bound peer validation scores gave a consortium a verifiable record of which models entered its mortality model and why, excluded grossly poisoned and substituted models and failed closed under collusion, at a cost of about 0.01 AU-ROC against local models with large hospitals and none when hospitals were small. It did not detect attacks that preserved pooled discrimination, and its recalibration needed a robust, median-based form. Its value is auditable governance rather than accuracy, and it now needs testing on a multi-node permissioned network across independent hospitals.
