REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. Abstract

The effects of stroke on the developing brain are thought to depend on age at stroke onset, but the nature of this relationship is unclear. While it has been suggested that younger age at stroke onset might facilitate recovery, there is also evidence that critical developmental periods in early childhood confer increased vulnerability to injury. Associations between age at stroke onset and childhood stroke outcomes are mixed and often inconsistent, and the roles of lesion factors such as size, laterality and location are poorly understood.

We investigated how age and lesion factors relate to chronic (6+ months post-onset) sensorimotor, expressive language, receptive language, and cognitive/behavioral impairments in 469 childhood stroke survivors enrolled in the SCOUTS study (median age at stroke=9.3 years; 57% male). We used ordinal logistic regression models to characterize associations between outcomes and age at stroke onset, left and right hemispheric lesion volumes, and years to chronic assessment, and we used voxel-based lesion-symptom correlations to evaluate associations with lesion location. We also tested whether age at stroke onset moderates relationships between lesion factors and chronic outcomes.

When considered in isolation, younger age at stroke onset was significantly associated with worse outcomes across all domains, but in multivariable models, statistically significant associations were observed only for language outcomes. Larger lesion volumes in each hemisphere were significantly associated with worse contralateral sensorimotor outcomes and with worse language and cognitive/behavioral outcomes. For language outcomes, associations with lesion volume were strongest for the left hemisphere, and older age at stroke onset amplified the detrimental effect of large left hemisphere lesions on expressive language outcomes. Lesion-symptom analyses mapped sensorimotor impairments to structures including the putamen, sensorimotor cortices, superior parietal cortex, and cortico-spinal projections. Expressive and receptive language impairments both mapped to mostly left-lateralized and partially overlapping frontal, temporal and parietal structures, with the strongest associations in the white matter underlying left posterior temporo-parietal areas. Cognitive/behavioral impairments mapped to bilateral regions associated with action mode and somato-cognitive action networks including right dorsolateral prefrontal cortex, left inferior frontal and supramarginal gyri, and inter-effector nodes of the right pre-central gyrus.

These results provide novel insights into how age at stroke onset and lesion factors contribute to childhood stroke outcomes, map domain-level impairments to specific lesion locations, and indicate that the vulnerability of left hemisphere language networks to injury may increase as language becomes more left-lateralized during development.

Introduction

Childhood stroke, defined as strokes occurring between 29 days and 18 years of age 1 , is an uncommon but significant source of long-term morbidity and disability that can greatly reduce quality of life, lead to long-term dependence, and place substantial burdens on patients, parents, and caregivers. While some childhood stroke survivors recover without significant long-term consequences, others suffer from chronic impairments in motor, language, and/or cognitive functions [1] [2] [3] . However, the factors underlying this variability in long-term outcomes are poorly understood, posing an obstacle to accurate clinical prognostication.

The effects of stroke on the developing brain are thought to depend, in part, on the age at stroke onset 1, 4, 5 , but the nature of this relationship is unclear. It has been proposed that injuries occurring earlier in life may be associated with better outcomes due to enhanced plasticity and greater capacity for reorganization in the immature brain [4] [5] [6] [7] [8] , but there is also evidence that earlyonset lesions might produce worse outcomes than lesions that occur after critical developmental periods 9, 10 , and findings on this topic are mixed and often inconclusive 4, 8 . For example, strokes occurring between 29 days and 6 years of age have been associated with worse impairments in cognitive flexibility, processing speed, and verbal learning compared to pediatric strokes occurring outside of this period 11 , but strokes occurring between 1 and 6 years of age have also been associated with better neuropsychological outcomes compared to strokes occurring earlier or later in childhood 12 . Interestingly, a recent study reported that strokes occurring in late childhood are associated with worse long-term outcomes than early childhood or neonatal strokes, but only after accounting for lesion factors 13 , suggesting the possibility of more complex dependencies among age at stroke onset, lesion characteristics, and long-term outcomes.

Lesion location is an important factor contributing to chronic stroke outcomes in adults [14] [15] [16] [17] [18] [19] , but its association with childhood stroke outcomes is poorly understood 4, 8 . Voxel-based lesionsymptom mapping (VLSM), a technique for relating outcomes after focal brain injury to the affected brain structures 20 , has been used extensively to map the anatomy underlying a wide range of post-stroke outcomes in adults [20] [21] [22] [23] [24] [25] . However, lesion-symptom relationships identified in adults cannot be assumed to generalize to children 3, 4 , and aside from a few recent studies that have applied VLSM to study outcomes after neonatal brain injury [26] [27] [28] , we are not aware of any studies that have applied VLSM to study chronic childhood stroke outcomes. Instead, prior pediatric lesion studies have primarily investigated how chronic outcomes relate to coarsegrained lesion features such as total infarct volume [29] [30] [31] , the affected arterial territories 32, 33 and whether the lesion affects cortical structures, subcortical structures or both, limiting the granularity of identifiable relationships. Findings regarding lesion-symptom relationships in children have been largely inconclusive, with conflicting evidence regarding basic but clinically relevant questions. For example, there are conflicting reports regarding whether lesion laterality contributes to the risk of chronic language impairments after childhood stroke 4, 8, [34] [35] [36].

We therefore aimed to comprehensively characterize how age at stroke onset and lesion size, laterality and location relate to chronic domain-level neurological impairments after childhood stroke. Because the effects of brain lesions may depend on the developmental state of the brain at the time of injury 8, 10 , we also aimed to evaluate whether age at stroke onset modifies the effects of lesion factors on childhood stroke outcomes. To this end, we analyzed lesion and chronic neurological outcome data obtained from a large multi-site pediatric arterial ischemic stroke cohort (N=469) enrolled in the Seizures and Children's Outcome after Stroke (SCOUTS) study in what is, to our knowledge, the largest lesion-symptom mapping study of childhood stroke outcomes to date.

Materials and methods

Dataset

Clinical imaging and outcome data were obtained from N=528 children (age 29 days to 18 years) who were prospectively enrolled in the VIPS 37 or VIPS II 38 studies. Acute arterial ischemic stroke was confirmed by review of clinical presentation and neuroimaging data, which were independently reviewed by a board-certified pediatric vascular neurologist (H.J.F.) and neuroradiologist (M.W.), respectively. Long-term follow-up data were collected through the Seizures and Children's Outcomes after Stroke (SCOUTS) study 39 . A flow diagram is provided in Supplementary Figure 1. The parent studies did not include neonatal stroke. Children with neuroimaging data sufficient for lesion segmentation were considered for inclusion in the analyses. Children were excluded if the time between stroke onset and neurological assessment was less than 6 months. A total of N=469 patients met inclusion criteria, with ages ranging from 29 days to 18 years. Clinical and demographic characteristics are summarized in Table 1.

Outcome measures

Outcomes were measured using the Pediatric Stroke Outcome Measure (PSOM) 40 , a validated measure of functional outcome based on clinical neurologic assessment that scores deficits in five domains: right and left sensorimotor, expressive language, receptive language, and cognitive/behavior. If PSOM data were not available, outcomes were measured using the Recurrence and Recovery Questionnaire (RRQ) 41 , a parent-administered assessment that correlates strongly with the PSOM 41 . PSOM and RRQ are scored on a 4-point ordinal scale. Higher scores indicate greater impairment.

Standard protocol approvals, registrations, and patient consents

The SCOUTS study was approved by an Institutional Review Board (IRB) at Vanderbilt University Medical Center, which served as the IRB of record for all participating domestic sites. Local IRB approved the study at all participating sites outside the United States in the SCOUTS study and at each enrolling site for the VIPS and VIPSII studies. This analysis was approved by the University of Iowa Institutional Review Board (Project ID 201705278). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants or parents of children, as applicable, in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. In some instances, chart review was performed for follow-up data in participants that had previously consented to research, and the need for re-consenting participants was waived as the research was deemed minimal risk. Follow-up data were collected through chart review with a waiver of consent for minimal risk research, or through standardized parental questionnaires.

Neuroimaging data

Neuroimaging data collection and image processing steps are described in full detail elsewhere 39 , and are only briefly described here. Typical pediatric stroke MRI protocols included T1, FLAIR, gradient echo, diffusion weighted imaging (DWI), and apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC) sequences. Raw imaging DICOMs were sent from the participating sites to the IPSS Neuroimaging Core at the Hospital for Sick Children in Toronto 42 , and from there to the University of Iowa IPSS Imaging Core. Lesions were manually traced in native space using FSL, with primary reliance on DWI and ADC. The native MRI and co-registered lesion mask were aligned to an MNI152 template brain via linear and nonlinear registrations implemented in ANTs. SynthSR was used to upsample and normalize clinical images to 1mm isotropic resolution prior to registration 43 . Enantiomorphic normalization was used to improve registration for unilateral lesions 44 , and cost-function masking 45 was used for bilateral lesions. All lesion segmentations and registrations were reviewed by experts, including A.D.B. and J.B., and any errors were manually corrected. Investigators involved in lesion tracing and editing were blind to behavioral outcomes. Lesion volumes were estimated as 1mm isotropic voxel counts in template space to remove variability due to factors such as head size or brain volume. Lesion segmentations were resampled to 2mm isotropic voxel resolution prior to VLSM analyses.

Statistical analyses

Ordinal logistic regression models of chronic neurological outcomes

To characterize bivariate relationships between each outcome and age at stroke onset, we first fit single-variable ordinal logistic regression models that included only age at stroke onset as a predictor. We then fit multivariable ordinal logistic regression models that included age, lesion size, and lesion laterality as predictors of interest. Time from stroke onset to chronic neurological assessment was also included as a covariate in these models and in subsequent VLSM analyses since it has the potential to influence associations among the variables of interest 8 . Time since stroke was used rather than age at assessment because the latter was colinear with age at stroke onset (r=0.83, P<0.001).

To test whether the effects of extensive damage to each hemisphere on chronic outcomes might depend on age at stroke onset, we defined interaction terms between each of the hemispheric lesion volume variables and age at stroke onset, and we used a bootstrapped (1000 iterations per outcome) Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) model selection procedure to identify the most likely model given the data 46, 47 . Specifically, we fit four nested models for each outcome that included the same set of predictors described above, but differed with respect to the included interaction terms: Model 1 included no interaction terms and corresponded to the "base" model, Model 2 included only an interaction term for right hemispheric lesion volume, Model 3 included only an interaction term for left hemispheric lesion volume, and Model 4 included separate interaction terms for left hemispheric and right hemispheric lesion volumes (i.e. "full" model). The model that was selected most frequently across bootstrap iterations was selected as the "final" model for interpretation, although for completeness, we also explored the moderation effects from the "full" model (i.e. Model 4) for each outcome.

We interpreted individual predictor effects in each multivariable model if the omnibus likelihood ratio test was statistically significant after Bonferroni-Holm family-wise error (FWE) correction across omnibus tests for all outcomes at PFWE<0.05. For completeness, we report both percomparison and Bonferroni-Holm P-values for each predictor in each model, with Bonferroni-Holm correction applied across all predictors included within each model. We also report 95% CIs on the adjusted cumulative odds ratios (aORs) for each standardized predictor. Selected models that included interaction effects were followed up with conditional effects analyses that estimated aORs and 95% CIs for the effect of a 1 standard deviation increase in the lesion volume of the relevant hemisphere at standardized age levels defined at -1.5, -1, 0, and 1 standard deviations from the average age, corresponding to ~3.5m, ~3y, ~9y, and ~15y of age.

Voxel-based lesion-symptom mapping

We conducted mass-univariate voxel-based lesion-symptom mapping (VLSM) analyses using the Iowa Brain Behavior Modeling Toolkit 48 , available at (https://github.com/jcgriffis/ibb_toolkit), running in MATLAB R2025b (The MathWorks), computing non-parametric Spearman correlations at each voxel to test for associations between voxel lesion status and each outcome. Mass-univariate VLSM allowed for the use of non-parametric statistics (i.e. rank correlations) that are appropriate for evaluating monotonic associations with ordinal outcomes that may be non-linear. We note that large-scale comparisons of mass-univariate and multivariate VLSM approaches have not identified a consistent advantage for multivariate approaches in inferential analyses 49 , and both approaches are similarly susceptible to result displacement when not accounting for lesion size 50, 51.

Voxels were analyzed if they were lesioned in at least 5 patients 51 . Analyses were conducted using both full and partial Spearman correlations to illustrate the raw lesion-symptom relationships and to provide clarity regarding the effects of covariate control on the results 52 . Partial Spearman correlation analyses included age at stroke onset, time from stroke onset to neurological evaluation, and left and right hemispheric lesion volumes as covariates since they have the potential to influence lesion-symptom associations 8 . We chose the conservative approach of separately modeling left and right hemispheric lesion volumes to allow for the possibility that associations between lesion volume and chronic outcomes might be lateralized, in which case covarying total lesion volume would misestimate the true effect of lesion volume in each hemisphere.

Statistical significance was determined using permutation testing (5000 permutation iterations). Permutation testing for partial correlation analyses used the Freedman-Lane procedure 53 . Wholebrain statistical thresholds were determined using the continuous family-wise error control (cFWE) procedure developed and validated for mass-univariate VLSM 54 . This procedure identifies FWE-corrected thresholds that control the probability of at least v false positive voxels across the whole brain at the nominal alpha level (i.e. 0.05), where v is a user-selected suprathreshold voxel count parameter. Here, we used v=100. Following the procedures described by Mirman and colleagues 54 , we also calculated the post-hoc "effective false discovery rate" (eFDR) at the selected v=100 threshold, reflecting the proportion of surviving voxels that are expected to be false positives given the selected v threshold, and the number of suprathreshold voxels. For example, if 2000 voxels survived at a v=100 threshold, the eFDR would be 5% (i.e. eFDR=100/2000). Results were considered statistically significant if the post-hoc eFDR was less than or equal to 5%.

For the primary LSM of sensorimotor outcomes reported in the main text, all lesions were flipped across the midline to render them mirror-symmetric, and the sensorimotor outcome variable was defined as the sensorimotor domain score corresponding to the body side contralateral to the stroke lesion; for patients with bilateral lesions, the sensorimotor domain with the highest impairment score was used, and flipped lesions included all lesion sites in both hemispheres. Separate analyses were also performed for each outcome using the original unflipped lesions.

Voxel-based moderation analyses of stroke onset age and lesion status

To interrogate whether voxel-level lesion-symptom associations varied depending on age at stroke onset, we performed voxel-wise moderation analyses by fitting ordinal logistic regression (proportional odds) models at each voxel that included (1) an interaction term between age at stroke onset and that voxel's binary lesion status, and (2) the main effects of each variable, along with left and right hemispheric lesion volumes and the time interval from stroke onset to chronic assessment as covariates.

Permutation testing (1000 iterations) was used to determine the statistical significance of the moderation effects using a permutation testing approach for logistic regression effects in the presence of covariates 55 . We used a smaller number of permutations than for the LSM analyses due to the substantially higher computational time required for permutation analyses of voxelwise ordinal logistic regressions, but prior work indicates that as few as 500 permutations is sufficient to produce stable and reliable P-values when computational demands are a concern 56 . The cFWE procedure was used to control the whole-brain FWE for each analysis at 0.05 with v=100. Results were considered statistically significant if the post-hoc eFDR was less than or equal to 5%. Statistically significant results were characterized via conditional effects analyses for peak voxels, analogous to those described for the multivariable ordinal logistic regression models above, evaluating the effects of a binary change in lesion status (i.e. 0 to 1) at each level of age.

Lesion-symptom mapping of chronic impairments

Contralateral sensorimotor outcome

The strongest and most robust associations were observed in the putamen, internal capsule, precentral gyrus, post-central gyrus, inferior parietal lobe, superior parietal cortex, insula, superior temporal gyrus, and portions of the deep white matter containing both projection and association pathways (Figure 3; Supplementary Figure 4). Discussion

Here, we aimed to characterize how age at stroke onset, lesion size and laterality, and lesion location relate to chronic neurological outcomes after childhood stroke. Younger age at stroke onset showed consistent associations with worse outcomes across domains when modeled in isolation. When age was included in the multivariable models, we observed domain-specific associations with worse language outcomes. Larger lesions in each hemisphere were associated with worse sensorimotor outcomes for the contralateral body side, and with worse cognitive/behavioral outcomes. While extensive damage to both the left and right hemispheres was associated with worse language outcomes, associations were strongest for the left hemisphere, and the risk of poor expressive language outcomes conferred by extensive left hemispheric damage increased with older age at stroke onset. Beyond characterizing these associations, we also generated the most comprehensive set of pediatric lesion-symptom maps from the largest pediatric lesion sample to date, made possible by a large international consortium collecting data for over a decade, which we will make available as a resource for clinicians and researchers. Together, these results provide novel insights into how chronic impairments after childhood stroke relate to age at stroke onset and to the underlying lesion anatomy.

Associations between age at stroke onset and chronic outcomes

There are opposing views on how age at stroke onset relates to outcomes after pediatric stroke. Strokes occurring earlier in life might allow for greater plasticity and more effective compensation that could facilitate recovery 5, 6, 8 , but strokes occurring in early childhood have been associated with worse outcomes relative to neonatal strokes 11, 33 , suggesting a period of increased vulnerability in early childhood 8, 10 . In our sample, younger age at stroke onset was generally associated with worse outcomes across domains (Supplementary Figure 2A), but when age was modeled jointly with lesion factors, it only showed significant associations with language outcomes (Table 2). This suggests that age at onset, lesion characteristics, and chronic outcomes are interdependent. Indeed, age at onset was weakly but significantly correlated with both left and right hemispheric lesion volumes in our sample (Supplementary Figure 2B), suggesting that the observed associations between age at onset and chronic outcomes were partially driven by correlated lesion laterality and volume effects. A recent study reported the opposite pattern, i.e. that older age at stroke onset increased the risk of poor outcomes, but only when onset age was modeled jointly with lesion characteristics 13 , although several differences complicate direct comparisons; for example, that study featured a substantially smaller patient sample than the current study (i.e. N=285) and examined stroke onset age using relatively large age bins (i.e. neonatal, 1 month to 5 years, and 5+ years). Even so, both studies point more generally towards the existence of dependencies among age at stroke onset, lesion characteristics, and chronic outcomes, such that the age-outcome relationships observed in a given sample might depend on how lesion characteristics are distributed across stroke onset ages, highlighting the importance of modeling lesion factors when evaluating age-outcome relationships.

Notably, we found that the detrimental effect of larger left hemisphere lesions on expressive language outcomes increased with older age at stroke onset (Figure 2B). Similar trends were observed for receptive language, and right hemisphere lesions also showed a similar trend in the opposite direction both when exploring the moderation effects from the "full" moderation models (Supplementary Figure 3) and when inspecting the unthresholded maps from the voxelwise moderation analyses (Supplementary Figure 7). Together, these observations suggest the hypothesis that the detrimental effects of extensive left vs. right hemisphere damage on expressive language outcomes may shift in opposite directions as language systems become increasingly left-lateralized during development 61, 62 . However, despite our large sample size, the only statistically significant moderation effect was that of age at stroke onset on the relationship between left hemispheric lesion volume and expressive language outcomes, and while the exploration of subthreshold and statistically non-significant effects is useful for hypothesis generation, these hypotheses require confirmation in larger patient samples.

Lesion-symptom mapping of sensorimotor outcomes

Impairments on the sensorimotor PSOMs have been associated with poor performance on neuropsychological tests measuring visuomotor coordination and fine motor skills 40 . We observed robust anatomical associations with sensorimotor outcomes that localized primarily to regions such as the putamen, internal capsule, primary somatosensory/motor areas, and white matter locations consistent with the trajectories of cortico-striatal, cortico-spinal, and other projection pathways (Figure 3, Supplementary Figure 4A). These results appear broadly consistent with prior work identifying lesion correlates of motor and somatosensory deficits [63] [64] [65] [66] [67] and functional outcomes 68 in adult stroke patients and in survivors of neonatal stroke 27, 28, 69, 70 , suggesting that lesion locations associated with chronic sensorimotor impairments may be relatively consistent across the lifespan 4.

Lesion-symptom mapping of language outcomes

Impairments on the expressive language PSOM have been associated with poor performance on expressive vocabulary and category fluency tests, while impairments on the receptive language PSOM have been associated with poor performance on receptive vocabulary tests 40 . While factors such as lesion laterality have not been consistently associated with language outcomes after pediatric stroke 4, 8, 34 , we found that chronic language impairments were more strongly associated with the extent of damage to the left hemisphere (Table 2, Supplementary Figure 3) and showed robust voxel-level associations with lesions affecting predominantly left-lateralized structures (Figures 4 5).

Classical models of language organization posited separate cortical modules for speech comprehension and production in the posterior temporal and inferior frontal cortices, respectively, but converging lines of evidence indicate that these processes depend on dynamic interactions across distributed fronto-temporo-parietal networks that are organized via dorsal and ventral processing streams 19, [71] [72] [73] [74] . Our results are generally consistent with this view 75 , finding no clear anterior/posterior distinction between lesion sites associated with expressive and receptive language deficits, and impairments in both sub-domains localized to a set of primarily leftlateralized regions including the posterior superior and middle temporal cortices, anterior temporal lobe, supramarginal gyrus, inferior frontal cortex, and deep white matter locations consistent with the trajectories of white matter pathways subserving the dorsal and ventral processing streams such as the arcuate, superior longitudinal, and inferior fronto-occipital fasciculi 72, 76, 77 (Supplementary Figure 4).

Notably, both expressive and receptive language outcomes showed the strongest associations with lesions affecting posterior temporo-parietal structures and the underlying deep white matter (Figures 4 5). There is evidence that these regions correspond to language network hubs 78, 79 , and white matter pathways supporting both the dorsal and ventral processing streams converge in the underlying deep white matter [80] [81] [82] . Accordingly, lesions affecting these structures have been associated with broad and enduring language impairments in adults 19, 80, [82] [83] [84] , including language comprehension deficits 85 and poor recovery of impaired naming abilities 86 , and with abnormal fMRI activation patterns during language tasks 79, 87 , suggesting that damage may disrupt distributed processing throughout the broader language network 79.

Developmental factors could also explain why damage to these regions is associated with poor language outcomes in children. Language network development involves two general stages, with the first stage corresponding to the development of bottom-up systems starting in the posterior temporal cortices and extending to the anterior temporal lobe across the first 3 years of life, and the second stage culminating in the development of capabilities for sentence-level processing of complex syntax via fronto-temporal pathways that are solidified during adolescence 88 . Lesions affecting posterior temporal structures and their connections during the first stage might impede the establishment of foundational bottom-up pathways in the temporal lobe, while lesions affecting the same structures during the second stage might disrupt the establishment and refinement of top-down fronto-temporal pathways, suggesting that critical periods of vulnerability may exist in both early and late childhood. Consistent with this view, a recent study of 53 children with neonatal stroke found that damage to posterior temporo-parietal regions was associated with increased need for speech therapy at 2 years of age 28 , suggesting that even very early damage to these structures can substantially impede normal language development.

Lesion-symptom mapping of cognitive and behavioral outcomes

Impairments on the cognitive/behavioral PSOM have been associated with lower full-scale IQ and with impairments in self-control, metacognition, and, most prominently, behavioral regulation 40 . Unlike the language outcomes, no clear laterality effect was observed for cognitive/behavioral outcomes, with extensive damage to each hemisphere independently conferring similar risk of poor outcomes (Table 2). While statistically significant results were only obtained for the full correlation analyses (Figure 6), the spatial topographies of the unthresholded maps obtained from the full vs. partial correlation analyses were highly similar (spatial r=0.93), indicating that the covariates had little impact on the spatial pattern of lesionsymptom associations but primarily served to attenuate the observed correlations.

Impairments localized, in part, to bilateral frontal, insular, and inferior parietal areas associated with networks involved in cognitive control processes such as the cingulo-opercular/AMN and frontoparietal control networks 89, 90 (Figure 6). Damage to some of the implicated structures, such as the inferior frontal cortex, supramarginal gyrus, insula, and precentral/postcentral gyri has also been previously associated with impairments on subtests of the WAIS in adults 23 , consistent with prior studies linking the cognitive and behavioral PSOM to measures of full-scale IQ 40 . Local peaks within the right pre-central gyrus cluster also showed close correspondence to inter-effector nodes of the SCAN (Supplementary Figure 6), which is thought to be involved in integrating motor/autonomic control with action planning, motivation, and volition 58 . These regions are also functionally connected with the AMN 58 , consistent with involvement in the regulation, modulation, and coordination of motor outputs to support goal-oriented behavior, and the right dorsolateral prefrontal cluster both overlapped with the AMN and was adjacent to a lesion site that has previously been associated with dysexecutive personality disturbances in adults 59.

Limitations

Our study has limitations that should be considered when interpreting the results and planning future studies in this area.

First, while the PSOM/RRQ are commonly used and validated tools for measuring outcomes after pediatric stroke, they are also very coarse-grained both in terms of the actual measurement scale (i.e. 4-point ordinal scale) and in terms of the granularity of the domain-level assessments. For example, the sensorimotor outcome scores do not differentiate among distinct types of sensorimotor deficits and can reflect a variety of underlying impairments including hemiparesis, hemiataxia, and loss of sensation. Similarly, the cognitive and behavioral outcome scores do not specify the nature of the corresponding cognitive and/or behavioral problems, complicating interpretation of impairment scores and associated lesion sites. Since this lack of granularity necessarily limits the specificity and interpretability of identified lesion-symptom relationships, future studies should prioritize the collection of more granular outcome measures.

Second, our sample may not have been sufficiently powered to allow for the identification of moderation effects, especially given the multiple comparisons corrections required by the voxelwise analyses. Power for moderation effects is often substantially lower than for main effects 91, 92 , and for voxel-level analyses, the power at any given voxel depends on the number of patients with damage to that voxel and the age distributions of patients with vs. without damage. Despite our large sample and broad lesion coverage, most voxels were damaged in fewer than 30 patients, complicating the estimation of stable voxel-level moderation effects. Even for ordinary multiple linear regressions, it is very difficult to accurately estimate power for moderation effects due to their dependence on multiple factors including the main effect magnitudes, the interaction effect magnitude, the correlation structure, and the shape of the interaction 92 , and this difficulty is amplified by our use of ordinal logistic regression models. It is therefore possible that our analyses lacked sufficient power at many or even most voxels, especially if age at stroke onset only weakly and partially modifies 92 the effects of lesion location, as suggested by the conditional effects analyses for the hemisphere-level moderation effects (Figure 2B; Supplementary Figure 3).

Finally, despite our large patient sample, patients with perinatal/neonatal stroke were not included. While this was by design in that the parent study focused on childhood stroke, it complicates direct comparisons against other studies that have evaluated effects of age at stroke onset in mixed samples that include both perinatal/neonatal and childhood stroke survivors. We therefore emphasize that our conclusions cannot be generalized beyond childhood stroke. Evaluating whether similar results are obtained in a sample that includes both neonatal and childhood stroke is an important avenue for future work.

Conclusions

We characterized how chronic neurological outcomes relate to age at stroke onset and lesion characteristics in childhood stroke survivors, and conducted the largest and most comprehensive lesion-symptom mapping study of childhood stroke to date. While younger age at stroke onset was consistently associated with worse outcomes when considered in isolation, multivariable models suggested this was partially explained by associations with lesion characteristics. We also found that the detrimental effect of extensive left hemisphere damage on expressive language outcomes increased with older age at stroke onset, and we identified subthreshold effects suggesting the possibility of interactions between age at onset and lesion characteristics for other outcome domains. Lastly, we identified robust fine-grained lesion-symptom associations with domain-level neurological outcomes, highlighting lesion location as a potentially important factor contributing to childhood stroke outcomes. Upon publication, we will also share the lesion-symptom maps generated by this study as a public resource for clinicians and researchers.
