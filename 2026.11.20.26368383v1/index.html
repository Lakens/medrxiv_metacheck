To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 3 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Metacheck does not yet analyze automatically whether sentences which include non-significant p-values are correct, but we recommend manually checking the sentences below for possible misinterpreted non-significant p values.

Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). Statcheck might still miss one-sided tests that are described in another sentence, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

1. Introduction

Amyotrophic Lateral Sclerosis (ALS), is a fatal neurodegenerative disease of the motor neuron network, marked by the progressive degeneration of upper and lower motor neurons. This neuronal loss leads to progressive muscle weakness, impaired mobility, and loss of essential functions causing complete paralysis over the years and untimely death due to respiratory failure (Goutman et al. 2022).

ALS is considered a multifactorial disease, wherein both environmental factors and genetic predisposition, along with their complex interactions, play critical roles in disease onset and progression (Vasta et al. 2022). ALS is predominantly a late onset disorder, typically manifesting around the fifth or sixth decade of life, however considerable variability in the age of onset has been reported, including earlier presentation in certain geographical populations, especially Asians compared to their western counterparts (Lin et al. 2020). Several epidemiological studies have consistently indicated a male predominance, with a male to female ratio of approximately 2:1 (Chio et al. 2020). Approximately 90-95% of the cases are sporadic, with no apparent family history, whereas the remaining 5-10% are familial and typically associated with inherited pathogenic variants in specific genes. However, advances in genetics have shown that the distinction between sporadic and familial ALS is not absolute, as genetic susceptibility also contributes to many apparently sporadic cases. Consequently, ALS is now recognised as a genetically heterogeneous disorder, with pathogenic variants in multiple genes disrupting diverse biological pathways that ultimately converge progressive motor neuron degeneration (Al-Chalabi, Andrews, and Farhan 2024; Menge, Decker, and Freischmidt 2025).

The widespread use of next generation sequencing has greatly expanded the number of genes linked to ALS. Variants in C9orf72, SOD1, TARDBP, and FUS account for substantial proportion of genetically resolved cases, while many additional genes contribute less frequently. Importantly, the distribution of these variants differs across populations. For example, C9orf72 repeat expansion are the predominant genetic cause of ALS in European populations, whereas SOD1 variants are encountered more often in Asian cohorts (Y.-P. Chen et al. 2021; Nishiyama et al. 2017; Z. Y. Zou et al. 2017) . These differences highlight the need for population specific studies, particularly in underrepresented populations where the genetic spectrum of ALS remains incompletely defined.

Currently, there is no cure for the disease. The FDA approved pharmacological treatments-Riluzole, and Edaravone offer only modest benefits by extending the life expectancy by a few months (Jaiswal 2019). Recently, the FDA has approved Tofersen, the first antisense oligonucleotide drug targeting specifically for patients with SOD1 mutations (Miller et al. 2020) , marking a significant advancement in mutation specific treatment. One of the reasons that makes the diagnosis of ALS and thereby therapeutic intervention extremely difficult is the heterogeneous clinical and genetic presentation of the disease which varies substantially between individuals.

ALS typically presents with an insidious onset, often beginning with focal limb weakness in one of the limbs in spinal onset cases, which gradually progresses to involve other regions, including bulbar muscles, leading to dysarthria and dysphagia. In bulbar onset ALS, the disease initially manifests as subtle speech disturbances or swallowing difficulties, followed by progression to limb weakness and eventual loss of ambulation. Some individuals may also exhibit cognitive decline and behavioural impairments with signs showing emotional vulnerability, thereby falling in the ALS-FTD (Frontotemporal dementia) spectrum (Chio et al. 2020; Rusina, Vandenberghe, and Bruffaerts 2021; Strong et al. 2017; Ziser et al. 2025).

Clinical diagnosis of ALS is primarily based on neurological evaluation, supported by electromyography (EMG) and nerve conduction velocity (NCV) studies to rule out other ALS mimics (Vidovic et al. 2023).

Diagnosis is further classified as definite, probable, or possible ALS according to the revised El Escorial criteria (Ludolph et al. 2015).

Since identifying SOD1 through linkage analysis and candidate gene screening nearly three decades ago (Rosen et al. 1993), recent advances in genetic technologies, particularly next-generation sequencing, have led to the discovery of over 40 candidate genes associated with ALS (Suzuki et al. 2022). These findings have significantly expanded our understanding of the disease's heterogeneous molecular aetiology, with ongoing research continuing to uncover additional genetic contributors.

While recent studies have improved our understanding of the genetic basis of ALS in Indian populations and have identified several diseases associated variants across different patient cohorts (Khurana et al. 2026; Narain et al. 2018; Vats et al. 2015 Vats et al. , 2017)). However, ALS is not defined by its genetics alone.

Patients often show striking differences in disease onset, progression, symptom profile, and overall clinical course, even when carrying variants in the same gene (Tzeplaeff et al. 2024). This variability suggests that understanding ALS requires not only the identification of genetic factors but also careful clinical characterization of affected individuals.

In recent years, it has become increasingly clear that ALS extends beyond the selective degeneration of motor neurons. Alongside progressive muscle weakness and functional decline, many patients experience non motor symptoms that substantially affect daily life and quality of life. Among these, sleep related disturbances are particularly common. Poor sleep quality, frequent nocturnal awakenings, excessive daytime sleepiness, and respiratory difficulties during sleep have all been reported in ALS patients (Ahmed et al. 2015; Boentert 2019). Despite their clinical importance, these symptoms often receive less attention than motor manifestations, and their relationship with disease characteristics remains incompletely understood.

With this background, the present study sought to examine ALS from both a genetic and clinical perspective. Patients were screened for established ALS associated genes and repeat expansions, while WES was performed in selected genetically unresolved cases. In parallel, disease severity was assessed using the ALSFRS-R, and sleep related symptoms were evaluated using validated questionnaires. By combining genetic analysis with detailed clinical and sleep assessments within the same cohort, this study aimed to provide a broader picture of disease heterogeneity and to better understand the diverse factors that contribute to the ALS phenotypes.

2. Materials and methods:

>Materials and methods:

2.1. Recruitment of study participants

A total of 73 patients diagnosed with ALS were recruited from Department of Neurology, Institute of Medical Sciences, Banaras Hindu University, a tertiary care academic medical hospital in Northern India. Diagnosis was established by experienced neurologists according to the revised El Escorial criteria following comprehensive clinical evaluation and supporting investigations, including magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), electrophysiology (EMG), and nerve conduction velocity (NCV) studies.

Detailed demographic and clinical information, including family history, was collected for each participant. Disease severity was evaluated using the Amyotrophic Lateral Sclerosis Functional Rating Scale-Revised (ALSFRS-R). Sleep related symptoms were assessed using the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI), Epworth Sleepiness Scale (ESS), and REM Sleep Behaviour Disorder Screening Questionnaire (RBDSQ). Individuals presenting with symptoms suggestive of ALS but lacking confirmation through clinical and neurophysiological investigations were excluded from this study. Age and sex matched healthy controls were additionally screened for rare or novel variants identified during genetic analysis. The study was conducted following approval from the Institutional Ethics Committee, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants and/or their family members.

2.2. Genetic Studies

5 ml peripheral blood was collected in a EDTA vial from each patient after proper written informed consent and subsequently genomic DNA was extracted using salting out method. DNA was quantified using Nanodrop spectral analysis (Thermo Fischer, USA) and its quality was assessed by agarose gel electrophoresis (100V, 50 mins, 0.8% agarose gel in 1X Tris-acetate-EDTA buffer).

Targeted genetic screening of ALS candidate genes (SOD1, TARDBP and ANG): Initially, mutation screening was done for 3 major ALS candidate genes including SOD1, TARDBP, and ANG, respectively.

For that, 100ng of extracted genomic DNA was amplified using a thermal cycler (Applied Biosystems, USA) by PCR. The reaction mixture contained dNTPs, PCR buffer, 10 pmol/μL each of forward and reverse primers, Taq polymerase, and the volume was made up to 25μL using MilliQ water. The PCR product was purified by using Exonuclease1 (Exo1) and Shrimp Alkaline phosphatase (SAP) (USB Affymetrix, USA). Purified PCR product was subjected to cycle sequencing using BDTv.1.1 (Applied Biosystem, USA) and post-sequence cleanup, the product was run on a 3130 or 3500 ABI genetic analyzer (Applied Biosystems). Sequencing results were analyzed using Finch TV software (v.1.4.0) and the obtained sequences were compared with reference sequences using the NCBI BLAST tool.

Screening of repeat expansion of C9orf72:

The samples were tested for the presence of pathogenic G4C2 hexanucleotide expansion in the first intron of C9orf72 gene using repeat-primed PCR and fragment analysis using an ABI 3500 Dx genetic analyser (Applied Biosystems, USA) according to the protocol as previously described by Shamim et al. 2019. PCR conditions and the primers used in the study can be made available upon request.

Screening of ATXN1, ATXN2, ATXN3 and ATXN7:

Trinucleotide repeat expansions in the ATXN1, ATXN2, ATXN3, and ATXN7 were also screened using repeat-primed PCR and fragment analysis using an ABI 3500 Dx genetic analyser (Applied Biosystems, USA) according to the protocol previously established by Sharma et al. 2022).

2.3. Whole exome sequencing:

Whole exome sequencing (WES) covering approximately 99% of coding regions and complete mitochondrial genome sequencing were performed using Illumina next generation sequencing platforms. Exome sequencing achieved an average coverage of 50-60×, while the mitochondrial genome was sequenced at a depth exceeding 1000X, with more than 90% of target bases covered at ≥ 20X. Sequence reads were aligned to the human reference genome (GRCh37/hg19) using BWA-MEM, and variant calling was performed following the GATK best practice workflow. Read duplication, base quality recalibration, and indel realignment were carried out using Sentineon tools, and variant were identified using the Sention HaplotypeCaller (Freed et al. 2017). A secondary variant calling pipeline based on DeepVariant was also employed for validation. Quality control filters were applied to exclude low confidence variants, and annotated variant datasets were generated using publicly available databases including OMIM, gnomAD, 1000 Genomes, GWAS Catalog, and MITOMAP.

Variants identified in the VCF file were prioritized based on their population frequency, sequencing quality and predicted functional impact. Variants with a minor allele frequency (MAF) below 1% or absent from population databases including 1000 Genome and gnomAD, were retained for further analysis. A minimum read depth of 20 and a CADD score of ≥20 was applied as additional filtering criteria. The potential functional effects of the prioritized variants were subsequently assessed in silico using SIFT, PolyPhen-2, PROVEAN prediction tools.

2.4. Statistical analysis:

Data were analysed using appropriate descriptive and inferential statistical methods. Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) while categorical variables were summarized as frequencies (%). Associations between categorical variables were assessed using Chi square test. Comparisons between two independent groups were performed using Mann Whitney U test or unpaired Student's t test, depending on the distribution of data. Relationship between clinical and sleep related parameters were evaluated using correlation analysis where appropriate. All statistical tests were two tailed, and a p value < 0.05 was considered statically significant.

3. Results:

>Results:

3.1. Clinical features of the patients with novel variations:

A total of 73 patients meeting the revised El Escorial Criteria for definite or clinically probable ALS were enrolled at the Institute of Medical Sciences, BHU, a tertiary referral centre in Northern India. The cohort predominantly comprised individuals from Northern India, with the majority originating from the Eastern regions of Uttar Pradesh, Bihar, and Chhattisgarh. The recruited patients were predominantly males (65.7%), with females comprising 34.2% reflecting a male: female ratio of 2:1 consistent with other population studies. The mean age of onset for all the patients was in the later years of the fourth decade (49.84 ± 10.37). Males exhibited a slightly earlier age of onset (48.54 ± 9.89) as compared to females (52.92 ± 11.13). Age wise distribution showed that, out of 73 patients, 23 patients (31.50%) were ≤ 44 years including 17 males and 6 females thereby indicating a young onset of ALS, 22 patients (30.13%) were between 45 and 54 with 16 males and 6 females, 22 patients (30.13%) were between 55 to 64 years with 12 males and 10 females and 6 patients (8.21%) were aged 65 years and above with 2 males and 4 females. Therefore, the age of onset was earlier than 50 years in 58.3% (28/48) in males and 44% (11/25) in females. There were no significant differences in the mean age between male and female patients. The earliest disease onset was observed in a male patient before 25 years of age, whereas the youngest female patient had onset before 30 years of age. About 72.60% of the patients diagnosed had spinal onset, while 27.39% patients had bulbar onset.

The mean age of the bulbar onset patients was 52.85±10.39, while that of limb onset was 48.98 ±10.40 (Table 1) and there was no female predominance in the case of bulbar onset in our cohort (male: female 11:9) (Table 2).

3.2. ALSFRS-R assessment:

The mean ALSFRS-R recorded was 25.63±6.92. Based on functional status, six patients (8.21%) had mild functional impairment (ALSFRS-R: 37-48), thirty-four patients (46.57%) had moderate impairment (ALSFRS-S 25-36), and thirty-three patients (45.20%) showed severe functional impairment (ALSFRS-R ≤24). There were no statistically significant differences between ALSFRS-R between the two groups of patients (limb onset vs. bulbar onset).

3.3. Sleep characteristics of the ALS cohort:

The mean ESS, PSQI, and RBDSQ scores were 9.78 ± 3.83, 10.91 ± 3.41, and 1.52 ± 1.60 respectively (Table 3). Excessive daytime sleepiness (ESS ≥10) was observed in 40 patients (54.79%), while poor sleep quality (PSQI>5) was present in 67 (91.7%). Symptoms suggestive of REM sleep behaviour disorder (RBDSQ ≥5) were identified in 5 (6.84%) patients (Table 4).

No significant correlation was observed between ALSFRS-R score and ESS, PSQI or RBDSQ scores (p>0.05) (Table 5). Similarly, comparison of limb onset and bulbar onset patients revealed no significant differences in ALSFRS-R, ESS, PSQI, or RBDSQ score (p>0.05 for all comparisons) (Table 6).

3.4. Targeted genetic screening of candidate genes:

Targeted gene screening by Sanger sequencing of all five exons of SOD1 revealed a potential polymorphism; p. Ser35Ile (Fig 1, Table7) however no known or novel pathogenic variants were identified by screening SOD1 or the coding exon of ANG in our cohort.

Analysis of TARDBP identified a heterozygous variant p.Ile383Val (NM_007375.4; c.1147A>G; rs80356740) in one patient (PS 18) (Fig 2, Table 7). This variant is classified as likely pathogenic in ClinVar and previously reported in Greek FTD patients (Charoniti et al. 2021). The carrier was a male patient in the 45-54 years of age group presenting with spinal onset ALS without bulbar involvement and a baseline ALSFRS-R score of 26. No additional pathogenic variants were identified in this gene.

3.5. Repeat Expansion Analysis:

Repeat primed PCR screening of C9orf72, ATXN1, ATXN2, ATXN3, and ATXN7 identified one female patient (PS 05) harbouring a pathogenic C9orf72 expansion exceeding the established disease threshold (>32 repeats). She presented with limb onset ALS that progressed to bulbar involvement over time, had a disease duration of 24 months, no significant family history, and demonstrated an ALSFRS-R score of 25 at evaluation. The patient also exhibited prominent sleep related complaints, including disturbed nocturnal sleep and excessive daytime fatigue.

Compared with controls, patients exhibited a higher frequency of intermediate and pathogenic expansions in ATXN1 and ATXN2. One individual (PS 43), a male patient within 55-64 years age group with a disease duration of 12 months and limb onset symptoms, carried a pathogenic ATXN1 expansion of 42 repeats (disease threshold is >39 repeats). Two additional patients both within the age group of 45-54 years; PS 15 with limb onset symptoms and PS 51 with limb onset that subsequently progressed to bulbar involvement, exhibited intermediate ATXN1 expansions (36 repeats each). An intermediate length ATXN2 expansion (30 repeats, disease threshold ≥32 repeats) was identified in one female patient (PS 52) within the age group of 55-64 years with bulbar onset symptoms . No intermediate or pathogenic repeat expansions were detected in ATXN3 or ATXN7.

3.6. Whole exome sequencing :

A subset of patients was selected for whole exome sequencing (WES) based on disease severity, as assessed using the ALSFRS-R scores. The clinical characteristics of the patients included in the WES analysis are presented in Table 8. WES identified rare and potentially pathogenic variants in multiple established ALS candidate genes, including SETX, ANXA11, SIGMAR1, ERBB4, and NEK1 (Table 9). In PS 57, a novel heterozygous, non synonymous, missense variant p.Val416Ala (c.1247T>C) in ERBB4 was identified; this variant was absent from population databases and predicted to be deleterious by various in silico pathogenicity prediction tools. PS 6 harboured a rare heterozygous missense variant p. Gln1349Lys (c.4045C>A) in SETX (MAF 0.000008), annotated as a variant of uncertain significance in ClinVar but consistently predicted to be damaging by various in silico pathogenicity prediction tools, along with a homozygous, non-synonymous variant c. *31A>G in the 3'UTR of SIGMAR1, predicted to be pathogenic. Notably, a novel NEK1 variant p. Gln592X (c.1774C>T) classified as likely pathogenic according to ACMG guidelines was identified in PS 39. A young onset ALS patient was found to carry a rare variant p. Arg452Trp (c.1354C>T) in ANXA11 together with a pathogenic variant in SIGMAR1 p.Arg208Trp (c.622C>T, rs11559048) as reported previuosly (Roychowdhury et al. 2024).

4. Discussion:

ALS is among the most devastating and fatal neurodegenerative disorders, phenotypically characterized by progressive paralysis and untimely death, affecting individuals of all ethnicities worldwide. The disease is increasingly recognized as a clinically and biologically heterogeneous disorder, with considerable variability in both its genetic architecture and disease presentation (Tzeplaeff et al. 2024). While substantial progress has been made in identifying genes associated with ALS, the factors contributing to differences in clinical manifestations, particularly non motor symptoms, remain incompletely understood. Therefore, in the present study, we aimed to explore the genetic basis of 73 ALS patients from one of the largest tertiary care hospitals in North India by combining targeted genetic screening, repeat expansion analysis, whole exome sequencing (WES) technique, and clinical assessment of sleep disturbances.

The demographic characteristics of our cohort were largely consistent with previous epidemiological studies. Similar to reports from other populations, the incidence was higher in males than females, yielding a male-to-female ratio of 2:1, (Longinetti and Fang 2019) (Chio et al. 2020). This male predominance has been consistently observed across multiple ALS cohorts and may reflect complex interactions between genetic, hormonal, and environmental factors (Czlonkowska et al. 2005; Trojsi et al. 2020). The majority of the patients in the present study had sporadic ALS, with only a single patient reporting a possible positive family history. This observation is in agreement with the widely accepted view that sporadic forms constitute the largest proportion of ALS cases worldwide (Feldman et al. 2022; Wolfson et al. 2023).

An interesting finding was the relatively young age of disease onset (49.84± 10.37 years) observed within our cohort which is significantly lower (p<0.05) than the reported pool means of 62.1-66.3 years old in European population (Al-Chalabi et al. 2014), (Sánchez-Martínez et al., 2019). A similar trend of young onset ALS cases has been seen in other Asian countries like Chinese population, where the mean age recorded was at 48-53 years (Gao et al., 2021), (Longinetti & Fang, 2019, ), (Chen et al., 2021). Our findings align more closely with those reported from other Indian and East Asian populations, where disease onset frequently occurs in the later part of the fourth decade of life (Khurana et al. 2026; Nalini et al. 2008). Population based studies have found that individuals with fALS tend to develop the disease earlier than that of the sALS patients, and for the sALS patients, mostly they are affected in the fifth or sixth decade of their life (Marin et al. 2015), however sALS patients in our cohort have the disease onset much earlier in the fourth decade, than those reported in the American or Caucasian population, with the mean age of onset at 49.84 ± 10.37 years. Previous studies have found that females have a predominance of bulbar onset compared to males (He et al., 2022), (Chio et al. 2020) but our study did not reveal any such correlation, where the male: female ratio was at 11:9.

Beyond demographic characterization, the present study also examined disease severity and sleep related symptoms. The mean ALSFRS-R score suggested that most patients were recruited during moderate stages of disease progression, although considerable variation was observed across the cohort. Such heterogeneity is expected in a disease like ALS and reflects differences in disease duration, progression rate, site of onset, and individual disease trajectories.

Sleep disturbances emerged as a prominent non motor feature within the study population. Poor sleep quality was the most frequently reported abnormality, with more than half of the patients (91.7%) demonstrating PSQI scores indicative of clinically significant sleep impairment. Excessive daytime sleepiness was also observed in a substantial proportion of individuals (54.79%), whereas symptoms suggestive of REM sleep behaviour disorder were observed in 6.84% patients which was comparatively less common in our cohort. These findings support growing evidence that sleep dysfunction represents an important but often under recognized component of ALS. While clinical attention is traditionally focused on motor disability and respiratory decline, sleep disturbances can substantially affect quality of life, cognitive performance, emotional wellbeing, and caregiver burden as can be seen in other neurodegenerative disorders as well apart from ALS (Bartolomei et al. 2018; Boentert 2019).

The mechanisms responsible for impaired sleep in ALS are likely multifactorial. Progressive muscle weakness can make repositioning during sleep difficult, leading to repeated awakenings and fragmented sleep. Muscle cramps, pain, fasciculations, and nocturnal discomfort may further contribute to [poor sleep quality (Boentert 2019; Lucia et al. 2021). Respiratory muscle involvement represents another important factor, as nocturnal hypoventilation can develop before overt daytime respiratory symptoms become clinically relevant (Gnoni et al. 2023; Lucia et al. 2021). In addition, psychological stress, anxiety, and concerns regarding disease progression may further exacerbate sleep disturbances. The coexistence of multiple contributing factors likely explains the considerable variability in sleep related symptoms observed among ALS patients.

Interestingly, sleep disturbances appeared to occur across the disease spectrum rather than being confined to specific clinical subgroups. No significant differences were observed between limb onset and bulbar onset patients, nor where there were any significant differences between male and female patients (Table 2). Furthermore, ALSFRS-R scores did not have any significant correlations with ESS, PSQI, or RBDSQ scores (Table 5). Although weak trends were observed, sleep related symptom did not appear to be directly linked to functional status at the time of assessment. This suggests that conventional motor disability scales may not fully capture the factors responsible for sleep dysfunction in ALS. As mentioned earlier, respiratory involvement, mood disorders, medication effects, pain and individual susceptibility may all influence sleep independently of overall motor impairment the absence of significant associations should also be interpreted in light of the cross-sectional design of the study, as sleep disturbances and motor decline may not progress in parallel throughout the disease course.

The genetic findings of this study further highlight the complexity and heterogeneity of ALS.

Comprehensive screening of SOD1 and ANG did not identify pathogenic variants within the cohort.

Although SOD1 remains one of the most extensively studied ALS genes worldwide, its prevalence varies considerably among different populations. Similarly, ANG variants have been reported in a small proportion of ALS patients and are often considered uncommon contributors to disease susceptibility.

The absence of pathogenic variants in these genes within our cohort may therefore reflect population specific genetic distributions. Only a single patient carried a likely pathogenic TARDBP variant (rs80356740). TARDBP encodes TDP-43, an RNA binding protein involved in multiple aspects of RNA metabolism, including splicing, transport, and transcriptional regulation (Balendra et al. 2025). Pathological aggregation and cytoplasmic mislocalization of TDP-43 represent one of the defining pathological hallmarks of ALS (Bilican et al. 2012; Smethurst et al. 2020). Interestingly, the identified variant had been previously reported in a patient with frontotemporal dementia, further emphasizing the clinical and pathological overlap between the neurodegenerative disorders (Charoniti et al. 2021). Although only one TARDBP positive patient was identified, this finding reinforces the central role of RNA dysregulation in ALS pathogenesis.

In our cohort, a single patient was also found to carry a pathogenic C9orf72 repeat expansion. While C9orf72 expansions represent the most commonly known genetic cause of ALS in many European populations, they appear to be less frequent in Asian cohorts (Liu et al. 2018; Z.-Y. Zou et al. 2017 ) (Vats et al. 2017). Our finding is consistent with previous studies in Indian ALS patients, where C9orf72 expansions have been reported in approximately 3.2% of patients and represent an established genetic contributor to disease susceptibility (Shamim et al. 2019; Vats et al. 2017a) . Although the frequency of C9orf72 expansions in India appears lower than that observed in several European cohorts, their identification remains clinically relevant. The detection of a repeat expansion in the present study further supports the inclusion of C9orf72 testing in routine genetic screening of ALS patients particularly as such mutations cannot be identified through conventional exon based sequencing approaches.

In addition to C9orf72 screening, repeat expansion analysis was performed in selected spinocerebellarataxia (SCA) associated genes; ATXN1, ATXN2, ATXN3, ATXN7. One clinically confirmed ALS patient was found to harbour an expanded pathogenic ATXN1 allele (42 repeats), while two other patients carried intermediate lengths repeat expansions (36 repeats respectively) in the same gene. An intermediate length repeat expansion (30 repeats) in ATXN2 was also identified in one patient in our cohort whereas no pathogenic expansions were detected in ATXN3 or ATXN7.

The coexistence of ALS and a pathogenic range ATXN1 expansion in the same individual appear to be rare. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first report of such an observation in an ALS patient.

Although pathogenic ATXN1 expansions are primarily associated with spinocerebellar ataxia type 1 (SCA1), intermediate length ATXN1 repeats have been suggested to increase the susceptibility to ALS.

Thus, the finding in our cohort is particularly noteworthy, although it remains unclear whether the pathogenic expansion contributed to the ALS phenotype or represents a coincidental finding. Similarly, intermediate ATXN2 expansions are recognized as genetic risk factors for ALS and are thought to increase disease susceptibility rather than cause the classical SCA phenotype. The presence of intermediate length expansions in both ATXN1 and ATXN2 is in accordance with previous observations suggesting that repeat length variations in these genes may influence ALS susceptibility or disease expression (Novy et al. 2024). These findings also point to the possibility of overlapping clinical features among repeat associated neurodegenerative disorders highlighting the complex genetic architecture underlying motor neuron disease (Morello et al. 2020; Novy et al. 2024; Tazelaar et al. 2020). The absence of expansions in ATXN3 and ATXN7 further suggests that suggest that such repeat expansions are unlikely to represent common contributors to ALS susceptibility within the present cohort.

Whole exome sequencing was performed in eight patients with an ALSFRS-R score <25, who remained genetically unresolved following targeted screening. Although the number of cases analysed was limited, the findings provide additional insight into the genetic complexity of ALS. Rare and potentially pathogenic variants were identified in several genes previously implicated in ALS; including ANXA11, SIGMAR1, SETX, ERBB4, and NEK1. Notably, a number of these variants were absent or extremely rare in publicly available population databases, highlighting the possibility that rare variants may contribute to disease susceptibility in at least a subset of patients.

These genes participate in diverse biological pathways relevant to motor neuron survival. ANXA11 has been implicated in vesicle trafficking and stress granule dynamics, processes increasingly recognized an important mechanism in neurodegeneration. SIGMAR1 plays a critical role in endoplasmic reticulum signalling, calcium homeostasis, and cellular stress responses. SETX encodes senataxin, a helicase involved in RNA processing and DNA repair, while ERBB4 contributes to neuronal development and synaptic signalling (Takahashi et al. 2013). NEK1 has recently emerged as an important ALS susceptibility gene involved in DNA damage repair, microtubule organization, microtubule formation, ciliogenesis all processes that are essential for neuronal integrity (Martins et al. 2021; Shalom et al. 2008) . Loss of function variants in NEK1 have been repeatedly reported to increase the risk of ALS (Mann et al. 2023), making the identification of a novel truncating variant in our cohort noteworthy.

The identification of variants affecting multiple biological pathways supports the view that ALS is not a single gene disorder but rather a genetically heterogeneous condition arising from disruptions in interconnected cellular processes (Maurel et al. 2018). Importantly, several variants identified in the present study were absent from the publicly available population databases, suggesting that they may represent rare or population specific genetic alterations. Such findings underscore the importance of conducting genetic studies in underrepresented populations, as variants that are rare or absent in European cohorts may contribute significantly to disease risk elsewhere.

Another notable observation was the detection of multiple potentially relevant variants within individual patients as seen in a previously reported young onset ALS case harbouring both ANXA11 and SIGMAR1 variants (Roychowdhury et al. 2024). This finding supports the emerging concept that ALS may, in some cases have an oligogenic basis, whereby the cumulative effect of multiple genetic variants contributes to disease susceptibility and progression. Similar observations have been reported in other ALS cohorts (Lattante et al. 2019; McCann et al. 2021).

Taken together, the findings of this study highlight heterogeneity in terms of both clinical presentation and genetic profiles of ALS in the Indian population. Sleep disturbances were common and represented an important non motor aspect of the disease, yet they were not directly associated with functional disability or clinical subtype. Simultaneously, genetic analyses revealed a diverse spectrum of novel, rare and potentially pathogenic variants, many of which affected distinct but biologically convergent pathways involved in neuronal survival. While the contribution of several variants requires further validation, their identification highlights the considerable genetic heterogeneity underlying ALS. As larger sequencing datasets and functional studies become available, improved interpretation of rare variants will enhance our understanding of disease mechanism.
