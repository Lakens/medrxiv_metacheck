Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Binge eating disorder (BED) is associated with obesity as well as behavioral and neurobiological changes in the reward and cognitive control systems of the brain. To better dissociate neural mechanisms of pathological eating behavior, functional connectivity (FC) analyses have shown promise. However, there is currently no replicable signature of BED derived from fMRI.

We investigated FC in 61 women with varying symptoms of binge eating. The three groups included 21 women with BED, 20 women with subsyndromal BED (subBED), and 20 controls without binge eating (noBE) matched in body mass index (BMI) and age. To identify neural signatures of BED and BMI, we used seed-based FC "fingerprints" (nucleus accumbens, NAcc, for reward and dorsolateral prefrontal cortex, dlPFC, for cognitive control) to predict group or BMI based on resting state and taskbased (food bidding) FC. Furthermore, we explored spatial patterns of FC to quantify associations with BED and BMI.

Both seeds of the reward and cognitive control networks robustly predicted BMI (rs = .33 -.46). The dlPFC seed predicted BMI across conditions (resting state: p=.002; task: p=.012) while the NAcc seed only predicted BMI based on task-based FC (p=.007). In contrast, FC failed to classify binge eating groups better than chance.

FC within reward and cognitive control networks primarily reflect obesity-related changes. Despite pronounced differences in eating behavior, BED could not be reliably dissociated from subBED or noBE using FC fingerprints. These results highlight challenges in identifying BED-specific functional markers.

Introduction

Binge eating disorder (BED) is the most prevalent eating disorder (1) with a lifetime prevalence of 0.6-1.8% in women and 0.3-0.7% in men (2). BED is characterized by a lack of control over eating, leading to the consumption of unusually large amounts of food in a short time (3). Since the definition of BED excludes compensation of binge eating episodes, it is typically associated with overweight or obesity (4). Therefore, patients with BED also show an increased risk for physical comorbidities associated with obesity including diabetes, metabolic syndrome, and cardiovascular diseases (5). Moreover, BED is associated with an increased incidence of mental disorders such as depression, anxiety (4), attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (6), or addiction (7, 8) that are characterized by higher levels of impulsivity (9, 10). Despite the known comorbidity of overweight/obesity and BED(4), discerning changes in brain function remains challenging. Elucidating differences in brain function that relate specifically to BED may facilitate improved treatment options for BED beyond psychotherapy (11).

On a neurobiological level, BED has been associated with altered function in the reward and cognitive control systems. Individuals with BED show increased activation of reward-related regions during the presentation of food stimuli in passive food-viewing tasks (12) and during food consumption in active food-tasting tasks (13) compared to matched controls. However, other studies found decreased neural activity in response to high-energy foods that may be consistent with those consumed during a binge eating episode (14). This pattern is consistent with the notion that greater severity is associated with heightened anticipatory reward processing ("wanting"), alongside relatively diminished consummatory reward processing ("liking"), specifically in response to food rewards (15).

On a behavioral level, rash-spontaneous behavior is more frequent (16) and cognitive control is decreased in patients with BED (17). Reduced cognitive control in patients with BED is reflected in lower functional connectivity (FC), lower fiber tract integrity, and higher structural connectivity between the frontal cortex and the striatum (18). Accordingly, resting state FC between the middle frontal gyrus (midFG) and the insula is lower in participants with BED (19). Similarly, FC between cognitive control (dorsolateral prefrontal cortex, dlPFC) and reward/salience networks is reduced in preadolescent children (amygdala and orbitofrontal cortex, (20)) and adults(striatum, (21)) . with BED. Taken together, BED is characterized by altered connectivity between cognitive control and reward networks, which may contribute to the loss of control over eating (21). Despite the growing number of studies examining FC in BED, it is not known whether presenting disorder-related stimuli (e.g., food) in a motivational context, such as bidding, would improve the accuracy of prediction and classification.

In contrast to BED, FC alterations in overweight and obesity have been more extensively investigated (22). Obesity has been associated with disrupted FC within large-scale brain networks, including the salience network, the default mode network (DMN), and the frontoparietal network (23). These alterations have been linked to heightened incentive salience of food cues, impaired cognitive control over eating, and increased interoceptive awareness of hunger signals (23, 24). Specifically, resting state FC of the anterior cingulate cortex (ACC) is reduced in obesity, whereas increased connectivity of the posterior cingulate cortex (PCC) and precuneus has been observed, indicating altered DMN organization (25). Additionally, altered resting state FC of the NAcc has been reported in individuals with obesity (26) and differences in FC with the primary ingestive cortex and the ventromedial prefrontal cortex predict changes in eating behavior in patients with depression (27), highlighting the central role of reward-related circuits. Although similar FC alterations have been observed during food-related tasks, with reduced DMN FC during food picture viewing (28) and increased FC within the salience network during food-related working memory tasks (29), many studies have not directly compared resting and tasks states.

Often, neuroimaging studies investigating obesity do not control for binge eating symptoms, potentially conflating associations of body weight with pathological eating (30, 31). Since BED is strongly associated with obesity, it is still unclear whether differences in brain function are largely driven by obesity or if specific signatures indicative of pathological eating can be isolated. To address this gap and disentangle shared and distinct FC alterations between BED and obesity, we examined FC in a sample of 61 overweight or obese women, including 21 individuals diagnosed with BED, 20 individuals exhibiting current binge eating behavior without meeting the diagnosis criteria for BED (i.e., subsyndromal BED, subBED), and 20 weight-matched controls without binge eating (noBE). Crucially, we investigated both resting state and task-based FC during a food bidding task, capitalizing on FC in response to relevant stimuli, which has been suggested to improve brain-behavior associations (32). To capture the functional relevance of reward and cognitive control processes, we used . seed-to-ROI (whole brain) FC profiles from the NAcc and dlPFC to predict BED and BMI.

The third group of participants reported having no lifetime history of subjective and objective binge eating or other eating disorders (noBE). The noBE group was matched to participants with BED according to age and BMI.

Experimental procedure

The study comprised an online assessment (36, 37) and two sessions of laboratory assessments: a behavioral session and an fMRI session. For the fMRI session, participants were asked to fast overnight (i.e., >8 h before the session) and we analyzed fasting levels of glucose, insulin, triglycerides, and ghrelin. We also collected repeated ratings of mood and metabolic state (38, 39). Thereafter, participants received a standardized breakfast, which ensured that they entered the MRI in a non-fasting state to increase inter-individual differences in brain responses to food. The fMRI measurement started with an anatomical scan, followed by resting state using the Inscapes movie (40), an effort allocation task (not analyzed here; (36, 41)) and the food bidding task.

The fMRI session lasted about 100 min. By the end of the session, participants received the previously earned snacks and money.

Food bidding task

In the food bidding task, participants were presented with standardized pictures of food and control objects (e.g., stationery; (42)) and asked to bid as much as they wanted to receive the presented objects by exerting physical effort. Food items were presented in 12 blocks (5 images per block) of four categories (high-calorie savory, high-calorie sweet, low-calorie savory, low-calorie sweet), and images were shown twice. Prior to each block, a fixation cross was displayed. At the end of the cue reactivity phase, participants were asked to indicate how much physical effort they were willing to exert to gain access to a buffet including the items shown previously. By pressing the grip force device, a blue ball shown in a vertical tube on the screen could be lifted, with the height of the ball being proportional to the force participants applied. The force was determined relative to each participant's maximum force that had previously been recorded in a training phase of an effort allocation task (41, 43, 44). Participants were asked to keep the ball at the height corresponding to the effort they were willing to exert for about five seconds during the bidding phase. The task took 14 min.

Data analysis Seed-based functional connectivity

To compute seed-based FC, we estimated seed-to-ROI connectivity vectors for each participant for both resting and task states using CONN (49). Fisher-z transformed correlation coefficients between each seed region and all other ROIs represented FC strength. To capture FC related to cognitive control and reward . processing, we selected two seed regions as defined by the Harvard-Oxford brain atlas, namely the NAcc (left and right) and the midFG (left and right), corresponding to the dlPFC (Fig. 1c). To compare our results with previous work, we also included the ACC as a seed, which has shown distinct FC profiles in obesity (25). To reduce the number of features (due to the moderate sample size), we averaged FC values for regions for both hemispheres. Hence, we used seed-to-ROI FC vectors (fingerprints) with 81 connections for each seed for prediction and classification.

Connectome-based prediction of BED groups and BMI

To classify group differences and predict BMI based on FC, we used seed-to-ROI FC vectors from resting and task states.

BED group classification.

To evaluate the discriminative capacity of FCs among distinct BED groups, we used a multiclass classification approach. The classification model consisted of a one-versus-all 3-class error-correcting output codes (ECOC) model with ensembles of decision trees as binary learners (100 ensemble learning cycles). This method involves training a separate binary classifier for each subset. The decision boundaries were defined by the ensemble learning method GentleBoost, a more stable and robust version of AdaBoost (51), which combines the predictions of multiple weak learners (decision trees). To guard against overfitting, we evaluated the model fit using 10-fold cross-validation (52). To assess the statistical significance of the predictive model accuracy, we performed permutation tests using a baseline model that included only age and BMI as confounders (1000 iterations; outcome was shuffled with confounders to keep their correlation structure; (53)). We fitted the model separately to resting and task state data for each seed region (NAcc, dlPFC, and ACC).

BMI prediction model.

To predict BMI, we used seed-to-ROI FCs fingerprints and elastic net regression (lasso, preset alpha = 0.5) with nested 10-fold crossvalidation (inner loop folds = 10; outer loop folds = 10) (see 53, 54). We iterated this process 20 times to select a typical model (i.e., R² closest to the median). Statistical significance was determined by using permutation tests (1,000 iterations), including age and group (BED, subBED, or noBE) as control variables. We performed two BMI predictions for each seed region using the seed-to-ROI resting state FC and task FC data. To further characterize the associations of FC with BMI, we explored the relationship between BMI and FC patterns in seeds that provided significant BMI.

predictions. We extracted the weights of the regions that contributed to the BMI prediction to create β-weight maps for all seed regions and resting and task states.

Seed-based FC maps and group analyses

To characterize the spatial patterns of FC related to the prediction of BED or BMI, we used exploratory whole-brain seed-based FC maps from the NAcc, dlPFC, and ACC seeds. To test for group differences, we used the individual seed-based maps in SPM12 (50). We estimated factorial designs for each of the three seeds and included the three groups, BMI, and age as predictors. Contrasts of interest included positive and negative correlations with BMI, as well as comparisons between the BED and noBE groups, indicative of a linear increase from noBE to subBED and BED.

To replicate the findings of Haynos et al. (21), we tested regions that showed significant resting state FC differences between individuals with BED and noBE with NAcc as the seed. We generated the ROIs as spheres with 2 mm radius using MarsBaR within SPM (55) at the reported significant peaks with the following MNI coordinates: x = -6, y = 52, z = 43 (left superior frontal gyrus; L SFG, BA 9); and x = -2, y = -54, z = 18 (left PCC, BA 23) and combined them into a single mask for small volume correction (SVC; Fig. 1c).

Statistical analyses and software

We preprocessed fMRI data using fMRIPrep 20.1.1 (46, 56), based on Nipype 1.4.1, CONN release 22.a (49), and SPM12 release 12.7771 (50). ROIs were created using MarsBaR. All statistical analyses were conducted with MATLAB R2022b or R 4.2.2 (57). We considered α ≤ .05 as significant. Data were visualized using Mango 4.1, ggplot2 (58), and cvms (https://github.com/ludvigolsen/cvms).

Results

To investigate differences in FC patterns in BED and obesity, we performed a comprehensive analysis involving the prediction of BMI and BED status, as well as the characterization of the spatial FC patterns using a seed-to-ROI approach.

Seed-to-ROI FC patterns do not differentiate BED

To assess whether FC patterns are indicative of the participants' binge eating behavior (BED status), we applied a supervised classification model on seed-to-ROI FC data. Seeds were selected within the reward (NAcc) and cognitive control (dlPFC) . networks, and both resting state and task-based FC data were evaluated (for the ACC, see SI). Across all seeds and modalities, the models did not predict participants' BED status above chance (all ps > .106, Fig. 2; See Fig. S2 for ACC results).

FC differences between BED and control groups

Since the prediction of BED by multivariate FC fingerprints was unsuccessful across all selected seeds and conditions, we explored whether this approach missed specific changes in BED. First, we attempted to replicate the results reported by Haynos et al. (21), in which the NAcc showed lower resting FC with the left SFG and left PCC in BED v. control. We compared the NAcc-to-whole-brain maps of resting state FC between noBE vs. BED using small-volume correction for the reported ROIs.

Contrary to our expectations, no significant group differences were observed in NAcc-SFG or NAcc-PCC connectivity (t = 0.00, p = 0.83, and t = -0.64, p = 0.88, respectively; Table S1). Moreover, while the participants belonging to the BED group in Haynos et al. (21) showed reduced NAcc-to-ROI FC compared to the control group, our results indicate that the BED group FC was slightly elevated relative to the noBE group when comparing the participant's mean FC for each ROI (NAcc-to-SFG: d = -0.20; NAcc-to-PCC: d = -0.42, where the negative sign indicates that the noBE group had a lower . mean FC than the BED group; Fig. 3). Additionally, we examined group differences in seed-to-voxel maps for both task and rest conditions for the NAcc, dlPFC, and ACC seed regions. No clusters survived whole-brain correction for multiple comparisons (see unthresholded maps in Figure S1 and neuroVault for use in future studies; https://identifiers.org/neurovault.collection:17852).

Seed-to-ROI FC patterns predict BMI

We next examined whether FC patterns were associated with participants' BMI.

Using seed-to-ROI FC from the resting state and task conditions, elastic net regression models significantly predicted BMI for multiple seeds. In the task condition, FC patterns of all ROIs predicted BMI (NAcc: p = 0.007, R² = 0.199; dlPFC: p = 0.012, R² = 0.166; ACC: p = 0.001, R² = 0.258). Statistical significance was determined using permutation tests (1,000 iterations) with a baseline model that included age and BED groups as confounders. In contrast, resting state FC was only predictive of BMI from the dlPFC seed (p = 0.002, R² = 0.243), while NAcc and ACC FC did not predict BMI (p = 0.69, R² = -0.029; and p = .67, R² = -.022, respectively; Fig. 4 and SI). To determine which connectivity patterns contributed to the significant BMI prediction, we examined the standardized elastic net regression coefficients, or βweights, from the seed-to-ROI models that predicted BMI: task-based NAcc-to-ROI, resting-state dlPFC-to-ROI, and task-based dlPFC-to-ROI connectivity. Regarding the NAcc seed, frontal and dorsal striatum regions were among the strongest contributors to the BMI prediction, although with mixed signs (higher connectivity with the putamen and lower connectivity with the caudate). Other contributions were observed for subcortical regions implicated in homeostatic and autonomic regulation, including higher hypothalamic connectivity and decreased connectivity with the nucleus tractus solitarius (NTS) and brainstem. For the dlPFC models, connectivity with the caudate contributed positively to BMI prediction in both resting-state and task conditions, together with frontal cortical regions, including the inferior frontal gyrus (Figure 5). As a follow-up analysis, we characterized spatial patterns using seed-to-voxel analyses. We tested where seed connectivity was associated with BMI both at an exploratory whole-brain level and within a reward circuit SVC mask (Figure S5; Table S2). Although task-based NAcc connectivity significantly predicted BMI, no corrected whole-brain or reward circuit voxel-wise effects were observed for the NAcc task analysis. The dlPFC seed-to-voxel associations were observed across the whole brain, with no corrected reward circuit SVC effects. The complete voxel-wise results are reported in Table S2.

Finally, to assess whether task-and resting-state FC captured similar or differential spatial patterns, we compared the standardized elastic net weights of ROIs selected in at least one of the modalities for the dlPFC seed. The task-based and resting state weight patterns showed a weak to moderate correspondence (median Pearson r = .18 and Spearman ρ = .39; Figure S6), with 14 predictors shared between the two models. Of these shared predictors, 92.9% showed weights in the same direction. These findings suggest that task-and resting-state dlPFC FC capture relatively distinct predictive patterns.

Discussion

BED frequently co-occurs with obesity (35) and has been associated with altered function in brain systems supporting reward processing and cognitive control (16, 17). However, disentangling neural mechanisms related to pathological eating behavior from those associated with obesity remains a major challenge. To this end, FC analyses have been proposed as a promising approach. Yet a robust and replicable fMRI-based neural signature of BED has not been established to date. Using . multivariate approaches, we examined FC patterns derived from a priori seed regions during resting state and task fMRI. FC patterns reliably predicted BMI beyond chance, whereas binge eating status could not be predicted using either resting state or taskbased FC. Among the examined seed regions, dlPFC connectivity emerged as the most robust predictor of BMI across brain states, while predictive effects of NAcc and ACC connectivity were primarily observed during task performance. Together, these findings suggest that alterations in cognitive control and reward networks are more closely related to obesity than binge eating behavior.

Contrary to our expectations, we found no reliable neural signature of binge eating behavior. Neither multivariate classification analyses nor mass-univariate comparisons of FC maps revealed significant differences between individuals with binge eating behavior and individuals without any history of binge eating. These findings contrast with previous work reporting altered frontostriatal FC in BED. For example, Haynos et al. (21) observed reduced resting state FC between the NAcc and frontal as well as posterior cingulate regions, which was associated with impaired reversal learning and increased binge eating frequency. Here, we were unable to replicate these findings. Instead, an ROI-based analysis suggested a numerically stronger FC among individuals with binge eating behavior in our sample. Differences in sample characteristics, preprocessing choices, or statistical thresholds may partly account for these discrepancies (59). In addition, both studies were characterized by modest sample sizes, which may limit reproducibility and increase sensitivity to sampling variability (60). The absence of statistically significant group differences does not preclude the existence of BED-specific alterations in FC. Instead, our findings suggest that potential BED-related FC differences are smaller than anticipated and potentially context-dependent or overshadowed by obesity-related alterations in brain function.

In contrast to binge eating status, BMI could be reliably predicted from FC patterns within cognitive control and reward networks. Among the examined regions, the dlPFC showed the strongest and most consistent predictive value across both resting state and task conditions. As a central node of the frontoparietal control network, the dlPFC is critically involved in executive functions such as inhibitory control (61), self-regulation (62), and the modulation of food craving (63, 64). The importance of the dlPFC for regulating eating behavior is further supported by preclinical work showing that prefrontal projections to the hypothalamus orchestrate cue-potentiated . food intake (65). In humans, stimulation of the dlPFC has been shown to reduce food craving (66). In line with this regulatory role, previous studies have often reported reduced dlPFC connectivity in obesity, particularly within frontoparietal networks (24) and lower global brain connectivity (67). At the other end of the spectrum, anorexia nervosa, which is characterized by excessive cognitive control over food intake, has been associated with increased dlPFC recruitment (68, 69). In addition to reduced FC to some brain regions (e.g., putamen and pallidum), we also observed that BMI prediction was driven by increased FC between the dlPFC and other regions of the motivational circuit, such as the ACC and the (70, 71) whereas decreases have been shown in anorexia nervosa (72) and bulimia nervosa (73). This suggests that obesityrelated alterations in dlPFC connectivity may not reflect a uniform reduction, but rather a more specific reorganization of network interactions. Guo et al. (74) recently reported increased variability of FC between the limbic and frontoparietal networks in individuals with higher BMI, suggesting greater fluctuations in the functional coupling between networks involved in reward processing and cognitive control. Increased connectivity may reflect compensatory processes or reduced neural efficiency, indicating that greater neural resources are required to maintain cognitive control in individuals with higher BMI.

Task-based connectivity further enhanced BMI prediction for reward-and control-related regions. While dlPFC FC predicted BMI in both brain states, predictive effects of ACC and NAcc FC emerged primarily during task performance. Participants completed a food-bidding task involving food cues (75) that reliably engage reward and cognitive control systems (76). Task states have been shown to enhance individual FC signatures (77) by increasing the ratio of within-subject to between-subject variability (32, 78). In line with this notion, food-related cognitive and motivational engagement during the task may have amplified obesity-related FC differences within reward and control networks (79). At the same time, the slightly reduced predictive strength of dlPFC FC during task performance may indicate that cognitive engagement partially compensates for intrinsic control deficits that are more apparent during resting state conditions.

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting the present findings.

First, the fMRI sample consisted only of women, reflecting the higher prevalence of BED in women (2). In addition, binge eating is influenced by ovarian hormones (80), and future studies may account for menstrual cycle phase or hormone levels. Second, . although comparable to many fMRI studies in the field, the sample size limits the robustness and generalizability of predictive modeling approaches. Larger datasets will be necessary to improve statistical power and model stability. Third, compared to the online assessment, the BMI range in our sample was limited by scanner constraints, restricting coverage of severe obesity. Finally, the cross-sectional design precludes causal conclusions about whether FC alterations precede or result from weight gain.

In conclusion, the present study demonstrates that FC within reward and cognitive control networks appears to vary primarily as a function of obesity rather than binge eating behavior in women. While BMI could be reliably predicted from FC patterns, we found no robust neural signature of binge eating behavior. These findings highlight the substantial overlap between obesity and binge eating behavior at the neural level and underscore the challenge of disentangling disorder-specific effects from obesity-related alterations. Future studies should therefore employ larger samples, longitudinal designs, and multiple brain states to better characterize individual variability in reward and cognitive control networks and to clarify their role in the development of obesity and disordered eating.
